Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper is a well written and timely evaluation of the impacts of climate change on future GoM ecosystem and fishery
productivity. It is unique in the aspect that it connects climate impacts on land (drought) to those in marine systems via
changes in freshwater nutrient inputs to GoM. This is an often postulated interaction (i.e., climate impacts on land and water
will impact marine systems) but there is a paucity of published papers that demonstrate or evaluate the phenomenon. The
authors use a combination of a 
nutrient-forced mass-balance ecosystem model (EwE) and mean future climate projections of
a drought index (PDSI) from an ensemble of 20 General Circulation Models (GCMs) representing a subset of CMIP5
ensemble members under the high baseline Representative Carbon Pathway (RCP 8.5). The authors derived a linear
relationship between historical nutrients and historical PDSI values and used the nutrient relationship to drive bottom up
productivity changes in the EwE model. The authors then force the EwE model with profiles across ranges in nutrient
changes (0 -2 fold 2014-2016 average nutrient levels) and ranges in fishing pressure (0-5 x average F from 2014-2016). This
represents an innovative application of an approach that has been applied successfully to evaluate potential climate change
impacts in other systems, albeit with different climate couplings (e.g., where temperature or zooplankton drive productivity of
EwE models; sensu 
https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.13471 ). In general this is the type of analysis that can be vital for risk
assessments of climate change impacts on marine fisheries as well as cumulative impacts of changes on land on marine
ecosystems. In general, I strongly support this type of approach, and applaud the authors for using an ecosystem model
(which captures indirect and cascading impacts) to evaluate climate change impacts on marine species. This can be a more
robust approach than a direct climate-linked single species modeling effort. 
However, there are a number of fundamental issues with the paper that I believe preclude it from being published until it is
revised. I hope the authors are able to correct and address these issues so it can be used to advance understanding of
climate change risk to marine ecosystems and fisheries. 
The largest concern I have is that the paper is predicated on a linkage between droughts and historical declines in GoM
catches that the authors do not demonstrate and which is not supported by the cited peer-reviewed papers (nor by the large
number of non-peer reviewed technical reports that are cited). The authors postulate that a long (~3 year) drought in the mid-
to late 1980’s induced a decline in biomass and catch that is reflected in the timeseries for both. These are plotted in Figure
1 (a) (e) and (f). However, one quickly notes that there are similar large drops in the PDSI index in ~2000 and ~2012 but no
similar subsequent drop in catch or biomass timeseries as was seen in the late 1980’s. In the discussion section (lines 160-
172) the authors try to address this and explain it as a difference in drought length (3 yrs for the earlier drought versus ~1
year for the others) and timing. This section is very speculative, without any citations save for one at the end of the paragraph
stating that the severity of the drought can be related to the contrast between preceding wet years. In fact the largest concern
I had in reading this section was that there was no mention of possible changes in fishery management or markets that could
also explain the decline in catch. Additionally, following the sharp drop in in both catches and biomass in the late 1980’s
catches remain low while biomass increases, pointing towards a non-biomass driver of low catches (e.g., shift in
management towards lower harvest mortality rates). Finally there are multiple disparate patterns in the magnitude and
sometimes the direction of biomass change and catch (this can be seen in 
Fig 1 (h) and (i); e.g., crab have the second
largest decline in biomass but increases in catch, similarly, younger menhaden had the larger declines in biomass than
older conspecifics but declines in older menhaden catches were larger than younger ones). This is of course a not
unexpected and well cited issue in fisheries, especially as biomass declines where fishers can increase effort to offset
declining biomass, or where catch declines but is stabilized over time and biomass increases due to implementation of

Similarly, the ESR (reference #1) states “The Gulf of Mexico Fisheries Management Council implemented a number of
Fishery Management Plans in the 1980s, and major Amendments restricting fishing effort were passed in the 1990s.“ (p22). 
Of course these management measures could have been implemented to address the decline that was driven initially by
drought. The way to address how much climate drove the decline is to run a hindcast or counterfactual simulation, although
these are challenging with ecosystem models because of model drift. So another approach would be to use a sensitivity
simulation to evaluate the hypothesis of prolonged drought versus short 1 year drought in driving declines. E.g., fixing the F
rate at a few different levels (e.g., those observed in the 1985-1990 period vs those observed in 2013), simulate a) 3 year
drought (sensu the 1985-1988) and b) a five year period with a 1 year drought. Something like this should be conducted to
demonstrate and support the postulation that underlies the projection simulations. In the absence of the sensitivity simulation
above, there is not sufficient evidence provided presently to rule out changes in harvest pressure, fisheries operations,
management or markets in driving declines in catch in the late 1980’s relative to early 1980’s. Given that the paper is
predicated on the postulated relationship between drought, nutrients, and declines in catches, establishing and supporting
this is extremely important. Additionally, I strongly suggest providing a historical time-series of harvest mortality by age for
menhaden (e.g., as a supplemental figure). It appears to my eye that the F rate for 1 year old fish is much higher during the
1985-1990 period than 2000 - 2016. Having the timeseries would help facilitate comparisons in relative fishing pressure
over time.. 
The second issue with the paper is that it is very difficult to discern precisely what methods were used in the simulations.
This needs to be clarified in the Methods simulation section. I have detailed a number of line by line suggestions below to
help clarify. There were two separate simulations conducted. For the first, I believe the correct methods were used for the
status quo versus future time series projections, but can’t confirm this based on the current methods section. 
For the first
simulation, the authors ran the EwE model for a 5 year simulation period across Nutrient and F profiles based on average

confidence intervals around projected declines may be large. 
A growing number of papers include comparisons of projections of high and low carbon emission scenarios, e.g., RCP8.5 vs
RCP2.6 or the more recent CMIP6 SSP126 vs SSP 585. These types of comparisons are extremely useful in IPCC and
other climate assessments because they reveal the relative risk of different levels of greenhouse gas emissions. To repeat
this analysis for RCP2.6 would be a major undertaking and so I am not suggesting that has to be done here, but including
multiple carbon emission scenarios is something the authors might consider in future work as it would help address an
important need for relative climate risk information for fisheries and marine ecosystems. 
In summary, I find the paper topic to be compelling, the methodology is established but novelly applied, and the figures and
text are clear and well executed. I appreciate the inclusion of the code and data. If the authors are able to address the
aforementioned concerns (and following line by line suggestions) I believe that this paper can be an important contribution to
the field of climate change and fisheries. While these suggestions are not trivial I also believe that they are achievable
revisions that will immensely strengthen the paper.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the ms by Berenshtein et al., the authors present data that indicates a severe drought in the southeast U.S., combined with
fishing pressure on ecologically important menhaden in the Gulf of Mexico, likely caused a decline in marine food webs and
fisheries catch in the 1980s. Using these results the authors modeled future fisheries in the GoM under simulated drought
conditions. First, I would like to thank the authors for expanding the knowledge on the ecosystem dynamics of this region,
and further exploring the role of freshwater/nutrient inputs in ocean ecosystems. I believe this could add to the growing body
of knowledge to an important socio-ecological system. With that said, I believe there are too many confounding factors that
could contribute to the lack of nutrients being the main driver to population dynamics in the region, and this would be
important for informing a more robust predictive model, and thus should be considered before publication. 
For the “historic” analysis on trends, I was a bit underwhelmed by the focus on 1982-86, then 1986-1992. The authors use
these two time series to compare trends in fisheries and environmental factors with the sole focus on drought and freshwater
inputs. Why not include the entire time series in an analysis that includes nutrient load relationships with fisheries, but also
the other known factors that influence marine food webs, such as temperature, wind/hurricanes, and the elephant in the
room…hypoxia? I think if this ms is to be published then it will be important to apply quantitative analytical approaches on a
beautiful historic time series that should be able to tease apart the influence of these various environmental factors from
1980-2016. I think without this analysis then the model is not fully informed. For example, in the time series in Fig. 1a, there
appears to be two other significant drought events in the region 1997-2000 and 2011-2014 (eyeballing), but fish biomass
(Fig. 1e) is stable or going up during those periods. What can explain that? 
Other Comments: 
Title: why phrase the title as a question? 
“This severe drought resulted in a reduction of more than 50% in the Mississippi River flow and nutrient input to the GoM.”
Having a difficult time fully understanding the point of this. I do understand that freshwater connectivity is an important
function of the Mississippi River/GOF system, but that same system in its current form brings an unnatural amount of
nutrients from ag runoff to the GOF, which causes an annual Dead Zone for fish. Given the well known relationship 
Intro: “Numerous studies indicated a significant correspondence between Gulf menhaden population dynamics and
environmental conditions such as temperature, wind, and river flow” What are those relationships between all of these
abiotic factors and fish? Will help the reader to better understand the system. 
“River to the GoM reached a record low annual and summer value of 107,717 and 41,560 t, respectively (Fig. 1c).” Would be
good to know what the historic pre-ag nutrient load was for the GoF. Are those data available or known? 
Figures: overall I thought they were impressive, but had to zoom in to 250% to read some of them. Also, the color schemes to
identify various species and groups are too challenging to interpret.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
A well written paper on a topic of great interest to scientists and managers. This work advances our understanding of
climatic impacts on marine ecosystems and fisheries due to changes in freshwater nutrient inputs. The authors evaluate the
impacts of severe drought, combined with fishing pressure for menhaden, on fisheries production in the Gulf of Mexico
(GoM). Firstly, the authors show that a prolonged and severe drought during the 1980s, combined with high fishing pressure
for menhaden, led to a depleted forage base, with cascading effects on other species and fisheries in the GoM. Secondly,
the authors use an ecosystem model to project changes in future fisheries production in the GoM under simulated drought
conditions. 
Overall, this is a laudable paper that covers an important topic on the impacts of drought on fisheries production in the GoM. I
applaud the authors for using an ecosystem model to evaluate climatic impacts on marine ecosystems and fisheries. The
methodology is well described and sound, and the findings support the conclusions in the paper. 
With that said, I found the paper to be lacking in its description of the mechanisms underlying the impacts of drought on
marine fisheries production published in the scientific literature. 
Furthermore, the paper would benefit from providing more
detailed information on the projected impacts of future droughts on the functional groups in the GoM. Nevertheless, the paper
is worth publishing through a more focused route with consideration of the comments and suggestions offered below. 
Major points 
In the introduction, the mechanisms underlying the impacts of drought on marine fisheries production should be better
described. Previous studies have shown that river flow is a ‘master variable’, which has a controlling influence on marine
fisheries production by regulating habitat availability, trophic dynamics, and fishers’ harvesting behaviour. Several key
references are missing and should be included in the paper (see the suggested additional citations at the end of this review).
It has been demonstrated that a trophic-driven mechanism for fisheries production is particularly important in the GoM, which
is an oligotrophic coastal region that receives limited nutrient supplies from oceanic currents and upwelling events (Wissel
and Fry, 2005). The inclusion of this information would help the reader to understand why the study focused on flow-induced

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

changes in nutrient inputs rather than habitat availability. 
More information on the projected impacts of future droughts on the functional groups in the GoM would be beneficial. For
example, how did the model projections suggest that more severe droughts will affect species composition – should we
expect less invertebrates and more piscivorous fish in the future? 
It is unclear why the study used catches rather than catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) as a dependent variable. Therefore, it
should be explained why catch rates, which account for changes in fishing effort, were not considered in the methods
section. 
Minor points 
Line 19: A ‘major depletion of fisheries’ should be better quantified. What constitutes a ‘major’ depletion? I suggest the
authors include the percentage reduction in fisheries production relative to the reference period. 
Line 40: The authors should include a sentence here to explain that previous studies have shown that variation in river flow
impacts fisheries production in the GoM mainly via changes in trophic dynamics rather than habitat availability. A trophic-
driven mechanism for fisheries production is particularly important in the GoM, because this oligotrophic coastal region
receives limited nutrient supplies from oceanic currents and upwelling events (Wissel and Fry, 2005). This would help the
reader to understand why the study focused on changes in trophic dynamics as the mechanism underling the relationships
between river flow and fisheries production in the GoM. 
Lines 60-65: The authors should better quantity the declines in biomass and catches. What was the decline in biomass and
catches in percentage terms relative to the reference period? 
Line 72: Previous studies describing the impacts of drought on marine fisheries production should be cited here (e.g.,
Gillanders and Kingsford 2002; Gillson, 2011; Broadley et al., 2022). 
Lines 73-76: If drought-induced increases in salinity can stress invertebrates in the GoM, then this leads to the question why
were these mechanisms not evaluated in the current study? It should be explained why this study focused on changes in
trophic dynamics and not habitat availability. Kimmerer (2002) provides an interesting evaluation of the effects of river flow
on estuarine fish via physical and trophic mechanisms. 
Lines 77-79: Once again, it should be made clear why this study focused on the impacts of river flow on fisheries production
in the GoM via trophic dynamics rather than habitat availability. The previous paragraph states that the physical effects of
river flow on invertebrates can be important in the study area, but the next paragraph ignores this point. 
Line 93: Please either define the abbreviation ‘MS’ or spell it out in full. 
Line 105 (and elsewhere): All percentages should be presented to one decimal place to ensure consistency throughout the
paper.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This paper is a well written and timely evaluation of the impacts of climate change on future 
GoM ecosystem and fishery productivity. It is unique in the aspect that it connects climate 
impacts on land (drought) to those in marine systems via changes in freshwater nutrient inputs to 
GoM. This is an often postulated interaction (i.e., climate impacts on land and water will impact 
marine systems) but there is a paucity of published papers that demonstrate or evaluate the 
phenomenon. The authors use a combination of a nutrient-forced mass-balance ecosystem model 
(EwE) and mean future climate projections of a drought index (PDSI) from an ensemble of 20 
General Circulation Models (GCMs) representing a subset of CMIP5 ensemble members under 
the high baseline Representative Carbon Pathway (RCP 8.5). The authors derived a linear 
relationship between historical nutrients and historical PDSI values and used the nutrient 
relationship to drive bottom up productivity changes in the EwE model. 
The authors then force the EwE model with profiles across ranges in nutrient changes (0 -2 fold 
2014-2016 average nutrient levels) and ranges in fishing pressure (0-5 x average F from 2014-
2016). This represents an innovative application of an approach that has been applied 
successfully to evaluate potential climate change impacts in other systems, albeit with different 
climate couplings (e.g., where temperature or zooplankton drive productivity of EwE models; 
sensu https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.13471 ). In general this is the type of analysis that can be vital 
for risk assessments of climate change impacts on marine fisheries as well as cumulative impacts 
of changes on land on marine ecosystems. In general, I strongly support this type of approach, 
and applaud the authors for using an ecosystem model (which captures indirect and cascading 
impacts) to evaluate climate change impacts on marine species. This can be a more robust 
approach than a direct climate-linked single species 
modeling effort.  
 
However, there are a number of fundamental issues with the paper that I believe preclude it from 
being published until it is revised. I hope the authors are able to correct and address these issues 
so it can be used to advance understanding of climate change risk to marine ecosystems and 
fisheries.
