Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript presents an operando X-ray diffraction (XRD) methodology, calibrated by Density Functional Theory (DFT),
to map hydrogen diffusivity and trapping in a complex precipitation-hardened steel (Hybrid 55). The authors claim this
approach allows for the direct spatial resolution of hydrogen transport regimes, distinguishing it from traditional bulk-
averaged permeation tests. While the combination of techniques is interesting, there are several critical issues regarding the
validation of the methodology and the interpretation of the trapping mechanisms that need to be addressed before
considering for publication. 
1. The authors rely heavily on DFT calculations to convert lattice strain into hydrogen concentration. However, the simulation
details raise concerns. In a complex alloy system like Hybrid 55 (VMoCrMnFe47NiAlSiC), a 54-atom supercell may not
statistically represent the random solid solution and local chemical environments accurately. Furthermore, introducing up to
5 Hydrogen atoms in such a small cell corresponds to a very high local concentration, potentially inducing artificial H-H
interactions that do not reflect the dilute limit or realistic clustering in the early stages of charging. 
2. During tempering, significant precipitation occurs, leading to the depletion of alloying elements (e.g., C, V, Mo) from the
solid solution matrix. Consequently, the chemical composition of the matrix in the tempered state differs fundamentally from
the nominal composition (or the as-quenched state).If the authors used the nominal composition for their DFT supercells to
calibrate the tempered samples, this would introduce systematic errors. 
3. The core premise is that the observed lattice strain is solely due to hydrogen lattice expansion. During electrochemical
charging, other factors may also induce strain, such as macroscopic stress generation due to the hydrogen concentration
gradient or surface stresses. The authors need to discuss how they differentiated between chemical expansion (volume
change) and elastic strain caused by the steep hydrogen gradient, especially in the "transient regime" mentioned. Has the
contribution of macroscopic stress been quantified or ruled out? 
4. While the methodology shows promise for BCC steels with fast diffusion kinetics, Could the authors discuss the
applicability of this technique to materials with significantly different diffusion characteristics, such as austenitic steels or
High Entropy Alloys (HEAs)? 
5. H is one of the environmental sources to embrittle alloys. Whether this kind of approach can be used for analyzing the
other light elements, like B, O, N, etc. Please provide some discussions on this based on the reference of "Environmental
embrittlement behavior of high-entropy alloys". 
6. The manuscript mentions the formation of precipitates during tempering (570 °C) and links them to embrittlement
resistance. There is a lack of direct microstructural evidence in the tested samples. TEM or Atom Probe Tomography (APT)
images of the tempered Hybrid 55 samples should be included to verify the size, distribution, and coherency of the Ni-rich
aluminides and carbides. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript reports an operando high-energy X-ray diffraction microscopy approach to quantify and map hydrogen
uptake and diffusion in high-strength steels with micrometer-scale spatial resolution. By correlating lattice-spacing (lattice
strain) changes with hydrogen concentration through DFT calibration, the authors generate spatiotemporal hydrogen
concentration maps and extract effective diffusion coefficients. The work further triangulates these findings with
electrochemical permeation and thermal desorption spectroscopy, providing a coherent mechanistic picture of how
microstructural trapping (e.g., precipitate-rich vs precipitate-free conditions) shapes hydrogen transport. 
In summary, I very much like this paper. It presents an innovative and compelling use of lattice-spacing information,
supported by strong experimental and modeling evidence, and it will be of broad interest to the hydrogen/materials
community. I recommend acceptance after minor revision addressing the points above. 
Overall, I find this study highly interesting and timely. Beyond the specific hydrogen-transport application, I especially
appreciate the deeper interpretation of lattice spacing as an information-rich observable: it does not only reflect elastic strain
or “structure,” but can be quantitatively linked—when properly calibrated—to underlying defect/solute populations. This
direction has been gaining momentum in recent years. For example, a 2023 Nature Communications paper
(https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36588-9) demonstrated that vacancy concentration can be quantitatively
extracted and mapped at high resolution by establishing a DFT–lattice-spacing correlation and using simulation to guide

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

experimental interpretation. Although the present manuscript does not address vacancies, the conceptual framework is
closely aligned: leveraging first-principles calibration to translate subtle lattice-parameter shifts into physically meaningful,
quantitative maps. I would encourage the authors to acknowledge such prior work and position these efforts as part of a
broader class of “lattice-spacing-enabled quantitative mapping” methodologies; this would be helpful for readers and would
strengthen the context and impact. 
Regarding the technical content, the study is thorough and the evidence is generally convincing. I have only a few minor
suggestions that would improve clarity and broaden the usefulness of the method: 
1. Terminology consistency for the two microstructures/samples. 
Throughout the manuscript, the two conditions are referred to using multiple naming schemes (e.g., “precipitate-
rich/precipitate-free” in some places and “tempered/solution-annealed” in others). I suggest adopting one consistent
terminology (with the alternative terms stated once early in the paper) and using it throughout to avoid confusion. 
2. Scope, applicability, and limitations of the lattice-spacing-to-hydrogen mapping approach. 
The method is fundamentally dependent on detecting very small lattice-spacing changes attributed to hydrogen-induced
lattice expansion. However, lattice spacing can be influenced by many other factors, including precipitate-related strain
fields, residual stress, and microstructural evolution under mechanical loading. In situations involving significant plastic
deformation or evolving defect structures, the hydrogen-induced signal may become difficult to disentangle from other
contributions and experimental noise. It would strengthen the manuscript if the authors explicitly discuss (i) the practical
operating window where the method is most reliable (e.g., minimal concurrent microstructural evolution), (ii) major
confounding factors and how they can be mitigated or controlled, and (iii) a short outlook on what future extensions could
enable. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
This study presents an innovative operando imaging technique based on synchrotron high-energy XRD, enabling real-time,
spatially-resolved, quantitative visualization of hydrogen diffusion in steel at the micron scale. It overcomes key limitations of
traditional bulk methods and offers novel insights into hydrogen embrittlement. However, the following issues should be
addressed: 
1. The manuscript should clearly define the hydrogen measured by different techniques. It must be clarified whether the
hydrogen quantified by XRD refers solely to lattice hydrogen or also includes hydrogen in reversible traps. Furthermore, a
systematic discussion is needed to elucidate the distinctions and correlations among XRD-measured strain-coupled
hydrogen, TDS-measured desorbable hydrogen, and the embrittlement-relevant "diffusible hydrogen.". 
2. For the annealed samples containing retained austenite, the potential interference of hydrogen-induced phase
transformation on the XRD strain measurements should be thoroughly considered. It is recommended that the authors
supplement the relevant data to either demonstrate or rule out the contribution of phase transformation strain. 
3. Given that XRD detects only lattice strain-inducing hydrogen while TDS measures total hydrogen content, why does the
XRD-measured hydrogen concentration for the precipitate-free sample in Figure 3 exceed the TDS value? 
4. The TDS spectrum shown in Figure 3b begins at approximately 100°C and does not include low-temperature desorption
signals. For BCC-structured materials, these signals correspond to hydrogen in very shallow traps or lattice sites, which is
crucial for assessing the amount of hydrogen. Please explain the reason for the absence of this data segment or consider
supplementing it with low-temperature TDS data. 
5. The diffusion kinetics analysis for the precipitate-rich condition (Figure 5) is excellent and in-depth. However, an
equivalent analysis for the precipitate-free condition is lacking. It is recommended that the authors supplement the
manuscript with corresponding analytical charts for the precipitate-free sample. 
6. The construction of the current DFT calculation model requires further clarification. Firstly, the employed supercell size is
limited. Secondly, in simulating the actual alloy composition, key alloying elements such as Al are not considered. It is
recommended that the authors clarify the basis for approximating the alloy composition within this finite model and discuss
the potential impact of omitting key alloying elements on the accuracy of the model predictions. 
7. The reliability of the DFT calibration curve in Figure 1c, particularly within the low-concentration regime relevant to the
experiment, is a concern. The calibration data points begin at a relatively high concentration (~341 wppm), and the values at
lower concentrations are heavily reliant on linear extrapolation towards the origin. This method lacks necessary validation
from either lower-concentration calculation points or experimental data and may introduce systematic errors. Furthermore,
the plot does not display the origin (0,0). It is recommended that the authors discuss the physical rationale for constraining
the fit to pass through the origin (0,0) or supplement the analysis with calculation points at lower concentrations to strengthen
the credibility of the calibration. 
8. It is recommended to experiment and simulate with pure Fe as benchmark test to strengthen the reliability of this method.

Response to Reviewers' comments: 
 
Reviewer #1: 
1. The authors rely heavily on DFT calculations to convert lattice strain into 
hydrogen concentration. However, the simulation details raise concerns. 
In a complex alloy system like Hybrid  55 (VMoCrMnFe47NiAlSiC), a 54 -
atom supercell may not statistically represent the random solid solution 
and local chemical environments accurately. Furthermore, introducing up 
to 5 Hydrogen atoms in such a small cell corresponds to a very high local 
concentration, potentially inducing artificial H -H interactions that do not 
reflect the dilute limit or realistic clustering in the early stages of charging. 
We thank the reviewer for this important comment and agree that the scope and intent 
of the DFT -based strain–hydrogen calibration must be clearly stated.  Both 
experimental and theoretical studies have consistently shown that lattice expansion 
induced by interstitial hydrogen in the studied BCC structure is approximately linear 
over a wide concentration range and only weakly sensitive to local alloying chemistry. 
This behaviour has been established experimentally and rationalised theoretically as 
a Vegard-like elastic response of the host lattice (Kiuchi and McLellan, Acta Metall., 
1983; Fukai, The Metal –Hydrogen System; Jiang and Carter, Phys. Rev. B, 2004; 
Pérez et al., Phys. Rev. B, 2007). While chemical disorder broadens local site 
energetics, the average elastic coupling governing diffraction peak shifts remains 
robust. This average elastic response i s precisely the quantity probed by operando 
XRD. 
 
From a DFT perspective, a 54 -atom BCC supercell is not small. In metallic systems, 
bulk elastic behaviour is typically captured with approximately four atomic layers. The 
employed supercell contains seven atomic layers along each Cartesian direction and 
therefore fully exhibits bulk elastic character. Increasing the supercell size beyond this 
point would dramatically increase computational cost without providing a measurable

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

improvement in the predicted lattice strain response. Supercell size selection was thus 
guided by physical convergence considerations rather than arbitrariness. 
 
To represent chemical disorder, random solid -solution structures were generated 
using MedeA’s random substitution algorithm. Se veral independently generated 
configurations were examined, and their calculated diffraction patterns were found to 
be indistinguishable. This demonstrates that, for diffraction-averaged lattice strain, the 
specific local atomic arrangement does not influence the extracted elastic response. 
 
We acknowledge that any periodic DFT treatment of hydrogen necessarily implies 
nominally high concentrations. Increasing the supercell size reduces the nominal 
hydrogen concentration only gradually . For example, introdu cing a single hydrogen 
atom into supercells containing 54–432 atoms still corresponds to approximately 300–

We respectfully note that there is no contradiction  in our manuscript. XRD measures 
d-spacings from which we infer hydrogen -induced lattice strain. A significant portion  
of absorbed hydrogen must reside (temporarily) in and diffuse via interstitial sites, 
primarily tetrahedral interstitial sites at room temperature. There a re 12 tetrahedral

Thus, lattice strain is our observable, and it is indeed strain-coupled. Hydrogen atoms 
in interstitial sites are primarily mobile, also referred to as diffusible hydrogen. In 
contrast, hydrogen atoms may also reside in traps where the associated lattice strain 
is negligible or below the detection limit. This is because XRD detects average lattice 
strain, whereas traps often produce localised distortions that can remain undetected . 
We want to emphasise that the paper’s scope is not characterising traps.
