Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript reports a facet-engineering approach using NiS
₂
 crystal planes ({100} and {111}) to stabilize the β-phase of
PVDF, achieving broadband electromagnetic (EM) loss from MHz to THz. The authors combine DFT calculations, structural
characterizations (SEM, TEM, XRD, FTIR, AFM-IR, PFM), and EM response analysis to demonstrate that facet modulation
enables enhanced polarization and relaxation dynamics, breaking the Kramers–Kronig trade-off. The topic is original, the
data are extensive, and the idea of facet-modulated ferroelectricity in polymers is both novel and technologically relevant.
However, several methodological, mechanistic, and presentation aspects require significant improvement before
acceptance. 
1. The manuscript claims that {100} facets of NiS
₂
 stabilize β-PVDF by lowering its energy below α-PVDF. However, the
underlying electronic or interfacial mechanism is insufficiently elaborated. 
2. How does charge transfer or dipole alignment differ quantitatively between facets? 
3. Can authors provide charge density difference maps or Bader charge analysis to support the interfacial polarization
claims? 
4. The statement that this mechanism “breaks the Kramers–Kronig constraint” seems physically overstated, since these
relations are fundamental. The authors should instead clarify that multi-relaxation mechanisms mitigate the apparent loss–
bandwidth trade-off. 
5. Is there any specific cause of choosing NiS2 as the filler material? 
6. Provide the investigation of 
a control composite with randomly oriented NiS
₂
 or another inert filler to confirm that the
observed effects are truly facet-specific. 
7. The role of NiS
₂
 conductivity in EM attenuation must be disentangled from ferroelectric polarization effects (conductivity
data in Table S1 suggest notable variation). 
8. The PFM data (Fig. 3D–G) show phase hysteresis, but there is no macroscopic polarization–electric field (P–E) loop. A
P–E measurement or Sawyer–Tower loop is essential to confirm bulk ferroelectricity. 
9. The reported coercive voltages (0.41 V and 0.62 V) are relatively low. Clarify whether these values refer to local switching
or macroscopic measurement. 
10. Table S2 shows strong EM loss compared to other materials, but normalization (per thickness, per filler loading, etc.) is
inconsistent. 
11. Please include a clear benchmark plot (e.g., attenuation constant vs. frequency) comparing state-of-the-art polymer
composites. 
12. The claim that the system “overcomes the limitation imposed by Kramers–Kronig relations” is conceptually problematic.
These relations are not breakable for causal, linear systems. The authors could instead argue that nonlinear ferroelectric
polarization and multi-relaxation processes expand the effective response bandwidth. 
13. The reported THz shielding effectiveness (≈30 dB for 0.25 mm) is impressive but could also arise from multiple reflection
and scattering effects. Consider adding complex refractive index or impedance spectra to clarify this. 
14. The correlation between β-phase fraction and THz attenuation should be statistically supported (e.g., regression of β-
phase ratio vs. SET). 
15. The SEM/TEM images (Fig. 1B–E) show morphological differences, but facet indexing and atomic models should be
more explicitly correlated. 
16. A surface energy or coordination number comparison would reinforce the claim that {100} facets are more polarizing. 
17. The Introduction could better emphasize how this work differs from previous PVDF–inorganic interface engineering (e.g.,
doped or hybridized PVDF systems).

reconsidered after thorough revision. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by B. Cai et al. reports a method for preferential crystallization of the ferroelectric β-phase of PVDF on
specific facets of NiS2 nanocrystals. The authors attribute this effect to enhanced S-H interactions at the {100} surface.
However, the experimental data do not convincingly support this conclusion, and several key interpretations are either
incomplete or unsupported. 
1. The XRD diffractograms (Figs. 2A,B and S11) show a dominant β-phase peak in all sample, while the differences in FTIR
spectra are minimal and even disappears at 25% NiS2 loading (Figs. 2C,D and S12). These variations fall within the usual
batch-to-batch variability of PVDF and cannot be taken as solid evidence of facet-induced β-phase enhancement. 
2. A more fundamental issue is that the influence of processing conditions is barely addressed. It is well known that
mechanical stretching and elevated temperatures promote α- to β-phase transition in PVDF
[https://doi.org/10.1039/C3RA45134H]. The hot-pressing method used by the authors can itself induce β-phase formation,
with nanoparticles acting as stress concentrators. A similar effect has already been reported for PVDF-BaTiO3 system
[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.polymertesting.2024.108516]. The authors fail to decouple the contribution of thermal–mechanical
processing from any alleged facet-specific effects. 
3. The characterization of the PVDF/NiS2 composites physical properties also raises concerns. The PFM data are not
convincing. V-shape amplitude loops such of those in Figs. 3E,G are typical of electrostatic artefacts caused by tip-sample
interaction, not of genuine piezoresponse [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ultramic.2019.112839]. For samples dominated by non-
polar α-phase, the authors should have used stiffer AFM probes, controlled surface potential via KPFM, and performed PFM
in both contact and non-contact modes to verify that the response is truly piezoelectric rather than electrostatic. 
4. The dielectric measurements are also problematic. The authors conclude (lines 253-254) that “…due to the low
conductivity of P/N composites (Figure S17, Table S1), the dominant loss mechanism in the 2-18 GHz range is polarization
loss…”. This is contradicted by their own data. Table S1 shows a 4-orders-of-magnitude (!) conductivity increase in
P/N{100}, when NiS2 loading increases from 15% to 25%. Such a dramatic change should strongly influence conductive
losses, yet Fig. 4E shows no effect at all. This discrepancy is ignored, making further discussion of the electromagnetic
properties unreliable. 
5. The novelty of the “facet engineering” approach proposed by the authors is overstated. Surface-induced polymorphism in
both organic and inorganic systems is well established in literature [https://doi.org/10.1002/adfm.201503169;
https://doi.org/10.1039/C8CS00283E]. β-phase enhancement in PVDF composites with inorganic nanoparticles has already
been demonstrated [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.polymertesting.2024.108516]. The work does not introduce a sufficiently new
concept or mechanism. 
In summary, the “facet engineering” concept presented here is not novel, and β-phase enhancement in PVDF-nanoparticle
composites has already been demonstrated. The experimental evidence is weak, key controls are missing, and several
interpretations are questionable or unsupported. Overall, the originality and scientific rigor of the work do not meet the
standards of Nature Communications, and the manuscript should not be accepted for publication.

Q5: Is there any specific cause of choosing NiS2 as the filler material? 
Reply: Thank you for raising this insightful question. Our selection of NiS2 as the filler 
material is based on a comprehensive evaluation of crystal plane control, material 
stability, electromagnetic properties, and economic and environmental sustainability. 
The specific reasons are as follows: 
1. Feasibility of Crystal Plane Control: Our research focuses on optimizing the 
ferroelectric phase transition of materials by controlling the exposed crystal planes. 
NiS2 can be synthesized via facile methods such as hydrothermal synthesis to 
effectively control its macroscopic morphology. This enables selective exposure of 
specific crystal planes featuring highly ordered and stable atomic arrangements, 
providing an ideal model for investigating the interaction mechanisms between crystal 
planes and ferroelectric polymers (Angew. Chem. Int. Ed., 2019, 131, 18849-18855). 
2. The Comprehensive Advantages of Nickel-Based Materials: We prioritize 
nickel-based materials for the following reasons: High Stability: Compared to iron-
based and cobalt-based materials, nickel sulfides exhibit superior stability in air and are 
less prone to oxidation. This ensures long-term effectiveness during material 
preparation and application. Simultaneously, NiS2 eliminates magnetic interference: As 
demonstrated by our electromagnetic parameter testing (Figure S44), NiS 2 exhibits a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 13 -->

10 
 
real magnetic permeability (μ') close to 1 and an imaginary permeability (μ'') near 0, 
indicating negligible magnetic properties. This eliminates magnetic losses from 
contributing to absorptive performance, enabling a purer investigation of dielectric 
polarization (including crystal-plane-induced interfacial polarization and dipole 
polarization) in electromagnetic wave attenuation. 
3. Economic and Environmental Considerations: Nickel is abundant in the 
Earth's crust, relatively inexpensive, and its compounds exhibit low environmental 
toxicity, aligning with the requirements for sustainable material development. 
In summary, NiS 2 combines stable, tunable crystal planes, excellent chemical 
stability, distinct nonmagnetic properties, economic and environmental advantages, 
aligning perfectly with the design objectives of our study. Consequently, it was selected 
as the ideal filler material.

This study breaks through the conventional paradigm of regulating PVDF 
performance by altering filler “type”, and pioneers the introduction of “crystal facet 
engineering” into this system. Instead of merely selecting different inorganic fillers, we 
achieve precise and controllable modulation at the atomic scale by accurately 
controlling the exposure of specific crystal facets ({100} vs. {111}). We systematically 
investigate how specific plane orientations mediate differential interactions of polymer 
chains at the interface, elucidating the key microscopic factors through which inorganic 
fillers influence the stability of the ferroelectric phase in PVDF. This work establishes 
crystal facet control of inorganic fillers as a new variable for manipulating the 
ferroelectric phase and molecular dipole distribution in PVDF. It reveals that, due to 
differences in atomic arrangement and electronic structure, different crystal facets

optimizes its thermodynamic stability and dielectric/ferroelectric activity. It represents 
an advancement in the structure-property relationship from merely pursuing content to

Q3: However, the butterfly loop is not fully saturated, the reason and a comparison

Reply: Thank you to the reviewer for their keen observation. This phenomenon is 
relatively common in conductive filler/ferroelectric polymer sample systems. The main 
reason for the unsaturated loops lies in the leakage current generated by the sample 
under high-voltage testing due to the introduction of conductive NiS 2 fillers. This 
leakage current superimposes on the intrinsic polarization signal from ferroelectric 
domain switching, preventing the loops from achieving full saturation and resulting in 
the observed slender shape ( Ceram. Int., 2021, 47, 23659-23664). This phenomenon 
has also been reported in similar conductive filler/ferroelectric polymer systems (such 
as graphene/PVDF composites ( J. Mater. Chem. A , 2023, 11, 4280-4291)) and 
represents a common challenge in the study of such materials. 
We note that similar non-saturated P-E loops have been repeatedly reported in 
related studies on ferroelectric composites (Adv. Mater., 2021, 33, 2106308, J. Mater. 
Chem. A, 2023, 11, 4280-4291, Nat. Commun., 2024, 15, 4702, Nat. Commun., 2025, 
16, 3071). The phenomenon of filler-induced leakage currents leading to loop non-

testing conditions. Therefore, these data remain effective for comparing the relative

Reply: Thank you for the questions raised by the reviewers. We would like to clarify 
that there is no contradiction between the experimental data and the conclusions. 
Although changes in electrical conductivity do contribute to conductive loss, such loss 
constitutes only a minor portion of the overall dielectric loss within the 2-18 GHz 
frequency range. Therefore, we maintain that polarization loss remains the dominant 
loss mechanism in this frequency band. 
Difference in electrical conductivity primarily stems from the distinct geometric 
packing characteristics of cubic and octahedral particles. Cubic particles can achieve 
dense packing more easily through face-to-face or face-edge contacts, resulting in a 
larger effective contact area between particles, which facilitates the formation of 
conductive pathways. In contrast, octahedral particles, with their numerous vertices and 
edges, tend to form point-to-point or line-to-point contacts during packing. This often 
leads to the creation of arch-like structures and voids between particles, thereby 
reducing the efficiency of constructing conductive pathways. Consequently, as the filler 
content increases, the cubic particle system more readily forms an efficient percolation 
network through dense packing, leading to a more pronounced rise in electrical 
conductivity (Nature, 2009, 460, 876–879).
