Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript comprehensively reports the metabolic differences between two cassava varieties that show capacity
differences on starch accumulation. Overall, the study's methodology and analyses are reliable and interesting. The
conclusion of interplays between phenylpropaniod pathway and starch biosynthesis is rational and agreeable with previous
studies. The data presentation is clear and attractive. If the authors could provide stable transgenic cassava to verify the
function of COMT8, the work will be more soundable and supportive. The storage root development is progressive and might
misleading using a certain point of storage root stage to elucidate the cause of whole phase. Several comments need to be
considered: 
1. In page 4, "sucrose can be hydrolyzed to fructose by enzyme SS-I and UDPG in tuberous roots" is wrong and misleading.
should be revised. 
2. When the authors mentioned the function of CWI, the reference of "Yan et al. 2019, Cell wall invertase 3 affects cassava
productivity via regulating sugar allocation from source to sink. Frontiers in Plant Science 10:541." should be cited and
discussed. 
3. the format of References should be unified according to the journal style. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their manuscript, Li et al. investigated two cassava varieties with differential starch accumulation in storage roots. While
variety FX01 accumulated high levels of starch, SC16 showed lower amounts. The authors used 13C pulse feeding and
untargeted metabolomics to identify differentially accumulating compounds and found an increase in lignin biosynthesis-
related metabolites in SC16. This prompted the authors to investigate metabolic fluxes in more detail. By this, the authors
identified the lignin biosynthesis pathway, more precisely the reaction from caffeic acid to ferulic acid, as pivoting point
towards more lignin and less starch. Indeed, silencing of COMT8, the enzyme catalyzing this reaction, led to increased ADP-
Glc contents. 
Generally, I found the manuscript a little bit hard to follow, which is at least partially due to shortcomings in the writing (such
as sentences ending abruptly (e.g. line 84) or awkward formulations (l59, 66, 127)), but mainly because the reader is not
sufficiently guided through the manuscript. Providing a little bit more background would certainly help the reader to better
follow the story. 
While I like the idea to track the carbon fluxes in cassava varieties with different starch levels, I have some serious concerns,
especially regarding the theoretical background that served as basis for the metabolic models discussed in the manuscript. I
would like to address my concerns point by point: 
- L. 40: 
It is stated that starch content in cassava storage root varies greatly in the dry matter, ranging from 70-90%. As far as I am
aware, starch content of storage roots correlates very well with dry matter content, to a point that dry matter is being used as

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

a substitute measure for starch content. Also, I could not find data on differential starch accumulation per dry matter in the
referenced papers. 
Related to this, in Fig.1f-g, units of lignin and starch content are mg/g FW, but the values seem too high to be determined on
basis of fresh weight (ca 600mg/g or 60% starch). If these measurements were done on dried material however, then even
the high starch variety FX01 does not reach the range given in the introduction. 
In addition, the plot in Fig8g shows the lignin content of the COMT silenced plants, but this time the unit is given as mg/g
DW. 
I am a little bit lost in the inconsistencies here, and since the differential accumulation of starch forms the basis of the main
experiment, it seems important to clarify this. 
- L.52f 
The author’s claim that cw- and soluble invertases participate in sugar loading and unloading and correlate positively with
storage root yield in cassava. This is not only not warranted by the two publications cited by the authors. In fact, while one of
the papers deals with sucrose transport in rice, the other one shows the exact opposite: Sucrose loading in source leaves as
well as unloading in storage roots occurs via symplasmic connections, as shown by several authors (Pan et al 2021, Mehdi
et al 2019, Rüscher et al. 2024). 
- L85f: 
“..enzyme activities between two cassava species.” No different species were investigated in the manuscript, so this needs
to be changed accordingly. 
- L111: 
If I understand it correctly, soluble sugars were determined by a rather unspecific method using anthrone reagent? To get a
better idea of the processes happening upon sucrose unloading in the storage roots, I think it would be more helpful to
determine the levels of relevant carbohydrates like hexoses and sucrose individually. 
- L117-122: 
Measuring cwi activity would only reflect sucrose unloading capacity in a plant with apoplasmic unloading strategy, which is
not the case in cassava (see above). 
The next paragraph is biochemically wrong: Sucrose is not hydrolyzed to fructose by soluble starch synthase and UDP
glucose pyrophosphorylase. Instead, sucrose is hydrolyzed by sucrose synthase to fructose and UDP-glucose. UDP-
glucose is then converted to glucose-1-phosphate by UGPase. Furthermore, UDP-Glc is not converted to starch. The basic
building block of starch is ADP-Glc. The cited reference here is a paper on fruit ripening in melon?! 
The figure corresponding to the activity measurement, Fig1h, at least is correctly labeled with sucrose synthase (however
the legend is mixed up with Fig1i). 
- L130f /fig2: 
I understand that the metabolomics experiment showed 60 metabolites with higher, and 373 metabolites with lower levels
when comparing SC16 to FX01 (from a total of 772 metabolites). What are the plots in fig2a and b referring to then? In the
heatmap in fig2a, there’s only ~100 metabolites depicted? And in the volcano plot, are there even 60 data points at the
upregulated side? It looks like there is not that many there. 
From the main text body, it is not obvious that a KEGG-pathway enrichment analysis was conducted. This passage is
somewhat hard to understand. 
- Fig3a: 
The sucrose interconversion pathways are not correct here, either. E.g. sucrose is not directly converted to fructose6P or
glucose6P. 
- L167f / Fig S4 
The authors state that a significant 13C enrichment was detectable in shikimate pathway metabolites and refer to FigS4.
When looking at the figure, I wonder where to see these differences? The size of the bubbles look very similar to me. 
- L169f / Fig 5 
The authors describe the 13C accumulation in some lignin biosynthesis intermediates as similar or diverging between
varieties. However, the description does not match the figures (e.g. 4-coumaroyl shikimate, 5-O-caffeoyl shikimate, ..) 
- L186f 
The authors speculate about the expression/activity of hexokinase in starch formation in storage roots. As mentioned above,
there are very good indications from several papers for symplasmic phloem unloading and sucrose synthase-mediated
sucrose cleavage as the main pathway towards starch in storage roots. Hexokinase-mediated phosphorylation of glucose
would then play only a minor role in starch formation. 
- L235f 
Based on cw inv activity, the authors claim an enhanced phloem unloading capacity for SC16. Again, in an symplasmic
unloading scenario, cw inv would play little to no role in directing sucrose to starch synthesis. The observed increase in
sucrose levels in sc16 might e.g. be attributed to backlogging of sucrose due to slower conversion to starch rather than
improved unloading.

- L269f 
It is claimed that higher hexokinase activity leads to higher levels of fructose-6-phosphate. While it is true that fructose may
as well be phosphorylated by hexokinase, the enzyme’s affinity for fructose is way lower than that of fructokinase, which is
why it is thought that fructokinase is the main enzyme for fructose phosphorylation. I wonder what led the authors to believe

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper uses 13C flux labelling to examine how carbon is partitioned in two cassava varieties, one that stores low
amounts of starch and the other high amounts of starch. Their data indicates that the two varieties metabolize carbon
differently and that this helps explain the different starch amounts within roots. Although I like the approach, I think 
their
interpretation is a bit naïve for reasons I outline below. 
Major points 
1. I am not an expert on flux analysis, but as I understand it issues can arise when a labeled metabolites occur in 2 separate
compartments. That will be the case for glucose 1-phosphate and glucose 6-phospahte which occur in both the plastid and
cytosol. This needs to be addressed within the manuscript. 
2. They do not consider the role so sucrose cycling via sucrose phosphate synthase. I think they need to measure this
enzyme under both substrate limiting and saturated conditions and include its role in the discussion. 
Minor points 
1. Their starch data (Fig 1g) probably has a calculation error in it. They say the varieties are between 50-65% starch on a
fresh weight basis. That seems very high. In figure 1, the text for panels h and i are incorrect. 
2. Line 120 they call SS-I soluble starch synthase 1. I assume they mean sucrose synthase as starch synthases do not
catalyze the reaction (sucrose degradation) that they say is being catalyzed by it. This needs to be clarified. If they do mean
sucrose synthase then the common abbreviation is SuS (or SuSy) to avoid confusion with starch synthase which is normally
SS. They do say in the discussion (line 238) that increased SS activity suggests higher capacity of starch conversion in one
of the lines, so it is possible they did measure starch synthase. Their interpretation would, in any case likely be wrong as
starch synthases don’t always affect flux into starch. 
3. Line 120 “…sucrose can be hydrolyzed to fructose …. and UDPG (UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase)…”. Do they mean
UDP-glucose? Or that UDP-glucose is then further metabolized to glucose 1-phosphate by UDP glucose
pyrophosphorylase? 
4. Details of the enzymatic assays are lacking. The methodology says that they used kits purchased form several
companies, but I would like to understand what they measured and how it was measured. For example, sucrose synthase
activity could be measured in either the synthesis of degradation directions as it is a reversible enzyme. A common method
would be to measure UDP-glucose production from sucrose in an enzyme linked assay, but I would like to know how all the
enzymes were measured. 
5. Although they measure ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase activity they should probably cite the critical paper examining its
role in cassava (Beyene et al. 2022 Plant Mol Biol 109, 283-299) 
6. In figure 7d the AGPase activity seems high. I am not an expert in cassava, but in potato tubers it is normally about 1
umol/min/gFW (see for example Tiessen et al (2002) Plant Cell 14: 2191-2213). I am not saying they are wrong as cassava
could be different, but I would like them to check their calculations 
7. In Figure 7i the y axis is labelled as starch accumulation rate (%FW). A rate needs a time unit.
