Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an innovative strategy for fabricating DNA hydrogels. Currently, most known DNA hydrogels are
primarily formed through strand hybridization and chemical crosslinking, exhibiting limited mechanical properties. While
chain entanglement is recognized as a potential enhancer of mechanical properties in DNA hydrogels, the entanglement
density has been insufficient to form robust, tough hydrogels. The authors developed a "fast-shrinking-induced
entanglement" strategy that enables the production of DNA hydrogels with remarkably high mechanical properties. This
process was never reported and is conceptually interesting and innovative. They identified an optimal dehydration phase
separation solvent to induce rapid shrinking of pre-entangled DNA solutions. The study demonstrates that the fast shrinking
kinetics, combined with the extremely long DNA chain length, restricts chain reptation and entanglement relaxation, thereby
resulting in high-density entanglement within the resultant DNA hydrogel. Remarkably, relying solely on this entanglement
mechanism, the resulting DNA hydrogel exhibits rubber-like material characteristics with high stiffness and toughness.
Additional chemical crosslinking can further stabilize this hydrogel material. The authors have systematically investigated
key factors influencing the gelation process, confirming that chain length, shrinking rate, and initial DNA concentration are
primary determinants - findings that strongly support their proposed entanglement-based mechanism. The observed elastic
modulus and stretchability characteristics are also consistent with an entanglement-dominated hydrogel. They further
demonstrated the potential application of this strategy in 3D printing and mold casting for magnetic soft robots. This
approach could significantly broaden the potential applications of DNA hydrogels and may represent a milestone in DNA
hydrogel development. The manuscript is well-written, and the data are convincing. I strongly recommend its publication in
Nature Communications. 
Some minor technique comments: 
1. Previous studies have utilized rolling circle amplification (RCA) to generate long-chain DNA polymers, investigated chain
entanglement as one of the key mechanisms for DNA hydrogel formation. Although these RCA-produced DNA hydrogels
exhibit relatively soft and weak mechanical properties, they represent pioneering work in investigating the entanglement
phenomenon for DNA hydrogel fabrication. These foundational studies (Nature Nanotechnology 2012, 7(12), 816–820;
Angewandte Chemie International Edition 2020, 59(6), 2490–2495) should be appropriately cited and discussed in this
context. 
2. On Page 4, Line 32, the authors describe the hydrogel as stable in a "moist atmosphere," but this term lacks precision. To
strengthen the study's reproducibility, the authors should clearly specify the humidity and temperature ranges defining this
condition, and provide quantitative data demonstrating how the hydrogel's mechanical properties (e.g., elasticity, tensile
strength) evolve over time under these storage parameters. 
3. In Fig. 2H-I and Supplementary Fig. 20, the authors examine how initial DNA concentration (5%-12.5%) affects the
mechanical properties of DNA hydrogels. While higher concentrations generally enhance properties, the unexpected
decline at 12.5% needs explanation. Additionally, the compression data in Fig. 2I only covers 5%-10% concentrations,
creating an inconsistency that requires justification regarding the exclusion of 12.5% data. 
4. The entire manuscript only employed salmon sperm DNA for demonstration, raising questions about sequence specificity.
It remains unclear whether this represents a general strategy applicable to biomass DNA from other sources. The authors
should validate the method's generality by testing it with various biomass DNA exhibiting different sequences. 
5. The authors hypothesize that the effectiveness of this strategy in forming the entanglement hydrogel lies in the ultra-long
chains of biomass DNA which could prohibit the disentanglement during the fast shrinkage while enhance the

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors report a novel Fast-Shrinking-Induced Entanglement (FaSIE) process for engineering DNA
hydrogels with outstanding mechanical properties, including high stiffness, toughness, and stretchability. They present a
thorough and systematic investigation of various processing parameters and successfully establish their relationship with
the mechanical performance of the hydrogels. Additionally, the utility of the hydrogels in 3D printing is also demonstrated. I
recommend publication after the authors address the following comments and concerns: 
1) While the reported mechanical properties are impressive, it must be clearly stated that many of these values are based on
non-swollen hydrogels. This distinction is critical, as it may mislead readers regarding the actual performance in practical
conditions (for example, in aqueous solution). In particular, the E-gel is not stable upon swelling and requires chemical
crosslinking to form E-Mg-gel or E-Mg-PEG-gel. The mechanical properties of these chemically crosslinked hydrogels are
significantly reduced compared to their non-swollen counterparts. The manuscript should reflect this nuance and include
more detailed comparisons of the mechanical properties in both states. 
2) The FaSIE method relies on the diffusion of acrylamide (AA) and Triton X (TX) into a DNA solution. This raises questions
about the homogeneity of the resulting hydrogel network. The authors should discuss how uniform the gel structure is across
its volume and whether any characterization (e.g., microscopy, fluorescence, or mechanical mapping) has been performed to
assess spatial uniformity. 
3) Given the importance of chemical crosslinking density in determining mechanical properties, the authors should estimate
the crosslinking density for both E-Mg and E-Mg-PEG hydrogels. Furthermore, the manuscript mentions that certain divalent
ions such as Mn, Fe, Cu, and Zn weaken the hydrogel. It would be valuable to clarify whether this weakening effect is
consistent across different ion concentrations or if a threshold or dose-dependent response is observed. 
4) The gels' visual appearance appears to vary, as shown in Fig. S21—where the 5% gel is opaque, while the 10% gel is
transparent. This observation should be systematically described and explained. The authors should discuss the underlying
causes of this difference—e.g., variations in polymer concentration, phase separation, microstructure,—and whether these
optical differences correlate with any structural or mechanical changes. 
5) The manuscript claims that the FaSIE approach is general and potentially applicable to polysaccharides. However, no
experimental data are provided to support this assertion. To strengthen this claim, preliminary results or at least a more
detailed discussion of the feasibility, potential challenges, and future plans for extending FaSIE to other biopolymers would
be helpful.

We would like to clarify that the effect of divalent ions occurs only at high 
concentrations, specifically at a concentration of 2 M in this case. At lower 
concentrations, swelling and disentanglement dominate the process, resulting in poor 
mechanical properties. In contrast, at high concentrations, the elevated osmotic pressure

the network. 
Regarding the weakening of the DNA hydrogel by Fe2⁺, Mn²⁺, Cu²⁺, and Zn²⁺ ions, we 
speculate that these ions act as chelating agents that interact too strongly with DNA 
(Nanoscale, 2022, 14(39): 14613-14622). The toughening effect of entanglement in the 
hydrogel network depends on its capacity to distribute stress acro ss neighboring 
polymer strands. Unlike Mg ²⁺ and Ca ²⁺ ions, which interact with the phosphate 
backbone of DNA to stabilize the hydrogel without significantly hindering chain 
reptation, these strong chelating ions immobilize the chains and restrict effective stress 
distribution. This restriction undermines the advantages of chain entanglement, thereby 
weakening the hydrogel. 
We have mentioned the crosslinking density in the main text and added the calculation 
details in the supporting information. The explanation of the weakening effect was also 
added to the paragraph discussing ionic effects in the supporting information.
