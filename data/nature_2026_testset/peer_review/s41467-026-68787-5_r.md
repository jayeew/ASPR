Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the current manuscript, Klein et al. perform several in vitro experiments to study the interaction of HsNatA with 80S
ribosomes and various RAFs as well as the regulatory protein HypK, both independent and in the context of the ribosome. 
The majority of the work presented relies on the structural analysis of complexes reconstituted in vitro and their comparison
with previously published work, as well as in vitro experiments assaying binding kinetics. The principal advance of the study
is represented by the observation that Ebp1-NatA and NatA-NatA complexes can exist on the ribosome in vitro. Moreover,
the authors identify a shared site on the surface of Naa15 contacted by a conserved binding helix of Ebp1, Naa10, and Nacα
similar to that identified for HypK. 
In cryo-EM studies the authors show the newly described assembly of the two NatA complexes co-existing on 80S
ribosomes and discuss the potential physiological relevance of this finding. 
Overall, it is novel and interesting to see that NatA can in principle bind the ribosome in the presence of Ebp1 and also
together with a second copy of NatA, in each case engaging the second protein via a contact helix binding the same site on
the Naa15 surface. However, the study suffers from a lack of in vivo evidence supporting the idea of a physiological
relevance of these in vitro findings. Further, based on the presented data, the authors’ conclusion that NatA may serve as a
central coordinator at the ribosome, is rather speculative. In conclusion, the manuscript presents interesting new insights on
the interplay of the ribosome-interacting factors NatA, Ebp1 and NAC which would be of interest to publish in Nature
Comms. However, before publication it would be highly desirable to probe the significance of the observed interactions by
mutational/biochemical experiments and, more importantly, to provide better evidence that the suggested interactions and
functions actually occur in vivo. 
Major comments: 
In vivo IP 
Page 11,15: The authors conclude that Ebp1 interacts with Naa15 in vivo based on co-IP experiments. However, Ebp1 is
enriched only very mildly and with low significance in the results obtained with Naa15. Moreover, in their co-IP with Naa10,

would expect clear enrichment in both cases. These data can thus not be interpreted in support of the author’s hypothesis of

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Recommendation: publish with minor revision 
In this manuscript, Klein et al. demonstrate that NatA serves as a central coordinator of co-translational protein maturation by
dynamically assembling with multiple ribosome-associated factors (RAFs) at the polypeptide tunnel exit. Biochemical
assays—including SEC-MALS and biolayer interferometry (BLI)—reveal that NAC can form ribosome-independent
complexes with MAP1, NatA, and NatE. In particular, BLI “order-of-addition” experiments confirm that NAC mediates the
interaction between NatA and MAP1. The authors further show that while Naa50 modestly impairs NatA’s binding to NAC,
HypK completely blocks this interaction. This is in particular surprising because a recent NatA-HypK-NAC-80S cryo-EM
structure showed that NAC can bind to NatA in presence of HypK at the ribosome, but a possible structural explanation is
discussed. Cryo-electron microscopy analysis uncovers that NatA can form a ternary complex with Ebp1 on the ribosome. In
this assembly, Ebp1 binding remodels rRNA elements and competes with factors such as NACα and HypK for a conserved
hydrophobic site on Naa15. Mass spectrometry experiments confirm that endogenous Naa15 interacts with Naa10, NACα,
HypK, and Ebp1. Moreover, reanalysis of a NatA–80S dataset reveals a second, proximal binding site at the ribosome that
positions the catalytic subunit Naa10 closer to emerging nascent chains, suggesting a mechanism for rapid substrate
acetylation. Notably, proximal Naa10 contacts distal Naa15 at a conserved surface—centered on a critical tryptophan
residue—similar to the binding sites used by NAC, MAP1, and Ebp1, indicating competitive interactions at the tunnel exit. 
Together, these findings position NatA as a versatile interaction hub that orchestrates multiple co-translational processing
events critical for proteostasis. 
This manuscript is well written and provides significant mechanistic insights into the co-translational regulation of protein
maturation—a fundamental process in eukaryotic biology with broad implications for proteostasis and disease. By
integrating high-resolution structural data with rigorous biochemical and biophysical analyses, the study not only expands
our understanding of the multifaceted roles of NatA but also unveils novel mechanisms at the ribosomal tunnel exit. The
discovery of a second, proximal binding site for NatA that enhances substrate accessibility is particularly innovative and
could reshape how we view the spatial organization of co-translational modifications. Given its broad relevance to cell
biology, structural biology, and biochemistry, this work is suitable for publication in Nature Communications. 
Weakness of the manuscript: 
The major weakness is that the study relies on in vitro biochemical assays and structural data and cellular experiments
would strengthen the physiological relevance of the observed assemblies. While the discovery of distal and proximal
binding sites for NatA is intriguing, the manuscript does not fully clarify how these sites differentially contribute to co-
translational acetylation and overall proteostasis in cells. 
The competitive binding observed among factors like NAC, HypK, and Ebp1 is well demonstrated in vitro. However, the
mechanisms that modulate this competition in a cellular context remain somewhat speculative. 
Furthermore, some flexible regions or extensions in the cryo-EM reconstructions are less well resolved, which may leave
uncertainties regarding the complete interaction interfaces. 
Since these questions open up further exciting directions of investigations and cannot be addressed in short periods of time,
these points should not detract from the overall high quality of the work. Many groups working on the topic would as well
benefit from these data, therefore the manuscript should be published as it is as 
an in vitro structural work. 
Suggestions: 
N-terminal acetylation is predominantly a co-translational modification. 
Might the assembly of NatA complexes in vitro in the
absence of ribosomes reflect potential post-translational Nt-processing events like Nt-acetylation? Would
immunopreciptiations from Naa15 or Naa10 from ribosomal and cytoplasmic fractionations show the same RAF interactions
partners?

Fig.1: It would be beneficial to add the single measured points or error barswhere appropriate. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors investigate NAC interactions with NATs and MAPs in vitro, demonstrating that NatA can form
high-affinity complexes with other ribosome-associated factors in the absence of ribosomes. Additionally, they resolve the
structures of EBP1 and NatA in complex with the ribosome and revisit a previous dataset to identify a NatA dimer interacting
at distinct positions near the tunnel exit. 
While the study identifies interesting interactions, ribosome docking positions, and tertiary and quaternary complexes, the
biological relevance of these findings remains unclear. The proposed functional roles of these complexes are largely
speculative, and it is uncertain whether they exist in vivo under physiological conditions. Moreover, while the structural data
provide valuable insights, they do not seem sufficient to support the proposed coordination mechanism. Strengthening the
connection between these structures and a well-defined biological function would significantly improve the manuscript. 
Major comments: 
1) While NAC can form a ribosome-independent complex with MAP1 and NatA in vitro, the biological relevance of this
interaction remains unclear. It has been proposed that NAC facilitates the release of HypK-mediated inhibition of NatA on
the ribosome, thereby localizing NatA activity at the ribosomal tunnel exit. However, the formation of a ribosome-

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

independent complex appears to contradict this model; a ribosome-independent NAC-NatA complex may activate NatA,
disrupting such NAC-mediated localization system and being detrimental for the cell. 
In agreement with the potential danger of NatA-NAC ribosome-independent complexes, the authors report that HypK
prevents the NAC-NatA interaction off the ribosome. This suggests that HypK may actively prevent off-the-ribosome NatA
interactions with NAC in the cell. Could this mechanism explain the observed data? Addressing this question would help
clarify the functional implications of the reported interactions. 
2) The authors use mass spectrometry (Co-IP-MS) to assess the biological relevance of the observed interactions. However,
cellular lysates were not treated with RNases prior to immunoprecipitation, meaning that polysomes remained intact. As a
result, immunoprecipitating a ribosome-associated factor is likely to recover entire polysomes, leading to the co-purification
of ribosome-associated factors that may not coexist on the same ribosome but are instead present within the same
polysome. To support their claims, the authors must demonstrate that their immunoprecipitation is specific to individual
ribosomes rather than polysomes. This limitation significantly weakens the conclusions drawn from these experiments and,
unfortunately, leaves the existence of these complexes in vivo unproven. 
3) Along these lines, can the authors comment on why EBP1 is only significantly enriched in the immunoprecipitate of
NAA15 but not of NAA10 (Supp. Fig. 13)? If EBP1 and NatA were in complex on the same ribosome, one would expect that
immunoprecipitating NAA10 would also lead to EBP1 enrichment. Overall, EBP1 enrichment appears relatively low
compared to other ribosome-associated factors. Together with the methodological limitations discussed earlier, these
observations further weaken the Co-IP-MS data as support for the existence of the proposed complex in cells. 
4) His-tags are known to enhance the affinity of proteins for ribosomes, as previously reported (Supplemental Fig. S6 in
Pech et al., J Biol Chem, 2010). Can the authors exclude the possibility that this effect is influencing their conclusions
regarding EBP1 affinity and the salt resistance of its ribosome interaction? 
5) I am not convinced of the relevance of the described proximal site of NatA or whether this binding site—or a dimeric state
—is significantly populated in vivo. First, the authors identified this site through reanalysis of their dataset, suggesting that it
is occupied by only a minor fraction of ribosomes in their cryo-EM sample. Additionally, NatA is present at substoichiometric
levels compared to EBP1 and NAC, and this proximal binding site would be mutually exclusive with these highly abundant
factors. If the proximal site is already sparsely occupied in an in vitro reconstituted system, it seems unlikely that it could
effectively compete with other ubiquitous ribosome-associated factors in vivo. Furthermore, the functionality of a NatA dimer
remains unclear. While the authors propose multiple potential mechanisms, these remain speculative. 
It is therefore possible that the observed dimeric state is an artifact of the in vitro reconstitution conditions used for cryo-EM.
This concern is further compounded by the limitations in the Co-IP-MS data discussed earlier. Addressing these issues,
either through additional biochemical validation or functional assays, would be necessary to support the proposed
mechanisms. So far, the authors’ data demonstrate that these factors are able to simultaneously bind a ribosome, this does
not imply that they represent a functional state or a coordination mechanism. 
6) The authors claim that NatA plays a coordinating role in multifactor complexes at the ribosomal tunnel exit, as reflected in
the title of the manuscript. However, the presented data do not support this conclusion. The authors observe that EBP1
shares the same binding site that NAC uses to displace HypK-cH for NatA activation. This raises the possibility that EBP1,
like NAC, could facilitate NatA activation at the ribosome. Given the ubiquitous presence of both NAC and EBP1 on
ribosomes, these findings suggest that NatA activation does not depend on a specific ribosome-associated factor but may
instead occur through a factor-independent mechanism. This interpretation seems plausible but does not contradict the
notion that NatA acts as a coordinator of other factors. 
7) The authors’ interpretation hinges on the assumption that a pool of HypK-free NatA exists in the cell. While they
acknowledge that this is not established, their own mass spectrometry data show that HypK co-purifies with NAA15 and
NAA10 at similar levels. Although Co-IP-MS is not strictly quantitative, this observation suggests that most cellular NatA
exists in complex with HypK. This would agree with the need of keeping NatA inactive until it associates with the ribosome.
Can authors comment on this? 
In summary, while the structural data presented in this study are valuable for understanding ribosome-associated
multicomplex interactions, I find that they are overinterpreted to propose mechanisms that remain hypothetical. Due to
technical limitations, particularly in the Co-IP-MS experiments, the biological relevance of the described interactions is not
convincingly demonstrated.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I thank the authors for their response, which addressed some of my initial concerns. I appreciate the effort to clarify points
raised in the first round, and I continue to value the structural insights presented in this study. That said, a few key issues
remain unresolved. 
Critical points 
1. Title of the manuscript and claim of NatA coordinating tunnel exit complexes 
I remain unconvinced by the title and the claim that NatA coordinates multi-factor complexes at the ribosomal tunnel exit.
Authors show that NatA can form multiple complexes with different factors at the ribosomal tunnel exit. Yet, evidence
showing that NatA regulates or coordinates the formation and activity of these complexes is not provided. 
On the contrary, the data suggest that NAC, and possibly EBP1, regulate NatA activity at the ribosome. For this reason, the
title should be revised to more accurately reflect the findings. 
2. Co-IP-MS experiments 
First, from a technical perspective, my concern regarding the Co-IP-MS data remains (point 2 of my previous review).
Although the authors attempted to validate the Co-IPs under RNase-treated conditions using WB, the Co-IP-MS itself must
also be repeated with RNase-pretreated lysates to ensure the data reflect direct ribosomal interactions. Without this, the
results represent proteins co-existing in polysomes rather than on individual ribosomes, which is misleading. This
experiment must either be repeated using RNase-treated polysomes or removed from the manuscript. 
Second, the new western blot evidence presented to support a direct NatA-EBP1 interaction on individual ribosomes
(generated by RNase treatment) is not convincing. Instead, it shows a marked reduction in the co-immunoprecipitation of
Naa10 and Naa15 upon RNase treatment, especially with RNase T1. The levels of co-immunoprecipitated Naa10 after
RNase T1 treatment approach those of the control (supplementary Fig 13, lane 3) rather than the untreated condition. This
raises concerns that the NatA - EBP1 interaction strongly depends on polysome integrity, making its in vivo co-existence on
the same ribosome uncertain. While I acknowledge that RNase digestion itself can affect ribosome binding and that the
NatA-EBP1 complex might be of low abundance, the authors should address this issue directly and temper their claims
regarding in vivo relevance. 
In summary, I strongly recommend that all Co-IP experiments performed without prior RNase treatment either be repeated
under proper conditions or removed. Claims derived from these experiments must be aligned with the actual data. 
Other comments 
3. Proximal NatA binding site 
It remains unclear whether the proximal NatA binding site exists in vivo and what its proposed function might be. So far, this
conformation has only been observed in vitro under restricted conditions and in the absence of HypK. Since the authors
show that NatA-HypK cannot self-promote ribosome binding, this pathway seems unlikely to relief HypK inhibition. The
suggestion that free NatA may serve this role is possible but remains speculative. Providing evidence that such a
mechanism is plausible would strengthen this interpretation. At present, any functional assignment to the NatA dimer is
speculative and the claims should be toned down accordingly. 
Overall assessment 
Despite these reservations, I want to emphasize that the structural data are of high value to the community and making them
available will be useful for the field. However, the lack of a clearly defined biological context or demonstrated function
reduces the overall impact of the study. 
I would support publication in Nature Communications provided that (i) the Co-IP-MS experiments are repeated with RNase-
treated lysates (or else removed) and (ii) the manuscript title is adjusted to better reflect the actual findings.

The binding space around the PTE is very limited, and RAFs (especially enzymes) that need to function while the nascent chain

seen for e.g. NatA-NAC-MAP1, NatA-MAP2, NatA-Ebp1, NAC-SRP).

Recommendation: publish with minor revision 
 
In this manuscript, Klein et al. demonstrate that NatA serves as a central coordinator of co-translational protein maturation 
by dynamically assembling with multiple ribosome-associated factors (RAFs) at the polypeptide tunnel exit. Biochemical 
assays—including SEC-MALS and biolayer interferometry (BLI)—reveal that NAC can form ribosome-independent complexes 
with MAP1, NatA, and NatE. In particular, BLI “order-of-addition” experiments confirm that NAC mediates the interaction 
between NatA and MAP1. The authors further show that while Naa50 modestly impairs NatA’s binding to NAC, HypK 
completely blocks this interaction. This is in particular surprising because a recent NatA-HypK-NAC-80S cryo-EM structure 
showed that NAC can bind to NatA in presence of HypK at the ribosome, but a possible structural explanation is discussed. 
Cryo-electron microscopy analysis uncovers that NatA can form a ternary complex with Ebp1 on the ribosome. In this 
assembly, Ebp1 binding remodels rRNA elements and competes with factors such as NACα and HypK for a conserved 
hydrophobic site on Naa15. Mass spectrometry experiments confirm that endogenous Naa15 interacts with Naa10, NACα, 
HypK, and Ebp1. Moreover, reanalysis of a NatA–80S dataset reveals a second, proximal binding site at the ribosome that 
positions the catalytic subunit Naa10 closer to emerging nascent chains, suggesting a mechanism for rapid substrate 
acetylation. Notably, proximal Naa10 contacts distal Naa15 at a conserved surface—centered on a critical tryptophan 
residue—similar to the binding sites used by NAC, MAP1, and Ebp1, indicating competitive interactions at the tunnel exit. 
Together, these findings position NatA as a versatile interaction hub that orchestrates multiple co-translational processing 
events critical for proteostasis. 
 
This manuscript is well written and provides significant mechanistic insights into the co-translational regulation of protein 
maturation—a fundamental process in eukaryotic biology with broad implications for proteostasis and disease. By 
integrating high-resolution structural data with rigorous biochemical and biophysical analyses, the study not only expands 
our understanding of the multifaceted roles of NatA but also unveils novel mechanisms at the ribosomal tunnel exit. The 
discovery of a second, proximal binding site for NatA that enhances substrate accessibility is particularly innovative and 
could reshape how we view the spatial organization of co-translational modifications. Given its broad relevance to cell 
biology, structural biology, and biochemistry, this work is suitable for publication in Nature Communications.
