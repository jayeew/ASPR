Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
REVIEW IS ALSO ATTACHED AS A FORMATTED PDF 
The authors provide a conceptually interesting approach to the challenge of predicting phase separation from sequence.
The paper is well written and the approach not unreasonable, but there are unfortunately some major issues. 
On the positive side of things, the paper is well written, the DL architecture is reasonable, and the authors have taken, I think,
a conceptually reasonable approach to training /test /validation. Further, the authors have done a good job of experimentally
testing predictions, although they make it extremely difficult to determine exactly what has been done (discussed below).
That said, I appreciate their conceptual approach here, in terms of using (GGS)n replacement (instead of just deletion) and
NC controls based on regions that other predictors predict to be important (instead of cherry-picking obviously unimportant
regions). 
However, I do have some major concerns. 
At a high level, I have philosophical concerns regarding whether deep learning predictors for “phase separation” are
sensible. Absent any constraints on concentration, binding partners, or solution conditions, the question of ‘will my protein
phase separate’ is simply “yes”. There will, of course, be solution conditions in which any polypeptide can be coaxed into
forming two existing phases, perhaps far from physiological relevance but nevertheless possible. Therefore, the approach
the authors are using here implicitly is not “a predictor of phase separation” but “a predictor of phase separation under
physiological conditions”. Do the training/test/validation data support such a model? 
The authors use predictions based on PhaSePred, which is trained on heterogeneous data from various sources, crucially,
with no constraints on the underlying conditions. A protein may be annotated as “phase separating” even if this requires
impossibly high concentrations of PEG. Similarly, the negative dataset used in PhaSePred does not include proteins that
are not known to phase separate, but rather all other proteins from a set of proteomes that are distinct (as defined by
similarity through CD-HIT) from those that have been “shown” to phase separate. From an epistemological standpoint, the
assumption that “things that have not been reported in the literature to phase separate are non-phase separating” is a
challenging position to support or defend.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

As such, we’re left with two problems: 
1. The authors presume it is reasonable to divide proteins into two classes: those that phase separate and those that do not.
I find this worldview particularly hard to subscribe to, given that we know all proteins that phase separate do so under certain
conditions, yet not under others, which immediately renders such a classification invalid. 
2. Second, having made peace with that first assumption, we presume PhaSePred is able to distinguish between these two
classes. Again, I am fundamentally unconvinced that methodologically, the approach used there makes any sense. 
These two issues undermine the applicability of the work presented here. Now, these rather foundational issues
notwithstanding, the work presented here is very good. But my major concern is that it’s built on a set of premises that do not
make sense. If this is the case, we might expect evidence that the methodology has limited predictive power. Unfortunately,
such evidence does emerge when testing the approach on sequences examined in this manuscript and beyond. 
For example, the authors use the (GGS)n sequence to replace the motifs identified and find those sequences show reduced
phase separation (Fig. 3). However, if you take the sequences (based on the information in the supplementary tables) and
reconstruct a GGS-replaced version of the IDR used in the correlet system, all those GGS-containing sequences are strongly
predicted to phase separate (despite the fact experimentally they do not). As such, my enthusiasm is somewhat dampened
by the fact that it is not entirely clear whether the approach has really learned the residues that underlie phase separation,
vs. those residues that co-occur with residues that drive phase separation. 
For context, extensive work from many groups (Mittal, Banerjee, Mittal, Pappu, Hyman, Alberti, Forman-Kay etc.) has clearly
shown how certain residues have an outside contribution to phase separation, yet in all studies to date glycine, proline, and
serine have consistently shown up as “spacer” residues, those that are co-incident with residues that drive attractive
intermolecular interactions but lack the requisite chemistry to drive attractive interactions to mediate phase separation.
These spacer residues are typically far more abundant and provide a flexible but relatively inert scaffold within which other
residues can sit. However, the compositional analysis in Fig. 4 suggests that the authors imply these residues underlie
phase separation, when in fact the residues that have been demonstrably shown to drive phase separation (notably aromatic
residues and arginine) are actually depleted from the motifs compared to general IDRs. Further, taking examples of
sequences characterized previously (e.g., FUS LDC vs FUS Y2S variant) and generating a predicted score yields identical
values, despite the fact that FUS Y2S is effectively infinitely soluble (no phase separation). Similar conclusions are arrived if
we test other sequences where specific mutations have been made to suppress or enhance phase separation. 
In short, my concerns here are essentially three-fold: 
1. Do binary predictors of phase separation make sense? I think this is a question the authors need to discuss extensively in
a revised version. 
2. Does the underlying approach for delineating phase separation vs. non-phase-separating proteins via PhaSePred work?
The PhaSePred suffers from the same issues described in this review, so I think to convince readers of this the authors need
to take as many examples of sequences where we have wildtype vs. variant data (of which there are many in the literature)
where variant either enhances or suppresses phase separation, and ask if PhaSePred can correctly delineate between
these two. 
3. Does PhaSeMotif actually identify relevant subregions in proteins where specific mutations have been made to suppress
or enhance phase separation? 
Comments: 
Point 1 
The set up for the work states “While it is known IDRs often mediate homotypic and/or heterotypic multivalent interactions
that promote PS detailed information about the specific sequence motifs and compositional determinants within IDRs that
drive PS remains limited.” but I would strongly contest this – there are an overwhelming number of studies that have – for
IDRs at least – systematically explored the precise determinants of condensate formation in vitro and more recently in vivo.
Over the last decade, literally hundreds of papers have explored this question, consistently arriving at the same conclusions.
As such, to claim we have “limited information” about the “specific sequence motifs and compositional determinants within
IDRs that drive PS” is a ludicrous statement. How those motifs and compositional determinants relate to function is indeed
an open question, but to claim that the common physiochemistry associated with them is poorly explored is incorrect. 
Point 2 
The authors identify various residues enriched for PS motifs and PS IDRs; however, it’s probably important to explicitly note
that the enrichment of residues does not necessarily mean that those residues drive phase separation. Case in point,
extensive prior work has shown that serine, proline nor glycine appear to offer substantial attractive interactions in driving
phase separation, yet these residues often co-occur in regions enriched for those residues that do underlie phase separation
(e.g. arginine in RGG domains, aromatic residues in prion-like domains). The authors propose that glycine is important… yet
their negative control involves inserting a GGS sequence, which reduces phase separation in most cases. As such, how
meaningful are the predictions being made here? 
Point 3 
The generative aspect of the work is conceptually great; however, having played with the tool, the sequences being

4. When describing the annotation of PS and non -PS IDRs, please elaborate on how the 3:1:1 
split was determined and how the authors ensured that each subset was diverse and balanced. 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s request for clarification on dataset diversity and balance 
during training. The 3:1:1 ration for the training, validation and testing  subsets was achieved

3） Similarity across subsets: We confirmed that there were no significant differences in the  
distribution of IDR length and amino acid composition across subsets (revised Supplementary

As a result, a total of 3,131 PS IDRs and 11,763 non -PS IDRs in human proteome were collected 
(Supplementary Data File 1). This dataset was randomly split into training, validation, and test subsets 
(3:1:1), which were confirmed to be balanced, diverse, and compositionally similar (Supplementary Fig. 1c-

6. The authors mention the use of an amino acid alphabet. How were the categories chosen? 
How does this classification compare to others, such as those indexed in AAindex 
(PMID:17998252) or the more recent AAontology (PMID:39053689)? 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this question, which allows us to clarify the context and 
rationale for using an amino acid alphabet. Our amino acid alphabet is a feature engineering step 
designed to group amino acids by their core  physicochemical properties (such as polar amino

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

acids, charged amino acids, etc.),  allowing us to incorporate essential physicochemical 
information without introducing excessive model complexity. This strategy has proven effective in 
tasks like protein fold ing prediction (R3) (PMID: 33937764). Our approach differs from 
comprehensive index such as AAindex (R4) and AAontology (R5) in granularity: they offer over 
500 distinct quantitative scales  and a hierarchical classification of those scales, whereas our 
alphabet provides a discrete classification. This categorical feature is more suited to our  model 
architecture. We have added a sentence to the  Materials and Methods section to clarify this 
rationale as follows: 
Data preprocessing . To represent the input sequence, we employed a dual -channel encoding 
strategy for each amino acid. We first grouped the standard amino acids into a reduced alphabet of 9 
classes based on their physicochemical side- chain properties (Supplementary Table 1). Each amino 
acid in the sequence was then converted into a 30- dimensional feature vector by concatenating two 
one-hot vectors: a 21- dimensional vector representing the specific amino acid type and a 9-
dimensional vector representing its corresponding cl ass in the reduced alphabet. The dual 
representation provides the model with both the specific residue identity and its general biochemical 
context. (lines 481-488)

To allow comparison across different IDRs, the attribution scores of each mutation site and 1000 randomly

The authors provide a conceptually interesting approach to the challenge of predicting phase separation from sequence. The paper is well written and the approach not unreasonable, but there are unfortunately some major issues.  On the positive side of things, the paper is well written, the DL architecture is reasonable, and the authors have taken, I think, a conceptually reasonable approach to training /test /validation. Further, the authors have done a good job of experimentally testing predictions, although they make it extremely diDicult to determine exactly what has been done (discussed below). That said, I appreciate their conceptual approach here, in terms of using (GGS)n replacement (instead of just deletion) and NC controls based on regions that other predictors predict to be important (instead of cherry-picking obviously unimportant regions).  However, I do have some major concerns. At a high level, I have philosophical concerns regarding whether deep learning predictors for “phase separation” are sensible. Absent any constraints on concentration, binding partners, or solution conditions, the question of ‘will my protein phase separate’ is simply “yes”. There will, of course, be solution conditions in which any polypeptide can be coaxed into forming two existing phases, perhaps far from physiological relevance but nevertheless possible. Therefore, the approach the authors are using here implicitly is not “a predictor of phase separation” but “a predictor of phase separation under physiological conditions”. Do the training/test/validation data support such a model? The authors use predictions based on PhaSePred, which is trained on heterogeneous data from various sources, crucially, with no constraints on the underlying conditions. A protein may be annotated as “phase separating” even if this requires impossibly high concentrations of PEG. Similarly, the negative dataset used in PhaSePred does not include proteins that are not known to phase separate, but rather all other proteins from a set of proteomes that are distinct (as deﬁned by similarity through CD-HIT) from those that have been “shown” to phase separate. From an epistemological standpoint, the assumption that “things that have not been reported in the literature to phase separate are non-phase separating” is a challenging position to support or defend. As such, we’re left with two problems: 1. The authors presume it is reasonable to divide proteins into two classes: those that phase separate and those that do not. I ﬁnd this worldview particularly hard to subscribe to, given that we know all proteins that phase separate do so under certain conditions, yet not under others, which immediately renders such a classiﬁcation invalid.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 38 -->

2. Second, having made peace with that ﬁrst assumption, we presume PhaSePred is able to distinguish between these two classes. Again, I am fundamentally unconvinced that methodologically, the approach used there makes any sense.  These two issues undermine the applicability of the work presented here. Now, these rather foundational issues notwithstanding, the work presented here is very good. But my major concern is that it’s built on a set of premises that do not make sense. If this is the case, we might expect evidence that the methodology has limited predictive power. Unfortunately, such evidence does emerge when testing the approach on sequences examined in this manuscript and beyond. For example, the authors use the (GGS)n sequence to replace the motifs identiﬁed and ﬁnd those sequences show reduced phase separation (Fig. 3). However, if you take the sequences (based on the information in the supplementary tables) and reconstruct a GGS-replaced version of the IDR used in the correlet system, all those GGS-containing sequences are strongly predicted to phase separate (despite the fact experimentally they do not). As such, my enthusiasm is somewhat dampened by the fact that it is not entirely clear whether the approach has really learned the residues that underlie phase separation, vs. those residues that co-occur with residues that drive phase separation.  For context, extensive work from many groups (Mittal, Banerjee, Mittal, Pappu, Hyman, Alberti, Forman-Kay etc.) has clearly shown how certain residues have an outside contribution to phase separation, yet in all studies to date glycine, proline, and serine have consistently shown up as “spacer” residues, those that are co-incident with residues that drive attractive intermolecular interactions but lack the requisite chemistry to drive attractive interactions to mediate phase separation. These spacer residues are typically far more abundant and provide a ﬂexible but relatively inert scaDold within which other residues can sit. However, the compositional analysis in Fig. 4 suggests that the authors imply these residues underlie phase separation, when in fact the residues that have been demonstrably shown to drive phase separation (notably aromatic residues and arginine) are actually depleted from the motifs compared to general IDRs. Further, taking examples of sequences characterized previously (e.g., FUS LDC vs FUS Y2S variant) and generating a predicted score yields identical values, despite the fact that FUS Y2S is eDectively inﬁnitely soluble (no phase separation). Similar conclusions are arrived if we test other sequences where speciﬁc mutations have been made to suppress or enhance phase separation.  In short, my concerns here are essentially three-fold: 1. Do binary predictors of phase separation make sense? I think this is a question the authors need to discuss extensively in a revised version.

2. Does the underlying approach for delineating phase separation vs. non-phase-separating proteins via PhaSePred work? The PhaSePred suDers from the same issues described in this review, so I think to convince readers of this the authors need to take as many examples of sequences where we have wildtype vs. variant data (of which there are many in the literature) where variant either enhances or suppresses phase separation, and ask if PhaSePred can correctly delineate between these two.  3. Does PhaSeMotif actually identify relevant subregions in proteins where speciﬁc mutations have been made to suppress or enhance phase separation?  Point 1 The set up for the work states “While it is known IDRs often mediate homotypic and/or heterotypic multivalent interactions that promote PS detailed information about the speciﬁc sequence motifs and compositional determinants within IDRs that drive PS remains limited. ” but I would strongly contest this – there are an overwhelming number of studies that have – for IDRs at least – systematically explored the precise determinants of condensate formation in vitro and more recently in vivo. Over the last decade, literally hundreds of papers have explored this question, consistently arriving at the same conclusions. As such, to claim we have “limited information” about the “speciﬁc sequence motifs and compositional determinants within IDRs that drive PS” is a ludicrous statement. How those motifs and compositional determinants relate to function is indeed an open question, but to claim that the common physiochemistry associated with them is poorly explored is incorrect. Point 2 The authors identify various residues enriched for PS motifs and PS IDRs; however, it’s probably important to explicitly note that the enrichment of residues does not necessarily mean that those residues drive phase separation. Case in point, extensive prior work has shown that serine, proline nor glycine appear to oDer substantial attractive interactions in driving phase separation, yet these residues often co-occur in regions enriched for those residues that do underlie phase separation (e.g. arginine in RGG domains, aromatic residues in prion-like domains). The authors propose that glycine is important… yet their negative control involves inserting a GGS sequence, which reduces phase separation in most cases. As such, how meaningful are the predictions being made here?
