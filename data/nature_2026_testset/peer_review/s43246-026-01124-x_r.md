Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents a compelling study on the electrochemical switching of adhesion in catechol-based copolymer
adhesives. The authors have elegantly designed a multifunctional copolymer system by incorporating three key monomeric
units: sulfonic acid groups to facilitate efficient proton transfer, hydroxyethyl groups to ensure strong initial adhesion through
hydrogen bonding, and catechol moieties to enable redox-controlled adhesion switching. This molecular design results in
an impressive lap-shear strength of 4.6 MPa, comparable to that of commercial epoxy adhesives. Remarkably, upon
applying a modest voltage, the adhesive undergoes a ~90% reduction in adhesion strength. The exceptionally high
adhesion strength, combined with the capability to electrically deactivate it on demand, marks an exciting advances in the
fields. The following points are suggested to further clarify the experimental details and enhance the overall mechanistic
understanding presented in the manuscript. 
1. The authors mention that removing water from the system is key to achieving high initial adhesion strength. However,
efficient proton transfer also relies on polymer segmental motion, which in turn requires the presence of some water.
Providing the Tg of the polymers used, along with adhesion performance data under varying humidity conditions, would help
elucidate the system more precisely. 
2. According to the Experimental Section, the adhesive layer thickness is approximately 1.8 mm, which seems rather thick
for efficient proton conduction. Does the thickness influence the overall adhesion performance? It would be interesting to
explore whether using a much thinner film (e.g., <10 µm) could achieve comparable proton conductivity even without the
addition of CNTs. 
3. What is the current level observed when applying a voltage of 9 V? If the CNT loading exceeds the percolation threshold,
it is possible that most electrons simply travel through the CNT network rather than through the polymer matrix. 
4. Following up on point 3, in Figure 4d, further clarification of the equivalent circuit model would be helpful. When additional
CNTs are incorporated, the appearance of a secondary CPE element is not well justified. Introducing a resistor representing
electron conduction through the CNT network, arranged in parallel with R
₂
 and W
₁
, might provide a more appropriate and
physically meaningful model. 
5. Which side of the adhesive fails upon voltage application? From the supplementary video, it appears that detachment
occurs predominantly from the (+) electrode, while the adhesive remains adhered to the (–) electrode. Does the failure
consistently occur at the positive electrode, or is it a random (approximately 50:50) distribution? 
6. Calculating Gc from the lap-shear model would be helpful for correlating the adhesive properties with those of other
systems. Please refer [Choi et al., Adv.Sci. 2025, e10512, Levine et al., Sic. Robot. 2022, 7, eabo2179] 
7. The effect of PHEMA is not entirely clear. In figure 3d, when PHEMA is replaced with PMEA, the electrochemical
switching of adhesion appears to be off, even though the presence of DMA should promote catechol oxidation to the
quinone form. Have the authors considered the mechanical properties of the copolymers? Since lap-shear adhesion
strength is strongly correlated with the square root of the system compliance, it is possible that the switching arises not only
from surface chemistry but also from bulk mechanical changes. For instance, a stiffer bulk network in PHEMA—due to
stronger hydrogen bonding—might become softer under current application, or hydrogen bonding could be screened by
mobile protons, leading to reduced adhesion. This could suggest that the observed adhesion switching is governed more by
mechanical compliance than by the surface properties associated with quinone formation. 
8. Where do the protons go during the switching process? Did the authors observe any evidence of hydrogen evolution?
Performing CV measurements could provide valuable insight into the underlying electrochemical processes. 
9. Is the adhesion switching reversible, or does the adhesive lose functionality once detachment occurs? If the process
indeed allows for reversible electrochemical switching, it would represent a particularly impressive and practically valuable
feature of this system.

Reviewer 1  
 
1. The authors me ntion that removing water from the system is key to achieving high initial 
adhesion strength. However, efficient proton transfer also relies on polymer segmental motion, 
which in turn requires the presence of some water. Providing the T g of the polymers us ed, along 
with adhesion performance data under varying humidity conditions, would help elucidate the 
system more precisely. 
 
We determined Tg values for our polymers using DSC and the values were all around 60C. We did not 
evaluate the effect of humidity on Tg values as we do not have the capability to perform the experiment 
in an environmental chamber. Given that the adhesion testing was performed at room temperature and 
well below the Tg values, the adhesives are glassy in these experiments. As such, segment motion of the 
polymer chains likely did not contribute to the increase in proton conductivity in our system. However, 
having it is potentially beneficial for having relatively high T g as the glassy state of the polymer 
contributed to elevated adhesive strength.  
We agree with the reviewer that increasing moisture content will drastically increase proton conductivity 
and potentially lowering T g. However, this will also decrease the mech anical property and adhesion 
strength of the adhesive. Our main objective was to simultaneously increase both the adhesion strength 
and proton conductivity. We added the new data as Supplementary Table 7 and added new texts to discuss 
this point.  
 
2. According to the Experimental Section, the adhesive layer thickness is approximately 1.8 mm, 
which seems rather thick for efficient proton conduction. Does the thickness influence the overall 
adhesion performance? It would be interesting to explore whether using a much thinner film (e.g., 
<10 µm) could achieve comparable proton conductivity even without the addition of CNTs. 
 
During protocol optimization, we tested thinner adhesive layers. However, due to the proximity of the 
metal substrates, it often resulted in short circuiting during the application of electricity when the surfaces 
are not aligned perfectly. This led  to poor reproducibility and unreliable measurements. In contrast, a 
thicker adhesive layer of approximately 1.8 mm consistently prevented electrical shorting while 
maintaining sufficient mechanical integrity for lap  shear testing. This thickness therefore provided a 
reliable balance between mechanical testing requirements and electrochemical deactivation experiments. 
We have also investigated ways to insulate the metallic surfaces surrounding the adhesive layer (e.g., 
placing non-conductive tape around the adhesive) to prevent the metallic surfaces from touching each 
others during electrochemical deactivation. However, the tape appeared to have interfered with adhesion 
testing, and we have not found a reliable testing method through insulating the metal surface. In the end, 
we settled on the adhesive joint preparation method as described in the manuscript. 
3. What is the current level observed when applying a voltage of 9 V? If the CNT loading exceeds 
the percolation threshold, it is possible that most electrons simply travel through the CNT network

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

rather than through the polymer matrix. 
 
The initial current observed upon applying a voltage of 9 V ranged from approximately 40 to 80 mA and 
gradually decreased during continuous voltage application throughout the deactivation process. We have 
added this description in the text. This current decay is clearly visible in the demonstration videos and is 
consistent with progressive electrochemical consumption of redox-active catechol groups. 
Regarding the percolation threshold, to the best of our knowledge, it is defined as the minimum 
concentration of a conductive filler (in this case, CNTs) required to form a continuous, sample-spanning 
conductive network. Below this threshold, CNTs remain isolated or form small clusters, and electron 
transport must occur via hopping or tunneling through the ins ulating polymer matrix, resulting in very 
low electrical conductivity. Exceeding the percolation threshold is, in fact, essential for achieving mixed 
electronic-ionic conduction (J. Mater. Sci., 42(10), 3408-3418). Once percolated, the CNT network acts 
as an efficient electron-transport pathway with low electronic resistance. Importantly, electrons are still 
consumed at the electrode through catechol oxidation  and CNTs do not bypass the electrochemical 
reaction but instead ensure that electrons can be delivered rapidly and uniformly to the redox-active sites. 
In addition, percolation behavior in CNT/polymer composites is highly dependent on filler dispersion, 
aspect ratio, and sample geometry. Numerous studies have shown that an interconnected CNT network 
can form at very low CNT loadings, enabling high electronic conductivity above the percolation 
threshold (Compos. Sci. Technol., 69(10), 1486-1498; RSC Adv., 2018, 8, 28048-28085). However, in 
our system, achieving ultra -low CNT loadings (<1 wt%) is practica lly unrealistic. Owing to the high 
fluidity of the polymer solution, adhesives composed solely of the polymer matrix are difficult to process 
and mold, particularly for uniform coating and reproducible sample preparation. 
 
4. Following up on point 3, in Figure 4d, further clarification of the equivalent circuit model would 
be helpful. When additional CNTs are incorporated, the appearance of a secondary CPE element 
is not well justified. Introducing a resistor representing el ectron conduction through the CNT 
network, arranged in parallel with R 2 and W1, might provide a more appropriate and physically 
meaningful model. 
 
We thank the reviewer for pointing out the correction of equivalent circuit model. We have revised the 
equivalent circuit model by introducing an additional resistor, R3 and further clarified it. We also added 
new texts and a reference.

appears that detachment occurs predominantly from the (+) electrode, while the adhesive remains 
adhered to the (–) electrode. Does the failure consistently occur at the positive electrode, or is it a 
random (approximately 50:50) distribution? 
 
We thank the reviewer for highlighting this  detail. Adhesive failure occurred exclusively on cathodic 
electrode. We have added the description of this detail in corresponding paragraph. 
 
6. Calculating Gc from the lap-shear model would be helpful for correlating the adhesive properties 
with those of other systems. Please refer [Choi et al., Adv.Sci. 2025, e10512, Levine et al., Sic. Robot. 
2022, 7, eabo2179]

7. The effect of PHEMA is not entirely clear. In figure 3d, when PHEMA is replaced with PMEA, 
the electrochemical switching of adhesion appears to be off, even though the presence of DMA 
should promote catechol oxidation to the quinone form. Have the authors considered the 
mechanical properties of the copolymers? Since lap-shear adhesion strength is strongly correlated

oxidation. We discussed the limitation of reversibility in our system in the Outlook section.

Reviewer 2  
 
1. How the addition of proton and electron conducting elements affects electrochemistry  redox 
control? Please provide in-depth research and experimental data on the impact of electrochemical 
performance. 
 
In the revision, we added new CV results to examine the redox properties of our adhesive. Additionally, 
we added DSC results to rule out the effect of polymer mobility in the contribution of proton conductivity. 
Additionally, the proton conductivity was determined using EIS and electrical conductivity was 
determined using four-point probe. We have correlated the changes in the conductivities with the changes 
in composition, adhesion data, and the level of applied electricity needed to deactivate the adhesive. Our 
collectively demonstrated that the addition of proton conductive AMPS and electrically conductive CNT 
demonstrated the ability to fully deactivate the adhesive with a voltage or 9V or less. Our discussion 
focused on the incorporation of AMPS to increase sulfonic acid groups within the adhesive to support 
anhydrous proton transport, which enabled proton-coupled electron transfer during catechol oxidation.  
CNTs formed a percolated electronic network (as visualized in SEM), facilitated rapid electron transport 
across the adhesive layer, ensuring uniform redox activation throughout the bulk.  Only when both 
pathways are present does rapid and complete catechol oxidation, and thus adhesive deactivation occur.  
 
2. What is the adhesion performance of the prepared switchable adhesives on different material 
surfaces? 
 
We appreciate this question. In the present study, adhesion tests were intentionally performed on metallic 
substrates to ensure electrical conductivity and reproduci ble electrochemical debonding during 
deactivation experiments. This is because the surface acts as an electrode for conducting electricity, 
necessary for deactivation. We demonstrated the ability for the adhesive to adhere to and detach from 
multiple metal substrates (titanium, steel, aluminum) in Figure 3e. In the Outlook section we discussed 
the limitation of this set up as the deactivation could not be achieved when the adhesive is attached to a 
nonelectrically conductive surface.  
 
3. The adhesive strength and variation range of the prepared adhesives need to be compared with 
recent literature. 
 
We added new texts and a Supplementary Fig. 19 to compare the performance of our adhesive with other 
electro-responsive adhesives reported in the literature. 
 
4. What are the advantages of electrically controlled switchable adhesives compared to existing 
optical controls? Please elaborate in detail. 
 
Optical controlled adhesives are limited by the need for light penetration. In the introduction, we 
specified that light responsive adhesives often suffer from limited substrate penetration of the light source 
and making them ineffective for use with opaque surfaces.
