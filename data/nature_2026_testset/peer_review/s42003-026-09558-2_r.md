Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This very interesting paper explores in the primate lineage the impact of HCN channels on the electrical properties of
supragranular pyramidal neurons. The authors and others have previously demonstrated that human supragranular
pyramidal neurons express and utilize HCN channels in the control of their excitability, a property not shared by lower
mammals such as mice and rats. The authors now seek using molecular and electrophysiological techniques if other
primates also express HCN channels in supragranular pyramidal neurons and if so do they have a similar impact on somatic
excitability. The authors electrophysiological work confirms in old and new world monkeys that HCN channels are
expressed by L2/3 pyramidal neurons, and shape subthrehsold somatic excitability. They find some species-specific
differences which they discuss. 
The work is timely and interesting. The experiments are well conducted. The limitations are clearly indicated. I would have
liked to see some development from the authors previous work, ie dendrtic recordings, measurement of current densities, but
as a first pass this work demonstrates that HCN channel expression in supragrnaular pyramidal neurons is broad across the
primate lineage. I have no specific comments regarding the manuscript, other than to worry why the authors have chosen to
study electrical activity at 32-34 oC and then relate findings to in vivo conditions,and recommend publication of a sound
manuscript.

Major comments: 
1, 
The advantage of Patch-seq is the combined electrophology, transcriptome and morphology within one neuron. I
encourage the author to give a profile about this dataset, including their transcriptome cluster, morphology and HCN-related
intrinsic membrane properties. Is the HCN-related intrinsic membrane properties different among different transcriptome

2, The expression of HCN genes is different between mouse and primate. How about the HCN-related intrinsic membrane
properties? 
A crossspecies analysis between mouse and primate may further our understanding of the distinction between
primate and rodents. 
3, While resonance response to chirp stimulation and increased sag-ratio are key electrophysiological features that
correlated with the HCN channels, the authors may also take advantage of their valuable datasets and interrogate other

intriguing findings, given the functional and connectivity differences between these regions. To further contextualize these
results, it would be valuable if the authors could perform additional analyses to elucidate the morphological, physiological,
and/or transcriptomic differences across species, linking the enriched HCN expression in primates L23IT neurons to their
functional implications.

8) Depth normalization (p. 11 and 14) 
The definition of cortical depth of a L2/L3 neuron in acute temporal and motor cortex as 
well as the organotypic (cultured) slices is crucial for this study. 1) Please define an 
exact definition how the depth from pia was measured and normalised across slices and 
cortical regions with curvature. Indicate whether depths were corrected for shrinkage in

We measured the distance of the recorded cell to the pial surface along the radial axis 
of the gyrus, aligned with the main apical dendrite using 20x montage images of the 
slices obtained during biocytin processing. DAPI staining was used to assign cortical 
layers and assist in estimating the radial axis of the gyrus. In cases where 20x images 
were unavailable, we used micrometer readings from the translation stage of the 
experimental setup to estimate cell depth. For these measurements we applied an 
empirical correction factor of .77 to account for differences between stage-based and 
image-based measurements based on recordings where both methods were available.  
We did not correct for tissue shrinkage. Additionally, we found no differences in the 
thickness of layer 2/3 between cultured and acute slices (p = 0.91, slice effect, 2-way 
ANOVA with slice type and area as factors). However, consistent with previous reports 
(Fischl and Dale, 2000), layer 2/3 was thicker in motor cortex compared to temporal 
cortex (p < 0.001, area effect, 2-way ANOVA with slice type and area as factors). Since 
our focus is on the temporal cortex and we did not perform direct cross-areal 
comparisons, we report absolute distances from the pial surface in the manuscript. 
Nevertheless, we observed similar depth-dependent properties when using normalized 
depth within layer 2/3 (see below). We have included these details in the Methods 
section (page 11, lines 15-25 and page 12 lines 1-3).

9) Temporal vs motor cortex (p. 13-15) 
Layer widths differ systematically across areas, so it is very likely that neurons in motor 
and temporal cortex may be assigned to different layers. Please clarify how this was 
accounted for. 
As in our previous studies (Berg et al., 2021; Chartrand et al., 2023, Lee et al., 2023, 
Dembrow et al., 2024), for each recording we used histological clues to delineate layers. 
In both areas, L2 could be identified by the presence of densely packed neurons with 
small cell bodies. Similarly, layer 4 could be clearly identified by a transition from the 
presence of large pyramidal shaped neurons (deep layer 3) to densely packed neurons 
with small cell bodies (layer 4) in temporal cortex. Dense myelination and the presence 
of the large somas of L5 ET neurons marked the start of layer 5 in motor cortex. For 
slices with a 20x image we also relied upon the DAPI stain to confirm the laminar 
position of the neuron. We have added these details to the methods (page 11, lines 7-
14).

which provided the largest dataset. To assess depth-dependent differences in 
resonance across cortical areas, we grouped neurons into three bins based on their

relative position within layer 2/3: Bin 1 (150–450 µm), Bin 2 (451–680 µm), and Bin 3 
(681–1150 µm). These bin ranges were selected to ensure approximately equal 
numbers of cells per bin. We then conducted z-tests for proportions to compare the 
number of resonant cells between motor and temporal cortex within each bin. 
Statistically significant differences were observed in Bin 1 and Bin 2, indicating a higher 
proportion of resonant cells in motor cortex at these depths. However, Bin 3 did not 
show a significant difference between areas. These findings suggest that resonance 
differences between motor and temporal cortex are most pronounced in the upper and 
middle portions of layer 2/3. We have incorporated this analysis in the results section 
(page 16, lines 13-15) 
 
 
 
 Although we did not account for cell type composition differences in our analysis, we
