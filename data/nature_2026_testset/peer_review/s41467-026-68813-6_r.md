Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study investigates how AlkB dioxygenase homologs influence paternal mitochondrial elimination (PME) in
Caenorhabditis elegans embryos. The authors present compelling evidence that AlkB homolog 1 (ALKB-1) and its
demethylase activity are essential for efficient PME. They also demonstrate that loss of ALKB-1 activity induces the
mitochondrial unfolded protein response (mt-UPR), elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and activates the stress-
response transcription factors ATFS-1 and SKN-1/Nrf2. Notably, RNAi targeting ATFS-1 or SKN-1 rescues the PME
phenotype, while ROS scavengers partially rescue it. The majority of experiments are conducted rigorously, with appropriate
controls, and the findings reveal a potential link between ALKB-1-dependent mitochondrial homeostasis and PME.
However, additional evidence is needed to substantiate the conclusion that ALKB-1 directly regulates PME, rather than its
effects being an indirect consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction. Furthermore, several points require clarification to
strengthen the study’s conclusions. 
Key Points for Consideration: 
1. Title and Claims: 
The study concludes that ALKB-1 is an “Epitranscriptomic Regulator of Paternal Mitochondrial Elimination” and “…provides
compelling evidence for the critical regulatory role of m1A, a conserved epitranscriptomic modification on tRNAs, in the rapid
clearance of paternal mitochondria.” 
While the evidence strongly supports that ALKB-1 is necessary for efficient PME, the claim that ALKB-1 regulates PME may
be overstated. The observed PME defects could result from indirect effects of mitochondrial homeostasis disruption in ALKB-
1 mutant worms, rather than a direct regulatory role. The authors’ model suggests that inefficient PME arises from increased
mtDNA levels due to mtDNA amplification triggered by mt-UPR in ALKB-1 mutants. This implies that ALKB-1’s role in PME
may be indirect and that any perturbation activating mt-UPR could similarly reduce PME. The study also shows that
paraquat-induced ROS elevation is sufficient to impair PME. To address this, the authors should soften their claims by
avoiding the term “regulator” in the title and discussion, instead emphasizing that mt-UPR modulates PME efficiency. 
2. Line 130: 
The authors describe a “demethylase inactive mutant strain, with the D247A substitution,” and note in Supplemental Figure
2i that D247 is an “ion-coordinating residue.” However, it would be more accurate to specify that D247 is an iron-
coordinating residue, as AlkB homologs catalyze iron-dependent oxidative demethylation. Additionally, the authors should
explain why this specific residue was chosen, referencing prior literature and including a sequence alignment of this residue
with other ALKBH1 homologs in Supplemental Figures. 
3. RNA-Seq Analysis: 
The authors perform ribosome nascent-chain complex sequencing (RNC-Seq) and proteomics on wildtype and ALKB-1
mutant worms but do not conduct standard RNA-Seq to assess global mRNA levels. Standard RNA-Seq is necessary to
make claims about translation efficiency, as reduced ribosome association could result from decreased mRNA abundance
rather than impaired translation. RNA-Seq would also clarify whether proteomic changes stem from transcriptional or
translational alterations. 
4. Line 186: 
The authors state, “Notably, translation efficiency was reduced for four mtDNA-encoded TCA cycle-associated transcripts
(atp-6, ctb-1, ctc-2, nduo-2), in alkb-1D247A worms (Fig. 2g).” However, the RNC-Seq method used is unsuitable for

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

analyzing mitochondrial mRNAs, as it targets cytoplasmic ribosomes. Mitochondrial ribosomes differ in sedimentation
properties and are unaffected by cycloheximide, which arrests cytoplasmic ribosomes. The authors should avoid concluding
that translation of mitochondrial mRNAs is reduced without performing mitochondrial polysome profiling. Alternatively,
reduced transcript levels could reflect decreased mitochondrial transcription due to disrupted homeostasis, which could be
tested using RNA-Seq or qRT-PCR. 
5. Line 247: 
The statement, “Treatment with the antioxidants N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) and Pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) effectively
reversed the PME delay (Fig. 4b, c),” should be revised to: “Treatment with the antioxidants N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) or
Pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) effectively reversed the PME delay.” 
6. Line 255: 
The observation that SKN-1 knockdown in either the paternal or maternal lineage alleviates PME defects caused by ALKB-1
inactivation, while ATFS-1 knockdown alleviates PME defects only in the paternal lineage, is intriguing. The authors should
discuss why SKN-1 plays roles in both paternal and maternal lineages, whereas ATFS-1’s role is restricted to the paternal
lineage. This distinction may provide insight into lineage-specific stress responses and their impact on PME. 
7. Lines 299–308: 
This section would be better placed at the beginning of the Discussion, as it provides context for the study’s findings and
sets the stage for interpreting the results. 
8. Line 193: 
The authors state, “Subcellular localization analysis, using the WoLF PSORT software, identified 15 nuclear-encoded
proteins that localize in mitochondria, and were significantly upregulated (Fig. 2j), indicating potential abnormal protein
aggregation in mitochondria.” However, increased protein levels do not necessarily indicate abnormal aggregation. Elevated
protein levels could result from transcriptional, translational, or post-translational changes, and may even be compensatory.
The authors should clarify this point and provide evidence for abnormal aggregation if this is their intended conclusion. 
9. Figure 5: 
The authors show that overexpression of ATFS-1 using the gain-of-function mutant atfs-1(et17), which constitutively
activates mt-UPR, partially restores the delayed PME phenotype in the alkb-1D247A; skn-1 RNAi mutant. However, it would
be informative to test PME in embryos from crosses involving atfs-1(et17) mutants alone, without the alkb-1D247A mutation
or SKN-1 RNAi. This experiment would reveal whether mt-UPR activation alone is sufficient to impair PME, pinpointing mt-
UPR as the primary driver of reduced PME efficiency. 
10. MTS Deletion Strain: 
The study finds that the MTS deletion strain (alkb-1(ajz102)) can carry out efficient PME similar to wildtype strain crosses,
suggesting that mitochondrial localization of ALKB-1 has little impact on PME. However, the authors should validate that
deletion of residues 2–35 effectively disrupts ALKB-1 localization to mitochondria in vivo within worms. This could be tested
by examining whether mitochondrial tRNA-Met retains the 5-formylcytidine (f5C) modification, as ALKB-1 is required for this
formylation. Additionally, the authors should assess whether mt-UPR is activated in the alkb-1(ajz102) strain. If mt-UPR is
activated without affecting PME, this would suggest differential effects of mt-UPR on PME in the alkb-1(ajz102) strain versus
the D247A substitution (alkb-1(ajz103) strain). 
11. Transgene Testing: 
To strengthen their conclusions, the authors could test different versions of their ajzIs214 transgene (tbb-2p::ALKB-
1::3×Flag). While they show that re-expression of wildtype ALKB-1 in the alkb-1D247A mutant strain rescues the PME
defect, testing transgenes expressing the ALKB-ΔMTS mutant or ALKB-D247A mutant could reveal whether these variants
can also rescue PME defects in the alkb-1D247A mutant strain. 
12. Figure 2J: 
In the volcano plot, there is a highly decreased protein in the top left corner. The authors should identify this protein, as its
significant difference may provide additional insights. 
13. Figure 6d: 
There is a typo in the schematic that reads “Delayed parternal mitochondrial elimination.” This should be corrected to
“Delayed paternal mitochondrial elimination.”

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work describes the effect of a putative tRNA demethylase, ALKB-1, that controls the exclusion of paternal mitochondrial
DNA in C. elegans. Demethylation of mRNA and tRNA has been described for a while now, but the biological phenotypes
and mechanisms directly linked to the demethylation activities have lagged behind. This work shows that ALKB-1, a putative
tRNA m1A demethylase, impacts the exclusion of paternal mito-DNA, potentially through its tRNA demethylation activity.
ALKB-1 inactivation disrupts translation, mitochondrial proteostasis, and increases ROS. This activates oxidative stress
response pathways to result in deficient mt-DNA removal. This study identifies a potential epitranscriptomic mechanism for
mitochondrial inherence during reproduction. If true, it would represent an important contribution to our biological
understanding of RNA demethylation.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

The main deficiency of this proposed mechanism is that very little tRNA characterization was done to justify it. The only
tRNA work was to use LC/MS in Fig. 2 to measure m1A levels in total tRNA which increased in cells containing a
catalytically inactivate ALKB-1 mutant. Specifically: 
1. tRNA-seq (multiple methods available in the literature) should be done to identify specific cytosolic and mitochondrial
tRNAs whose m1A levels change between WT and D247A mutant cells. 
2. tRNA-seq can also compare changes in tRNA abundance between these cells. In case significant changes are found for
specific tRNAs, these should be validated by Northern blots. 
3. Mammalian ALKBH1 also modifies their mito-tRNA-Met to generate 5-formyl-C (f5C) which impacts mitochondrial activity
(PMID 27497299, 28472312). Another mammalian cytosolic f5C tRNA substrate for ALKBH1 is LeuCAA. Does ALKB-1-
dependent f5C modification also occur in C. elegans tRNAs? This can be measured by LC/MS and multiple tRNA
sequencing methods in the literature). This point is very important, as f5C modification may also in part or even solely
account for the observed mito-DNA elimination effects. 
4. Is the recombinant C. elegans ALKB-1 protein active in the tRNA m1A erasing activities in vitro? Only a m6A-DNA
substrate was tested here. This can be tested using total tRNA followed by LC/MS. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a comprehensive and well-executed study uncovering a novel epigenetic mechanism by which the
demethylase ALKB-1 promotes paternal mitochondrial elimination (PME) in Caenorhabditis elegans. The authors provide
compelling evidence that ALKB-1 limits aberrant tRNA methylation, particularly m¹A, to maintain mitochondrial protein
homeostasis. Loss of ALKB-1 demethylase activity triggers mitochondrial stress responses, including UPRmt activation via
ATFS-1 and SKN-1/Nrf2, as well as elevated ROS levels. Impressively, genetic suppression of these stress pathways or
pharmacological ROS reduction mitigates the PME defect in the alkb-1(D247A) mutant, highlighting a functional link
between ALKB-1, mitochondrial quality control, and inheritance. 
The work is supported by rigorous experiments with appropriate genetic and molecular tools, and the manuscript is clearly
written with a logical experimental flow. Furthermore, the authors extend their findings to assess the physiological relevance
of PME in the context of sperm function and embryo viability, enhancing the broader significance of the study. 
Overall, this study makes an important contribution to the field of mitochondrial inheritance and epigenetic regulation, and it
provides a strong foundation for future mechanistic investigations into the role of RNA modifications in mitochondrial
homeostasis. 
However, to make it a strong candidate to publish in Nature Communications, a few concerns have to be addressed. 
1. The authors observe slower mtDNA elimination in the alkb-1 knockdown paternal background (Fig. 1A), which could be
due to higher initial heteroplasmy levels in mutant males. It would be valuable to directly measure heteroplasmy levels in
sperm from wild-type and ALKB-1 KO/KD males to better understand whether this is contributing to the PME defect. 
2. The authors discuss changes in mitochondrial mass and mtDNA copy number in sperm (Fig. S3 and Discussion), but the
provided quantifications using whole males (e.g., Fig. 3J) may not fully reflect the changes in sperm specifically. I
recommend quantifying mitochondrial mass and mtDNA copy number in mature sperm from the ATFS-1 and SKN-1 mutants
to directly link these factors to PME efficiency. 
3. The study would benefit from an investigation into whether ALKB-1 influences sperm development and/or sperm count.
This could help clarify whether ALKB-1’s role in PME extends to broader aspects of spermatogenesis. 
4. To further clarify the functional significance of ALKB-1’s demethylase activity in PME, it would be informative to evaluate
the rescuing ability of the ajzIs214 transgene (tbb-2p::ALKB-1::3×Flag) in different mutant backgrounds. Specifically, testing
whether ajzIs214 can rescue the PME defects in strains carrying either the ALKB-1 ΔMTS or ALKB-1 D247A mutations
would help distinguish the respective roles of ALKB-1’s mitochondrial localization and catalytic activity in the regulation of
PME. 
5. To better understand whether ALKB-1 acts primarily in sperm or germline cells, the authors could re-express ALKB-1 in a
sperm-specific or germline-specific manner in the alkb-1(D247A) mutant background. This would clarify whether ALKB-1’s
expression in sperm or the germline is most crucial for regulating PME. 
6. The authors demonstrate that overexpression of ATFS-1 (via the gain-of-function mutant atfs-1(et17)) partially restores the
PME defect in the alkb-1(D247A); skn-1 RNAi mutant. However, it would be informative to test PME in embryos from crosses
involving only atfs-1(et17) mutants. This experiment would determine whether the activation of UPRmt is sufficient to impair
PME and could help pinpoint UPRmt as the key driver of PME dysfunction. 
7. To further strengthen the conclusions regarding the role of UPRmt in PME, I suggest including additional controls, such as
testing atfs-1 or skn-1 mutants or RNAi alone, without the alkb-1(D247A) mutation (Fig. 3A, Fig. 5d). This would provide a
more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between UPRmt activation and PME efficiency. 
8. The manuscript mentions autophagy regulation by ALKB-1, particularly through the lgg-1::GFP transgene strains. To

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

explore the potential interaction between autophagy and ALKB-1 in PME, it would be insightful to test whether autophagy is
required for ALKB-1-mediated PME. Moreover, an epistasis analysis between ALKB-1 and autophagy could clarify how
these processes interact during paternal mitochondrial elimination. 
9. In both flies and worms, Endonuclease G was reported to participate in PME. It would be interesting to test the relationship
between ALKB-1 and Endonuclease G in the PME process. This could further elucidate the molecular pathways involved
and strengthen the overall mechanistic understanding of PME regulation.

The revised manuscript presents new results that strengthen the conclusion that ALKB-1 is essential for efficient PME. The
authors have also addressed previous concerns by making corrections and enhancing the clarity of the manuscript. Overall,
the revision represents a significant improvement compared to the original submission. 
However, we believe the new title remains overstated, despite the authors removing the term “Regulator.” The current title,

subsequently disrupt protein synthesis and proteostasis. While many proteins can cause proteostasis defects when lost, this
does not necessarily qualify them as regulators of proteostasis.

proteostasis or in paternal mitochondrial elimination. For instance, the title could be rephrased as: 
• “tRNA m1A Methylation by ALKB-1 is Essential for Efficient Paternal Mitochondrial Elimination” 
Or 
• “ALKB-1-Dependent tRNA Methylation is Required for Efficient Paternal Mitochondrial Elimination.” 
Aside from this issue, the authors have satisfactorily addressed nearly all concerns raised in the initial review.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have satisfactorilly addressed all my concerns and I recommend publication of the paper. 
This study presents a compelling and timely investigation into the epitranscriptomic regulation of paternal mitochondrial
elimination (PME) in C. elegans. The authors identify ALKB-1, an evolutionarily conserved demethylase, as a critical
regulator of PME through its tRNA m¹A demethylase activity. The mechanistic depth is impressive: ALKB-1 inactivation
causes tRNA m¹A hypermethylation, which disrupts protein translation, perturbs mitochondrial proteostasis, and elevates
ROS levels. This cascade activates SKN-1/Nrf2 and triggers the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) via
ATFS-1, ultimately leading to mitochondrial and mtDNA accumulation in sperm and delayed PME. 
The novelty of this work is substantial. While extensive research has focused on autophagy, mitophagy, and ubiquitin-
proteasome pathways in PME, this is the first study to characterize the role of epitranscriptomic modifications in this
fundamental process. The elegant dissection of ALKB-1's substrate specificity—demonstrating that m¹A demethylation
rather than 6mA demethylation underlies PME regulation—represents a significant conceptual advance. The epistatic
relationship established between SKN-1 and ATFS-1 provides a coherent framework for understanding how oxidative stress
signals converge on mitochondrial quality control. 
This work makes an important contribution to reproductive biology, mitochondrial genetics, and epitranscriptomics. The
findings have broad implications for understanding mitochondrial diseases, infertility, and transgenerational inheritance.
