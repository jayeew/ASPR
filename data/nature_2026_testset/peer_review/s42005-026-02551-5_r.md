Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a novel approach for investigating the structural properties and intrinsic heterogeneity
(polydispersity) of lipid vesicles, a topic of considerable interest across biophysics and materials science. The key of this
research lies in successfully implementing X-ray diffraction experiments on single and aerosolized lipid vesicles using
femtosecond X-ray pulses at the European XFEL, thereby circumventing the fundamental limitation of ensemble averaging
inherent in conventional Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS). Analyzing single-particle diffraction patterns separately is
meaningful. It moves beyond obtaining only the average structure to potentially revealing crucial structural subtypes that are
otherwise masked by sample heterogeneity. This capability represents a substantial methodological advance using XFELs
in the study of soft matter and biological assemblies. However, the manuscript's strong claims regarding the structural
resolution and the exclusion of ensemble averaging hinge critically on several technical aspects that require further
descriptions. As authors mentioned, while the CDI technique is powerful, its success depends on obtaining extremely high-
quality diffraction patterns with good signal-to-noise ratio at high-q and effective handling of the missing center. The authors
should explicitly discuss the uniqueness of this approach in the context of existing CDI method (e.g., in terms of unique
information obtained from data). The impact of the aerosol injection process on the hydrated and functionalized state of the
vesicles is also a critical concern that needs robust evidence. 
I recommend that the manuscript be considered for publication after the authors address the significant technical and
mechanistic details outlined in the detailed comments below.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) is performed on forward-scattering intensity from typically more than 10^10 particles.
The two-dimensional scattering pattern is azimuthally averaged to obtain a one-dimensional profile. By fitting this 1D profile
with a physical model, often supported by prior information from other measurements, one can obtain an overall description
of the sample structure. Because SAXS does not require crystallization and can probe samples in solution at conditions
close to physiological ones (room temperature, liquid environment), it remains an important structural method even in the era
of powerful direct imaging techniques such as cryo-EM. At the same time, SAXS has intrinsic limitations: the averaging
leads to loss of particle-specific structural information, and polydispersity makes analysis difficult. 
In this work, the authors analyze diffraction patterns from individual lipid vesicles, recorded using an XFEL-based coherent
diffractive imaging (XFEL-CDI) setup, using a SAXS-style analysis (Single-Particle SAXS, SP-SAXS). They demonstrate
that this approach can, in principle, overcome the main limitations mentioned above. I also consider the work significant
because it proposes an XFEL measurement mode that is different from conventional XFEL-CDI or serial femtosecond
crystallography (SFX). However, in order for readers to fully appreciate the significance of this new approach, I believe that
(i) the relationship between XFEL-CDI and SP-SAXS, and (ii) the fitting procedure and its reliability, need to be clarified. In
addition, there are several presentation issues. If the points below are addressed, I believe the impact of this study would
meet the standard of Communications Physics. 
Major comment 1: Relationship between XFEL-CDI and SP-SAXS 
In the Introduction, the authors write that “we deduce structural properties by classical model-based diffraction analysis,
rather than by model-free inversion of the data, which failed under given conditions of size, scattering strength, and detector
gaps.” I interpret this to mean that the main advantage of their SP-SAXS approach is that it can still provide structural
information in cases where model-free reconstruction by XFEL-CDI fails. However, I am not fully convinced that this framing
is accurate in its current form, for several reasons: 
(1)The stated reasons why CDI reconstruction is not possible are not fully convincing 
The manuscript cites particle size and detector gaps as reasons why model-free reconstruction could not be achieved. I am
not sure these factors alone are sufficient to prevent reconstruction. Regarding “size,” I assume the concern is that the
oversampling ratio becomes too small (or samples are larger than an illuminated beam size). However, many of the target
systems discussed here (the vesicles in this work and the proteo-liposomes mentioned as a future target) are basically
below ~100 nm in diameter. With a reasonable experimental geometry, it is not obvious that the oversampling ratio would
become so low that reconstruction is fundamentally impossible. 
For the detector gap, I also found the explanation incomplete. Even though the experiment uses a setup at which XFEL-CDI
measurements have been successful, the present data appear to have very large missing regions on the detector, and this is
presented as a key reason why CDI cannot be performed. 
If particle size and detector gaps are kept as the main reasons why CDI reconstruction failed, it would help to explain (a) why
these specific conditions were unavoidable in the present experiment, and/or (b) what benefit is gained by designing an
experiment in which CDI-style reconstruction becomes impractical due to size and detector gaps. 
(2) The influence of the model may be more important than the influence of azimuthal averaging. 
The other reason given for the failure of CDI reconstruction is the low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the single-shot diffraction
patterns. Indeed, to reconstruct internal bilayer structure of a lipid vesicle from CDI, the required SNR would be extremely
high. 
However, when explaining why SP-SAXS succeeds, the manuscript emphasizes the SNR improvement obtained by
azimuthal averaging of the diffraction pattern into 1D (for example, the last two sentences of Section 2.2). My reading is that
the text suggests: after azimuthal averaging, the higher SNR makes it possible to extract structural information. 
I believe the situation is more subtle. In my view, the main advantage of SP-SAXS compared to model-free CDI is not only
the SNR improvement by azimuthal averaging, but rather the explicit use of a structural model as prior information. In other
words, the essential difference is “model-based vs. model-free,” rather than only “higher-SNR 1D data vs. lower-SNR 2D
data.” I think the manuscript should make this point clearer. 
(3) Suggested reframing of how SP-SAXS is positioned in the paper. 
Taking (1) and (2) together, I suggest revising the positioning of SP-SAXS. At present, the narrative is “SP-SAXS is valuable

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

in cases where CDI reconstruction fails.” A more convincing and, in my opinion, more accurate message would be: “SP-
SAXS provides added value not only when CDI reconstruction is not possible, but also even when CDI reconstruction is in
principle possible.” 
In practice, based on Fig. 3c, if one were to reduce the detector gap and also apply 2D binning (for example 21×21 pixels) to
improve SNR, then a model-free reconstruction of an ellipsoidal projection should be feasible, and one could discuss the
vesicle radius R. However, even in that optimistic case, it would still be essentially impossible to reconstruct the internal lipid
bilayer structure directly in a model-free way. This is exactly where SP-SAXS becomes powerful: it uses the same diffraction
data, but it extracts bilayer-level structural parameters through model-based fitting. 
Therefore, I suggest that both the Introduction and the Conclusion shift from the message “SP-SAXS is relevant when CDI
fails due to limited SNR or detector gaps” to the message “SP-SAXS exploits model-based analysis to provide
complementary structural information, even in cases where CDI-based shape reconstruction would in principle be possible.”
I believe this change would help readers understand the significance of the method without raising unnecessary doubts. 
Major comment 2: Reliability and precision of the fitting 
The impact of SP-SAXS depends on how much reliable structural information can actually be extracted from the
experimental data. For this reason, I think the manuscript should include, in the main text, a clearer discussion of the
reliability and precision of the structural parameters obtained from both the single-vesicle analysis and the batch (in silico
purification) analysis. 
In its current form, I was left with the question of whether the reported structural parameters are mainly driven by the model
assumptions, or whether they are directly supported by the measured data. (I acknowledge that I am not a SAXS specialist,
so this may reflect standard SAXS practice. But because this question is central to the claim of the paper, I believe it should
be addressed explicitly.) 
For example, I think the main text should clarify the following points: 
* What initial values were given to each fitting parameter? 
* Over what range (bounds) were those parameters allowed to vary during fitting? 
* If the initial values are changed, do the fits converge to the same final values, or are there multiple local solutions? 
Providing even a brief statement on these questions would help readers evaluate how robust the extracted parameters are. 
In addition, the Supporting Information compares two models, referred to as a “core-shell vesicle model” and a “core-shell
vesicle with soft decay model,” and shows that the latter gives a better fit. This result currently appears only in the Supporting
Information. I think a short version of this comparison should also appear in the main text, because it is important evidence
that the measured scattering profiles actually contain subtle information about the lipid bilayer structure, and are not only
dominated by overall vesicle size and shape. 
Related to this point, I also think the manuscript should comment on resolution. The data extend only up to about q ≈ 3
nm^-1 (which corresponds to a real-space length scale on the order of ~2 nm), and the SNR above ~2 nm^-1 is relatively
low. Yet the manuscript extracts features on the order of ~1 nm in the lipid headgroup region. The authors should explicitly
explain why this is justified: to what extent is this information truly encoded in the measured data, and to what extent does it
come from the assumed bilayer model?

hydration shell? Well, the most important evidence are the SAXS results themselves! An

Comment 3:

Reply:  The stochastic nature of the self-amplified spontaneous emission (SASE) process can lead 
to intense pulses but is also associated with strong pulse-to-pulse fluctuations, not only in intensity,

pulse generation process, even at the expense of reduced average flux, can hence guarantee overall 
better results. We expect that progress towards an increasingly better defined X-ray layer, including 
seeding and possibly also the very recent demonstration of a Free Electron Laser Oscillator – see 
the preprint in : https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-7274597/v1_covered_43f57fd2-8cc3-
48d3-83ac-af17de2c6002.pdf  - can substantially benefit the hit rate and overall performance of the 
single-particle SAXS method with XFEL (SP-SAXS).
