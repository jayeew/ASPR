Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors appear to have made a good-faith effort to respond to both sets of referee comments about their previous
submission, including discussions about how their scheme is distinct from previous works. An important addition in this
version of the manuscript is the inclusion of a proof-of-principle experiment about the core part of their proposal extended to
16x16-by-16x16. While this is not large scale by the standards of neural networks, it is large enough to convince me that the
claimed practicality of the scheme is reasonable. 
However, the only figure about the 16x16-by-16x16 experimental setup is apparently Figure 11, and it is quite brief and high-
level. I recommend adding at least two additional figures to the SM: (1) a figure showing a full experimental schematic
including diagrams of the components used (e.g., gratings, SLMs, switches...) specialized to the 16x16-by-16x16 case that
was implemented (at the moment I cannot tell what the authors built, besides understanding that they used some kind of
time-multiplexing of modulators because they didn't have 512 modulators available to them - but I do not currently have
enough detail in Figure 11 to be able to replicate the experiment they reported); (2) an annotated photograph of the 16x16-
by-16x16 setup (like is included in Figure 32 for the 4x2-by-2x4 setup). 
The manuscript contains a number of grammatical mistakes. Before it is published, I would recommend these be corrected.
They are not subtle errors, so I would imagine any of the authors speaking English as a first language will be able to find and
correct them without difficulty. 
I recommend publication in Nature Communications, provided that my two comments above (giving enough detail for the
authors' experiments to be replicable; fixing grammatical mistakes) are addressed.

FIG. 1. (a) Concept and (b) Schematic of the 16 × 16-by-16 × 16 MMM ONN processor with sufficient equipments, where 512
pcs EO modulators and 31 different wavelengths will be used.
As shown in Fig. 1, the 16×16-by-16×16 MMM ONN processor can be implemented when sufficient EO modulators
(512 in total) and laser wavelengths (31 distinct wavelengths) are available. The data/weight modulators are divided
into 16 groups; each group contains 16 EO modulators for data encoding and another 16 EO modulators for weight
encoding, and each group requires 16 different wavelengths. After parallel diffractive beam routing, the receiver
photodetectors are also divided into 16 groups, each containing 16 photodetectors and ADCs for data decoding, as
indicated in Fig. 1.

However, due to limited EO modulator numbers and A WG channel numbers, we perform the 16×16-by-16×16 MMM
ONN processor with the current 2 × 4-by-4 × 2 setup. We successively inject lasers at different wavelengths into the
same modulators at different time steps (this is what we refer to as optical switching / optical wavelength switching).
The laser output wavelengths are programmed using a Python script, and the input ports of the commercial WDM
are manually reconfigured to match the programmed laser wavelengths. For each wavelength setting, the receiver
fiber array is then manually shifted by four fiber-array pitches to record a new set of signals, as illustrated in Fig. 2.
As an example, the elements Y1,1, Y1,2, Y2,1, and Y2,2 of the 16 × 16-by-16 × 16 MMM are obtained by summing the
measurement results from four wavelength settings (for instance, Y2,1 = Y2,1,s1 + Y2,1,s2 + Y2,1,s3 + Y2,1,s4), where in
each step the wavelengths used are λ1, λ2, λ3, λ4; λ5, λ6, λ7, λ8; λ9, λ10, λ11, λ12; and λ13, λ14, λ15, λ16. In this way, all
16 receiver channels of the parallel diffractive beam routing are utilized, further demonstrating its scalability. During
the wavelength-switching process, no realignment or modification of the free-space setup before the receiver fiber array
is required, indicating the robust performance of the system.
