Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Moldogazieva and coauthors are presenting a review paper that aims to connect redox chemistry from geochemical,
prebiotic reactions up to biological (metabolic) reactions. That redox chemical processes (electron flow) are essential for life
and in extension its emergence is by far not a new thought, but the effort the authors to give an all-encompassing overview of
geochemical, prebiotic and biochemical redox reactions it applaudable. What I found very positive about this review is a
neutral approach towards different hypotheses which is not an easy feat in this usually very disputed field. The presented
reference library is mostly very balanced throughout different OoL approaches and focusses on the facts rather ideology, I
partly also attribute to the authors’ “new to the field” background (that’s how the manuscript reads anyways). At least in the
beginning – later, when it comes to organic synthesis, the focus shifts almost entirely toward hydrothermal vent
environments. What I miss at many places of this manuscript is proper placement of the described results into the correct
context. The extensive review sometimes reads just like a list of paper summaries which became very apparent to me in
chapter 3 “Redox chemistry and thermodynamics of the primordial organic synthesis”. Although this chapter is a detailed
recap of a lot of prebiotic CO2 fixation with a variety of different references, followed by a very short conclusion – I really
would expect a lot more connection between the mentioned research and “build up” (Why is this important, hat does this
mean in context with other results?). Chapter 3 is what I personally have the best background in, but I see similar patterns in
the rest of the chapters. 
This said I would recommend the authors to probe the entire manuscript to give it an overall, recurring question, actively
connecting ideas within individual chapters but also throughout the manuscript. That said I have some exemplary comments
on recurring observations I made in the manuscript: 
1) Sometimes, there are references missing where there should be some, e.g.: “Early forms of life primarily thrived an anoxic

## Opinion and Summary 
The review paper presents a highly ambitious review on the role of redox processes in the origin and evolution of life. It aims
to survey early Earth environments—from the Hadean to the Phanerozoic—and to synthesize atmospheric, surface, and
hydrothermal redox chemistry into a unified framework. While the review is integrative in intent, I find the treatment of the
Hadean and early Archean deeply problematic. The authors make many claims with a level of confidence out of proportion
to the evidence, conflate incompatible scenarios, misrepresent prior literature and include serious errors in at least some of
the figures. In its current form, the paper risks misleading the community. I therefore recommend rejection. 
## Scope of the Review 
I will lay out my major criticisms, and then a list of minor criticisms. I will also flag those sections of the paper I did not review
because some of this paper lies well outside my own area of expertise. 
Also, the minor comments will be instances of incorrect or misleading citation or overconfidence of claims, and I limit myself
to the first 15 such instances. These are far from the only such instances; if the editor and/or authors agree, it would be their
job to provide a comprehensive rewrite of this paper to fix all its problems: to address all the major criticisms, all the minor
criticisms, including the first 15 citation problems, and then all the rest of the citation problems with the paper that this review
does not touch. 
I have written a long review with two goals in mind. First, in the effort to convince the editor that this review should not be
accepted for publication. Second, in the effort to convince the authors that there are many unresolved problems with this
paper, in the hopes they would completely rewrite the paper, addressing these problems, before trying to publish this paper
anywhere else. 
## Major Comments 
1. **Overconfidence in Hadean reconstructions.** 
The Hadean rock record is absent, as the authors acknowledge (Line 631, among other place). In addition, the mineral
record is sparse and often contradictory. This is unsurprising, given mineral inclusions in zircons may not always be pristine,
and even when they are, would tend to represent a local environment, and there is much work required to go from that single
picture of a local environment to the persistent global conditions of the Earth at the Hadean. Some of the data the authors set
as anchor, such as Trail et al. (2011) concerning mantle redox do provide firm constraints that are known to high confidence,
but these are the exception. Examples: 
1. The claim that prokaryotic life emerged in ocean surface waters during the Archean (Lines 128, 129). 
2. The transition of prokaryotic to eukaryotic life was driven by the atmospheric oxygenation in the Proterozoic (Lines 129,
130). 
3. The authors (Lines 142-145) mention "several controversial hypotheses" that involve continental crust during the Hadean,
but then state that the presence of continental crust during the Hadean is "widely accepted". These statements don't agree
with each other. It would be better to cite a study that lays out the range of possible ways to explain the scant geological
data, which are consistent with anywhere from zero continental crust to as much continental crust as we have today. See
Harrison (2009). 
4. The authors (Lines 153-154) claim that Hadean geomorphology ultimately created habitable conditions on early Earth,
citing two papers about plate tectonics, suggesting that plate tectonics is a necessary condition for habitable conditions. This
may be true! 
The authors could clearly state this possibility, and also point out, with appropriate citations, that there is little
real evidence at present supporting this connection between tectonics and habitability.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

5. Arc volcanism can release volatile substances from the mantle into the atmosphere (Lines 167-168). So can other kinds of
volcanism. 
6. The authors make strong claims in Section 2 and elsewhere about the reducing power in Hadean hydrothermal vents, but
there are reasons to suspect they may not have had much reducing power at all, owing to the higher concentration of
dissolved silicate in the ocean at the time (Tutolo et al. 2020). 
7. Claims about the LHB (Lines 246-248) without presenting other literature, e.g. Zellner (2017) that argue there never was
an LHB such as the authors describe. 
8. Strong claims (Lines 316-317) about the onset of oxygenic photosynthesis, despite the fact that the onset of oxygenic
photosynthesis is not well-constrained, and may have been quite early (see Bosak et al. 2009). 
9. I finish with a note about various places the authors discuss reaction energetics and reduction pathways. Sometimes the
authors suggest that favorable energetics will mean a reaction will occur spontaneously, but this it not known unless reaction
barriers and intermediates are resolved. In addition, there are claims about reaction pathways that reduce many things, such
as the formation of the carbon-carbon bond discussion (Lines 523-527), where it is asserted a certain chemistry will happen
without any support from citation, experiment or model. In addition, it's not even clear how the chemistry would work,
because many molecules are reduced, but no molecule the authors list is being oxidized. Where is the redox balance? 
2. **Conflation of environmental conditions.** 
The authors present a wide-ranging picture of the Hadean, from the atmosphere, to the surface, to the oceans, to subsurface
vents, and cite many studies that invoke a variety subaerial or submarine environments, but often combine these studies as
though the products and chemical reactions would have been accessible everywhere. The authors mix atmospheric
photochemistry, surface aqueous chemistry, and hydrothermal vent chemistry, without showing how these processes relate,
how scenarios invoking these processes or settings conflict, or how products could be transferred between settings. For
example, HCN generated in the atmosphere cannot plausibly accumulate in hydrothermal systems. The most serious
examples is found in Sections 2 and 3, esp. Lines 201-243, where the authors bring up impact-driven sources of reducing
power, hydrothermal sources of reducing power, photochemical generation of HCN, nitrogen availability in vents, bouncing
between these without contrasting them, or even categorizing which processes would happen in which geochemical
environments and whether or how material would move from one environment to the other. Section 3 is also littered with
examples, but one particularly striking one is Lines 469-472: 
> Branscomb and Russell suggested that hydrothermal systems in the Hadean oceans may have provided transition metal
ions (Fe2+, Ni2+, Co2+, and Zn2+) dissolved from the crust as well as materials such as HCN, NH3, H2S, H2, and CH4
through photochemical processes and impacts (Branscomb and Russell 2013). 
This sentence by itself combines both atmospheric and hydrothermal sources of redox power in a way that makes no sense.
How will the HCN, diluted in the ocean, reach the hydrothermal vent? How would the transition metal ions from deep sea
hydrothermal systems come in contact with the atmosphere? Not so say this *absolutely couldn't ever* be done, but to say
that the presentation is entirely confused, and there is no effort to clarify or make these kinds of connections. In addition, and
this can qualify as my first example, Branscomb and Russell (2013) never make the suggestion that hydrothermal systems
had access to materials such as HCN, NH3, H2S, H2 and CH4 through photochemical processes and impacts. They may
claim some of these, esp. H2S, H2 and CH4 may have been produced by serpentinization, but not sourced from the
atmosphere. 
This confusion is also clear in Figures 1 and 2. These are beautiful figures, and the schemes they draw would be of great
use to the community, if they are accurate. Figure 1 for example appears to have CH4 broken apart by UV to make 3 H2 and
an HCN under the ocean. If not under the ocean, how does the dissolved iron (Fe2+) get in the atmosphere? Figure 2 even
has dissolved iron escaping from the top of the atmosphere, and has placed ATP in the atmosphere alongside CO2 and
CH3 radicals. All these processes take place in very different environments. Even the atmospheric chemistry does not
happen all in the same part of the atmosphere, see Rimmer and Rugheimer (2019). 
The result of the authors overall discussion is a potpourri of scenarios, not a consistent picture. Figures confuse distinct
pathways without photochemistry or flux constraints. 
3. **Misrepresentation of the literature.** 
Several sources are used in ways inconsistent with what the authors actually argue. These errors undermine confidence that
the authors are in a position to provide a useful review for the origins community. Specific examples of this will be listed
below in minor comments.

The authors chose to cite, for the definition of nitrogen fixation, a paper about engineering a "first-generation

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This review takes on a lot. It offers a chronological review of the Earth system from the Hadean to the end of the Proterozoic,
some details of origin of life chemistry, and some details of the transition to eukaryotic life. I appreciate the ambition here and
lot of work had clearly gone in. However, there are some key revisions to make before publication, in my view. 
1. Consensus vs. opinion

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

Firstly, I would ask that the authors go through the paper with a critical eye and attempt to remove all statements that come
across as assertions about major topics that are actually hotly contested. For example: 
“it is widely accepted that during the early Hadean, Earth's surface was in a molten state, and primordial continents formed
from protocrust.” 
This is not widely accepted. There is plenty of dissent about how the first continents formed and when. If the authors want to
touch on that topic, they need to discuss the issue much more widely and include the many suggestions that have not yet
been discussed. Many authors still argue that continental crust was all but absent before 3 Ga, for example. Personally, I
have no preference, but the lack of consensus must be recognised. 
This goes for plenty of other areas. Early Earth studies are controversial in general and reviews must be careful not to
present readers with a sense of consensus, as this is misleading and will cause confusion as they attempt to work in the
field. 
More examples of this: 
"These events likely contributed to the diversification of microscopic and multicellular eukaryotes and the rise of metazoans".
Did they? You need to say why, and account for the many arguments that exist that this is not the case and that metazoans
may have originated earlier than we think. 
Similarly, please simply review and don't present things as if they are new, e.g., "we hypothesize that redox conditions on
early Earth may have fostered an environment conducive to crucial stages of the origin and evolution of life". This isn't really
a hypothesis. It seems clear from evidence that early Earth gave rise to life, but that is just inductive logic from the age of
fossils. The review doesn't have to hypothesise anything, certainly not something so broad. If you want, feel free to argue for
vents over ponds, though this will need more evidence. 
2. Origin of life chemistry 
I enjoyed that the article mentioned both vent chemistry and HCN chemistry, as often proponents of each ignore the other.
However, there are issues with what is currently written. 
The review contains numerous implicit assumptions that need to be defended. In no particular order: 
- Assumes that vents closely analogous to modern examples existed on the prebiotic Earth. This needs to be justified since
we likely had different crustal compositions, heatflow styles, ocean composition, and so on. 
- The review presents a series of pieces of evidence that vent minerals may have contributed to prebiotic chemistry,
However, why are we restricting ourselves to deep sea vents? What is about deep sea vents that the authors are so drawn
to? Can you not find equivalents in lakes and other shallower basins? Are the minerals of interest absent elsewhere? Would
they have been absent in shallow surface environments, if they existed, on prebiotic environments? All of this is implicit, but I
am not sure it is actually known that it is true. And, if it isn't true, then focussing only on deep sea vents makes less sense to
me. 
- The key criticisms of deep sea vents are not properly discussed. Vents face a concentration problem, wherein molecules
produced are continuously pumped out into a dilute ocean where they are lost to diffusion and subsequent hydrolysis. 
- Whilst the paper does lay out the experimental support for vents, the paper fails to make clear the experimentally
demonstrated weaknesses. For example, the range of molecules that vent chemistry has produced in nature or in the lab is
actually quite small and come with possible paradoxes in formation conditions. For example: 
- Nucleobases (e.g., adenine, cytosine) can form from HCN under vent conditions, but often require additional steps like UV
radiation. 
- Nucleosides and nucleotides have been synthesized under hydrothermal-like conditions (e.g., 90°C, pH 2.5), but yields are
low, and products like AMP or GMP are often isomeric mixtures, not the canonical forms.

the only such instances; if the editor and/or authors agree, it would be their job to provide a
comprehensive rewrite of this paper to ﬁx all its problems: to address all the major criticisms,
all the minor criticisms, including the ﬁrst 15 citation problems, and then all the rest of the
citation problems with the paper that this review does not touch.
I have written a long review with two goals in mind. First, in the effort to convince the editor
that this review should not be accepted for publication. Second, in the effort to convince the
authors that there are many unresolved problems with this paper, in the hopes they would
completely rewrite the paper, addressing these problems, before trying to publish this paper
anywhere else.

2. Conﬂation of environmental conditions. The authors present a wide-ranging picture
of the Hadean, from the atmosphere, to the surface, to the oceans, to subsurface vents,
and cite many studies that invoke a variety subaerial or submarine environments, but
often combine these studies as though the products and chemical reactions would have
been accessible everywhere. The authors mix atmospheric photochemistry, surface
aqueous chemistry, and hydrothermal vent chemistry, without showing how these
processes relate, how scenarios invoking these processes or settings conﬂict, or how
products could be transferred between settings. For example, HCN generated in the
atmosphere cannot plausibly accumulate in hydrothermal systems. The most serious
examples is found in Sections 2 and 3, esp. Lines 201-243, where the authors bring up
impact-driven sources of reducing power, hydrothermal sources of reducing power,
photochemical generation of HCN, nitrogen availability in vents, bouncing between
these without contrasting them, or even categorizing which processes would happen in
which geochemical environments and whether or how material would move from one
environment to the other. Section 3 is also littered with examples, but one particularly
striking one is Lines 469-472:
Branscomb and Russell suggested that hydrothermal systems in the Hadean
oceans may have provided transition metal ions (Fe2+, Ni2+, Co2+, and Zn2+)

CH4 through photochemical processes and impacts (Branscomb and Russell

This sentence by itself combines both atmospheric and hydrothermal sources of redox
power in a way that makes no sense. How will the HCN, diluted in the ocean, reach the
hydrothermal vent? How would the transition metal ions from deep sea hydrothermal
systems come in contact with the atmosphere? Not so say this absolutely couldn't ever
be done, but to say that the presentation is entirely confused, and there is no effort to
clarify or make these kinds of connections. In addition, and this can qualify as my ﬁrst
example, Branscomb and Russell (2013) never make the suggestion that hydrothermal
systems had access to materials such as HCN, NH3, H2S, H2 and CH4 through
photochemical processes and impacts. They may claim some of these, esp. H2S, H2
and CH4 may have been produced by serpentinization, but not sourced from the
atmosphere.
