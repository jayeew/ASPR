Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript by Tahiri et al., the authors investigated the molecular basis of dimorphism in the human pathogenic
fungus Mucor lusitanicus, as well as the conservation of gene families and expression patterns in Mucor and Rhizopus
species within the Mucorales order. Dimorphism in fungi, especially in human pathogenic fungi, is crucial for the transition
from an environmental form to a parasitic (host) form. Thus, this study addresses an important question in the fungal field.
However, the manuscript offers a moderate conceptual advancement in the field of fungal dimorphism. 
The experimental
approach includes transcriptomics performed with M. lusitanicus grown to different developmental stages (yeast-like, primary
mycelium, reverse transition, mature mycelium), gene deletions, and characterization of their effect on transcriptome
changes, and pull-down assays using DNA probes. Main concerns are the frequent overinterpretation of results, and the lack
of experimental details. The transcriptome results and the generated mutants can be useful to the Mucorales researchers,
but the scope remains limited.

Below are some major points that can be considered to improve the manuscript: 
-Abstract/background: The concept of dimorphism has been overemphasized without the description of many dimorphic
fungi. The many members of the Onygenales order, including Coccidioides, Histoplasma, Blastomyces, Paracoccidioides
species are thermally dimorphic fungi causing serious diseases in humans. In addition, many other fungi across the fungal
kingdom, including Taloromyces marneffii, Sporothrix schenckii, Cryptococcus neoformans, C. gattii, are considered
dimorphic and cause significant human diseases. None of these fungi are mentioned in the manuscript. There is a
significant amount of transcriptome data published in many of these fungi. If the intent is to look at the evolution of
dimorphism or gene families involved in dimorphism, then the integration of some of these datasets should be considered. 
-Abstract/background/results: The concept of gene expression is completely misused. Transcriptome studies do not
measure gene expression; rather, they measure the abundance of transcripts at a given time in a given condition. The
authors provide no evidence to claim some genes are “exclusively yeast expressed,” or “exclusively mycelial expressed,” or
“constitutively expressed”. The results can be divided in terms of enrichment of a given transcript in a given state, or changes
in abundance; however, RNAseq experiments do not reveal any information on the level of gene expresssion. 
-Fig 1/results: The authors should reconsider the RNAseq analysis especially the determination of the DEGs. There is a
significant number of genes with high pvalues (closer to bottom) that still pass their significance filter. Perhaps because of

other dimoprhic fungi. In addition, pathway analysis revealed no clear enrichment in any pathway, thus it is unclear why
Fig1e is included in the main text. 
-Fig 2/results: There is an overemphasis on the bidirectional promoters and paralogs showing differential expression in

form versus the other, and this is perhaps advantageous for the pathogen. The most exciting data in this figure are those in
panel b, which show the phenotypes of double mutants, corroborating their phase-specific expression. Surprisingly, these
mutants were not used for follow-up RNAseq experiments, but their contribution to the yeast or mycelial transcriptomes
would be significant based on their phenotypes. 
-Fig 3/results: What are the importance of the motifs identified here? Do these motifs have any predictive power in the
existing datasets. For example, does the motif on left found in the majority of the genes with yeast-enriched transcripts? 
-Fig 4/results: The figure itself looks aesthetically pleasing; however, there is no significant difference in gene distribution in
any groups reported here. 
-Fig 5/results: It is unclear why primary mycelium and reverse transition were selected to assay for yeast and hyphae specific
transcriptional regulators. A gel image validating the pull-downs would have been good to include here. It is peculiar that the
two proteins identified through these assays were not transcription factors. Nevertheless, dfl seems to contribute to yeast-like
growth. 
However, based on the panel g, its contribution to the transcriptome profiles is not extensive. It is also unclear what
the comparison is in panel g (reverse transition versus ?) 
-Fig 6/results: This is a nice attempt to include other fungi for comparative analysis; however, the division of orthogroups is
not well defined.

Response: We thank the reviewer for these insightful comments, which give us the 
opportunity to clarify our results. Primary mycelium and the reverse transition were 
selected as starting material to represent aerobic and anaerobic conditions, respectively. 
Our reaso ning was that potential mutants in a regulator required to inhibit yeast -cell 
production under anaerobic conditions could pose challenges for subsequent experiments 
in these conditions. As it turned out, the regulators we identiﬁed, dﬂ and dql, indeed affect 
yeast-cell formation. Importantly, however, the reverse transition can still be induced under 
anaerobic conditions, which allowed us to further examine the effects of mutations in these 
genes. 
As the reviewer correctly notes, the identiﬁed proteins are not transcription factors and do 
not contain canonical DNA-binding domains. Therefore, they are unlikely to interact directly 
with promoter DNA, and for this reason, we considered a DNA pull-down validation gel not 
to be appropriate. Instead, our ongoing work focuses on testing whether these proteins form 
part of a larger complex and identifying additional components that may mediate DNA 
interaction, as discussed in the revised manuscript. Moreove r, in our puriﬁcation 
experiments, the entire protein extract was analyzed by HPLC followed by mass 
spectrometry, enabling detection of both high- and low-abundance proteins. In contrast, a 
gel-based approach would primarily visualize only the most abundant proteins, limiting the 
scope of identiﬁcation. 
Regarding Figure 5g, we appreciate the opportunity to clarify. Our aim was to ﬁrst illustrate 
(in the WT column) the set of genes that are differentially expressed during the dimorphic 
transition, and then to examine how the expression of these same genes changes in the two 
mutant strains (columns 2 and 3). This design allowed us to highlight how genes 
differentially regulated in the wild type become deregulated in the mutants. We 
acknowledge that this was not sufficiently clear in the original text and ha ve revised the 
Results section accordingly (lines 3 10–321) to provide a more precise explanation of this 
analysis.
