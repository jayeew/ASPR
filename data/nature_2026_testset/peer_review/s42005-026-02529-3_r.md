Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Rydberg atom-based microwave sensors have gained significant popularity recently. However, due to the discrete nature of
atomic energy levels, Rydberg-atom microwave electrometers exhibit extremely high sensitivity when measuring microwave
fields that are resonant with Rydberg transitions. Yet, this sensitivity drops significantly for far-off-resonant fields.
Consequently, achieving high sensitivity across a broad frequency range has become a key research focus in this field.
Building on a comprehensive review of prior work, the authors propose a magnetic-field modulation technique that enables
high-sensitivity measurements over the 1–40 GHz range; this constitutes a timely and valuable contribution to the field. I
recommend its publication in Communications Physics. Here, I would like to offer some detailed comments for
consideration: 
C1: The authors employ cuboid ferrite magnets to produce magnetic-field modulation near the atomic vapor cell, and they
note that the magnets are much larger than the cell to ensure field uniformity. Providing additional information on the
uniformity—such as magnetometer measurements—would strengthen support for the reported results. 
C2: In Fig. 1(a) 
the authors place the cuboid ferrite magnets adjacent to the vapor cell. They should also discuss whether
microwave reflections from these magnets could influence the measurement. 
C3: In Fig. 1(a), there appears to be a baffle/plate in front of the antenna tip. It is unclear whether this is a drawing error or
introduced for a specific purpose; the authors are encouraged to clarify. 
C4: 
A space should be inserted between numbers and units, e.g., “7.884 GHz” in the caption of Fig. 2, this expression is also
used similarly in the main text. 
C5: About Fig.2, the authors should add error bar in Fig.2 (c), the same comment to Fig. 4. And by the way, the abbreviation
for the magnetic-field unit “gauss” is “G”, not “Gs”, this expression is also used similarly in the main text. Also, several
abbreviations deviate from common usage: “Experiment” is usually shortened to “Expt.” rather than “Exp.”, and “Theory” to
“Theo.” instead of “Theor.”

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This research utilizes magnetic field-controlled atomic energy levels to achieve continuous detection in the 1-40 GHz range.
Furthermore, by modifying the locking frequency, it enhances the electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT) signal,
addressing the degradation of the EIT signal amplitude caused by the introduction of a magnetic field and ensuring high
sensitivity and ultra-wideband measurements. Furthermore, the paper provides a theoretical basis, and the experimental
results agree well with this theory. However, the novelty of this paper requires clarification before it can be accepted. 
I have the following questions and suggestions regarding this paper: 
1. The paper mentions that the magnetic field is provided by a ferrite magnet. However, in many other studies, the magnetic
field is provided by a Helmholtz coil. Why was a magnet used in this work instead of a coil? 
2. In this study, the magnetic field is adjusted by varying the distance between the magnet and the atomic cell. What is the
value of this distance, how is it precisely adjusted, and how is the magnitude of the magnetic field measured after
adjustment? How is the effect of the magnet position change on the atomic cells in other modules compensated for? 
3. The orientations of the three magnets in this paper are not exactly the same, which means that the battery in the
measurement module is inevitably affected by the magnetic fields from other modules with different orientations. Will these
magnetic fields introduce additional noise? How was mutual isolation between magnetic fields achieved experimentally? 
4. The energy level diagram in Figure 1b is not intuitive. It is recommended that the diagram in the article
(https://doi.org/10.1364/OE.505488) be used as a reference when redrawing. 
5. For Figure 3c, it is recommended that a comparison with the EIT signal under no-magnetic-field conditions be included. 
6. During the superheterodyne experiment, does the magnetic field negatively affect the noise floor? If so, how was this
issue addressed?

C1: The paper mentions that the magnetic field is provided by a ferrite magnet. However, in many 
other studies, the magnetic field is provided by a Helmholtz coil. Why was a magnet used in this work 
instead of a coil? 
Answer & revision: Thank you for your professional comment. There are roughly three reasons：
(1) Helmholtz coils generate magnetic field by metal coils, and we consider that the scattering of 
the metal coils may affect our measurement of the microwave field. Certainly, the scattering of the 
magnets might also have an impact on the measurement. However, our tests have shown that, on 
the one hand, the influence of the magnets is within our acceptable range; on the other hand, we can 
reduce this influence by replacing magnets of different thicknesses and adjusting the distance 
between the magnets and the vapor cell. (2) Based on the actual experimental conditions, Helmholtz 
coils require more space, but our current experimental platform does not have enough space to place 
the coils. In contrast, magnets only need a small amount of space  to generate sufficiently uniform 
magnetic field, and they are relatively easy to operate with lower costs.  (3) In response to future 
application requirements, we aim to integrate the magnetic field adjustment function and the vapor 
cell into an integrated measurement probe with adjustable resonant frequency in the future. 
Compared with Helmholtz coils, the integration of magnets and the vapor cell is more convenient 
and straightforward.  
In summary, magnets can meet our current testing needs, with lower costs and simpler operation. 
However, we do not deny that Helmholtz coils are also a good option. 
 
C2: In this study, the magnetic field is adjusted by varying the distance between the magnet and the 
atomic cell. What is the value of this distance, how is it precisely adjusted, and how is the magnitude 
of the magnetic field measured after adjustment? How is  the effect of the magnet position change on 
the atomic cells in other modules compensated for? 
Answer & revision: In the paper, the adjustment range of the magnetic field strength we require is 
from several tens of gauss to 400 G. We mainly adjust the magnetic field strength by replacing 
magnets of different thicknesses and changing the distance between the magnet and the atomic vapor 
cell. We have prepared three types of magnets with different thicknesses, including 10mm, 20mm, 
and 30mm. For magnetic fields below 200 G, magnets with a thickness of 10 mm are mainly used. 
For magnetic fields above 200 G, magnets with thicknesses of 20 mm and 30 mm are primarily 
employed; Additionally, the magnetic field strength at the vapor cell is adjusted more precisely by 
modifying the distance between the magnet and the vapor cell. In the experiment, the distance 
adjustment range used is about from 100 mm to 25 mm.  Distance adjustment mainly relies on a 
precision displacement stage. The magnet is fixed on the displacement stage, and the forward and 
backward adjustment can be achieved with the precision of 10 μm. The magnet adjustment method 
for other modules basically adopts this approach.  The measurement of magnetic field intensity 
mainly uses a teslameter, with a test accuracy of 1% and a minimum resolution of 0.1 G. The 
magnetic field measurement is mainly achieved based on a Hall sensor probe. The magnetic field

C1: The authors employ cuboid ferrite magnets to produce magnetic-field modulation near the atomic

Answer & revision: Thank you for pointing this out. It’s found that the uniformity of the magnetic field 
may influences the modulation efficiency of energy level, so we chose magnets much larger than the 
vapor-cell to ensure the uniformity. Three types of magnets were tested to provide magnetic fields of 
different strengths. The thicknesses are 10 mm, 20 mm, and 30 mm, respectively, with the same length 
(150 mm) and  width (100 mm). Magnets with different thicknesses can generate magnetic fields of 
different intensity ranges. For example, a 10mm thick magnet is used to produce magnetic field strength 
less than 200G. For higher magnetic field strength, a thicker magnet should be used. Here, we verify the 
uniformity of the magnetic field under different distance s from the vapor cells using a calibrated 
magnetometer. As shown in Fig 1(b), for the 10mm thick magnet, the difference between the maximum 
and minimum magnetic fields at the  vapor cell is less than 3%. For other thickness magnets, it shows 
that their uniformities are basically consistent with that of the 10mm thick magnet. We believe that this 
level of error can be neglected in the experiment.

C1: The paper mentions that the magnetic field is  provided by a ferrite magnet. However, in many 
other studies, the magnetic field is provided by a Helmholtz coil. Why was a magnet used in this work 
instead of a coil? 
Answer & revision: Thank you for your professional comment. There are roughly three reasons：
(1) Helmholtz coils generate magnetic field by metal coils, and we consider that the scattering of 
the metal coils may affect our measurement of the microwave field. Certainly, the scattering of the 
magnets might also have an impact on the measurem ent. However, our tests have shown that, on 
the one hand, the influence of the magnets is within our acceptable range; on the other hand, we can 
reduce this influence by replacing magnets of different thicknesses and adjusting the distance 
between the magnets and the vapor cell. (2) Based on the actual experimental conditions, Helmholtz 
coils require more space, but our current experimental platform does not have enough space to place 
the coils. In contrast, magnets only need a small amount of space  to generate sufficiently uniform 
magnetic field, and they are relatively easy to operate with lower costs.  (3) In response to future 
application requirements, we aim to integrate the magnetic field adjustment function and the vapor 
cell into an integrated measure ment probe with adjustable resonant frequency in the future. 
Compared with Helmholtz coils, the integration of magnets and the vapor cell is more convenient 
and straightforward.  
In summary, magnets can meet our current testing needs, with lower costs and simpler operation. 
However, we do not deny that Helmholtz coils are also a good option. 
 
C2: In this study, the magnetic field is adjusted by varying the distance between the magnet and the 
atomic cell. What is the value of this distance, how is it precisely adjusted, and how is the magnitude 
of the magnetic field measured after adjustment? How is the effect of the magnet position change on 
the atomic cells in other modules compensated for? 
Answer & revision:  In the paper, the adjustment range of the  magnetic field strength we require 
is from several tens of gauss to 400 G. We mainly adjust the magnetic field strength by replacing 
magnets of different thicknesses and changing the distance between the magnet and the atomic vapor 
cell. We have prepared three types of magnets with different thicknesses, including 10mm, 20mm, 
and 30mm. For magnetic fields below 200 G, magnets with a thickness of 10 mm are mainly used. 
For magnetic fields above 200 G, magnets with thicknesses of 20 mm and 30 mm are primaril y 
employed; Additionally, the magnetic field strength at the vapor cell is adjusted more precisely by 
modifying the distance between the magnet and the vapor cell . In the experiment, the distance 
adjustment range used is about from 100 mm to 25 mm.  Distance adjustment mainly relies on a 
precision displacement stage. The magnet is fixed on the displacement stage, and the forward and 
backward adjustment can be achieved with the precision of 10 μm. The magnet adjustment method 
for other modules basically adopts this approach.  The measurement of magnetic field intensity 
mainly uses a teslameter, with a test accuracy of 1% and a minimum resolution of 0.1 G. The 
magnetic field measurement is mainly achieved based on a Hall sensor probe. The magnetic field 
measurement process is divided into the following steps: ①remove the atomic vapor cell, ②place

the sensor probe at the position where the atomic vapor cell was originally placed to measure the 
magnetic field intensity, ③remove the sensor and pu t the atomic vapor cell back. The magnetic 
field measurement scenario is shown as follows . When the magnetic field intensity in the 
measurement module is changed, the magnetic field intensity at the vapor cell in other modules 
needs to be adjusted to the s ame as measurement module , and the magnetic field intensity 
adjustment and measurement methods are basically consistent with those in the measurement 
module. To avoid mutual interference between the magnetic fields generated by magnets in different 
modules, we have adopted two methods . O n one hand, when setting up the optical path, we
