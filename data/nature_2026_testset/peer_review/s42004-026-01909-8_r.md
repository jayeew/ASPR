Reply to Comment: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. The primary objective 
of our study was to develop a biocompatible and reproducible photothermal platform for the 
treatment of endometrial cells using an 808 nm NIR laser. Citrate-stabilized AuNPs were 
selected as the photothermal agent due to their well-documented biocompatibility, photophysical 
stability, and extensive history of use in biomedical research. Importantly, citrate-stabilized 
AuNPs are FDA-approved, which further underscores their safety profile for translational 
applications. 
We fully agree that anisotropic nanostructures such as gold nanorods (AuNRs) exhibit superior 
photothermal conversion efficiencies because of their strong longitudinal surface plasmon 
resonance. However, their synthesis typically involves cetyltrimethylammonium bromide 
(CTAB), a surfactant well known for its significant cytotoxicity and limited biocompatibility. 
While alternative ligand-exchange strategies exist to replace CTAB, these often compromise 
colloidal stability, reproducibility, or scalability. Therefore, to maintain a balance between

photothermal performance, biosafety, and ease of synthesis, we chose citrate-stabilized spherical 
AuNPs for this work.

Reply to Comment: We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comment. As mentioned in the 
Introduction, our current study focuses on evaluating the in vitro and ex vivo performance of our 
Au-PLGA-IL nanoparticles (NPs) using 12Z endometrial epithelial cells and adult human female 
blood. The goal was to test their photothermal response and neutrophil-mediated targeting under 
conditions that mimic endometriosis. 
Our Au-PLGA-IL NPs are highly biocompatible as shown in the cytotoxcicty assay (section 2.3) 
prior to photothermal treatment, hence we expect them to be safe towards healthy cells as well. It 
is important to note that the selectivity of our NPs comes from the association towards 
neutrophils rather than the epithelial cell type under consideration. The therapeutic efficacy is 
dependent on laser irradiation (808 nm, 1 W/cm2) i.e., localized irradiation of cells leading to 
cellular death at sites where our NPs are present due to the photothermal effect.  
Although we agree that healthy cells NPs are likely to also be damaged under laser exposure, but 
note that in our intended application, the laser treatment would be selectively applied at 
endometrial sites only where the neutrophil recruitment is much higher than normal tissue. The 
localized targeting should help minimize any other side effects such as damage to nearby healthy 
cells, and since our NPs are inherently biocompatible, any particles that reach non-endometrial 
sites should be cleared from the system through immune clearance pathways.  
We emphasize that further in vivo studies are crucial to confirm this hypothesis and better 
understand biodistribution and clearance in healthy systems, which we intend to do in the next 
phase of this project.

The fundamental mechanism of the photothermal effect relies on the plasmonic absorption and 
non-radiative decay of the gold core. The IL coating, owing to its unique electrostatic properties, 
can improve the interfacial thermal transport between the gold core and the surrounding medium. 
In particular, the dense and ion-rich IL layer, potentially more thermally conductive than a purely 
aqueous interface, can facilitate more efficient heat dissipation from the gold core to its 
surroundings, thereby reducing thermal losses and enhancing the local temperature rise under 
photothermal irradiation. 
Also, IL coatings have been shown to prevent aggregation further and allow uniform dispersion 
of NPs, as aggregation can lead to plasmonic coupling and further lead to more scattering over 
absorption, hence our IL-coated NPs can potentially facilitate both efficient interfacial heating 
and prevent aggregation, leading to higher photothermal conversion efficiency than bare 
(uncoated) NPs.
