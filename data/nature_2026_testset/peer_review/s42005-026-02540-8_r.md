Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript addresses one of the key unresolved issues in orbitronics—the unexpectedly long decay length of orbital
angular momentum (OAM). As clearly described in the Introduction, several experiments have argued for a long OAM decay
length, whereas theoretical calculations predict an extremely short one, creating a striking contrast. I use the term “argued”
for the experimental observations because it remains uncertain whether the measured quantity truly represents the OAM
decay length or something else. In this regard, the topic discussed in this manuscript is both timely and of crucial importance.
The authors attempt to address this puzzle by presenting two conflicting theoretical pictures in separate subsections: (1)
“Symmetry-Protected Hot Spots and Long-Range Transport,” and (2) “Local Generation of Orbital Polarization by Charge-
Current Gradients.” While comparing these two approaches could indeed help readers grasp the central issue, there are
significant misconceptions (or inaccurate descriptions) in subsection (1) concerning the physics of hot spots. These must be
corrected before this work can be considered for publication. 
A. The hot-spot physics reported in Ref. 17 is relevant only for OAM transport in ferromagnets (FM), not in non-magnetic
normal metals (NM). However, the current manuscript gives the impression that the same mechanism also applies to NMs.
This ambiguity must be clarified in the revision. 
B. Hot-spot physics typically accounts for relatively small effects, such as transverse Hall currents, which are much smaller
than the corresponding longitudinal currents. A representative example is the spin Hall effect in FMs [Phys. Rev. B 99,
220405(R) (2019)], where the spin Hall current is much weaker than the longitudinal current. This difference in magnitude is
natural because the volume of the hot-spot region in momentum space is tiny compared with the full solid angle sampled by
electrons moving along all k_x, k_y, and k_z directions. Moreover, hot-spot physics becomes relevant only when an electric
field is applied directly to a system possessing such hot spots, as in the spin Hall effect of FMs. In contrast, Ref. 17
considered OAM Hall currents generated in an NM that are injected into an FM, arguing that the hot spots in the FM are
responsible for long-range orbital torque. Although such hot spots indeed exist, their contribution should be minuscule
because their momentum-space volume is extremely small. Consequently, only a negligible fraction of the injected OAM
current would couple to these hot spots. Given that the injected OAM Hall current itself represents only a small portion of the
total charge current, the resulting OAM current associated with FM hot spots scales as (small)^2 and thus cannot explain the
experimentally observed long-range orbital torque. This is in sharp contrast to spin-current absorption by FM, for which the
entire Fermi surface (with nearly same exchange splitting) contributes, resulting in an efficient spin torque. 
C. Ref. 17 focused exclusively on intrinsic responses without properly accounting for disorder. This omission is problematic
because disorder is unavoidable in real samples, and disorder-induced extrinsic effects can sometimes cancel intrinsic
contributions—as well established for the intrinsic spin Hall effect in the Rashba model and more recently for the intrinsic
orbital Hall effect [Phys. Rev. Lett. 133, 186302 (2024) and the works of Thierry Valet]. Even if one ignores extrinsic
mechanisms, treating disorder inadequately in a finite system (as done in Ref. 17) can lead to an unrealistic persistence of
quantum coherence that would not survive in real samples measured at room temperature.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their “Perspective” manuscript the Authors address a current issue in orbital transport, namely, the length of the orbital
diffusion length. This property has become discussed intensively recently. 
The manuscript is concise, and the Authors provide a good, short introduction to the topic. Understandably, there are
meanwhile many more publications in this lively research field than those mentioned in the introduction. 
To address the differences in the orbital diffusion lengths reported in various experiments, the Authors take the viewpoint
that there exist two competing models, a hot spot model (Ref. 17) and a local vorticity generation model (Ref. 23). This is a
somewhat restricted view, as there is more work that has been done than these two articles. 
Also, it deserves to be noted that the two models are treated as if they are on the same level, and hence “competing
paradigms” but I feel that this is not justified. The work of Ref. 17 is based on ab-initio calculations for a large system to verify
the scientific proposal. Reference 23 is conversely based on several ideas. The validity of these ideas for the explanation of
orbital transport is uncertain. For example, the intraband orbital Hall current is said to appear second-order in spin-orbit
coupling (Ref. 23); however, other calculations of the orbital Hall conductivity predict it to be independent of spin-orbit
coupling (i.e., zero-order, Refs. 5-7). According to Ref. 23, there is an interband component which does not represent
transport (as written on page 4). However, it is known from many works that for instance the anomalous Hall effect is an
interband component and it does reflect real transport. The current vorticity proposed in Ref. 23 could lead to some magnetic
polarization – it is basically the same idea as generating a magnetic field through a charge current in coils. It is however
difficult to imagine that in a linear material there would be a nonzero current rotation, as illustrated in Fig. 3c. There is no
reason why there would be a symmetry-breaking (curls going only in one rotational sense). The model remains thus
questionable. 
Another explanation for the observation of relatively long orbital diffusion lengths was proposed by Idrobo et al
(arXiv:2403.09269). In samples with nanometer grains orbital angular momentum will be generated at the grain boundaries
through the orbital Rashba-Edelstein effect, which requires a local spatial symmetry breaking. This does therefore give local
generation of orbital moments but not due to circulating currents. In addition, a reduction of the crystal field will lead to a
reduced quenching of the orbital angular momentum. This can give improved orbital transport as has been reported recently
Peng et al (Nature Materials, 2025) and also Ref. 10, Gao et al. 
My recommendation is that the Authors revise their article trying to take the above comments into account. As a suggestion,
in Figure 3 they could insert a panel to illustrate that OAM can be generated locally by the orbital Rashba-Edelstein effect at
grain boundaries or defects in the sample. It can then likely also be transported over some distance as suggested by Ding et
al (Ref. 4). I believe that this would improve the manuscript and help the broad readership to comprehend better the
contemporary discussion in this area and provide a fair picture of the recent ideas.
