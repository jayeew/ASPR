Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Larrieu and colleagues describes a blood-brain axis assay that measures in vitro neurogenesis in the
presence of serum collected from study subjects, both mice and humans. They detected LPA 16:0 in the serum, with
correlation to trait- and state-anxiety in high risk individuals, and evaluated LPA receptor 1 (LPA1) expression in the human
hippocampus from published snRNA databases. A series of studies is then described, testing LPA16:0 and an LPA1
anatagonist in rodents for effects on neurogenesis and stress resilience. Induced thrombocytopenia also appears to reduce
LPA16:0 levels and increase both stress resilience and neurogenesis. The findings of this study are quite interesting. The
cross-species approach in rodents and humans, and the use of peripherally available sample to assay in vitro both
strengthen the translational relevance of the findings. 
This study would be of general interest to the readers of this journal and is generally well-represented. However, there are
some alternative explanations for the authors’ findings, which should either be resolved with an additional experiment, or
appropriately addressed in the discussion. Namely, the effects of circulating LPA 16:0 or systemically-administered LPA
16:0 and LPAR1 antagonist could all be exerting effects on stress susceptibility through non-neuronal cells. The
temozolomide experiment would be affecting non-neuronal lineages as well. In light of the RNAseq data that shows high
levels of expression in oligos, astrocytes, and microglia, this possible alternative mechanism should be addressed. By the
way the human RNAseq data that are presented are confusing; wouldn’t you expect a larger abundance of dentate gyrus
granule cells, the most abundant cell type in the hippocampus? 
Below are some suggestions, as well as other points that would strengthen the manuscript. 
- We suggest an experiment combining an shRNA for LPA1 and retrovirus delivery to directly target the dentate gyrus and
neural lineage progenitor cells in vivo. This is within the expertise of the senior author, and might show whether LPA 16:0
and Ki16425 effects are specific to neurogenesis effects in the DG. 
- Alternatively, additional characterization of experimental manipulation effects on gliogenesis or neurogenesis elsewhere in
the brain, might address this point. 
- Or if the authors do not have an experimental method to address the role of non-neuronal lineages, this should be
mentioned in the discussion. 
- From a more big-picture perspective, how do the authors view the role of LPA 16:0 among other circulating factors (stress
hormones, inflammatory cells, etc) that might be affecting anxiety levels and stress-resilience?

identify in particular LPA 16:0 that correlates with trait anxiety in both mice and men. They further go on to show that SNPs in

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Larrieu et al. interrogate systemic factors regulating adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) in the context of anxiety and
resilience to acute and chronic stressors. Much attention has been given to blood-borne regulators of AHN in the past
decade, including in ageing, neurodegenerative disease, and physiological states like exercise. However, anxiety and
stress resilience have received less attention. In this sense, the study is novel and a welcome addition to the field. The
combined use of animal and in vitro models for mechanistic interrogation and human (patient) data for clinical relevance is a
noteworthy strength of the manuscript. Furthermore, the multiple levels of analysis and significant efforts in establishing
causal relationships are commendable. The manuscript does a good job of identifying a possible causal mediator of stress
resilience and establishing its relevance to the human condition. 
We have listed a few points that we believe are worth addressing to further strengthen the manuscript. 
Major comments 
1. Anxiety score (lines 426-442): it would be good to include supplementary figures of the raw data from all anxiety-related
behavioral tasks. The idea of using a composite, standardized anxiety score is excellent. However, a caveat is that min-max
scaling of the relativized outcome measures (e.g. relative time spent in the closed arm of the EPM; lines 430-431) obscures

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

the original data distributions and interpretability. Indeed, it is theoretically possible for all relativized measures to indicate no
putative anxiety (i.e. roughly equal time spent in anxiogenic vs safe environments); yet, the min-max transformation will still
output values between 0-1. In this case, while a mouse with the score 1 would imply the highest level of anxiety, the original
data would suggest no anxiety at all. Seeing as the outcome measures used for the anxiety scores are already relative and
based on time, these could be aggregated without min-max scaling. This would preserve biological interpretability. It would
be good to address how the scaled data correspond to the original data. Finally, the one-sample t-test used to “assess
anxiety levels in each group” (lines 437-442) appears somewhat redundant. Since a median split of the min-max scaled data
(which should be expected to have a median ~0.5) is used to define the groups, the one-sample t-test could reasonably be
expected to significantly reject the null hypothesis that each group mean is equal to the theoretical parameter 0.5. It seems
Figure 1I is sufficient to make this case. 
2. Related to point 1 above, it is not clear from the method why different behaviors were used for “state” and “trait” anxiety
scores (lines 429-432). In human literature, this distinction usually refers to acute and chronic feelings of anxiety,
respectively (e.g. as in the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, STAI). However, it is not immediately obvious that this distinction
neatly maps onto the listed behavioral tasks. Would chronic compared to acute stress in rodents only be evident in one task
(e.g. EPM) but not another (OFT)? It would be good to comment on this distinction and its behavioral relevance in rodents,
and to motivate the differential inclusion of tasks in the anxiety scores. 
3. aNPC (e.g. lines 91-112; 119-133; 317-338): the primary culture is based on dissociated, adult dentate gyrus tissue.
Plating such a cell suspension (i.e. compared to a purer suspension such as dissociated neurospheres) will yield a mixed
culture, as noted in REF 97. Yet, the methods and results sections seem to imply that cultures are pure adult neural
precursor cells (aNPCs) and, thus, that proliferation must be attributable to expansion of aNPCs. However, the tissue
harvesting procedure does not involve any obvious purification step. Additionally, the representative pictures of BrdU in vitro
(e.g. Figure 1J and 1R) only include nuclear DAPI staining, precluding any conclusions about lineage. Therefore, it would
be prudent to comment on this limitation and/or rephrase the language in terms of general proliferation throughout the text. 
4. 
Lines 122-128: The division of mice into low- and high-proliferation groups appears rather arbitrary. No motivation for the
sub-grouping threshold in Figure 1T is provided. Indeed, the top three mice (i.e. the ones that are not reliably different from
the control group) could be said to be “high proliferative”, making the rest low-proliferative. Conversely, the sub-grouping in
Figure 1V is in line with definitions earlier in the manuscript (but see comment 1 above). The application of receiver
operating characteristics (ROC) curves to the data in Figure 1V seems inappropriate (cf. Figure 2G). ROCs are meant to be
used to evaluate various decision thresholds in a classifier model based on labeled data (i.e. target labels that are defined a
priori, such as “patient” or “control”, or the classification in figure 2). Here, the sub-groups (i.e. the labels) are defined a
posteriori from the data (i.e. the threshold 0.5). The same data is then used to predict the threshold that was used to sub-
group the data. This will obviously result in a perfect model (AUC = 100%) because the argument is circular. Figure 1T-X
can be simplified by showing the correlation/linear relationship between state anxiety score and proliferation, either stratified
by group (i.e. CTRL vs CRS) or using residualized data (i.e. by regressing out the effect of group from all data and
correlating the residuals). The former alternative is likely preferable and most visually intuitive. 
5. In discovery -omics, p-values are adjusted or complemented with false discovery rate estimates ) to account for the
multiple comparisons problem. Thus, it is more appropriate to report FDR as opposed to nominal p-values. It is not clear if
the three metabolites on lines 158-159 passed the FDR < 0.05 threshold. This should be stated. Relatedly, because FDR
depends on the number of features tested, the total number of metabolites assessed should also be stated. The authors
should include a supplementary table with all tested metabolites, their statistics (i.e. fold-changes, t-values, F-values, etc),
nominal p-values, and FDR. Moreover, lines 157-161 mentions “correlation”. Usually, discovery -omics are reported as log
fold changes; if another statistic was used (e.g. correlation), this should be reported. The statistics used to test differential
abundance should be further clarified on lines 590-597. The most common way to summarize this type of data is using
volcano plots. The authors should consider including plotting the statistic versus the nominal p-value and highlight features
that pass FDR threshold. 
6. Figure 2V-W: given the lack of neural stem and precursor cell populations in these datasets, why were they chosen? The
authors state that LPAR1 is primarily expressed on NSCs, but the chosen datasets do not contain any subsets of the
hippocampal neurogenic trajectory. Several studies have interrogated adult hippocampal neurogenesis in humans using
scRNA-seq (see Tosoni et al., 2023, Neuron, for a recent review). It would be more convincing if any of these datasets were
used to examine the expression of LPAR1, as this aligns better with the study’s aims and subsequent experiments.
Otherwise, a motivation should be given for why the current datasets were selected. Currently, there is some contradiction
between the statement on line 172 and the results in Figure 2W.

General 
 
1) Next to their loss of function effects, is similar data available on a gain of f unction and what effects 
occur or are e.g. expected after promoting neurogenesis specifically in combination with LPA1 
antagonism? 
 
The aim of the TMZ treatment (loss of neurogenesis) was to investigate the causal contribution of adult 
neurogenesis in the beneficial effects of LPA1R antagonism in resilience to acute and chronic stress.  
While promoting neurogenesis in combination with LPA1R antagoni sm may induce a more robust 
phenotype of stress resilience, this interesting question was beyond the scope of t his study. Of note, 
physical exercise is associated with the increase production of PF4 by platelets that mediates a strong 
increase in adult hippocampal neurogenesis  (PMID 30905740). It is therefore likely that the effect of
