Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study proposes a green electrochemical process for producing FDCA from biomass-derived HMF via an anion-
exchange membrane–based oxidation reaction (AEM-HMFOR), achieving a high Faradaic efficiency (94.6%) and FDCA
yield (96.2%) with high purity. In addition, the authors demonstrate the techno-economic and environmental feasibility of the
process through comprehensive TEA and LCA. Notably, the proposed system achieves a production cost of approximately
$2,300/ton and significantly lower global warming potential compared to conventional FDCA production. Overall, this work
offers a promising direction for replacing fossil-derived plastics with biomass-based alternatives. However, several issues
must be addressed before this manuscript is suitable for publication in Nature Communications. 
1. The authors should clarify the specific advantages of the AEM-HMFOR process over conventional chemical-catalytic
oxidation routes in terms of selectivity, operational conditions, environmental impacts, and potential for coupling with
renewable electricity. 
2. While chemical oxidation processes typically benefit from economies of scale, the scalability of membrane-based systems
such as AEM-HMFOR may face limitations due to membrane cost, durability, and stack integration complexity. The authors
should discuss how such scale effects may impact the long-term competitiveness of the proposed system at commercial
scale.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript, the authors present a well-designed approach towards industrial scale FDCA synthesis, showcasing the
route from HMF to PEF, and providing context in terms of LCA and TEA for the process. 
They increase the electrode area
and stack design while providing inside into the engineering within the work. With the novelty in stack design in this
application and product purification, the content of the article is worth publishing. 
Yet, the manuscript lacks some details on experimental conditions and some aspects require revision and clarification prior
to publication. As there are no line numbers, the following comments refer to figures, chapters, and counted lines on each
page: 
1) Data transparency in introduction in reference to Figure 1: 
The authors mention “industry research” (page 2 line 8) as their source for data and mention a report in the caption of Figure
1a) which for which there is no citation. It is advised to make the data source more available (citation with DOI or other
unique references). 
The data for Figure 1c) is even less traceable, with no specific report given, making verification and a validity check
impossible. This should be remedied. Further, clarification in the caption is necessary, whether this is a projection (when,
under what circumstances) or current market share of products. 
2) Relevance and clarity of Figure 1 b: 
While the authors include a TEA and an LCA within their work, there is no localization of production capacity and no
coupling to solar energy supply. Therefore, Figure 1b adds little value to the content of the manuscript. Further, there is no
explanation for the box on the bottom right corner, in which, additionally, the color scheme does not seem to be applied. This
needs to be clarified. 
3) Missing data in Figure 1 d: 
The focus in Figure 1d) on STY over concentration is a new interpretation of existing data, which is highly valuable from a
scale-up point of view. The authors specified that the data collected is from recent publications without further specification.
Yet, there appears to be some data missing (e.g. 10.1021/acsenergylett.4c03257 – 200mM HMF, 83 mg/cm²/h, or

10.1021/acssuschemeng.4c04754 – 1M HMF, 140mg/cm²/h.). It is advised to revisit the data included or specify the basis on
which data was curated. 
4) Visibility of Data for Charge Transfer resistance (Rct) 
On page 5 line one the authors describe the Rct from Figure S5. However, due to the large dots in Figure S5, it is hard to
distinguish individual lines/data points. Revisiting and redesigning (e.g. smaller dots/excerpt) the figure is recommended. 
5) Missing Information/accessibility of methods: 
The authors test different flow channel widths; however, the flowrate is not apparent, neither in the method section in the SI,
nor in the manuscript. It could be the same as for Figure 3, but specification is needed. 
Further, the “three-electrode system” (page 4 line 28) is not described in the methods, it needs to be specified if this is an H-
cell, beaker or flow-cell. 
For example, a table in the SI that links the results of the (sub) Figures to the experimental conditions (such as electrode are,
CA or CP, current/voltage applied, flow rate, electrolyte, charge for evaluation, …) could increase accessibility. 
6) Inconsistency in data interpretation: 
On page 6 line 11 and following, the authors claim that HMF concentrations above 200mM lead to AEM degradation and
increasing ohmic losses. However, this is not apparent in Figure S5 and its interpretation on page 5 line 1. Further, this
conclusion is not mentioned when regarding elevated concentrations for the 100cm2 electrolyzer and its implications for
elevated HMF concentrations. 
7) Calculation of activation energy with reference to Figure 3a. 
The authors show the resulting cell voltage in Figure 3a with and without flow channels. The general result is plausible. Yet,
for the 200 mV difference (displayed in Figure 3a) the main single contributor is the higher activation potential in Figure S14,
which is not specified in the manuscript. Clarification on why the activation potential changes with the addition of flow
channels is required. 
8) Clarification on Error Bars in Figure 3b: 
Figure 3b is the only figure with error bars (for STY and FE) – clarification on what these display (and the absence of error
bars on yield) is necessary. 
9) Missing current profile for Figure 3c: 
The authors provide an STY for different flow rates after identical charge has passed through the electrode. The resulting
STY (at roughly equal FE) can only result from increasing current. Since the difference is very high between 0.25 and 1
ml/min, displaying the current/time plots for these graphs in the SI would be beneficial as it is only implicitly given in the
current form. Further, if the electrolyzer is “undersupplied” with HMF at low flow rates, this should be mentioned as, in this
case, the reason would not be mass transfer limitations but the absence of HMF in the electrolyzer. 
10) Altering charge as basis for evaluation. 
The authors evaluated the experiments in Figure 3b and Figure 3c after different charges (2000 C and 2300 C) in Figure 3.
Clarification on why these points were chosen is necessary. Further, Figure 3c is the only such diagram that lacks the FDCA
yield, which should be included. 
11) Voltage-Yield relation inconsistency/Electrical resistance in P-Stack: 
On page 8 line 4, (Figure S20) the authors describe the lowered FE at elevated voltage (above 1.6V), which they attributed
to OER, which is plausible. Yet, in Figure 4f, the authors describe, show how a cell voltage of 1.8 and ~2V produces a yield
and FE of close to 100%. This inconsistency needs to be mentioned, and the phenomenon needs to be described. The
“internal resistance” of the P-stack needs to be described and measured in more detail. Contact resistance needs to be
accounted for at the elevated current and actual current distribution should be measured in the individual cells. This could
give an indication of HMF conversion in the individual compartments. 
12) Clarification on differences and comparison between S-Stack and P-Stack: 
On page 9 line 5 and following the authors describe how in the S-stack the electrolyte flows similar to an electrolyzer without
a flow-channel, while in the P-stack, the electrolyte flowed as in an electrolyzer with a flow channel. However, the authors do
not sufficiently make clear why this is the case. It should be clarified whether there are flow channels in both electrolyzers
and why the flow is different (possibly via a sketch in the SI). If there are significant differences, they should be named.
Furthermore, a fair comparison should be made. 
The comparison is also hindered by the flow-velocity in both stacks. The flow rate in the P-stack is 5 times higher than in the
S-stack (page 9 line 11). This would result in an actual flow velocity that is 27 times as high as in the S-stack (5*7,18/1,33)
as there is a sequential flow in the P-stack while (as in a longer tube) than in the S-stack (as in a wider tube through parallel
flow cells). This would result in vastly different mass transfer properties. Here, clarification and reevaluation of the
comparison, especially in reference to the results in Figure 3c is necessary. 
13) Potentially misleading comparison on setups in Figure 5b): 
The Spider-web diagram in Figure 5b is somewhat misleading in terms of SEC. For all other metrics, the favorable number
are on the outside, while for the SEC, it would be more favorable to be at the center. This implies a superior performance of
the 80A setup, while in terms of SEC, this is not the case. Therefore, this metric should be reconsidered and could be
replaced by e.g. the amount of FDCA produced per kWh.

On page 9 line 23, the authors mention that a lower flowrate can destabilize electrocatalysis which is a highly interesting

Missing information on FDCA purification process: 
The authors purified FDCA from the solution, which is highly relevant for industrial application. However, the manuscript

The authors report on the color of PEF when polymerized from purified FDCA (page 11 line 20). While it is clear that an

analysis, especially regarding critical impurities that might require different purification steps. 
17) TEA: 
a. The authors state that even at current market prices, the AEM-HMFOR system would achieve reasonable profits (page 13
line 4). However, the products would remain in solution (in 1.2M KOH, according to the caption of Figure S34), which would

numbers in Note S8 and the manuscript. This should be revised. 
b. While the authors design a system to purify FDCA, in the TEA the authors assume purification costs to be 30% of the
electrolyzer costs. If there is a reason the authors do not refer to their purification system, it should be discussed, otherwhise,

c. Additionally, other costs (such as that the MEA costs 3% of the electrolyzer and the MEA will run as long as the

d. In the SI, the authors calculate an offset for H2 and K2SO4 price but do not specify whether this is included in the FDCA

combination with the purification steps. Furthermore, paired electrolysis should be mentioned as a possibility to increase the

10.1039/D1GC00914A) 
e. The color choice in graph Figure 5e makes distinguishing between H2O and KOH costs fairly difficult, a line connecting
KOH with the respective part in the diagram could assist. 
18) LCA: 
The authors provide numbers on FDCA GWP and other factors which is highly relevant. In the discussion of these numbers,

19) General lack of presentation of cathode side: 
In all Figures except Figure 1c, there is no input of water on the cathode side on all diagrams. Additionally, there is a lack of

through the AEM. If this was not measured, the possibility should at least be mentioned in the discussion.

2. In page 9, “ The  purity of the resultant crude FDCA was 99.2%, as determined by 1H 
NMR.” Relevant reference or calculation method needs to be provided.  
Response: Thanks for your constructive suggestions. Protons on the furan ring are clearly 
discernible in the 1H NMR spectrum (Fig. R2). To determine the purity of FDCA, impurities 
generated from side reactions during HMF oxidation (including humin and other coupling 
products) necessitated that all proton signals in the 1H NMR spectrum be conservatively 
assigned as individual protons , followed by the integration of corresponding peaks . The 
peak at 6. 5 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum of the obtained FDCA is attributed to the two 
protons on the furan ring. The peak at 5. 9 ppm is attributed to an impurity. Assuming this 
impurity peak is integrated as one proton, its maximum molar content is calculated to be 
0.8%. This indicates that the FDCA we prepared has a minimum purity of 99. 2%. 
Consequently, the FDCA purity by this method represents a minimum purity, which features 
a conservative lower bound. The calculated FDCA purities in the Manuscript are > 99.2% 
(without membrane separation) and > 99.8% (with membrane separation).

Reviewer #2: This study proposes a green electrochemical process for producing FDCA 
from biomass -derived HMF via an anion -exchange membrane –based oxidation reaction 
(AEM-HMFOR), achieving a high Faradaic efficiency (94.6%) and FDCA yield (96.2%) 
with high purity. In addition, the authors demonstrate the techno -economic and 
environmental feasibility of the process through comprehensive TEA and LCA. Notably, 
the proposed system achieves a production cost of approximately $2,300/ton and 
significantly lower global warming potential compared to conventional FDCA production. 
Overall, this work offers a promising direction for replacing fossil -derived plastics with 
biomass-based alternatives. However, several issues must be addressed before this 
manuscript is suitable for publication in Nature Communications. 
1. The authors should clarify the specific advantages of the AEM -HMFOR process over 
conventional chemical -catalytic oxidation routes in terms of selectivity, operational 
conditions, environmental impacts, and potential for coupling with renewable electricity.  
Response: We sincerely appreciate  your valuable suggestions. The oxidation of HMF to 
FDCA can be achieved through various routes, including thermocatalysis, electrocatalysis, 
and chemical oxidation. Among these, electrocatalytic oxidation , particularly using anion 
exchange membrane (AEM)-based systems, has emerged as a promising sustainable  route 
for FDCA synthesis coupled with green H2 generation, which features several advantages: 
1. High FDCA yield , space-time yield (STY) , and selectivity. In this work, the AEM-
based electrocatalytic HMF oxidation reaction (AEM-HMFOR) system can be operated 
and maintained for over 400 hours at a watt scale. Based on this, a hundred -watt scale 
AEM-based stack (164.8 W) was constructed, achieving high Faradaic efficiency

19. General lack of presentation of cathode side: In all Figures except Figure 1c, there is no 
input of water on the cathode side on all diagrams. Additionally, there is a lack of data in 
the method section on the cathode side of the experiment. It is advised to clarify H 2

Response: Thanks for your careful check and suggestive advice. We have revised the 
cathode-side scheme to prevent misinterpretation. The cathode is a dry process without an 
external water supply in our AEM -HMFOR system. As mentioned in the Supporting 
Information, for the AEM-HMFOR electrolyzer test, to avoid carbon loss (ACS Energy Lett. 
10, 571-578 (2025)), the anode flow rate is set to an optimized value, while the cathode is 
not fed with electrolyte. The cathode relies on the spontaneous transmembrane transport of 
solution. Water produced at the anode migrates across the AEM to the cathode, where i t is 
consumed in the production of H2 and OH− (Fig. R29). The OH− simultaneously migrates 
back through the AEM to the anode, maintaining a dynamic balance in the paired 
electrolysis process.  AEMs predominantly facilitate the transport of anions while 
demonstrating repulsion of cations. To date, research has been limited on the rate and extent 
of cation permeation and its impact on electrolyzer stability . Future work will analyze the 
concentration of leaked cations in the catholyte and investigate their potential impact on 
electrolyzer performance.  
In addition, following your suggestion, we have supplemented the experiments to measure 
the purity of H2 (99.99%, Fig. R30a) at the cathode as well as the FEH2 (~99.8%, Fig. R30b).
