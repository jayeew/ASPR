Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
General comments 
In this study, Bhattacharya et al. investigate the role of the Staphylococcus aureus collagen-binding adhesin (Cna) in soft
tissue skin infection. The authors used a mouse model of abscess formation to demonstrate that S. aureus Cna+ strains
cause skin infections with significantly lower bacterial loads and reduced inflammation. They employed the USA400 MW2
WT strain (Cna
⁺
) and its isogenic cna deletion mutant, as well as the USA300 wild-type strain (Cna-) and a complemented
version carrying a plasmid expressing cna. 
In this model, inflammation and metalloprotease activity were assessed using multiplex ELISA, and immune cell recruitment
was analyzed by flow cytometry in experimentally induced skin abscesses. 
The authors highlight a previously unrecognized role of S. aureus Cna in the pathophysiology of cutaneous infections. This
finding is important, as certain Cna- strains (particularly the USA300 clone) are widely disseminated. 
Majors comments 
From a general standpoint, approximately 60% of S. aureus strains are Cna-negative and, to my knowledge, are not
overrepresented among isolates from human skin abscesses. In fact, the results presented in the manuscript support this
statement. This raises the question of the role of PVL, which is well known to promote skin and soft tissue abscess
formation. Since mice are not sensitive to PVL, the relevance of the presented findings to human infection is difficult to
assess. Humanized mouse models could be highly valuable for evaluating the role of Cna in a PVL-sensitive context.
Alternatively, rabbit models, which are reported to be susceptible to PVL, may provide a more physiologically relevant
system. 
The model of experimental abscess used by the authors rely on quite high bacterial inoculum that can likely overloads the
phagocytic capacity of innate immune cells. In clinical settings, abscesses can originate from very low bacterial loads. It
would therefore be highly informative if the authors could assess whether Cna contributes to abscess formation under low
inoculum conditions. Given that phagocytosis appears to be more efficient for Cna- strains, it is plausible that such strains
could be more readily cleared when the host immune system is not overwhelmed. 
The study relies heavily on a mouse model, which presents inherent limitations when it comes to translating the findings to
human disease. Incorporating complementary approaches using human-derived materials (such as primary cells, tissue
explants, or organoid models) could substantially strengthen the physiological relevance of the conclusions 
The investigation of cna prevalence among clinical isolates is valuable, but the number of isolates tested is quite limited.
The authors could easily expand this analysis by mining publicly available genome databases to identify S. aureus STs or
clonal complexes lacking cna, and to assess the sequence variability of cna among cna+ strains. This in silico approach
would reinforce the genetic context of their findings

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a very interesting manuscript aiming to identify the role of collagen-binding adhesin (Cna) in establishing and
maintaining Staphylococcus aureus skin infection. The pathogenesis part of the manuscript is well performed and does
bring novel insights about the role of Cna in MRSA USA300 infection (which naturally lacks Cna) and other strains of S.
aureus. Studies related to the capacity of Cna to use C1q to infect phagocytes have also been done well. 
My main concerns
are related to the experiments aiming to dissect the role of Cna in eliciting inflammation. 
1) The authors performed a comprehensive inflammatory mediator array but hand-selected inflammatory mediators to justify
their study. A better rationale for their studies is needed to understand how Cna/C1q cross-talk influences the inflammatory
response. 
2) The multispectral imaging is not well done. It is tough to see specific staining. It lacks negative controls for each of these
antibodies. Some of the images are overly exposed. The descriptions of their findings are not well thought out. The authors
did a lot to merely provide the same information that a flow cytometer would provide. The critical part of these experiments is
the spatial distribution of specific cells, rather than just the overall cell percentage.

3) The linking between MMPs and LTB4 is also lacking. How MMPs cleave and generate peptides vs. LTB4 is not well
addressed, and how LTA4H is activated was not described. Both the peptides and LTB4 are proinflammatory mediators.
The attempts to dissect the differential role of these events are not well done. The authors performed an ELISA for LTA4H,
which is insufficient to determine activation. This portion of the manuscript is full of assumptions, without actual evidence. If
the authors want to move forward with these datasets, experiments using BLT1 antagonist/KO, plus specific LTA4H
inhibitors that specifically target LTB4 production vs the peptides, must be done. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Staphylococcus aureus is a major human pathogen that causes a range of invasive diseases including sepsis and
necrotizing pneumonia. Furthermore, S. aureus is responsible for soft skin and tissue infections (SSTIs), diseases which are
most common amongst the human population but are categorized as less severe because of antibiotic intervention. The
current understanding underlying the mechanism of SSTIs is linked predominantly to exotoxin production which include, to
name a few, alpha-hemolysin, phenol soluble modulin peptide toxins as well as the Agr quorum sensing system. Here,
Battacharya et al., delve deeper into this arena and investigate a relatively unstudied virulence factor, the collagen binding
adhesin (Cna). Notably, Cna has been described to play a role in other staphylococcal diseases by other players in the field
but this would be the first time Cna has been explored in the context of SSTIs. 
A major message of their work indicates that SSTI pathology is negatively correlated to the presence of Cna. By comparing
a Cna-expressing S. aureus strain (MW2 USA400), as the model organism, and an isogenic cna mutant (Δcna), the authors
find a striking phenotype in a mouse model of SSTI whereby Cna deficiency in S. aureus results in significantly greater
lesion sizes, more CFU at the site of infection and enhanced inflammatory responses. Using flow cytometry and beautiful
fluorescent imaging from lesions of infected mice, the authors show this is linked with increased neutrophil death. Then, they
convincingly show in rigorous detail that these effects are associated with the ability of Cna to interact with c1q, a major
component and main effector involved in the activation of the classical complement pathway. 
Overall, it is a very interesting topic and the authors have done a thorough job in extending previous reports on the function
of Cna, including inhibition of the complement activation cascade via binding to c1q and what the in vivo consequences are.
However, as the authors discuss, previously circulating clones that were Cna+ such as MW2 USA400 have been replaced
by CA-MRSA USA300, which do not express Cna. Thus, the observed mechanistic effects with Cna are only relevant in
specific situations and does not reflect a general mechanism that further promotes our understanding of how S. aureus
causes SSTIs in the staphylococcal field. 
Additionally, there are several major concerns with their data that make it difficult to interpret easily or even at times
contradict their claims. 
They are as follows: 
To assess the contribution of Cna, the authors complement the Δcna with a high copy number plasmid and replicate the
phenotype in the WT. This inclusion of these experiments validate the importance of Cna. Using such a system poses
questions, which could be avoided if the cna gene was introduced genomically. For instance, did the WT in these
experiments harbor an empty vector? If so, were bacteria grown in antibiotics? Did the authors plate the bacteria from mouse
skin onto agar plates harboring antibiotics to check that the plasmid was still maintained at 3 and 7 days? These questions
also apply to the use of LAC and the complemented strain. 
Could differences in phenotype be due to differences in the expression levels of Cna or fitness cost (e.g. growth curve data?)
