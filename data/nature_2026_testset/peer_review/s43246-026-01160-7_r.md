Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The work aims to distinguish between pop-ins on the load-penetration depth curves generated during nanoindentation due
to dislocation movement and those due to cracking. It was performed on layered muscovite mica. The results and
conclusions clearly show that based on the released energy and velocity of the pop-ins, plastic deformation and cracking
events can be clearly distinguished. Moreover, they depend on temperature. 
The manuscript demonstrates a very high level of understanding of the problems, a systematic approach to solving
problems, consideration of various aspects affecting possible results, rigorous justification of the methods and approaches,
statistical treatment of the data and their critical consideration, all written in a readable way. 
I consider it highly qualified work
worth publishing almost in the "as it is" state. There are only two minor questions/suggestions: 
1. Why was such a blunt (radius 2.3 μm) indenter used? It is closer to a spherical indenter than to Berkovich tip, which would
affect the analysis due to a different dependence of contact area with the depth. The use of such an indenter should be
justified. 
2. A clear definition of the aim of the work is missing. The paragraph at the end of the Introduction (rows 97-116) reminds a
slightly extended abstract...Is it really necessary is a regular abstract is present before the Introduction? Instead of it, a well-
defined aim of the work should be considered (and it would also slightly shorten the length of the paper). 
I recommend accepting the manuscript for publication as it is, or only with the above minor improvements. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
In this manuscript, Afful et al. investigated the nanoindentation-induced pop-ins in muscovite, introducing a mechanistic
framework to distinguish plasticity-related and fracture-related events through quantitative classification of pop-in widths.
The work attempted interpreting the nanoindentation data in layered materials via statistical analysis. However, overall the
paper lacks thorough experimental proofs to support the conclusion drawn from nanoindentation data analysis. Therefore, I
cannot recommend the paper for acceptance. Some more detailed comments: 
(1) While the data analysis is detailed and thorough, all the experiments conducted were only nanoindentation, and the
authors attempted to assign mechanisms without much experimental proof on microstructural changes. The gap between the
tests and the conclusion is large. 
(2) More specifically, for instance, the authors claimed that the ‘large’ pop-in indicates fracture. Then a way to verify this
would be to check if there is any crack, on the surface or in the material underneath indentation (via cross-section
characterizations), when no ‘large’ pop-in exists in the P-h curve. What would be more beneficial would be an extra set of
test showing fracture occurs when ‘large’ pop-in appears. 
(3) While the authors conducted many data analysis in estimating parameters like energy and velocity, eventually the
judging criterion is still simply based on the pop-in width, which then makes this paper no difference from any prior studies
also utilizing pop-in width. 5 nm also seems a rather arbitrary choice, with no solid physical basis or experimental proof,
although 3, 4, and 5 nm were considered as alternatives. 
(4) Moreover, this 5 nm threshold was taken for the whole temperature range applied in this study, but such threshold may
also change with temperature. This aspect was overlooked. 
(5) Many assumptions or approximations were made during each step of the data analysis, which often is also arbitrary and
may ultimately lead to misinterpretation of the results. For example, pop-ins were identified when tip velocity exceeds 7
times the background value and this led to only 90% of data captured. 
(6) “The first pop-in event is typically associated with non-fracture processes” and “does not contain contributions from prior
plastic deformation events.” These conclusions also lack experimental proof. The microstructure right after first pop-in can be
checked to confirm. Alternatively, at least, a simple nanoindentation test can be performed right before the typical first pop-in
load; if a fully elastic response (overlapped loading-unloading curves) was obtained, then it can prove there was indeed no
prior plastic deformation. 
(7) On page 20, “fracture-related pop-ins in muscovite were generally 340 associated with energies less than 7 pJ (7).” It
seems to be a typo and probably should be “more than 7 pJ”. 
(8) On page 25, “the radius of the Berkovich tip… falls in the range expected for spatial statistics-dominated pop-in
behavior…”: the ~2 μm tip radius used here is actually considered fairly small and dislocation nucleation is expected to be
dominating. 
(9) On page 26, “when crystal lattice contracts to its original dimensions and therefore some crack healing may occur.” Is
there any experimental proof (from literature, at least) suggests such crack healing behavior during cooling?

Reviewer#2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
In this manuscript, Afful et al. investigated the nanoindentation-induced pop-ins in 
muscovite, introducing a mechanistic framework to distinguish plasticity-related and 
fracture-related events through quantitative classification of pop-in widths. The work 
attempted interpreting the nanoindentation data in layered materials via statistical 
analysis. However, overall the paper lacks thorough experimental proof to support the 
conclusion drawn from nanoindentation data analysis. Therefore, I cannot recommend 
the paper for acceptance. Some more detailed comments: 
(1) While the data analysis is detailed and thorough, all the experiments conducted 
were only nanoindentation, and the authors attempted to assign mechanisms without 
much experimental proof on microstructural changes. The gap between the tests and 
the conclusion is large. 
Author response: We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful comment regarding the link 
between the experiments and the proposed deformation mechanisms. While

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

nanoindentation was the primary experimental technique in this study, the manuscript 
integrates several complementary datasets and builds directly on prior microstructural 
investigations of muscovite.  
Specifically, our analysis is based on several hundred nanoindentation tests, 
temperature-dependent Raman data (Fig. 2 in the manuscript), and post-indentation 
SEM imaging (Fig. 10 and S6 in the manuscript). In addition, earlier studies on the 
same material system have employed scanning electron microscopy and scanning 
probe microscopy to observe deformation mechanisms such as kink band formation and 
fracture following nanoindentation (Refs. (1,3–5)). These studies provided 
microstructural evidence connecting specific deformation mechanisms to characteristic 
pop-in behaviors and suggested that pop-in width may reflect the underlying 
mechanism. 
In the present study, temperature-dependent Raman data and post-heat-exposure 
Raman data show that no structural changes occur in muscovite with temperature, while 
nanoindentation data show that the populations of pop-in events, reflective of underlying 
deformation mechanisms, have differing behavior at temperature. We have used 
extensive statistical analysis and cross-correlation of results. These intentional choices 
are part of the experimental design and are motivated by the extreme sensitivity of 
nanoindenter transducers and their ability to generate large, statistical data sets. Direct 
observation by, e.g., in situ SEM/nanoindentation lacks sufficient resolution for imaging 
nanometer-scale events and in situ TEM/nanoindentation is easily impacted by the 
sample preparation and electron beam exposure. Both imaging techniques are 
inherently low throughput which is detrimental to statistical analysis, and cross-
sectioning using focused ion beam fundamentally changes the boundary conditions for 
loading by relaxing the constraint on the basal planes. Our approach is therefore 
intentionally designed to leverage the sensitivity and throughput of nanoindentation 
performed in situ to extract statistically meaningful information on deformation 
mechanisms across a wide temperature range.  
 
(2) More specifically, for instance, the authors claimed that the ‘large’ pop-in indicates 
fracture. Then a way to verify this would be to check if there is any crack, on the surface 
or in the material underneath indentation (via cross-section characterizations), when no 
‘large’ pop-in exists in the P-h curve. What would be more beneficial would be an extra 
set of tests showing fracture occurs when ‘large’ pop-in appears. 
Author response: Direct correlation between large pop-in behavior and microstructural 
evidence of fracture has been established in prior work on muscovite by some of this 
manuscript’s authors and is cited as Ref. 6. Specifically, a nanoindentation study on 
muscovite reported that fracture was only observed following the observation of large 
pop-ins in load-displacement data, and the energies released during these events

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

typically exceeded ~7 pJ (see Ref. (6)). Similarly, another nanoindentation study on 
muscovite showed that  surface indentation marks with evidence of fracture were 
observed by scanning electron microscopy only when large pop-ins occurred in the 
corresponding load-displacement curves; in contrast, no evidence of fracture was 
detected when such pop-ins were absent (see Ref. (3)). Representative images from 
these studies are shown in Fig. R1 below, and these demonstrate that large pop-ins are 
associated with fracture.  
 
Fig. R1. a) Scanning electron microscopy image of an indented region when the 
indenter was loaded up to 500 mN in muscovite. The inset shows a magnified picture of 
the deformation inside the indented region with extensive delaminations and cracking 
that occurred during the large pop-in event. Image is reproduced from Ref. (3). b) 
Scanning probe microscopy image of an indent showing radial fracture originating at the 
contact point of an indent following a large pop-in. Image is reproduced from Ref. (6). 
While these prior studies did not define a quantitative threshold width for classifying 
pop-ins as “large” or “small,” their microscopic observations suggest that pop-in 
characteristics, particularly width, are dependent on the underlying deformation 
mechanisms. Other studies on muscovite have also made qualitative attributions of 
large pop-ins to fracture and small pop-ins to mechanisms such as dislocation glide or 
kink band formation (Refs. (5,7)).

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

Building on this established experimental foundation, the present work advances the 
field by providing a statistically grounded framework to distinguish pop-in populations 
based on width, energy, and velocity metrics derived from large datasets. Rather than 
relying on post-indentation imaging of individual events (after the load has been 
released and some crack healing may have occurred), we demonstrate that a pop-in 
width of approximately 5 nm reliably separates populations exhibiting energetic and 
kinetic signatures consistent with fracture from those associated with non-fracture 
deformation mechanisms. 
To clarify this connection and explicitly acknowledge the existing microscopic evidence, 
we have added the following text to the manuscript (page 25, lines 413-424): 
“The application of a statistically defined 5 nm threshold allows us to examine the 
effects of temperature on plasticity and fracture separately. We refer to pop-ins with 
widths less than 5 nm as small pop-ins and those larger than 5 nm as large pop-ins. 
This distinction is based on prior work, where fracture was only observed 
microscopically following the occurrence of a large pop-in during nanoindentation of 
muscovite (41,42), demonstrating that large pop-ins are characteristic signatures of 
fracture. In the absence of pop-ins in the load-displacement traces, muscovite 
deformation under the nanoindenter tip is linear elastic (42). These studies did not 
establish a quantitative width threshold for defining ‘large’ pop-ins, so here we rigorously 
address this remaining unknown. In the present study, we statistically identify ~5 nm as 
a reliable discriminator between pop-in populations based on correlated energetic and 
kinetic signatures of fracture. In keeping with the prior studies (1,41,42), large pop-ins 
reflect fracture events while small pop-ins correspond to dislocation glide and kink band 
formation.” 
 
(3) While the authors conducted many data analysis in estimating parameters like 
energy and velocity, eventually the judging criterion is still simply based on the pop-in 
width, which then makes this paper no difference from any prior studies also utilizing 
pop-in width. 5 nm also seems a rather arbitrary choice, with no solid physical basis or 
experimental proof, although 3, 4, and 5 nm were considered as alternatives. 
(4) Moreover, this 5 nm threshold was taken for the whole temperature range applied in 
this study, but such threshold may also change with temperature. This aspect was 
overlooked. 
Author response to (3) and (4): The reviewer has raised important points regarding the 
population-classification criterion and its temperature dependence. Although pop-in 
width is used as the criterion for differentiating populations, it is used in conjunction with 
two independent physical quantities: event velocity and released energy; these 
quantities are directly connected to the underlying deformation mechanisms (see Ref.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

(6,9)). Moreover, pop-in width offers greater reproducibility across nanoindentation 
instruments having different measurement time constants, and is commonly used in 
pop-in based fracture toughness analyses, where width is related to crack length.  
Our approach first identifies fracture events based on fundamental physical indicators 
(energy release and event velocity) consistent with Griffith-Irwin theory of crack 
formation and propagation. We then examined the joint distributions of pop-in widths, 
energies, and velocities and found that a threshold of 5 nm provides the clearest 
separation between a population exhibiting energies and velocities consistent with 
fracture and a population of slower and lower energy deformation events. Other 
thresholds, including 3 and 4 nm were evaluated but these thresholds resulted in 
greater overlap between the corresponding velocity distributions, thereby reducing the 
ability to statistically distinguish the two populations (see Fig. R2a-c). The 5 nm 
threshold therefore represents a statistically motivated cut-off length that maximizes the 
ability to distinguish between pop-in populations rather than an arbitrary selection. Even 
when velocity is used as the discriminator, where events with velocities above 1000 
nm/s are regarded as large pop-ins (consistent with previous findings on crack velocity 
in muscovite), average fracture toughness estimates were more comparable with that of 
the 5 nm threshold than the other thresholds (Fig. R2d-f). In addition, the room 
temperature fracture toughness estimates obtained from using the velocity discriminator 
were comparable with that obtained from our post-heating and post-indentation SEM 
crack measurements. However, as explained in the manuscript, we employed pop-in 
width instead of velocity as a discriminator to allow for easy comparisons across 
nanoindenters having different measurement time constants; moreover, prior studies 
have qualitatively distinguished pop-in mechanisms by pop-in widths.  We have 
included Fig. R2 in the Supplementary Information as Fig. S7 and added a sentence to 
the main text on page 23, Ln 394-396 to direct readers to the information: 
“Further comparisons of the effectiveness of the 5 nm thresholds with other thresholds 
are shown in Fig. S7 in the Supplementary Information.” 
There is some nuance here that we did not describe in good enough detail in the

Author response: We agree that transparency in these choices is essential for 
reproducibility and interpretation. We believe we have identified the assumptions and 
approximations necessary for a reader to evaluate or reproduce our analysis, but we 
are willing to clarify any that the reviewers find insufficient. 
Pop-in events were identified using a velocity-based criterion in which the instantaneous 
tip velocity exceeds seven times the background velocity, defined as the mean velocity 
of the load-displacement dataset. The 7x threshold was not chosen arbitrarily; rather, it 
was determined through systematic sensitivity analysis comparing automated pop-in 
detection against visual identification across a range of velocity multipliers. 
Specifically, lower multipliers resulted in a substantial number of false-positive events, 
where pop-ins were identified in the absence of any visually discernible displacement 
burst. In the limiting case of using the mean velocity alone, pop-in counts were grossly 
overestimated (Fig. R3, with values exceeding 100% of visually identifiable pop-ins). 
Conversely, higher multipliers increasingly excluded visually identifiable pop-ins, leading 
to false negatives. A multiplier of 7x captured more than 90% of visually identifiable pop-
ins while minimizing false positives. Importantly, the missed events were randomly 
distributed across the load range and therefore they do not bias the population-level 
analyses. Representative data from the sensitivity analysis at 25 oC and 300 oC are 
shown in Fig. R3, and the figure has now been included in the Supplementary 
Information (Fig. S2). Also, a few sentences have been added to the main manuscript 
on page 16, Ln. 283-285:

(8) On page 25, “the radius of the Berkovich tip… falls in the range expected for spatial 
statistics-dominated pop-in behavior…”: the ~2 μm tip radius used here is actually 
considered fairly small and dislocation nucleation is expected to be dominating.  
Author response: We thank the reviewer for this comment regarding the role of tip 
radius in governing deformation behavior. The apex radius of lightly used Berkovich tips 
are typically several hundreds of nanometers and such tips sample material volumes 
that may be defect-free, favoring homogeneous dislocation nucleation. However, prior 
studies have shown that for tip radii exceeding 1 µm (like the tip used in our study), the 
probability of sampling pre-existing defects in the stressed volume beneath the indenter 
increases (see Ref. (10–14)). In such cases, the defects sampled within the deformation 
volume result in a range of incipient plasticity mechanisms which compete with 
homogeneous dislocation nucleation and thereby influence the observed temperature 
dependence of pop-ins. As a result, in this regime, pop-in behavior reflects a 
combination of defect-mediated and nucleation-controlled processes which then mask 
the true temperature dependence of distinct plastic deformation mechanisms (Ref. 
(13)). 
 
(9) On page 26, “when crystal lattice contracts to its original dimensions and therefore 
some crack healing may occur.” Is there any experimental proof (from literature, at 
least) suggesting such crack healing behavior during cooling? 
Author response: We thank the reviewer for highlighting this point. Experimental studies 
in the literature indicate that cracks in mica can partially “heal” under certain loading 
conditions. For example, cracks created by wedge loading in muscovite have been 
shown to close upon unloading, with a measurable hysteresis between the initial loading 
through virgin material and subsequent loading through the partially healed crack. The 
presence of adsorbed water and other environmental species at the crack surfaces 
contribute to the extent of crack healing (9). Such partial crack closure complicates ex 
situ measurements of crack length, as slight closure can lead to an underestimation of 
the true crack length and therefore an overestimation of fracture toughness. Because 
crack healing is affected by the presence of water and environmental species (9), these 
effects are expected to become more pronounced at temperature and with subsequent 
cooling. All these challenges also motivated the use of fracture-induced nanoindentation 
pop-ins as a robust metric for assessing fracture behavior and toughness in muscovite 
across temperatures.

We have included a few sentences in the manuscript to clarify the prior experimental 
evidence of crack healing in muscovite on page 28, Ln 482-488: 
“Prior studies in the literature have shown that cracks in mica can partially “heal” under

water and other environmental species at the crack surfaces, which in turn affect the
