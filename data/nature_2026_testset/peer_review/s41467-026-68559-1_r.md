Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
In the study by Chikina et al., the authors investigated the spatiotemporal dynamics of 
cellular energetic charge—defined by the ATP/ADP ratio—in immune cells, particularly T 
cells. They demonstrated how anatomical location, environmental nutrient availability, and 
circadian rhythm influence the metabolic state and effector function of these cells. The 
primary tool employed was SPICE-Met, a technology developed to measure metabolic and 
energetic states in distinct immune cell populations. This approach utilizes transgenic 
“SPICY” mice that express a fluorescent reporter for ATP/ADP ratios, enabling real-time 
assessment of cellular energy status, particularly when used alongside metabolic inhibitors 
to infer pathway contributions. 
The authors observed that effector T cells modulate their energetic state in response to 
environmental cues. For instance, T cells isolated from blood showed higher ATP/ADP 
ratios compared to those in lymph nodes, correlating with increased glucose availability. 
This metabolic shift was accompanied by functional changes, such as enhanced IFN-γ 
production. 
While the study provides spatial and temporal resolution and introduces an elegant 
technological tool, the biological insights into T cell metabolism and function largely 
reiterate well-established concepts. The finding that peripheral effector T cells are more 
glycolysis-dependent, while lymph node-resident or memory-like T cells rely more on 
oxidative phosphorylation, is consistent with a decade of prior literature. 
Authors: We respectfully disagree with the comment. Our study does not focus on the known 
metabolic pathways used by immune cells but rather on oscillation in energetic charge in 
T cells in time and space and their impact on T cell functions. Specifically, we believe that the main novelties of our
study are: 
- The differences in steady-state ATP:ADP ratio among various immune cell types 
- The dynamic changes in T cell energetic charge during T cell trafficking 
- The role of glucose availability in boosting T cell energetic charge in the blood 
- The circadian oscillation of energetic charge in immune cells 
- The causal role for fluctuation in energy status in shaping several T cell functions 
We have modified the discussion to better highlight the novelty and focus of the paper. 
(Discussion section p.12)
 
I appreciate the highlighting and clarification of the findings as explained by the authors. 
This paper has done a beautiful job of clearly demonstrating the metabolic changes experienced by migrating cells. Yes, this
has been hypothesized for a while now, but the data presented in this paper show direct, single cell readout of the energetic
“status” in response to the microenvironment.
 
Reviewer3: Many of the figures remain descriptive and repeatedly confirm these known paradigms 
without offering substantial mechanistic depth or novel insights. Moreover, a critical 
limitation of the study is the lack of in vivo manipulation of glucose availability. Altering 
systemic glucose levels through dietary interventions, insulin administration, or genetic 
approaches could have directly tested the causal relationship between glucose availability 
and energetic charge across different environments. Unfortunately, such mechanistic 
experiments are absent throughout the manuscript. Lastly, the dataset addressing 
circadian regulation appears loosely integrated with the rest of the study. This section 
would benefit from either a more thorough analysis and mechanistic exploration or should 
be removed to maintain a clearer focus. In summary, while the SPICE-Met platform is 
technically innovative and may prove useful in future studies, this work does not provide 
sufficient biological advances in our understanding of T cell metabolism.

Point-by-point  Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author):  The study by Chikina and colleagues utilized their previously published method, SPICE-Met, to examine the spatiotemporal regulation of T cell energy production and function. Immune cells that were more glycolytic showed an increased energetic charge, based on the increased ratio of ATP to ADP. Both CD4 and CD8 effector/memory T cells had higher ATP:ADP ratios when localized in the blood compared to the lymph nodes (LN). The reduction in ATP in T cells in the LN correlated with reduced glucose availability in the microenvironment. Further, the study demonstrated that T cell energetic resources and metabolism change with the light-dark cycle. Elevated ATP:ADP in T cells was associated with an increased ability to produce perforin and cytokine production. Overall, the findings of this study show that intracellular energetic charge is modulated by nutrient available in the microenvironment and diurnal rhythms, which impact T cell function. However, there are several concerns that need to be addressed.   1. In Fig. 4, the authors suggest that cells upregulate Glut1 expression to compensate for low levels of glucose in the LN microenvironment, which correlates with a reduced ATP:ADP ratio. Do the Glut1+ cells have increased intracellular glucose compared to Glut1– cells in the LN, and if so, why do the cells still maintain a low ATP:ADP ratio?  Also, increased Glut1 expression can be correlated with increased mTORC1 signaling, a central driver of cellular metabolism – what was the status of mTORC1 signaling in Glut1+ and Glut1– cells?    These are interesting suggestions. We have now analyzed intracellular glucose concentration and mTORC1 activity in GLUT1+ versus GLUT1- effector T cells in the lymph node. Using iGluco transduced T cells. We found that GLUT1+ T cells indeed showed increased intracellular glucose, yet GLUT1+ T cells displayed decrease mTORC1 activity. These results are consistent with upregulation of GLUT1 as results of low energetic status and low mTORC1 activity, leading to increase glucose flux as a compensatory mechanism.   -> Data showing the level of intracellular glucose and mTORC1 activity are shown in Figure S2
