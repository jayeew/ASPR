Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper posits an innovative cascade-ensemble deep learning framework as a solution for diagnosing cervical
spondylosis, presenting a novel methodology aimed at addressing the diagnostic challenges associated with this condition.
The authors' strategy, involving a cascading architecture for vertebral body detection followed by a degenerative diagnosis
and further bolstered by an ensemble of multiple degenerative indicators within a multi-task learning framework, stands out
as a sophisticated approach with the potential to navigate the complexities of cervical spondylosis diagnosis effectively.
Nevertheless, several critical concerns merit attention: 
Upon review of the manuscript, a detailed description or presentation of the proposed cascade-ensemble deep learning
framework is conspicuously absent. The lack of mathematical formulation and detailed architecture for the proposed
ensemble deep learning algorithms significantly impedes understanding and evaluation of the purported methodology. 
With the existence of over 100 ensemble deep learning (EDL) algorithms documented in the literature, the authors' oversight
in engaging with relevant existing studies is a notable gap. A comparative analysis or discussion contextualizing their
proposed framework within this extensive body of work would significantly enhance the manuscript's contribution to the field.
The manuscript suggests a particular sensitivity of the model to certain indicators, such as the SVA value and
spondylolisthesis distance. However, there is a lack of discussion regarding the model's performance variability across
different conditions and its potential vulnerability to overfitting on these indicators. Addressing overfitting is crucial to ensure
the model's applicability and effectiveness in diverse real-world scenarios, where patient presentations can vary widely. 
While claims of the model's performance being comparable to that of senior radiologists and clinical doctors are made, there
is an evident deficiency in details regarding the clinical validation process. Specifically, the manuscript does not elucidate
how the deep learning model's diagnostic outcomes were compared with longitudinal patient outcomes or their integration
into existing clinical workflows. The true value of a diagnostic tool is not solely in its mimicry of expert opinions but also in its
demonstrable impact on patient management and outcomes. 
To address these concerns, it is strongly recommended that the authors elucidate the mathematical formulation of their
proposed framework and undertake a comparative analysis with recent ensemble deep learning algorithms. Additionally,
reference to comprehensive reviews such as "Ensemble deep learning: A review" could provide valuable insights and
context for refining and substantiating their contributions to the field.

Response 1. Thanks for the useful comments. We believe our method is a
multimodal frameworks since we take multimodal input to provide a complementary
reports for the doctors. And different indicators in our report are simultaneously
learned with multi-task manner. All the indicators are ensembled in the reports.
As the reviewer pointed out, we use two image modulates, including X-ray and
MR images, both of which examinations are universally regarded as one of the most
important imaging studies for cervical spondylosis, from the point view of worldwide
cervical spine research. For instance, a German team conducted an in vivo study
on the biochemical effectiveness of imaging technologies such as X-rays and MRIs in
human cervical degenerative disc diseases [29]. A Chinese team completed a long-
term follow-up study on cervical disc arthroplasty based on X-rays and magnetic
resonance imaging [28]. Another team conducted a retrospective cohort study on
the application of X-rays and MRIs in cervical spondylotic myelopathy [27].
We agree that in most previous works the multi-modal data are combined into
some common feature space to predict a definitive prediction or conclusion.How-
ever, in contrast to those works combining multi-modal features together,
the cervical spondylosis requires the collaboration of multiple modalities
to provide richer indicators for clinical diagnosis, due to the diversity
of clinical appearances. So, according to the particular requests from clinical
doctors, our multimodal fusion framework consists of several stages: 1) Stage one
(indicators detection), providing more available and accurately detected indicators
for doctors; 2) Stage two (degeneration classification), classifying the degeneration
of different indicators. And all the results will be provided for the clinical doctors
to generate operation plan.
Let us illustrate with a representative case of cervical spondylosis for instance: a

Although the prediction of these degenerative factors is based on the coordinates
of vertebral key points, we optimize the coordinates using the loss of each task,
in addition to optimizing the coordinates themselves. Because our further goal is
to detect the degenerative factors, which are crucial for making a diagnosis of a
cervical spondylosis. The vertebra coordinates are used as the intermediate results,

As the reviewer suggested, a typical type of multi-task learning could be "lo-
calization + classification", like Faster-RCNN [6] shown in R2 a. In the Faster
R-CNN, the image goes through the backbone convolutional network, obtaining the
intermediate feature map, then goes through two branches, one of which generates
coordinates for localization, and the other generates scores for classification. The
ground truth label for localization and classification serve as supervision, and the
loss gradients of the supervision is calculated as back propagation, which is used to
update the parameters of the backbone network. The training process on these two
tasks together improves the performance of the backbone network. In summary, we
agree to the reviewer that the core of "multi-task learning" is to "learn" in different
tasks.
Similar to multi-task learning in R2 a, our goal is also to learn a neural network
model in multiple degenerative factors diagnose tasks (see R2 b). In our framework,
the image goes through the backbone convolutional network, obtaining the coor-
dinates of cervical vertebral key points. Based on these coordinates, we compute
different degenerative indicators, such as Cobb angle, SVA value, spondylolisthesis
angle, etc. Then we use the ground truth values of these indicators as supervision,
and back propagate the gradients of loss to improve the performance of the backbone
network. The training process on every single degenerative indicator is considered a
task, and these different tasks can jointly improve the performance of the vertebral
key points prediction module.

Cervical spondylosis primarily arises from static compression of the cervical spinal
cord or nerve roots (such as cervical disc herniation, cervical spinal canal stenosis, or

a mixed type where both are present.

The model for processing X-ray images is divided into two stages. The first
detection stage detects the coordinates of cervical vertebral key points, while the
second prediction stage predicts multiple degenerative indicators. Our detection
module extracts the feature map from the input images and then generates the
heatmap on the highest resolution feature map, inspired by the HRNet[4, 2] and

several branches, where one branch focuses on high-resolution features, while others
progressively downsample the input to create low-resolution representations(see R6).

branch, there are low-resolution branches at different scales (1/2, 1/4 the origi-

low-resolution feature maps, using upsampling and convolution operations. After
obtaining the feature map through the above backbone network, we use deconvo-

final high resolution heatmaps. Then we use the Differentiable Spatial to Numeri-
cal Transform (DSNT)[1] to get the mapped pixel coordinates from the normalized
heatmap prediction, and finally group the keypoints of different instances. Finally
the predicted values of various degenerative indicators are calculated from the linear
layers based on the coordinates obtained by the DSNT mapping.

gion proposals generated by the RPN are then fed into the Region of Interest(RoI)

corresponding locations in the feature map generated by the backbone, converting
each proposed region into a fixed-size feature map, regardless of the proposal’s orig-

consisting of the bounding box and the classification scores.
