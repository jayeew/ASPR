Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this article, the authors introduce a novel self-healing molecular crystal, 2-methyl-4-nitroimidazole (MNI), which can rapidly
self-repair under mechanical stress. Research shows that this self-healing ability stems from the special layered structure
and hydrogen bond interactions within the crystal, as well as the local symmetry breaking that occurs at the crack tip. In
addition, the activity of the nonlinear second harmonic (SH) caused by mechanical disturbance surges six-fold, further
confirming the existence of local symmetry breaking in crystals that originally had a centrally symmetrical structure. This
work is certainly interesting from a scientific perspective. However, the originality and discussion of the results in this paper
remain deficient. I will give further consideration after the authors address the following issues. 
1.While the paper verified local symmetry breaking via second harmonic generation, it did not fully explain its mechanism—
specifically, how it occurs and drives the self-repair process. The article's main innovation lies in the self-healing of
centrosymmetric crystals, which the author attributes to the breaking and rearrangement of interlayer molecular interactions.
Is the driving force for this bond rearrangement consistent with the uneven charge distribution resulting from broken polar
bonds in non-centrosymmetric crystals? 
2.The paper mentions a six-fold enhancement of nonlinearity at the fracture. Since centrosymmetric crystals theoretically
lack SHG, how was this multiple derived? Moreover, since the second harmonic generation can be detected at the crack,
whether there is also a second harmonic generation signal at both ends of the crystal? 
3.Concerning MNI crystal's reported elastic modulus of 13.3±0.5 GPa: Is it anisotropic? If so, what are the values for other
crystal planes? How does this modulus compare to other self-healing molecular crystals, and is it linked to MNI's self-
healing capability? 
4.In the previous work (Nat. Commun. 2023, 14, 6589), the authors used SHG to verify the self-healing process of non-
central symmetric crystals, and the SHG intensity at the crystal fracture was significantly reduced. Does this conflict with the
conclusion of this paper? 
5.The text states that plastic regions prevent further crack propagation. How do these plastic regions relate to plastic
crystals? Given that elastic crystals can also exhibit self-healing (Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A., 2025, 122(7): e2417901122.),
what is the flexibility of MNI crystals? Does the macroscopic elastic/plastic behavior of crystals influence their self-healing
ability? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors demonstrate healing in 2-methyl-4-nitroimidazole (MNI) molecular crystal up on stress-induced failure. The
structural analyses of the crystal shows restoration of crystallinity post-healing and indicates the formation of plastic zone at
the crack tip. The images presented in the manuscript indicate a crack opening and a subsequent healing around a defect
point in under 4 ms, and the efficiency in mechanical strength on failure is limited to 40%. These results are interesting and
provide evidence for repeated cycling in molecular crystals (a topic that is of broad interest to the community). The authors,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

however, then claim that the fracture in the crystal followed by healing is attributed with the ``symmetry-breaking'' in crystals
observed in MNI. This latter reasoning/analyses is what we find to be tenuous and not well-supported based on the field's
understanding of reversible symmetry-breaking. We identify some comments and questions for the authors below, and hope
that our review contributes to the strengthening of their work. 
(1) Symmetry-breaking in crystalline systems is often referred to the phenomena when the unit cells belonging to higher-
symmetry group deform to a lower-symmetry group. This symmetry-lowering or symmetry-breaking mechanism generates
multiple, crystallographically equivalent variants. A characteristic feature of this symmetry-breaking phenomena is that each
atom maintains the same nearest neighbors before and after transformation, and the bonds neither broken nor newly created
in this process. Now, in the current work, the authors report a centrosymmetric unit cell in the ``pristine'' region of the
molecular crystal and a loss of inversion symmetry in the ``deformed'' region of the molecular crystal. This deformed region,
however, is the region in the vicinity (perhaps also within) the crack i.e., region where bonds are broken and two new
surfaces are created. It's not clear what is the material system whose symmetry is being characterized or measured? And
why do they call it symmetry-breaking and not a simple failure or separation of interfaces? 
(2) The authors correlate crystal symmetry with the healing process. But from what we see in their results it appears that the
weak van der waals attraction when the newly formed surfaces are close enough contributes to the cross-linking of the
polymer chains. Can the authors explain if and why the crystal symmetry is contributing to the self-healing behavior? How
does its interplay with other factors such as molecular attraction, layered structure of the crystals, and other environmental
factors play a role? Would other layered molecular crystals also be expected to exhibit similar behavior? 
(3) The authors argue the origin of plasticity on page 6 – "The presence of a combination of dispersive and hydrogen
bonding interaction regions in the crystal structure of MNI seem to provide a degree of plasticity which allows for elasto-
plastic deformation". In supplementary note 4, the underlying mechanism of plastic deformation is described in terms of
dislocation however, the formation and motion of dislocations is not described in the manuscript. 
(4) on page 10 it is also described that psd measurement differ due to different crystal symmetry of the interface layer of
pristine area as compared to deformed area—is it possible to determine the crystal symmetry of the fractured region? How is
the crystal or unit cell defined in this context? 
(5) While the experiments demonstrate nearly 100% recovery of crystallinity after self-healing, the recovery of mechanical
strength is limited to approximately 40%. The authors have discussed in Supplementary note 3 the trade-off between
crystallinity and mechanical strength for different healing mechanisms. However, a more detailed discussion of the factors
limiting mechanical recovery, despite full crystallinity restoration, would provide valuable insights for future work.
Additionally, the 40% efficiency is reported for the thickness of 100 μm; in reference to mechanical characterization provided
in Supplementary Fig. 12, is the efficiency expected to be affected by crystal thickness.

Comment 5: The text states that plastic regions prevent further crack propagation. How 
do these plastic regions relate to plastic crystals?  
Response: We thank the reviewer for this question. In case of MNI crystals shown in this 
work, the presence of dispersive zones (methyl groups) in structure lends softness to the 
crystal and gives rise to local plasticity at the crack -tip which prevents further crack 
propagation.  
In contrast, p lastic crystals (PCs) consist of molecules (generally globular or cylindrical 
shaped) having orientational freedom at their lattice positions , while maintaining long 
range order in their plastic  crystalline phase. The characteristic of a plastic crystal is a 
solid-solid phase transition before the melting point, observable in DSC thermograms. 
They also deform plastically and can be mold into different shapes like thin films, stretched 
rods, compressed rods, etc. There are other class of plastically bendable crystals which 
can be bent on selected faces, but they cannot be usually casted into thin films or 
stretched/compressed rods. In both these cases, the deformation is observed at the 
macroscopic as well as local level. 
In case of MNI, we do not observe any macroscopic plasticity, consistent with its DSC 
data (see figure below) . Nonetheless, any crystal can experience elastic or plastic 
deformation locally under mechanical stress. The plastic deformation discussed here  
refers to such kind of local deformation (plastic zones) at the crack tip which arrests crack 
propagation (see Supplementary Note 4), and not the bulk or macroscopic plasticity.

We thank the reviewer for bringing this interesting recent report to our attention.  In our 
work, we have not observed any noticeable or large mechanical flexibility in MNI crystals. 
The MNI crystals are inherently brittle, and upon application of external stress they 
undergo crack-formation prompting symmetry breaking,  which leads to self -healing. In 
this sense, ours is a unique example and does not compare directly with other reported 
cases. 
The report mentioned (Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A., 2025, 122(7): e2417901122) shows 
the presence of elastic flexibility as well as self-healing behaviour in non-centrosymmetric 
BEzH crystals (space group P21) where the two phenomena are attributed to anisotropic 
intermolecular interactions along different crystallographic axes. It is important to note 
that elastic/plastic bending are macroscopic phenomen a owing to the molecular 
movements/ slippage of molecular layers under mechanical stress. However, we believe 
that diffusionless self -healing in our MNI system is a symmetry -driven process where 
dangling bonds generated at cracked surfaces and the broken local symmetry among the 
layers closer to the surface promote  rejoining to return to previously symmetric state, 
resulting in self-healing. Since the crack in this particular case follows an elliptical shape, 
there might be elastic strain in the slightly curved surface, prompting the layers to spring 
back to normal, helping the self -healing. Hence, intermolecular interactions and elastic 
energy may play a role in both mechanical flexibility as well as self -healing. However, to 
conclude these suggestions, experimental and theoretical evidence through further 
studies will be required. We feel that the self -healing in organic crystals is a very new 
topic and further exploration to draw connections among various behaviours of crystals 
from the structure-property angle would be exciting.

Response: We thank the referee  for giving us an opportunity to  explain the context in 
which we used the term “symmetry breaking”. Symmetry breaking generally refers to the 
disruption of an existing symmetry in a system . The comments made by the referee are 
particularly true in the context of ferroelectric solid-to-solid phase transitions, wherein “the 
phenomenon in which some symmetry elements of the paraelectric  (high-temperature, 
high symmetry) phase are absent in the ferroelectric  (low-temperature, low-symmetry) 
phase is known as symmetry breaking .” (ref 5 of revised manuscript). In such cases, as 
both the solid phases are highly crystalline  (in different so lid phases) , each atom 
maintains the same nearest neighbours  through multiple, crystallographically equivalent 
variants, which is either refer red to as symmetry breaking (for e.g., going from space 
group P21/c to P21) or symmetry lowering (for e.g., going space group P212121 to P21). In 
the latter case, both the initial and final space groups are non-centrosymmetric. 
In the present work, we refer to a symmetry breaking phenomenon by external 
mechanical stress, which causes the locally deformed areas to go from an initially 
centrosymmetric state (high symmetry – no SHG signal) to a new non -centrosymmetric 
state (lower symmetry – presence of SH G signal). In our case, the deformed regions, 
which emit SHG signal, are not same as what is observed in systems where solid -solid 
phase transitions occur. The breakage of non-covalent bonds involved in our case refers 
to the scenar io at the “crack interface”, where the layers separate leading to fracture . 
Since here, the material layers are separated, the interlayer bonds have to be broken. 
However, the subsurface layers (those close to the  fracture surface on either side)  will 
have “broken symmetry ” with some distortions in the noncovalent interactions as 
compared to the pristine or bulk layers (see Supplementary Figure 1). We have the proof 
for symmetry breaking (SHG signal) at the crack junction, but proving the structure in an
