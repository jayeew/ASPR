Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Dear Authors, 
I have reviewed your work with interest, as it presents potentially important results in the field of water-soluble scintillating
materials for X-ray imaging. 
While the topic is relevant and the application promising, the study is currently built on several unsubstantiated theoretical
assumptions regarding the luminescence mechanism. These fundamental concerns must be thoroughly addressed before
the manuscript can be considered for publication. The major points requiring revision are as follows: 
Major Concerns: 
1. Proposed Luminescence Mechanism:The mechanism illustrated in Figure 1a is highly unconventional for lanthanide ions.
With the exception of Eu³
⁺
 and Yb³
⁺
, most lanthanides do not possess accessible charge transfer transitions in the UV
region. The more common and accepted pathway involves non-radiative energy transfer from the host matrix to the
lanthanide ion, often mediated by defects or excitons. The authors are strongly encouraged to consult the foundational
theoretical work on lanthanide luminescence by renowned experts in the field (e.g., O.L. Malta, P. Dorenbos, A. Meijerink,
Xiaogang Liu, P. Tanner, K. Binnemans) to revise the proposed model. 
2. Claim of Cl-Er / Er-Cl Emissions: The assignment of emissions to "Cl-Er" or "Er-Cl" transitions is highly improbable in the
cited spectral regions. To substantiate such a claim, it is essential to provide theoretical support. For instance, the authors
should calculate the expected energy levels for these transitions using established models, such as the Dorenbos model, to
verify if they align with the observed emissions. 
3. Rationale for Lanthanide Selection: The justification for selecting Yb³
⁺
 and Er³
⁺
—based on their "relatively high-energy
confined 4f electrons"—is conceptually unclear. This property is common to nearly all trivalent lanthanides, which would
imply that almost any lanthanide could be used. This is likely not the case, and the specific reason for choosing these ions,
presumably related to their energy level matching with the host's excitonic emission, needs to be clarified and properly
justified. 
4. Alternative Mechanism: A more plausible explanation for the observed enhancement is that the incorporation of trivalent
lanthanides creates charge-compensating defects, which in turn intensify the intrinsic excitonic emissions of the perovskite
host. A key experiment to test this hypothesis would be to dope the material with a closed-shell lanthanide ion such as La³
⁺
or Lu³
⁺
. If a similar enhancement in scintillation is observed, it would indicate that the mere presence of a trivalent ion (and
its associated defects) is sufficient, independent of its specific energy levels, provided there is no resonant energy transfer
from the exciton to the lanthanide. 
5. Lanthanide Concentration and Incorporation: The manuscript reports lanthanide concentrations in the perovskites. It is
critical to clarify whether these values represent the nominal amounts added during synthesis or the actual incorporated
concentrations quantified by an analytical technique such as Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) spectroscopy. Determining
the true incorporation efficiency is vital for correlating structure with properties. 
6. Solution vs. Colloidal Dispersion: The authors state that dissolving the material in DMSO results in a "clear solution." It is
essential to confirm that this is a true molecular solution and not a colloidal dispersion of nanoparticles. A simple Tyndall
effect experiment is recommended to rule out the formation of nanoscale colloids, which is a common occurrence for
perovskites in solvents and can be mistaken for a genuine solution. 
Recommendation: 
In light of these significant conceptual and experimental concerns, my recommendation is that the manuscript requires major
revisions. A successful revision would necessitate a thorough re-evaluation of the proposed luminescence mechanism,
additional control experiments (e.g., doping with La³
⁺
/Lu³
⁺
), and stronger analytical evidence for the claims made. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
In the article by Li et al., the luminescent properties of a new hybrid perovskite with the general formula (BDPA)2MCl4 (M2+
= Cd2+, Zn2+) doped with Er3+ and Yb3+ were presented. The authors report a very high quantum efficiency (QE) of the
obtained samples – 66% in the as-prepared state and 90% when dissolved in DMF or DMSO. However, XRD diagrams
showed that almost all the prepared samples are not single-phase! Many additional diffraction lines appear for the samples
at temperatures around 16°, 18°, 19°, 20°, and 26°. Taking into account the still high concentrations of dopants and their
mismatch of charges and ionic radii, there is a high probability that the dopants may also be located in the foreign phase.
Additional diffraction lines have not been assigned. The study of non-single phase materials is always controversial and

may be acceptable for known materials and application studies. However, if we are presenting the physicochemical
properties of a new material, we must ensure that the samples are phase-pure. For this reason, I suggest rejecting the paper;
it should not be published in Nature's Communications Materials. 
However, if the authors obtain phase-pure samples and confirm the obtained properties, they may have a chance of
publishing these results in a reputable scientific journal. Before resubmitting the manuscript for review, the following

- a large part of the introduction is off-topic, the introduction should be rewritten 
- Fig. 1b showing SEM-EDS shows that the sample is very inhomogeneous 
- Raman spectroscopy would allow the analysis of structural defects 
- I don't understand the rationale behind introducing such high Er and Yb dopant values when there's no luminescence from

luminescent properties of these ions. It can be assumed that using other lanthanides in the same way will produce a similar

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript entitled Unlocking Ultra-Stable Blue Emission from Ytterbium- and Erbium-Doped Metal Halides needs a
major revision to clarify the following points: 
1. The authors report the formation of new Er–Cl and Er–C bonds, supported by theoretical calculations. Can these bonding
interactions be experimentally validated using spectroscopic techniques such as FTIR or Raman spectroscopy? 
2. The authors claim that hole migration from the donor material to the rare-earth ions is effectively restricted, leading
electrons in the high-energy levels of the rare-earth ions to recombine with the valence band of the donor material rather
than relax to lower-energy rare-earth states. Given that this process is unlikely to be fully efficient, could the authors estimate
the proportion of electrons that recombine with the donor’s valence band versus those that relax within the rare-earth ions? 
3. The manuscript suggests that the closely packed BDPA
⁺
 cations form a protective shell, preventing water intrusion
around the metal halide polyhedron. Could the authors elaborate on the mechanism by which this protective layer is formed
and sustained? 
4. The authors discuss two possible electronic transition pathways in the solid samples: Cl → Yb/Er and Cl → C. While the
Cl → Yb/Er transition is addressed for samples mixed in DMSO, the fate of the Cl → C transition under these conditions
remains unclear. Could the authors clarify what happens to this transition in the DMSO-mixed samples?
