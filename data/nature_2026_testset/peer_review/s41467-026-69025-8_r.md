Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper presents two significant advancements in mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics: 
1. Optimization of narrow-window data-independent acquisition (nDIA) using two different supercharging solvents, with the
goal of maximizing protein coverage. 
2. Development of a solubility profiling method for protein target elucidation, demonstrated across multiple model cell lines. 
While both contributions are impressive and advance the field, they should be evaluated somewhat independently. 
Concerning nDIA optimization, previously reported gains in MS sensitivity through the use of DMSO as a co-solvent have
been extended to the nDIA method on the new Orbitrap Astral instrument. The study demonstrates a significant increase in
the number of identified proteins and peptides and determines the optimal co-solvent concentration. However, the validity of
these results warrants closer scrutiny when the specific details of the nDIA acquisition method are considered. 
Notably, unlike the original implementation of nDIA described in reference 28, the current method uses a highly restricted
m/z range—from 495 to 745. This effectively excludes all ions outside this window, potentially introducing substantial bias in
downstream analyses, such as the data shown in Fig. 1E–I. This limitation should be explicitly discussed, and ideally, a
systematic evaluation should be conducted to justify the use of such a narrow mass range. 
Another point of concern is the protein load. The use of 1
 
μg of protein exceeds both vendor recommendations and common
practice in the field. Previous studies have shown that the number of identified proteins begins to plateau at around 200
 
ng,
suggesting that a 1
 
μg load—especially in combination with supercharging and elevated ion current—may lead to instrument
overloading and distortion of results. While this is partially mitigated by the use of an unusually narrow isolation window (1
Th), such a window would also inherently reduce the ion current gains from supercharging. A comprehensive evaluation of
optimal protein load, isolation window width, and m/z range would strengthen this aspect of the study. 
Regarding the second major contribution—the solubility profiling approach—it would be valuable to compare this method to
other established techniques for protein target deconvolution, such as Thermal Proteome Profiling, Limited Proteolysis, etc.
One would reasonably expect that proteins showing substantial changes in solubility would also be detected by these
alternative methods. Although a direct experimental comparison may fall outside the scope of this already ambitious and
impressive study, a fair comparison would enhance the manuscript. 
Therefore, I recommend a revision of this manuscript in light of the comments above.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author):
In the manuscript, the authors studied drug-induced protein abundance and solubility changes in 
three types of human cells (i.e., U2OS, HeLa, and HEK293A) treated with the proteasome 
inhibitor MG132, the SUMO-activating enzyme inhibitor ML-792, and both of them. First, they 
optimized narrow-window, data-independent acquisition (nDIA) MS with DMSO/m-NBA as a 
supercharging co-solvent to boost the signal intensities. Then they quantified the solubility (Pellet 
in the Triton X-100 buffer as the insoluble fraction) and abundance changes of proteins in three 
cell lines treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and/or the SUMO-activating enzyme 
inhibitor ML-792. Besides chromatin-associated proteins in the insoluble fraction, they also 
identified proteins annotated to other cellular components in the insoluble fraction. It is not 
surprising that some protein abundance and solubility changed in cells with the drug treatment. 
There is a major concern regarding the data quality (see below), and furthermore, the novelty is 
an issue. Overall, in my view, the work may not be suitable for being published in a high-profile 
journal as Nature Communications.
We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment and the opportunity to clarify key aspects of our 
study. We have implemented rigorous quality control throughout both experimental and analytical 
workflows, including multiple biological replicates (n = 3) and stringent false discovery rate (FDR) 
thresholds (FDR < 0.01 at both the protein and peptide levels). To empirically validate FDR control, 
we performed a two-species entrapment analysis, which confirmed the robustness of our data 
quality control. Detailed clarifications and additional supporting data addressing the reviewer’s 
concern regarding data quality are provided in the point-by-point responses below.
While it is expected that drug treatments such as MG132 and ML-792 induce protein abundance 
and localization changes, our study advances the field by systematically profiling protein solubility 
changes at proteome scale using an optimized, high-throughput nDIA-MS platform with 
supercharging co-solvents. The protein solubility dimension of protein regulation is challenging to 
access comprehensively and quantitatively with existing methods.
Our optimized nDIA-MS workflow with supercharging co-solvents achieved unprecedented depth 
and sensitivity, enabling identification of ~9,600 proteins from HeLa cell digest in 15 min. This 
accelerates proteome-wide screen of solubility shifts that occur independently of abundance 
changes.
The resulting solubility proteomics dataset includes 8,694 quantified proteins, with 294 showing 
significant solubility shifts induced by drug treatments across multiple cell lines. A substantial 
fraction are novel candidates potentially regulated by ubiquitination, proteasome inhibition, or 
SUMOylation pathways, expanding the current understanding of protein state dynamics beyond 
well-characterized targets. To further support the biological relevance of the solubility changes 
observed, we performed validation experiments using complementary techniques—western 
blotting and immunofluorescence microscopy (Figure 3K-M, 4K-M, 5J-L, and Supplementary 
Figure 2). The results highlighted alterations in chromatin binding, nucleocytoplasmic shuttling, 
and phase transitions.

In summary, our study introduces a robust, scalable approach for proteome-wide interrogation of 
protein solubility and demonstrates its utility for identifying drug targets and elucidating 
mechanisms of drug sensitivity and resistance, establishing relevance to applications beyond 
basic cell biology.
What are the false discovery rates (FDRs) at the peptide and protein levels in each dataset? How 
were they calculated and controlled? For proteomics studies, without well control of the data 
quality, the results should not be published.
We appreciate the important point and agree that rigorous FDR control is essential for ensuring 
reliable proteomic data.
In this study, all data were processed using DIA-NN (v1.8.1) ( Demichev et al., Nat Methods 
2019). DIA-NN estimates and controls FDR using a target-decoy competition (TDC) strategy, a 
standard approach in proteomics for estimating false discoveries ( Elias et al., Nat Methods, 
2007). Benchmarking results from Demichev et al. (2019) demonstrated that DIA-NN outperforms 
other DIA software tools — including OpenSWATH (Rost et al., Nat Biotechnol, 2014), Skyline 
(MacLean et al., Bioinformatics, 2010), and Spectronaut (Bruderer et al., Mol Cell Proteomics, 
2015) — in identification performance. Those findings have been further supported by recent 
large-scale proteomics studies (Guzman et al., Nat Biotechnol, 2024), which have adopted DIA-
NN as a preferred analysis tool. 
In our study, FDR calculation and control were implemented as follows:
1. Peptide-Level FDR: We used a Q.Value ≤ 0.01 cutoff, corresponding to an estimated ≤1% FDR 
at the precursor level.
2. Protein-Level FDR: We filtered protein groups using PG.Q.Value ≤ 0.01, also reflecting ≤1% 
FDR, based on the highest-confidence peptides supporting each protein group.
All thresholds were applied using the DIA-NN R package (https://github.com/vdemichev/diann-
rpackage) for reproducible and transparent data processing.
To directly address the reviewer’s concern, and validate FDR control empirically, we followed the 
reported strategy and implemented a two-species entrapment analysis ( Demichev et al., Nat 
Methods 2019 ), combining 20,663 human and 39,209 maize protein entries, with filtering to 
exclude shared peptides (treating leucine and isoleucine as equivalent). Since our samples were 
purely human, any peptide identifications assigned to maize represent false positives by definition. 
In the 1 µg HeLa digest sample, an average of 1.35% of proteins and 0.18% of peptides were 
assigned to maize (Figure R1A-D). In the solubility proteomics samples, an average of 1.05% of 
proteins and 0.16% of peptides were assigned to maize ( Figure R1E-H). These results are well 
within accepted ranges for empirical FDR in proteomics, confirming that the target-decoy–based 
q-values and empirical entrapment analysis are consistent.
Overall, these findings demonstrate that our workflow provides robust FDR control and high-
quality peptide/protein identifications suitable for downstream analysis. The DIA-NN–based

in external collaborations, consistently producing reliable and reproducible results.

From a mechanistic standpoint, MG132 inhibits the proteasome and thereby stabilizes proteins 
that would normally be degraded. Thus, its primary effect is an increase in protein abundance, 
not a decrease. Down-regulation in this context would typically arise from indirect or cell-specific 
secondary responses—such as changes in transcription, translation, or solubility—and is 
expected to be both less common and more variable across different cell types. In this light, the

biologically plausible. Additionally, U2OS, HeLa, and HEK293A are biologically distinct, with 
different tissues of origin, genetic backgrounds, and proteostasis networks, making cell-line–
specific responses plausible.
We acknowledge the reviewer’s concern about data quality and would like to emphasize that we 
applied stringent quality control measures throughout the DIA-MS workflow. As detailed in our 
response to the previous comment above, we analyzed all datasets using DIA-NN (v1.8.1) with a

“Taken together, these results suggest that DMSO and m-NBA enhance MS performance through 
distinct mechanisms, …” What are possible mechanisms?
We thank the reviewer for this helpful suggestion. In the manuscript, we mentioned that DMSO 
and m-NBA appear to enhance MS performance through distinct mechanisms based on their 
differential effects on signal intensity, charge state distribution, and proteome coverage.
DMSO has been previously reported to enhance electrospray ionization (ESI) efficiency by 
modifying droplet surface tension and solvent evaporation, which can improve ion desolvation 
and transmission into the mass spectrometer ( Hahne et al., Nat Methods 2013; Strzelecka et 
al., Int J Mass Spectrom. 2015; Sterling et al., J Am Soc Mass Spectrom 2011). DMSO may 
stabilize certain peptide conformations in solution and increase the efficiency of protonation, 
thereby enhancing signal intensity across a wide m/z range. These effects tend to improve both 
MS1 and MS2 sensitivity without substantially altering charge state distributions.
m-NBA (meta-nitrobenzyl alcohol) is a supercharging reagent known to shift the charge state 
distribution of analytes, especially peptides and proteins, toward higher charge (Sterling et al., J 
Am Soc Mass Spectrom 2009; Iavarone et al., J Am Chem Soc 2003; Going et al., Anal 
Chem 2015). This supercharging effect is believed to occur by decreasing surface tension and

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

the energy barrier for proton transfer during droplet fission in the ESI process. The resulting 
increase in multiply charged ions can enhance MS/MS fragmentation efficiency and detection 
sensitivity, particularly in DIA workflows.
As suggested, we have added relevant discussion of these mechanisms in the revised manuscript 
(line 517-523).

Was the same LC solvents solvent program and column used for both proteins and peptides?
We appreciate the question and the opportunity to clarify this important methodological detail.
We employed a bottom-up proteomics approach, which is one of the most widely used strategies 
for large-scale proteome analysis. In this approach, proteins are enzymatically digested into 
peptides prior to LC-MS/MS analysis. Therefore, only peptides are directly analyzed by mass 
spectrometry, and protein-level information is subsequently inferred computationally from these 
peptide data. To ensure consistency and reproducibility, all LC–MS/MS analyses were performed 
using the same chromatographic setup: a reversed-phase C18 column (CoAnn Technologies, Cat. 
No. HEB07502001718I, 75 μm × 20 cm) and identical mobile phases composed of water with 0.1% 
formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase B), with or 
without supercharging cosolvent as specified. The solvent gradient and flow rate were held 
constant across all runs. Protein identification and quantification were achieved through peptide-
to-protein inference using DIA-NN software.

Regarding the second major contribution—the solubility profiling approach—it would be valuable 
to compare this method to other established techniques for protein target deconvolution, such as 
Thermal Proteome Profiling, Limited Proteolysis, etc. One would reasonably expect that proteins 
showing substantial changes in solubility would also be detected by these alternative methods. 
Although a direct experimental comparison may fall outside the scope of this already ambitious 
and impressive study, a fair comparison would enhance the manuscript. 
We appreciate the helpful suggestion. In response, we now performed a simplified TPP 
experiment, In the experiment, cells treated with either vehicle, MG132 or ML-792 were exposed 
to a temperature gradient from 37°C to 67°C (in 5°C increments), and the resulting soluble protein 
fractions were analyzed by mass spectrometry.
We processed the data using two complementary workflows: 
1) Full TPP analysis: We fitted melting curves and calculated the melting temperature (Tm) for 
each protein under different treatment conditions. Proteins showing a ΔTm > 1°C or < –1°C and 
p < 0.05 (drug-treated vs. vehicle-treated) were defined as significant hits. Using this method, we 
identified 20 and 102 protein hits in the MG132- and ML-792-treated groups, respectively ( new 
Supplementary Table 6).
2) Two-temperature TPP (2T-TPP) analysis: We compared the protein abundance at 57°C versus 
37°C and calculated the ratio between drug- and vehicle-treated samples following a previously 
described approach ( Yanling Zhang, et al., Nat Commun 2024 ). Proteins with an abundance 
ratio > 2 or < 0.5 and p < 0.05 were considered differential hits. Using this method, we identified 
1,012 and 831 protein hits for MG132 and ML-792, respectively (new Supplementary Table 6).
We then performed an overlap analysis among the hits identified by the two TPP approaches and 
our solubility profiling dataset. As shown in Figure R6, there were a total of 40 and 23 protein hits 
shared by solubility profiling and TPP analysis when the cells were subjected to treatment with 
MG132 or ML-792, respectively. Functional clustering of these shared proteins revealed that the 
overlapping hits in the MG132 group were enriched for mitosis-related processes, whereas in the 
ML-792 group the overlaps mapped to kinases and proteases.
The partial overlap highlights that solubility profiling and TPP interrogate different-yet-
complementary biophysical aspects of the proteome: thermal stability changes (as measured by 
TPP) and changes in protein solubility/aggregation or partitioning (as measured by our method). 
Relevant discussion was incorporated into the revised manuscript (line 537-546).
