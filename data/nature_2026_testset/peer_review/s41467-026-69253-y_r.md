Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an innovative and well-structured study on the development of an enzyme-mimicking
functionalized single-atom catalyst for the efficient polymerization and dehalogenation and detoxification of halogenated
organic compounds. The findings highlight that axial nitrogen induces the electronic structure and spin states of the Fe sites
and induces short-range ETP processes for the dehalogenation-polymerisation reaction of halogenated phenols in the
semiconducting nature of the substrate. A comprehensive systematic integration of in situ characterization, isotope
experiments and theoretical simulation calculations systematically reveals the key intermediates of the polymerization
reaction as well as a continuous dehalogenation-hydroxylation-coupling pathway. The superior performance and stability of
the catalytic process was confirmed by comprehensive characterization and systematic evaluation in both model and real
aqueous systems. Interestingly, this study confirms for the first time that the value-added processing and economic value of
oligomers in aqueous chemical polymerization processes does not remain only a discussion at the level of advanced
catalyst synthesis and mechanism revelation. This study provides valuable insights to advance the understanding of cost-
effective polymerization oxidation processes and selective polymerization mechanisms. Therefore, I suggest that after
resolving the following minor issues, the manuscript be published in Nature Communications. 
1. Introduction. Line 103: "TCP removal > 80% within 60 days", but in line 554-555 it is described as "~100% TCP removal,
above 80% TOC removal and 90% dechlorination over 60 days". There is a contradiction in terms. Please make revisions
and double-check such issues in full manuscript. 
2. The submitted manuscript contains two errors regarding the confusion of catalyst signatures. The first is in Line 163-165:
"The comparable coordination environments between Fe-CN and FePc confirmed the planar Fe-N4 configuration in Fe-
CN". It needs to be modified to "the planar Fe-N4 configuration in Fe-NCN". The second is in Line 305: "As expected, the
open loop potential (OCP) and timing currents of Fe-CN". 
3. A variety of quenching agents were used to verify the active species of the SACs/PMS system, but the quenching effects
of reagents such as OA, KSCN and K2CrO7 on the catalytic system were not discussed (in Fig. S19). It would enhance the
mechanistic exploration if their quenching effect during the reaction could be complemented. 
4. PMS utilization efficiency is an important indicator for evaluating catalyst performance. The authors are encouraged to
provide a comparison of the PMS utilization efficiency with other advanced nano-atomic scale catalysts to highlight the
superiority of this catalyst. 
5. The authors can complement the toxicity evaluation of Polychlorinated biphenyls compounds produced in CNT/PMS
systems by emphasizing the need for dehalogenation to reduce polymer toxicity (Line 346-349). 
6. Some key statements in the manuscript would benefit from supporting references to enhance their validity. Such as
experiments related to ferulic acid and DMPO (Line 451-455) and "typical mineralization-based AOP catalysts (i.e. zeolite
azolium salt framework-based iron monoatom and commercial nano zero-valent iron)" (Line 582-583). 
7. Since the P-AOP system only oxidatively transfers organic pollutants to the catalyst surface, the term "degradation" clearly
does not apply to this catalytic system (Line 522-523). It is suggested that it be modified to "removal", as it is elsewhere in
the manuscript. 
8. Please clarify how and under what conditions continuous flow reactors operate. For packed-bed reactors, why are river
rocks chosen as fillers? Do these fillers promote PMS activation?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study developed an axial-nitrogen-coordinated single-atom iron catalyst (Fe-NCN), which triggers an electron-transfer
pathway (ETP) by enhancing the electronic pulling effect on 2,4,6-trichlorophenol (TCP). This approach achieves near-
complete dechlorination of TCP and its catalytic conversion into polymeric products. Overall, this study provides novel
insights in polymerization-based advanced oxidation processes; however, there remain shortcomings in the generalizability
of the mechanism and the in-depth exploration of its details. The detailed comments are as follows: 
1. The source of the XlogP (hydrophobicity index) data in Supplementary Fig. 1 should be clearly specified. 
2. The authors mention that the pre-coordinated template exhibited a product yield of up to 45.0%, which is attributed to
interlayer cross-linking. It is necessary to clarify: (1) How does this interlayer cross-linking process occur? (2) Why can it
increase the loss of C and N? (3) At high temperatures, will the loss of C and N still be constrained by the cross-linking
effect? 
3. It is inappropriate for the authors to rule out the existence of Fe-C and Fe-O bonds solely through XPS. In fact, XPS peak-
splitting results may correspond to multiple chemical states of Fe. Therefore, it is overly arbitrary for the authors to assume
that all coordinating atoms of Fe are N during EXAFS fitting, and further conclude that the Fe-N coordination numbers are
4.28 (for Fe-CN) and 5.13 (for Fe-NCN). Additional characterization methods should be employed to probe the actual
coordinating atoms of Fe, such as Time-of-Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (TOF-SIMS). 
4. The results in Supplementary Fig. 13b show that electron-deficient organic pollutants (e.g., ATZ) can still be effectively
removed (with approximately 70% degradation efficiency for ATZ). If this reaction is dominated by non-radical species, the
authors need to explain this phenomenon. 
5. The amount of chloride ions released during the reaction should be detected to further confirm the dechlorination-
polymerization process of TCP. 
6. In Fig. 3c, the concentrations of the organic substrate (TCP) and PMS, as well as the catalyst loading, should be clearly
stated. Additionally, all reaction conditions should be specified in the figure caption. 
7. The authors claim that "the greater the distance between TCP and CNT/PMS*, the stronger the C-Cl bond". What is the
theoretical basis for this assertion? 
8. The IpCOHP analysis indicates that axial N coordination enhances both the Fe-O bond strength and the d-band center of
Fe, which are interpreted as evidence for strengthened PMS adsorption. However, the adsorption energy calculations show
the opposite trend (weakened PMS adsorption). The authors must explain this contradiction. 
9. In Lines 364–365, the authors state that DFT calculations reveal that axial N coordination reduces both the number of
transferred electrons and the adsorption energy. This result is inconsistent with the authors’ conclusion—this inconsistency
requires clarification. 
10. The authors claim that "axial N coordination leads to a staggered energy rearrangement of the dx2-y2 and dz2 orbitals,
consequently reducing the number of unpaired electrons in the eg orbitals". Please explain the underlying mechanism of this
process. 
11. For Fe-CN-activated PMS, the key intermediate for 1O2 generation is the SO5•
⁻

transfer from PMS to the catalyst. The authors mention that "the energy difference for charge transfer from the eg orbital
center of Fe-CN to PMS increases significantly, reversing the direction of electron transfer (from PMS to the catalyst) and
thus generating SO

12. What characteristics of Fe-NCN enable its high-efficiency polymerization performance? Can the authors propose a
general structural descriptor for this performance, or discuss relevant design principles? Additionally, can this structural
descriptor be applied to other single-atom transition metal materials (e.g., Co-based, Cu-based single-atom catalysts)? 
13. Regarding the claim of "mimicking the enzyme’s hydrophobic/polar binding pocket microenvironment", the authors
should address the following: (1) Why does Fe-NCN possess a hydrophobic and polar microenvironment? (2) The paper
focuses on enzyme-mimetic catalysis—what similarities exist between the structural features of natural enzymes and those
of Fe-NCN? (3) Why does Fe-CN not exhibit enzyme-mimetic functionality? (4) What are the decisive factors that determine
whether the catalyst’s active center has a hydrophobic/polar microenvironment? These points should be specifically
discussed in the revised manuscript. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study successfully designed and synthesized an axial nitrogen-coordinated single-atom iron catalyst (Fe–NCN) for
efficient activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS), achieving nearly complete dechlorination and directed polymerization
conversion of a typical halogenated organic compound, 2,4,6-trichlorophenol (TCP), into plastic products with potential
application value. The work not only provides in-depth insights into the unique "dehalogenation–hydroxylation–
polymerization" reaction mechanism but also demonstrates the application potential of this technology through techno-
economic analysis and life cycle assessment. Spanning multiple levels from atomic-scale catalyst design and mechanistic
studies to practical application evaluation, the research is well-supported by detailed data and robust argumentation. It holds
significant importance for advancing advanced oxidation processes based on resource recovery and is recommended for
acceptance after minor revisions. 
1.The formatting of section headings is inconsistent. For example, the heading style of “Results” is the same as that of its
subheadings such as “Synthesis and characterization of Fe-SACs”, while the heading “Methods” differs from its
subheadings like “Material Preparation”. It is recommended to unify the heading styles and ensure distinction between main
headings and subheadings.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

2.There are spelling errors in the manuscript, such as “1,10 phenanthrene” in line 625 of the main text and in the supporting
information. The correct term should be “1,10-phenanthroline”. 
3.The phrase “mono-atomic dispersion” is unconventional. A more precise expression would be “single-atom dispersion”. 
4.The study confirms the two-electron transfer process in the Fe–NCN/PMS system through RRDE tests. However, the data
processing procedure and fitting error range for the electron transfer number are not presented in sufficient detail. It is
recommended to include necessary explanations and data in the supporting information. 
5.The price of the recovered modified polyphenylene oxide particles is set at 0.9 times that of commercial polyphenylene
oxide. A brief rationale for this factor should be provided. 
6.The techno-economic analysis (TEA) is detailed, but potential costs such as solvent recovery and recycling are not
included in the calculation. It is recommended to address this in the TEA discussion, noting that the current cost assessment
does not account for solvent recovery and reuse, to make the analysis more comprehensive. 
7.The article describes that the TCP removal and dechlorination rates remained high during 60 days of continuous
operation. However, it is not clarified whether this stable performance was due to the inherent stability of the catalyst or the
application of periodic regeneration operations. It is suggested to specify in the supporting information whether any catalyst
regeneration was performed.

Response: We appreciate the Reviewer’s insightful comment regarding the importance 
of PMS utilisation efficiency (PUE) as an important performance metric. In response, we have 
incorporated a detailed comparison of PUE values for our Fe–NCN catalyst with other 
reported nano-atomic-scale catalysts (Table 1, Revision comments ). Interestingly, our 
analysis reveals that polymerization-based advanced oxidation systems (including the 
present Fe–NCN/PMS system) exhibit PUE values exceeding 100%, markedly higher than 
those observed in mineralization-dominated systems.
This phenomenon arises from the intrinsic difference in electron requirements between 
polymerization and mineralization pathways. The polymerization route requires only 1–2 
moles of electrons to activate 1 mole of PMS, whereas full mineralization to CO 2 demands 
substantially more electrons (for example, the complete oxidation of phenol requires 
approximately 14 moles of electrons per mole of substrate). Consequently, the significantly

clarify the apparent contradiction between the ICOHP, the d-band center and the trend of 
adsorption energy calculations, we would like to first clarify the distinct physical meanings of 
these parameters. 
COHP quantifies the effect of bonding states (negative contribution) and antibonding 
states (positive contribution) on bonding stability by decomposing the interaction energies of 
atomic orbitals in the energy band structure; the larger the absolute value indicates stronger 
covalent bonding (more bonding, fewer antibonding states). 
The d-band center model proposed by Hammer and Nørskov suggests that the position 
of the d-band center with respect to the Fermi energy level directly affects the bonding 
strength of the adsorbate to the metal ( Adv. Catal. 45, 71, 2024): an upward shift of the d-
band center toward the Fermi level implies enhanced orbital overlap and bonding between 
metal d orbitals and adsorbate orbitals, corresponding to stronger Fe–O bond interactions. 
Therefore, both ICOHP and d-band analyses describe bonding strength at the atomic scale, 
namely the intrinsic Fe–O covalency within a stable adsorption configuration. 
By contrast, the adsorption energy (Eads) in DFT calculations reflects the energy change 
of reaction intermediates or small molecules (e.g., PMS) upon adsorption on the catalyst 
surface and depends on multiple factors, such as electronic structure, charge transfer, and 
spatial coordination. Although the Fe center in Fe-NCN has a higher positive charge (lower 
electron density, Fig. 1f  in  Revised manuscript ), which theoretically should favour 
electrostatic attraction with the negatively charged PMS, it has fewer spin electrons (fewer 
unpaired electrons, Supplementary Fig. 44  in Supporting Information ), resulting in a 
reduced degree of charge transfer to PMS. Furthermore, axially coordinated N atoms create 
an off-plane geometry at the Fe center, which changes the adsorption configuration of PMS 
from "horizontal-type adsorption" to "inclined-type adsorption" (Figure R2) and increases the 
spatial separation between the PMS molecules and the substrate. 
Consequently, Fe–NCN exhibits a lower adsorption energy than Fe–CN, not because of 
weaker Fe–O bonding, but due to geometric and spin-related factors that lower the total 
binding energy. The local Fe–O bond itself remains stronger and more covalent, as evidenced

polymerization performance of Fe–NCN. The introduction of an axial N ligand modulates both 
the electronic configuration and spin state of the Fe center, thereby tuning the non-radical 
electron-transfer pathway (ETP) toward the selective formation of the electrophilic PMS 
intermediate*. This Fe–PMS* complex exhibits a moderate oxidative potential, strong enough 
to drive electrophilic substitution and coupling reactions, yet mild enough to prevent over-
oxidation or mineralization. Consequently, organic pollutants are selectively converted and 
immobilized as stable oligomeric species on the catalyst surface, achieving both 
detoxification and upcycling.

pCOHP reflects the Fe–O bond covalency and serves as a descriptor for the ease of PMS* 
complex formation, while HOMO and the d-band center describe the redox potential and 
electron-donating ability of the Fe center within PMS* complexes. These parameters 
collectively provide a quantitative framework for correlating the catalyst’s electronic structure 
with its polymerization efficiency.
