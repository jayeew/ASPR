Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper "Multi-omic Analysis of PHACTR1 Signaling Networks" is a functional study of PHACTR1. It explores the gene’s
biological roles through multi-omics integration (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics) in PHACTR1-
overexpressing and knockdown cells. This paper provides valuable insights into the functional impact of PHACTR1 across
multiple cellular processes, particularly its roles in cell cycle regulation, mitochondrial function, and iron homeostasis. 
Major comments 
C1. HT1080 fibrosarcoma cells are a cancer cell line and may not accurately represent vascular or neurological cells. The
choice of HT1080 cells may limit the generalizability of the findings to vascular and neurological tissues. Consider
discussing how the cancerous nature of HT1080 cells may impact results and validate findings in non-transformed vascular
or neuronal cells. 
C2. Consider discussing the limitation of the proposed multi-omics approach. While multi-omics studies provide valuable
insights into functional pathways, they do not establish direct molecular interactions. The study lacks physical validation of
PHACTR1’s interactions with key signaling proteins. Techniques such as co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) could confirm
whether PHACTR1 directly binds its proposed interactors. Additionally, super-resolution microscopy could verify its
subcellular localization, particularly its unexpected mitochondrial presence. Without such validation, the study’s conclusions
remain correlation-based rather than mechanistically proven. 
C3. The study found no significant metabolic changes in PHACTR1-KD cells. Could this be due to the detection method's
limitations? 
C4. The study uses an FDR cutoff of 0.1, which is less stringent than the common FDR < 0.05. This could lead to false-
positive findings. Did the authors conduct sensitivity analyses using stricter FDR thresholds (e.g., <0.05 or <0.01)?

General Impressions 
There is a lot to like about this study. The primary value of the manuscript is that It tackles Phactr1 biology using a multiomic
approach and validates several new insights derived therefrom. The methods look fairly solid though certain parts could
stand with fleshing out. 
A few factors sap from the overall reading experience and conveyed insight. Specifically, the manuscript offers a half-
hearted attempt to offer a methodsy workflowy level of insight that seems pedantic and falls flat. i.e. comparisons of GO vs
IPA offers little value in 2025 and actually detracted from my enjoyment. Also, I will confess to being frustrated by

manuscripts (>80% that I review) that insist on doing pair-wise statistical comparisons with multiple groups yielding multiple

A few points. 
1. In the methods, the authors specify which software and web packages were used. However, since both tend to have
several options and since non-users of those packages do not necessarily know, it is best to specify the following
parameters for each "ome". 
a. How, precisely, was each data set processed prior to statistical analysis? 
i. What is the exact index of analyte abundance being measured (i.e. total ion intensity, median ion intensity, precursors? 
ii. Was the data Logged? Centered? How was it normalize or scaled? 
iii. What precisely is Perseus-type imputation? 
b. What exact statistical test was used? 
c. For pathway analyses, what constituted the reference background. Was it whole-genome or the dataset? 
2. All omic analyses benefit from an initial assessment by PCA. PCA is essentially the passport or quality control index for
each data set as a whole. For cell biological “omes” one would expect to see good clustering. A PCA biplot should really be
panel A for each ome. 
3. The authors have opted to go with a set of pairwise comparisons for each ome. Fair enough (I’m still not a fan of Figure 2
though). But in future, one might consider global LIMMA 
p-value filtering followed by clustered heatmaps. For instance,
would it not offer value to be able to visualize, outright, which clusters most closely correlate with Phactr1 expression? Or

4. Technically, for the transcriptome the authors are using LIMMA-voom, right? The original LIMMA is suited for microarrays
(continuous intensities, normal distribution), whereas LIMMA-voom was developed explicitly for discrete RNAseq counts as
it does a better job of modeling the mean-variance relationship. That should be specified. If LIMMA was used, it would be
best to re-run the stats with LIMMA-voom. 
5. Incidentally, it is not specified, but LIMMA (original) would be an excellent choice for the proteomics data set, as it has
been well-validated. 
6. I concur with the authors that finding cross-ome correlations is valuable. However, there was a missed opportunity to look
for anticorrelation, particularly between the transcriptome and the proteome, which often yields potential leads on
microRNAs. 
7. There is quite a bit of good data in the supplemental figures that would be better off highlighted in the manuscript figures.
This rigorous testing of “ome”-derived hypotheses is one of the strengths of the manuscript. You can leave the negative data

8. All the data sets should be deposited in repositories. i.e. GEO, PRIDE, Metabolights etc. 
9. For transparency and reproducibility, the actual data tables for all the omes should be included in the supplemental tables,

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigates the biological functions of PHACTR1, a gene linked to various vascular and neurological
diseases. The authors developed stable cell lines with PHACTR1 overexpression or knockdown in HT1080 fibrosarcoma
cells and analyzed them using multi-omics (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics). Through pathway

integration and experimental validation, they identified three novel roles for PHACTR1: regulation of cell cycle progression,
iron homeostasis, and mitochondrial biogenesis/bioenergetics. The study provides a comprehensive view of PHACTR1-
mediated cellular processes and demonstrates the value of integrated multi-omics approaches for understanding disease
mechanisms, though it still has some problems to answer. 
Major comments: 
1. The selection of HT1080 fibrosarcoma cells is not convincing. The authors claim that the role of PHACTR1 in vascular
and neurological cell subtypes is unclear, so they chose a more general cell line to avoid the confounding effects of cell-
type-specific characteristics. However, there are no published studies indicating any important functions of PHACTR1 in the
HT1080 cell line. Even though they identified three novel pathways related to PHACTR1 in HT1080, this does not mean
PHACTR1 has the same roles in vascular and neurological diseases. Also, the author should specify whether HT1080 cell
line carries any PHACTR1 SNPs. A more direct approach would be to conduct experiments on both vascular and neuronal
cells to identify common pathways. 
2. It would be helpful to see the overlapped pathways across different pathway databases, at lease do this for the gene
expression analysis, which will help evaluate the performance of different databases. 
3. The authors should provide a reason for choosing IPA over GO:MF, which also gave higher significance. Just because
the total number of DE pathways is smaller does not mean the results are unreliable. In fact, it may help remove false
positive results. The authors should explain why IPA’s results are more biologically meaningful or relevant to their
hypothesis. Also, it is worth running the integration pipeline to see the overlapped pathways across four omics using GO:MF.
4. The authors should explain why the three novel pathways identified by simple overlapping were not captured in the
correlation analysis. This creates confusion about which analytical approach is more reliable for identifying biologically
meaningful relationships. 
5. The manuscript should specify whether all DE features were used for pathway analysis. If so, it might be beneficial to set
some cutoffs to remove noise. 
6. The manuscript claims to develop a “multi-omic integration pipeline” but does not clearly describe the novelty of their
approach. Many existing tools perform similar integrations. 
7. Again, despite identifying novel pathways, the authors do not clearly connect these findings back to the vascular and
neurological diseases in Discussion. Explaining how cell cycle regulation, iron homeostasis, and mitochondrial function
might contribute to these pathologies would strengthen the translational impact of their work. 
Minor comments: 
1. To better visualize the results across all four omics, consider adding a Venn diagram or heatmap of the top differentially
expressed features.

In response to this recommendation, we explored the application of global LIMMA 
analysis across all three conditi ons (PHACTR1 overexpression, PHACTR1 
knockdown, and scramble control) for all four omics datasets. For the transcriptomics, 
proteomics and metabolomic  (PHACTR1 ov erexpressing only) datasets, LIMMA 
identified numerous differentially expressed features. This confirmed the presence of 
robust changes in these omics layers. However, when applied to the lipidomics 
dataset, our smallest dataset , the global LIMMA analysis did not yield any features 
passing our FDR < 0.1 significance threshold. 
 
This outcome highlig hts a key challenge in applying a single analytical framework 
uniformly across omics datasets with widely varying characteristics. We believe that 
that the primary reasons for LIMMA not detecting significant changes in the 
metabolomics and lipidomics datasets, in contrast to our pairwise findings, are: 
 
1. Subtlety and Sparsity of Changes in Lipidomics:  The changes in the 
lipidomics were inherently more subtle and involved a smaller proportion of 
features compared to the widespread shifts observed in transcrip tomics and 
proteomics. LIMMA, while powerful due to its empirical Bayes moderation, 
applies a single, global multiple testing correction (FDR) across all features and

conditions simultaneously. For datasets where true biological changes are 
subtle and spar se amongst a large number of features, this global correction 
can be exceedingly stringent. The comprehensive statistical modeling required 
by LIMMA for multi -group comparison, followed by this stringent global FDR 
adjustment, can reduce the statistical po wer to detect smaller, yet biologically 
relevant, effects when these effects are not uniformly strong across all groups
