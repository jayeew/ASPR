Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary of Main Findings: 
The authors investigate the role of BF (basal forebrain) vGlut2-expressing neurons in odor processing and associative
learning. They report the following key findings: 
1. Odor responsiveness at baseline: Nearly half of BF vGlut2 neurons exhibit either excitation or inhibition in response to
odor presentation. However, at baseline, these neurons do not encode odor identity or valence at either the single-neuron or
population level. Their molecular markers and circuit-level connectivity remain unclear. 
2. Changes following associative learning: After odor-outcome associative learning, the population-level decoding accuracy
of BF vGlut2 neurons for odors increases, suggesting substantial changes in population coding. Notably, a subset of
previously non-responsive neurons becomes activated or inhibited by the odors following learning. However, the extent to
which these newly recruited neurons contribute to the improved decoding performance is not clearly addressed. 
3. Chemogenetic and optogenetic manipulation: Chemogenetic inhibition of BF vGlut2 neurons impairs associative learning
performance in mice. Furthermore, optogenetic activation or inhibition of these neurons induces place avoidance or
preference, respectively, and this valence can be transferred to odors paired with the stimulation. 
Together, these findings lead the authors to conclude that BF vGlut2 neurons are involved in the encoding of odor valence
and contribute to associative learning. 
Major Concerns: 
1. Disentangling associative learning from repeated exposure: 
The authors report that after associative learning with pentanol and hexanol, previously non-responsive BF vGlut2 neurons
become activated or inhibited. However, it remains unclear whether these changes are specific to associative learning or
simply due to repeated odor exposure. It would be important to analyze the neural representations of octanol and heptanol—
odors that were not associated with reinforcement—between the first and last exposure to address this concern. 
2. Contribution of newly recruited neurons to odor decoding: 
While the study shows that associative learning recruits new BF vGlut2 neurons responsive to odors, it is not clear whether
these newly recruited neurons significantly contribute to the improved decoding performance. More detailed analyses are
needed to assess their specific role. 
3. Single-cell vs. population-level analysis in Fig. 2: 
Line 213 mentions “an increase in single-cell response levels following stimulus-paired odor associations,” but Fig. 2 shows
average traces across all recorded neurons, which could be inflated simply by including more responsive cells after
learning. This conflates increased recruitment with increased response magnitude at the single-neuron level. The authors
should provide direct comparisons of individual neuron responses before and after learning. 
4. Concerns about the associative learning paradigm: 
○ The number of exposures to each odor is not balanced across conditions, and appropriate controls are lacking. 
○ The positive and negative reinforcers differ substantially (high-fat food vs. footshock) in both sensory modality and
intensity, which may introduce bias in associative learning strength and neural responses. These differences should be
more carefully controlled or discussed. 
5. Decoding improvement without increased Euclidean distance: 
The authors argue that improved odor decoding after associative learning is due to altered neural representations. However,
pentanol does not show significantly greater Euclidean distance from octanol, despite improved decoding. How can
decoding accuracy increase without a corresponding increase in representation separability?

6. Effect of repeated odor exposure on decoding and trajectories: 
Does repeated odor exposure alone improve decoding accuracy? Are there changes in neural trajectories between first and
last exposure to non-reinforced odors? In Fig. 4I, after just two exposures to heptanol, BF vGlut2 responses already show a
trend toward divergence, similar to that seen for pentanol. Pentanol’s more pronounced changes may simply reflect the
higher number of exposures (seven days), whereas heptanol was only presented twice. Data from octanol—an odor
repeatedly presented but not paired with reinforcement—should be shown to clarify this point. 
7. Causal role of BF vGlut2 neurons remains uncertain: 
The authors show that optogenetic activation or inhibition of BF vGlut2 neurons induces place avoidance or 
preference,
suggesting these neurons broadly influence behavioral state or valence. Therefore, manipulation of BF vGlut2 neurons
during learning may impair performance via altered motivational state, not necessarily due to specific disruption of odor-
value association. To strengthen causal claims, targeted manipulation of odor-responsive BF vGlut2 subpopulations would
be more appropriate. As it stands, the data support a correlation between BF vGlut2 representations and associative
learning, but do not provide strong evidence of a causal role. 
Minor Points: 
1. Odor response plots in Fig. 1: 
Although eight odors were tested, only response data for four odors are shown. Please clarify or include the remaining odors
for completeness. 
2. Clarification of Fig. S1E: 
It is unclear which neurons and which time windows are represented in Fig. S1E. What does each data point signify? 
3. Missing values in pie charts: 
Many pie charts lack absolute numbers or percentage values. Please indicate the total number of recorded neurons and the
proportions in each category. 
4. Clarification of cell inclusion in summary plots: 
In Fig. 1E and Fig. S1C, are all recorded neurons included, or only those that were responsive to odors? 
5. Scale bar visibility: 
The scale bars in Fig. 1C and 1D are hard to read. Please improve the visibility and labeling. 
6. Data presentation in Fig. 1H–J and Fig. 3: 
The meaning of the plots in these panels is not clear without referring to the figure legend. Consider improving figure
annotations and axis labels for better standalone clarity. Similar issues apply to Fig. 3 and should be addressed throughout
the manuscript. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary 
In this study, the authors investigate the role of glutamatergic basal forebrain neurons (vGlut2BF) in encoding and learning
associative odor valence. Based on calcium responses of vGlut2BF neurons, the authors show that while these neurons do
not encode innate valence or odor identity, associative learning alters their response dynamics and the associative odor
valence can be decoded from population responses. Further experiments coupling chemogenetic manipulation with a go/no-
go task demonstrate that vGlut2BF neurons are necessary for learning a valence-dependent odor discrimination task. The
study closes with results suggesting a potential role of the polarity of vGlut2BF activity for switching associated odor
valence. 
Comments 
The results are very interesting and the data on the involvement of vGlut2BF during associative learning will be highly
relevant for a broad readership. The experiments are generally well-designed and executed with appropriate controls, and
the results are presented and discussed comprehensively. 
A notable limitation, however, is the lack of counterbalancing of odors between positive and negative valence associations.
This is particularly relevant for the last result, where the authors conclude that bidirectional manipulation of vGlut2BF
neurons can induce opposite associative odor valence. The observed effects are modest, and the current experimental
design, which uses three different odors assigned exclusively to the three groups, is insufficient to rule out innate odor
preferences or biases. 
If the authors can address this major concern and implement the minor issues below, this manuscript will be a strong
candidate for publication in Nature Communications.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper by Chin et al, the authors use single-cell calcium imaging of vGlut2 expressing neurons in the horizontal limb of
the diagonal band (hDB) to describe how calcium activity of these neurons change with exposure to odors. The authors
show initially, vGlut2 neurons in the hDB exhibit small responses to novel odors. This calcium response is potentiated
following associative learning, when odors were paired with either a positive or negative outcome. The authors then use
chemo- and optogenetic tools to show vGlut2 hDB neurons are necessary for associative learning in a Go/No-Go task, and
the activity of vGlut2 hDB neurons can alter behavior in an odor-preference test. 
Overall, this paper fills a gap in the literature by highlighting the functional significance of glutamatergic neurons in the basal
forebrain (BF). However, this manuscript can be improved with clarifications to the introduction, figures and results, as well
as additions to the discussion. Furthermore, the inclusion of additional experiments will better support the authors
conclusions of a role for vGlut2 BF neurons in odor associated learning.

We appreciate the reviewer’s important point. To further address whether the observed recruitment of 
vGlut2BF neurons is specific to associative learning rather than just repeated odor exposure, we have now 
analyzed both cellular composition and population-level representations of non-reinforced odors over time. 
As an example, we examined the odor Heptanol, which was repeatedly presented over three days without 
reinforcement. We found that only a small percentage of previously non-responsive neurons became active 
after habituation (15.88%) (Extended Figure 2F, P7 line 211), and that 22.75% (38 neurons out of 167 
neurons) were recruited from the pool of non-responsive neurons for the non-reinforced odor Octanol, but 
this proportion was notably lower than the recruitment observed with reinforced odors and was not 
significant (Figure 2B). This suggests that repeated exposure alone to any odorant has a limited effect on 
vGlut2BF recruitment compared to the associated odors.  
To further assess changes in neural responses/representation, we also compared Euclidean distances 
between population activity before and after habituation. The trajectory shift for Heptanol was not 
significantly greater than that of Octanol, an odor only presented during pre- and post-tests, indicating that 
repeated exposure does not substantially alter the population-level representation. (See Extended Data 2G-

interpretation that the observed changes specifically to reinforcement stimuli are linked to associative 
learning.

R1: 3. Single-cell vs. population-level analysis in Fig. 2: 
Line 213 mentions “an increase in single-cell response levels following stimulus-paired odor

inflated simply by including more responsive cells after learning. This conflates increased

We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comment and agree that the apparent increase in population 
response could indeed reflect both the recruitment of additional responsive neurons and increased 
response magnitude in individual cells. To address this, we separately analyzed the responses of newly 
recruited neurons (cells that became responsive only after learning) and stable neurons (cells that were

across sessions. These results indicate that the enhanced responses observed after learning are not solely

result in a new Extended Data Fig. 2A, and show here.

8. Can the authors provide an explanation for Euclidean Distance (line 218)? 
Thank you for this request for clarification. The Euclidean distance measures how different the activity 
patterns of the neuronal population are when responding to different stimuli. Specifically, we create a vector 
representing the mean population response to each odor, with each element corresponding to the activity of 
an individual neuron. We then calculate the Euclidean distance between these vectors, which quantifies the 
overall dissimilarity between the two response patterns. Larger distances indicate more distinct 
representations of the odors across the recorded neurons. We have added this explanation to both the 
Result and Method section to improve clarity. (See line 253-262, Method line 933-939).

12. In distinct parts of the BF, cholinergic afferents co-express vGlut3 (Barabas et al, 2024). Can the

13. In the discussion, the authors mention multiple subtypes of vGlut2 BF. What are these subtypes

We appreciate this inquiry, given it’s an outstanding question we also have. Our data show that some vGlut2
