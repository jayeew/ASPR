Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript titled "Visualizing phenotypic heterogeneity and single-cell morphology in situ during gut infection" by
Nicholas V. DiBenedetto et al., explores how to visualize the behavior of pathogenic C. difficile within mammalian intestines,
particularly the spatial distribution of toxin gene expression, at a single-cell resolution. Below are review comments for this
manuscript: 
1. The arrangement of figures needs to be revised, as extended figures are uncommon in Nature Communications papers.
More data currently placed in the extended figures should be moved to the main figures to strengthen the core narrative. 
2. In addition to the large intestine, it would be valuable to investigate the biogeography of C. difficile in the small intestine, if
feasible.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

3. For both fluorescent proteins used in this study, there appears to be strong background fluorescence from food debris
(e.g., Figure 3b). Therefore, how can the authors be confident that the observed signals are actual C. difficile cells? Is
morphology alone sufficient for identification? I suggest the authors perform fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to
confirm the identity of the observed cells in the images. 
4. To more precisely determine the localization of C. difficile, mucin staining is recommended. To avoid quenching of the
fluorescent proteins, Bouin’s solution (J Nutr Sci Vitaminol, 2002, 48: 311), which is known to effectively preserve mucin in
gut tissues, may be a suitable fixation method. 
5. While the paper emphasizes the advantages of the proposed method, it would benefit from a discussion of its potential
limitations and possible biases.

Fig 3b. Because C. difficile and plant fiber both autofluoresce in the green channel, it is important to 
show a negative control with colonic samples of mice infected with WT cells with no reporter

We thank the Reviewer for making this critical point. Including a proper negative control is essential 
for microscopy analyses. We had previously analyzed mice infected with an unmarked WT C. 
difficile strain and observed minimal background signal. However, we have systematically 
addressed this concern by infecting multiple mice with an unmarked WT C. difficile strain, and then 
processing and imaging the colonic tissue sections using the same settings as those applied to the 
reporter strain samples. Specifically, the brightness and contrast for each channel were matched to 
ensure direct comparability. 
 
We observed minimal autofluorescence in the green channel under these conditions. Notably, the 
autofluorescence of C. difficile in the green channel is highly medium dependent (Ticer et al. 2024, 
Oliveira Paiva et al. 2022), so our finding that C. difficile is minimally autofluorescent in the green 
channel during infection strongly suggests that the metabolites that contribute to this 
autofluorescence are not present in C. difficile under these growth conditions. In contrast, in the 
mScarlet-I3 channel, a handful of rod-shaped structures resembling cells were visible (revised 
Extended Data Fig. 4). We suspect that these rod-shaped structures are likely debris that share a 
similar shape as C. difficile. Across entire colonic cross-sections, we detected between 0-5 such 
“cell-like” objects per section - negligible compared to the >1000 fluorescent cells typically observed 
in tissues infected with reporter strains. Therefore, this autofluorescent debris represents less than

However, in the course of conducting these analyses, we realized that a more accurate approach 
for quantifying toxin gene expression was to normalize the PtcdA::mNG toxin reporter signal to the 
constitutive Pcwp2::LP-mScI3* reporter signal for each cell. This normalization produced a more

meaningful representation of toxin gene expression. Thus, we normalized toxin gene expression 
data in this manner throughout the revised manuscript. (see Fig. 6d and 7a below).
