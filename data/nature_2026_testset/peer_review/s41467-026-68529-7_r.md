Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper introduces new tools to determine the ultimate precision limits of quantum detectors. Detector characterization
involves determining the associated POVMs, a task addressed by quantum detector tomography (QDT). In QDT, an
unknown element \Pi_k is characterized through a set of known measurements using known input probe states. By
performing many repetitions on multiple copies of each probe state, one can estimate \Pi_k, thereby allowing the inverse
problem to be solved. 
Following the pioneering work of Ian Walmsley's group, QDT has been performed for a wide range of detectors, including
SPADs, time-multiplexed detectors, transition-edge sensors, superconducting nanowire detectors, and even the human eye.
Due to the complexity of the task, various advanced methods have been developed, such as the Fitting of Data Patterns,
self-guided tomography, compressed sensing tomography, and techniques based on neural networks. To the best of my
knowledge, all of these approaches rely on fidelity (or variants thereof) for benchmarking. 
The current work introduces a novel tool: the detector quantum Fisher information (DQFI). The concept is elegant and the
theoretical development is clear. The authors propose two definitions of DQFI—one tighter than the other. While this is
insightful, I would appreciate a clearer operational preference or justification for using one over the other. 
The authors draw an analogy with the standard quantum Fisher information (QFI). But we know that for pure states, QFI
reduces to the variance of the generator. In this sense, QFI provides a lower bound on the mean square error (MSE), which
can be related to fidelity. From a benchmarking standpoint, I question whether this represents a significant advance over
existing approaches in QDT. 
More importantly, it is well known that a key requirement for QDT is that the set of probe states must be experimentally
feasible. This means the states must be well-characterized themselves. In standard tomography once the QFI is known and
the quantum Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB) is shown to be saturable, one can advantageously identify the optimal probe states
for characterization. The authors focus on the single-parameter case, where the situation is relatively simple. Can this be
done for QDT? 
However, a complete QDT requires a multiparameter scenario, where the QCRB is generally not saturable. In standard
QDT, the set of reference states must be tomographically complete; i.e., they must span the space of POVMs to enable
inversion. In photodetector measurements, this is relatively straightforward. Coherent states of an optical beam serve as
ideal candidates: they are overcomplete and can form a tomographically complete set by varying amplitude (e.g., via
attenuation with a beam splitter) and phase (e.g., with a delay line). Crucially, such states are easy to generate using lasers. 
A question that remains is whether the proposed DQFI can be used to identify optimal probe states for full QDT. In the
standard multiparameter QFI framework, one can convert the QCRB matrix into a scalar bound (e.g., via a trace with a
weighting matrix) and thereby derive optimal states. Would a similar approach be possible or meaningful in the DQFI
context? 
In summary, this is a well-written and rigorous work introducing new concepts that may prove important in refining the limits

the readability and practical impact of the paper. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors investigate the precision achievable in estimation of parameters of general quantum measurements. The
authors introduce detector quantum Fisher information, investigate its properties and present applications of the established
formalism. The present study is comprehensive and it nicely complements the earlier results established of optimal
estimation of parameters encoded in quantum states. As pointed out by the authors in the Supplemental material, there are
close connections between quantum measurements and quantum processes. The quantum measurement can be seen as a
specific instance of a quantum process (or quantum channel), where the output state is one of orthogonal states associated
with the measurement outcomes, c.f. Eq. (SM8.3) when traced over the second degree of freedom. Nevertheless, the
quantum measurements play so important role that it is fully justified to treat them separately and establish a theory which
provides bounds on precision of estimation of parameters encoded in the measurements. Given the central role that
sophisticated quantum measurements play in modern quantum technologies and quantum computing architectures the
potential impact and importance of the present work are high enough to in principle deserve publication in Nature
Communications. 
There are several points that should be considered by the authors before the manuscript could be published: 
1) In the Supplemental Material 7 the authors consider a scenario where two copies of the measurement are available. In
this case, entangled probe states may be optimal and may provide advantage. What about using entangled probes when
probing a single copy of the measurement. Suppose a pure bipartite entangled states of systems A and B is prepared and
the measurement is applied to system A. Subsequently, also system B is measured and the measurement on B can depend
on the outcome of the measurement performed on system A. Could such scenario provide any advantage with respect to
using only a single system A to probe the characterized quantum measurement? Note that the entanglement of A and B
need not be maximal. 
2) The authors base their work on the widely utilized concept of Fisher information. To saturate the Cramer-Rao bound, an
unbiased estimator is usually required. The authors should clarify in more details which estimators do they have in mind in
general, whether they assume the maximum likelihood estimator in the asymptotic limit of very large number of samples. 
3) In the Supplemental Material 3, the authors provide an example of a linear estimator of parameter p, Eq. (SM.3.2). What
are the signs of the coefficients alpha and beta in this estimator? It seems that one of the coefficients is negative. In principle,
the estimator can yield a negative value of the estimated parameter, which would be unphysical. This may happen in case of
low number of samples and if the true p is close to 0. While such situations may be rare in occurrence, the authors should
comment on this, because e.g. changing the negative p to p=0 could introduce a bias in the estimator. 
4) The authors should check Eq. (SM.8.16). 
5) In Figure 1 in the Supplemental Material, symbol theta denotes both the angle of a probe quantum state and also the
unknown parameter to be estimated. The authors should check the notation used in this figure.

The authors draw an analogy with the standard quantum Fisher information (QFI). But we
know that for pure states, QFI reduces to the variance of the generator. In this sense, QFI
provides a lower bound on the mean square error (MSE), which can be related to fidelity .
From a benchmarking standpoint, I question whether this represents a significant advance
over existing approaches in QDT.
We agree with the reviewer that for parameters encoded unitarily onto pure states, the variance of
the generator can be used to lower-bound the minimum mean-squared error (MSE). However, unitary
dynamics cannot directly describe most noisy measurement processes, because the parameter depen-
dence here is typically less structured. For instance, for the dephased detector considered in Example 2
(Eq. (10), main text, pg. 5), the unitary-based reasoning fails produce a lower bound to the MSE. The
same is true for the tomography problem in Example 3 (Eq. (14), main text, pg. 6), where the param-
eter dependence cannot be converted into a unitary . In such cases, the QFI can only produce a fidelity
bound for a specific input state; there is no notion of optimality or a universal bound.
More generally , for characterising measurements subjected to noisy channels, as well as for most
measurement tomography problems, the variance of the generator does not produce a lower bound
on MSE and the optimal probe states cannot be identified by this method. In contrast, our DQFI
technique can produce tight precision bounds for such problems and identify the optimal probe states.
In practical settings, the parameters of interest would often correspond to either lossy effects or noisy
interactions with the environment (e.g., dark counts in single photon detectors), neither of which can
be accounted for unitarily . By producing informative precision bounds for these non-unitary settings,
the DQFI makes a major advance over existing approaches.
While the lower bound on MSE can be related to reconstruction fidelity , it is important to note that
the Fisher information is not concerned with fidelity itself, but rather with the scaling of fidelity with
the number of samples, N. This is because, from the quantum Cram ´er-Rao bound, the minimum
(unscaled) MSE decreases inversely proportionally with increasing N, meaning that fidelity increases
with increasing N, ideally converging to 100% in the asymptotic limit. Effectively , our tools quantify
the maximal rate of this convergence, thereby benchmarking how efficiently the task of QDT has
been performed, which is not captured in the fidelity metric alone. When applied to experimental
setups where fidelity is used as a metric, our tools can lead to higher reconstruction fidelities using
reduced numbers of samples, via the optimal choice of probe states. More broadly , our work enables
benchmarking existing approaches in QDT, and comparing alternative proposals (e.g., ancilla-assisted
strategies, as proposed in Phys. Rev. Lett. 93, 250407 (2004) and demonstrated in Phys. Rev. Lett.
108, 253601 (2012)) to better understand their merits and limitations. In these ways, our work
significantly advances the topic of QDT.
For the multi-parameter setting, our proposed quantum Cram´er-Rao bound also serves as a finer metric
than fidelity , for instance allowing the prioritisation of parameters through a weight matrix. Notably ,

We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful question. Our proposed DQFI can identify the optimal probe

we address the QDT of on-off detectors in Example 3 (main text pg. 6)), where we identify the

measurement imperfections typically characterised are of the order of 1% (as noted previously on
pg. 14 of Supplemental Material IV).

probe states for full QDT. In the standard multiparameter QFI framework, one can convert
the QCRB matrix into a scalar bound (e.g., via a trace with a weighting matrix) and thereby

context?
The reviewer is correct that in multi-parameter QDT, the set of reference states must be tomograph-

optical setups, we agree that they constitute a convenient probing strategy for complete QDT of pho-

does not identify the optimal measurements when unattainable. Our revisedConclusion and Outlook
