Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors investigate the role of PDLIM5 in the tumor microenvironment, focusing on its effects on tip cells
and vascular normalization, immune cell infiltration, and response to anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy. Using a combination of
integrative gene expression analysis, single-cell RNA sequencing, and functional experiments, they demonstrate that
PDLIM5 expression is increased in tumor endothelial cells, associated with tumor neuovascularization, and functionally
validated using in vitro cell assay and in vivo tumor modeling that PDLIM5 is linked to tumor vascularization, reduces tumor
hypoxia, and enhances T cell infiltration, thereby improving the efficacy of immune checkpoint therapy. Using molecular
docking and co-IP, the study reported that PDLIM5 interact with the ACTIN2/4, hencing regulating filopodia formation and
angiogenesis. The study spans multiple tumor models, including Hepa1-6, MC38, and LLC, to validate these findings. The
study has applied a comprehensive molecular and cellular approaches to evaluate the effect of PDLIM5 on tumor vasuclar
functions, the molecular interactions with ACTIN2/4, and its implications for cancer therapy. 
While the manuscript employs a variety of experimental approaches, the work is also hindered by several limitations,
including the conclusion on Tip endothelial cells, the molecular mechanisms linking PDLIM5 to normal vascular functions in
tissues and tumor neovascularization, the prognostic value of PDLIM5 expression and patient outcome, as well as critical
components of the immune microenvironment. Furthermore, there are quite a number of mistakes in the figures and
supplementary figures, which will need significant proof reading and checking the original results. Detial comments for these
concerns are highlighted below. Carefully addressing these are required before any possible consideration of the study for
publication. 
1. One major conclusion of this study is the PDLIM5 affects endothelial tip cell filopodia formation and tumor angiogenesis.
Tip cells are a very small fraction of endothelial cells leading the vascular angiogenesis. While tip cells are almost absence
in adult normal tissues, this endothelial phenotype is increased in tumors and essential for tumor neovascularization.
Furthermore, tip cells are well characterized with the expression of classic markers such as 
CXCR4, ESM1, DLL4. The
current study had used COL4A1, PLVAP, COL4A2 as markers for TipECs, which are not correct (Figure S2C). These are
mixed markers for EndoMT EC phenotypes. These jeopardize the conclusiveness of the results and conclusion made based
on Figure 1 and related figures. 
For example, in TEC (Figure 1d), the fraction of tip ECs could not be over 20% of the entire ECs. Furthermore, the increase
expression of PDLIM5 is not specific to TipECs. 
According to the data presented in Figure 1b, as well as checking the gene
expression data provided by the protein atlas database, PDLIM5 is higher expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells,
skeletal monocytes, fibroblasts. Similarly, the increased PDLIM5 was not restricted to TipECs, as noted by the authors in the

(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper investigates the role of the cytoskeletal scaffold protein PDLIM5 
in promoting angiogenesis by tumor endothelial
cells (TECs). The authors show that PDLIM5 stimulates vascular sprouting in the retina of healthy mice as well as in tumor
grafts. By combining KO of PDLIM5 with anti-PD-L1 treatment, 
they further show that this PDLIM5 function can be exploited

to decrease tumor growth and promote tumor immune cell infiltration, suggesting that small molecules directed against
PDLIM5 might hold promise as a new antitumor treatment. To address the mechanism underlying this PDLIM5 function and
identify potential PDLIM5 surfaces to target for drug development, the authors examine the interaction between PDLIM5 and
ACTN1/ACTN4, which has previously been reported in other cell types. In contrast to Nakagawa et al (2006), they find that
PDLIM5 binds to ACTN1/ACTN4 through its Lim domain instead of its PDZ domain. They further narrow the PDLIM5-
ACTN1/4 interaction down to two residues in the third LIM domain (S593 and F596) and present some data that suggests
this interaction enhances the binding of ACTN1/ACTN4 to F-actin, thereby stabilizing and promoting filopodia formation and
angiogenesis. In line with this, expression of the PDLIM5 double mutant in PDLIM KO mice 
hampers tumor growth,
angiogenesis and immune cell invasion. The merit of this work mainly lies in the large body of data supporting PDLIM5 as a
potentially interesting antitumor target for a great many different 
types of cancer. However, the described underlying
mechanism is not fully supported by the data presented. Thus, to substantiate the claim that this study identified a druggable
surface on PDLIM5, the authors will have to address the issues raised below. 
Fig. 4: The authors claim that PDLIM5 recruits ACTN1/4 to actin in filopodia, however the linescans from the images shown
in Fig. 4e-f do not show that higher PDLIM5 signals correspond to higher ACTN1/4 signals. In addition, the
immunoprecipitation assay in Fig. 4g, shows varying levels of PDLIM5 in WCL, but does not confirm that this also
corresponds to varying levels of PDLIM5 bound to actin after IP. Hence, they cannot conclude that PDLIM5 regulates the
amount of ACTN1/4 bound to actin. To claim that PDLIM5 recruits ACTN1/4 to F-actin, they need to show that the level of
PDLIM5 bound to F-actin after immunoprecipitation changes similarly as the level of ACTN1/4 bound to F-actin after
immunoprecipitation. 
In addition Line 193: The authors state that PDLIM5 colocalizes with ACTN1/4 
in tip cells (Fig. 4e). Are the cells that are
shown in the images and that are used for the linescans actual tip cells? 
Fig 5: The authors should explain why Fig. 5g shows that PDLIM5 mutants bind less ACTN1/4, however the reverse IP in
Fig, 5c-d does not show the same result. Is this perhaps due to the fact that these IP experiments were performed with full
length PDLIM5, which can bind to F-actin and ACTN1/4. The latter implies that the interpretation of the IP results could be
perturbed because there are two binding interactions to account for, especially given that the authors do not demonstrate
that the LIM domain mutations do not change the overall conformation of PDLIM5 such that it affects its interaction of the
PDZ domain with F-actin. To make matters worse, ACTN1/4 are bone fide F-actin binding proteins, irrespective of whether
their binding is increased by PDLIM5, and thus it should be taken into account that PDLIM5 binds to F-actin bound by
ACTN1/4. To work around this complex situation and avoid any confusion, the mutant PDLIM5 IP experiments should be
repeated with the mutations introduced in the LIM domains only. 
This is contrary to Fig. 5h-i. In order to conclude that these mutations fail to recruit ACTN1/4 to F-actin, the authors should
confirm that 
full length PDLIM5 mutants bind equally well to F-actin as wild type PDLIM5.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the study entitled “Endothelial PDLIM5 promotes tip cell filopodia formation and tumor angiogenesis by regulating
ACTN1/ACTN4-dependent actin bundling”, the authors investigate the role of PDZ and LIM domain 5 (PDLIM5) in tumor
endothelial cell (TEC)-mediated angiogenesis. They demonstrate that PDLIM5 is highly expressed in lung cancer TECs,
correlates with poor patient survival, and plays a crucial role in filopodia formation by interacting with actinin-1/actinin-4
(ACTN1/ACTN4) to facilitate filamentous actin (F-actin) bundling. Endothelial-specific deletion of PDLIM5 disrupts sprouting
angiogenesis, reduces tumor growth, normalizes tumor vasculature, and enhances immunotherapy response in resistant
tumors. These findings suggest that PDLIM5 may serve as a novel therapeutic target to improve cancer treatment outcomes. 
Comments: 
The results presented in the manuscript provide substantial evidence on the role of PDLIM5 in TECs and its potential impact
on tumor angiogenesis. However, a number of comments should be addressed to support the claims and findings presented
in the manuscript. These include: 
• Additional functional studies are needed to convincingly demonstrate that endothelial PDLIM5 regulates tip cell function.
For instance, in vitro migration assays and competition sprouting assays, where a mixed population WT and PDLIM5-
silenced cells compete for the tip cell position, could provide additional strenght. 
It would be also important to investigate how the tip cell subset (as well as the additional subtypes) is affected in the
endothelial-specific PDLIM5 KO in mice. 
• While the authors propose that endothelial PDLIM5 deletion induces tumor vessel normalization (TVN), Figure 2 suggests
vessel pruning (reduced vessel density), which is likely responsible for the observed reduced tumor growth. This
discrepancy warrants an explanation. TVN is often associated with improved metastasis outcomes rather than with an effect

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

on primary tumor growth. It would be useful to investigate the relationship between vessel pruning and reduced tumor growth
and explore whether TVN by PDLIM5 deletion affects metastasis. 
• The mechanism by which endothelial PDLIM5 regulates T cell infiltration needs further investigations. Are increased T cell
infiltration levels due to enhanced delivery upon TVN, or is there an effect on adhesion molecules? ICAM-1 clustering and
function has been shown to be directly linked to ACTN4 (Celli et al. J Immunol. 2006). Thus, it would be crucial to assess
whether PDLIM5 deletion alters ICAM-1 clustering. Additionally, as actin-binding proteins can differentially regulate EC
stiffness (Schaefer, et al. J Cell 2014), it would be valuable to investigate whether PDLIM5 deletion leads to changes in EC
stiffness that might favor T cell transmigration. 
• ACTN4 is known to bind endothelial eNOS and inhibit its basal activity (Hiroi Yet al. FASEB J. 2008). Furthermore, as
previoulsy shown by the authors, PDLIM5 phosphorylates eNOS to regulate NO generation, which can affect vessel
permeability. It would be insightful to assess whether these mechanisms, particularly in relation to NO regulation and
endothelial permeability, contribute to the in vivo phenotype observed upon PDLIM5 deletion. Could these factors also play
a role in promoting T cell infiltration by modulating the permeability of tumor vessels? 
• Given the evidence for improved perfusion in tumors with normalized vasculature, it would be useful to consider the
potential effects of PDLIM5 deletion on chemotherapy efficacy. Better tumor perfusion could result in more effective drug
delivery, independent of whether the treatment is chemotherapy or immunotherapy, unless the central mechanism is
immune-mediated. 
Minor: 
In Figure 4I-M, the indication of sh vs. scr conditions appears to be swapped. 
Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
(General comments) 
This study investigates the role of endothelial PDLIM5, an actin binding cytoskeletal protein with PDZ and LIM domain 4, in
tumor angiogenesis. The authors demonstrate that PDLIM5 is highly expressed in tumor endothelial cells (TECs) in lung
cancer, correlating with poor patient survival. Mechanistically, PDLIM5 promotes tumor angiogenesis by interaction with
ACTN1/ACTN4 via its S693/F596 residues, facilitating F-actin bundling and tip cell filopodia formation. Using EC-specific
PDLIM5 KO mice, the study shows that loss of PDLIM5 not only suppresses tumor growth but also improves tumor vascular
function, reduces intratumoral hypoxia, enhances T cell infiltration, and increases the efficacy of immune checkpoint
inhibitors (anti-PD-LI antibody treatment). 
The experiments from Figure 1 to Figure 6 are well designed and demonstrate the
role of PDLIM5 in promoting tip cell filopodia formation and tumor angiogenesis via interaction with ACTN1/ACTN4 via
S693/F596 using PDLIM5 KD cells and KO mice knocked in with either adeno-WT or S588A/F591L mutant PDLIM5.
However, there are two major concerns with this study: 1) Figure 7 does not integrate well with the rest of the study, as it
shifts the focus to CD8+ and CD4+T cells in LLC tumors from WT or PDLIM5 KO mice in the context of immune checkpoint
inhibitors therapy; 2) Previous study (PMID:29228678) shows that PDLIM5 promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion and
migration via binding to AMPK. However, the potential interaction between PDLIM5 and AMPK in TECs has not been
investigated in this study. These issues should be addressed. 
(Major comments) 
1) Figure 5g: The IP:HA and IB: Myc/Flag blots are not convincing. Please replace these blots with better ones. 
2) Figure 7: The inclusion of Figure 7 disrupts the logical flow of this study by shifting the focus to CD8+ and CD4+T cells in
LLC tumors from WT or PDLIM5 KO mice treated with anti-PD-LI antibody This section should be separate paper by adding
more detailed mechanisms. 
3) Given that previous study shows that PDLIM5 promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion and migration through its binding
to AMPK (PMID:29228678), it is important to investigate whether PDLIM5 also binds to AMPK in TECs. Additionally, authors
should examine the relationship between PDLIM5’s interaction with AMPK and its binding to ACTN1/ACTN4 in promoting
tip cell filopodia formation.

TipEC might be relatively high. For instance, in the study conducted by Jermaine Goveia and colleagues,

Nat Genet. 2025). It is thus evident that there are substantial individual differences in the proportion of 
TipECs in tissues from different patients. In comparison, in our research data, the proportion of TipECs is 
approximately 23%, which falls within a relatively normal range.

monocytes, and fibroblasts. However, it should be emphasized that the objective of this research is to 
uncover the expression, potential functions, and regulatory mechanisms of cytoskeleton-related proteins

discovering the high expression of PDLIM5 in TECs, we further analyzed it among different endothelial 
cell subsets to reveal which endothelial cell functions might be more significantly influenced by the highly 
expressed PDLIM5. We found that PDLIM5 was upregulated in AECs, CapECs, LECs, and TipECs.

cells is significantly increased. Since TipECs are the most crucial endothelial cell subset for driving 
angiogenesis, we believe that PDLIM5, as a cytoskeleton-binding protein, is highly expressed in TipECs 
and must be essential for angiogenesis-related functions of TipECs, such as migration and sprouting. This
