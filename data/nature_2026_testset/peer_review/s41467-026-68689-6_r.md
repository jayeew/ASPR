Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Eulalio et al. investigates the link between genotoxic cancer treatments and the development of cardiovascular
disease (CVD) in childhood cancer survivors from the SJLIFE cohort (n=2938), focusing on the role of DNA methylation
(DNAm) as a molecular mediator. The authors build on previous research from the same cohort (Song et al. 2021; Dong et
al. 2023). Their approach integrates epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) to identify CpG sites linked to
cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRFs) and to determine those CpGs potentially influenced by cancer treatments. They further
employ causal mediation analysis in the context of available followup data and cis-eQTM mapping to pinpoint CpGs that are
not only significantly associated with CMRFs but also provide insight into underlying biological mechanisms. After
validation, these CpGs may have translational potential as biomarkers for risk stratification and as therapeutic targets to
improve outcomes for cancer survivors. 
I believe the authors made, generaly, an excellent and methodologically sound work. And the topic is definitely of interest
and relevance. My main criticisms are related to fundamental study design aspects that get lost or hiden by the large amount
of results being reported, which should be made more visible/available to the readership in the text. More detailed comments
follow below. 
1) What are the noteworthy results? 
The main message of this study is that genotoxic cancer treatment induces DNA methylation (DNAm) changes, which are in
turn associated with increased cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRFs) among childhood cancer survivors. The study identifies
around 2000 CpGs associated with CMRF prevalence, with only 9% shared among risk factors—primarily in genes involved
in metabolic and inflammatory pathways. They also identify an obesity-specific epigenetic signature in survivors and two
genes (ANTRX2 and LRIG1). 
Despite these interesting findings, the description of cardiometabolic outcome definitions and the follow-up design is
insufficient to assess the potential for bias, specially given the emphasis that authors pose on the prospective causal
mediation analysis. Important questions are left unanswered: 
• Were standard CMRF definitions used (e.g., ADA, WHO, ATP III)? 
• Were baseline and follow-up criteria consistent? 
Often, baseline definitions involve laboratory measures, while follow-up may rely on registry data, which affects
ascertainment quality. 
• What was the attrition rate across follow-up? 
• Were losses to follow-up differential in terms of baseline cardiometabolic risk? 
• Was follow-up time variable across participants, and how was this handled? 
Differential attrition and variance in follow-up might eventually introduce bias and should be explicitly addressed using
epidemiologic best practices. 
2) Will the work be of significance to the field and related fields?

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

This study contributes meaningfully to survivorship research by linking cancer therapy → epigenetic alterations → CMRFs
using prospective mediation analysis—a largely unexplored area. While previous studies have examined treatment impacts
on DNAm and separate works have linked DNAm to cardiometabolic health, this is among the first to integrate all three into
a coherent causal framework using prospective data. 
The use of causal mediation analysis with DNAm is innovative, but the authors must clearly discuss key assumptions such
as: 
• No unmeasured confounding between exposure–mediator and mediator–outcome, 
• No exposure–mediator interaction, 
• Multiple mediators being independent, an assumption questioned when CpGs are spatially correlated. 
The authors should discuss these assumptions thoroughly and, if possible, conduct sensitivity analyses. Recent
methodological reviews on high-throughput mediation and comparative frameworks of omics mediation stress the
importance of addressing these issues and conducting sensitivity analyses to validate causal inference. 
3) Does the work support the conclusions and claims, or is additional evidence needed? In my opinion, the conclusions are
appropriately drawn and qualified, with the authors clearly noting the need for external validation, especially in multiethnic
cohorts. The use of the EWAS catalog to validate findings externally is a strength, though not equivalent to cohort-level
validation. 
Nevertheless, smoking and inflammation, both influential on DNAm and cardiovascular outcomes, need more careful
evaluation. 
• Recent evidence shows smoking-linked DNAm associations in atherosclerotic tissue and blood, closely related to CVD
risk. 
• Similarly, epigenetic trends tied to environmental/behavioral risk factors illustrate smoking-induced CVD epigenetic
mechanisms. 
The authors should add sensitivity analyses, clarify whether these factors were/should be included in adjustment models,
and frame their rationale according to established cardiovascular epidemiology (e.g., adjustment sets including 
age, sex,
physical activity, diet, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, lipid- and blood pressure-lowering medications, diabetes, systolic
blood pressure, BMI, and renal disease). 
The rationale for including/excluding each variable per CMRF outcome must be clearly provided. 
4) Are there any flaws in data analysis, interpretation and conclusions? 
There are no fatal flaws; the authors present the limitations appropriately, especially the time lag between cancer diagnosis
and DNAm sample collection, and the relatively short period between DNAm measurement and clinical follow-up. This
timing affects causal interpretation, but the authors acknowledge it. 
In high-dimensional omics research, cross-validation or regularization is essential to avoid overfitting. This manuscript does
not report using methods such as k-fold cross-validation or bootstrap procedures. Terms suggesting clinical “precision
medicine” should be tempered unless cross-validation is available. 
5) Is the methodology sound? 
Yes, overall the methodology is strong, but software and package versioning (e.g., minfi, mediation) should be added to
ensure full reproducibility. The methods section is quite thorough, with code and GEO data available. With version numbers
included, the work is reproducible. 
*Final Recommendation* 
This study is valuable and addresses an important gap. However, the following moderate revisions are required for rigor and
clarity: 
1. Provide detailed follow-up information and harmonized cardiometabolic outcome definitions grounded in accepted
guidelines. 
2. Evaluate potential confounding by smoking and inflammation, including sensitivity analyses; clarify adjustment sets per
outcome. 
3. Fully articulate causal mediation assumptions, and introduce sensitivity checks. 
4. Implement or acknowledge cross-validation/regularization for high-dimensional DNAm signatures; revise predictive
language accordingly. 
Once addressed, this manuscript will offer a robust, insightful contribution to cancer survivor health research.

Reviewer #1

The authors have made a substantial effort to address my previous comments. Consequently, the manuscript has improved
in clarity and rigor. 
However, I still have one remaining and critical concern, as several key aspects of the study design remain unclear. The
additional explanations provided in this revised version to clarify the design are still ambiguous. 
The authors state that follow-up was defined as the time from DNA methylation (mediator) assessment to incident outcome.
This definition remains conceptually problematic in the mediation analysis. The follow-up should not originate from the
mediator, but rather from a time point that precedes the mediator (e.g., treatment) in the (I presume, as not much information
on the outcome and mediator models is given) time-to-incident CMRF model. Consequently, the outcome model in the
mediation analysis needs to be corrected, with some considerations. 
Specifically, the authors define treatment as: “All chemotherapy classes and RT exposures were defined as binary variables
indicating whether a given treatment was administered within the first five years following cancer diagnosis. Each of the
eleven treatment classes was considered separately.” 
This clarification is helpful, but it raises additional questions: 
What is the reference category for each categorical treatment variable? Are comparisons made against individuals who did
not receive that specific treatment (i.e. against those who received other treatments than the one coded as 1)? 
The reference
category for interpretation of results must be unambiguous in the text. Importantly, how did the authors handle participants
who received multiple treatments? 
Also, were recurrences or additional treatments after the first five years considered? If participants experienced recurrence,
the time of the last completed treatment might be a more appropriate time origin for analyses. A sensitivity analysis
considering this possibility would greatly strengthen the study. 
Importantly, there may still be heterogeneity in time since diagnosis across individuals at the time of inclusion, which could
introduce bias. Clarifying how time since diagnosis was handled in the analyses is essential. 
Without a clearly defined temporal sequence—from treatment (exposure) to DNA methylation (mediator) to outcome it is
impossible to determine whether the assumptions underlying the mediation analysis are satisfied. Given the uncertainties
regarding the study design and the modeling of treatment in the mediation analyses, the authors should refrain from using
causal language throughout the manuscript. All references to “mediation” or “causal pathways” should be reframed to
describe statistical associations only. 
Clarifying these points will ensure that the interpretation of the analyses remains valid and consistent with the underlying
study design. 
Relatively minor comment: 
Regarding Figure 1 (“Study design for identifying and characterizing mediator CpGs linking treatment exposures to CMRFs
in survivors”): this figure does not fully represent the epidemiologic study design, but rather the statistical analysis strategy. If
the authors agree with this interpretation, I recommend revising the title of Figure 1 to replace “study design” with “statistical
analysis strategy”.

RESPONSE: 
We thank the reviewer for the thoughtful summary and agree that several aspects of the 
study design and CMRF definitions should be more clearly presented; we have revised 
the manuscript accordingly and addressed each point below. 
i. Were standard CMRF definitions used (e.g., ADA, WHO, ATP III)? 
CMRFs were defined using standardized, clinically accepted thresholds based on a 
modified version of Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, v4.03). 
Where applicable, grading thresholds were based on established guidelines, including 
the National High Blood Pressure Education Program (for hypertension) and CDC BMI 
percentiles (for obesity), with pediatric-specific thresholds applied. For other conditions 
(e.g., abnormal glucose, hypercholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia), case definitions

were based on specific clinical assessment and CTCAE. For example, 
hypertriglyceridemia was defined as fasting triglycerides >300-500 mg/dL or having 
taken one lipid lowering agent (CTCAE grade ≥2). These definitions are detailed in the 
Supplementary Methods. We have clarified these sources in the revised Methods 
section (included below). 
ii. Were baseline and follow-up criteria consistent? 
We have expanded the Methods section (included below) to clarify that CMRFs were 
ascertained within the SJLIFE cohort through standardized protocol that integrates 
retrospective medical record abstraction, patient- or proxy-reported health 
questionnaires, and comprehensive in-person clinical evaluations involving laboratory 
measures conducted consistently at baseline and follow-ups (approximately every five 
years thereafter). Outcomes were subsequently graded using a standardized rubric 
adapted from CTCAE v4.03 definitions to enhance clinical relevance and reproducibility. 
This process ensured consistent ascertainment across baseline and follow-up clinical 
visits, as clarified in the revised Methods (see below). 
iii. What was the attrition rate across follow-up? 
We appreciate the reviewer’s request for more detail on follow-up patterns in the 
SJLIFE cohort. Because SJLIFE employs an active, rolling, re-contact and scheduling 
system rather than fixed visit waves, attrition cannot be represented by a single 
summary statistic. Survivors are invited to on-campus comprehensive evaluations every 
five years, with some returning more frequently through ancillary studies.  
To provide context, as of June 30, 2023, among 5,587 campus visit participants, 
3,362 (60%) had already completed a second visit, 218 were scheduled, and 1,733 
were in the queue for follow-up. True attrition was low, with 178 participants deceased 
and 55 providing a hard refusal. Importantly, Howell et al. (Int J Epidemiol, 2021, DOI: 
https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyaa203) directly compared survivors who returned for follow-
up with those who declined and found the groups were largely similar with respect to 
age at visit, age at diagnosis, sex, and race/ethnicity, with only modest differences in 
diagnostic group distribution (see Howell et al., Table S2 included below).  
To minimize attrition, the SJLIFE study team implements multiple retention 
strategies, including tracing procedures and regular address verification. Within our 
analytic set used for this current manuscript, we directly evaluated differences in follow-
up by baseline CMRF status and adjusted all models for follow-up duration, which 
yielded concordant findings across incident-only and worsening endpoints. We had 
added this clarification to the Methods and Discussion.

iv. Were losses to follow-up differential in terms of baseline cardiometabolic risk?  
We quantified loss to follow-up (i.e., no clinic visits thus far after the blood draw for 
DNAm and will be scheduled for future clinical visits) by baseline CMRF status. Across 
conditions, survivors with a CMRF at baseline were less likely to be lost than those 
without (overall 21.5% vs 35.4%, Chi-square p < 10-3), with consistent patterns for 
abnormal glucose, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia, and hypertension; 
obesity showed no difference. Because the incident endpoint is defined among 
baseline-negative survivors, this differential attrition would likely underestimate 
incidence (missed events among those not re-assessed). In contrast, our primary 
endpoint (incident or worsening), is less susceptible given higher retention in baseline-
positive participants. For completeness, incident models also account for follow-up 
duration. Consistent with this interpretation, an incident-only mediation sensitivity 
analysis (restricting outcomes to new onset after DNAm) yielded directionally 
concordant ACMEs with similar magnitudes and FDR patterns, which did not reject our 
findings based on the primary end point (see Reviewer 1, Comment 2, Page 8).  
v. Was follow-up time variable across participants, and how was this handled? 
We defined follow-up time as the interval from the date of blood draw for DNAm to the 
last clinical assessment. We compared follow-up time by incident-event status within 
each CMRF using Kruskal-Wallis tests with BH adjustment. Cases had modestly longer 
follow-up for several conditions (e.g., abnormal glucose metabolism, obesity: BH p = 
0.041). To account for the difference in follow-up time, we included follow-up time as a 
continuous covariate in all incident-event regression models and added text to the 
Methods and Discussion acknowledging how this potential bias was handled (see 
below).
