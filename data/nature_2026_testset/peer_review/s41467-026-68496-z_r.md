Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the synthesis and characterization of morphotropic phase boundaries between the tetragonal and
antiferroelectric orthorhombic phases in Lu-doped HZO bulk crystals is presented. The authors use optical floating zone
growth combined with quenching to stabilize the t/o-AFE interface. A dielectric constant of 57 is obtained. The authors
attribute dielectric enhancement to tensile strain in the tetragonal phase that results in softening of the low-frequency Eu
phonon mode. After additional analysis, clarification of some of the experimental techniques, and elaboration on the
relevance to CMOS-compatible thin films, I would recommend publication. My comments are as follows: 
1. Can the authors explain why they have chosen the title “Beyond morphotropic phase boundaries,” since the work focuses
on MPBs? 
2. Further details should be provided about the quenching technique, since it is central to the results. The authors claim that
the crystals were quenched within ~2 seconds. How sensitive to variations in the quenching process is the stabilization of
the desired MPB? 
3. How were the XRD scans performed to identify different phases in different regions of the crystal? Was this a
microdiffraction experiment? 
4. The differences in the XRD data in Fig. 2b are hard to see due to the chosen scale. This figure should be adjusted so that
the reader can more easily see the highlighted regions. Additionally, the authors claim that the pattern from the St region
matches the t-phase reference and has no evidence of o-phase diffraction. This seems like a challenging claim to make due
to the many similar reflections of these phases. Can the authors clarify this statement? 
5. How were the crystals prepared for the dielectric measurements, and how were specific regions measured? 
6. The STEM analysis is very rigorous; however, only a few selected interfaces are shown. One issue with TEM analysis is
that it only represents a small sample of the crystal. Have the authors verified these conclusions in other locations? 
7. Is the transitional phase a distinct crystallographic structure, or a highly strained tetragonal region? This should be
clarified. 
8. The argument that the tensile strain induces softening of the Eu phonon mode is not supported by experimental evidence.
Is it possible to provide evidence of this via Raman spectroscopy with a measurement in the tetragonal region taken
adjacent to the interface vs. a region far from the interface? As it stands, this argument is supported only by theoretical work
and the link between tensile strain and dielectric enhancement is primarily correlative. 
9. The manuscript would benefit from discussion about how the findings here can be applied to HfO2-based thin films for
CMOS applications, especially since the authors state that their work, “establishes a generalizable design paradigm for
developing high-κ dielectrics in fluorite-structured materials, advancing next-generation CMOS integrated functional
devices.”

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper reports that a high dielectric constant could be achieved by synthesizing HfZrO bulk crystals using high-
temperature rapid thermal processing and forming a t/o-AFE interface. Using XRPD, Raman analysis, electrical
measurements, and TEM analysis, the details of the t/o-AFE MPB are carefully analyzed. This is a very interesting study
from the perspective of crystallography. 
However, the authors' claims that this study will contribute to applications in CMOS and DRAM are not supported by any
experimental evidence, making them completely unconvincing. Semiconductor devices require uniform and homogeneous
material properties on the nanometer scale. In contrast, the XRPD, Raman, and electrical properties observed in the bulk
crystals in this study are presumably averaged properties analyzed over a scale several orders of magnitude larger. The
paper does not disclose the electrode dimensions or arrangement used to measure the dielectric properties. On the other
hand, TEM analysis also confirmed that tensile strain and compressive strain compete locally, raising concerns that
performance variations may occur when applied to semiconductor devices. The temperature used in this crystal synthesis
was too high compared to semiconductor manufacturing processes, and the practical feasibility has not been demonstrated. 
Submission to a ceramics journal is encouraged.

quantitative discussion comparing the proposed strain–phonon mechanism with the established field-induced transition
model, supported by experimental or theoretical evidence (e.g., phonon mode softening vs polarization switching data).

1. Can the authors explain why they have chosen the title “Beyond morphotropic phase 
boundaries,” since the work focuses on MPBs? 
Response: Thanks for raising this important point regarding the title. We appreciate the 
opportunity to clarify our rationale. Our work indeed focuses on a specific type of morphotropic 
phase boundary (MPB), and the revised title (removing this “beyond morphotropic phase 
boundaries”) aims to precisely capture the paradigm shift  our study introduces within the 
MPB framework for HfO2-based dielectrics.  
Conventional high-κ MPB strategies in HfO 2 primarily exploit the boundary between the 
tetragonal (t) and orthorhombic ferroelectric ( o-FE) phases. While effective for boosting the 
dielectric constant ( κ), such t/o-FE MPBs often suffer from intrinsic instability, leading to 
significant κ degradation with temperature.  
Our work introduces and validates a novel MPB design strategy : the t/o-AFE phase 
boundary. Guided by energy barrier analysis, we identified that the t/o-AFE transition has a 
significantly higher barrier (~68.8 meV per f.u.) than the t/o-FE transition (~46.0 meV per f.u.), 
suggesting a path toward greater stability. Inspired by metallurgical quenching, we successfully 
fabricated t/o-AFE MPBs in bulk HfO2 crystals, achieving a high κ (~57) comparable to state-
of-the-art t/o-FE MPBs. Crucially, these t/o-AFE MPBs exhibit superior thermal stability , 
with less than half the κ variation rate of their t/o-FE counterparts over 30–200°C. 
Furthermore, we go beyond simply reporting a new MPB composition by elucidating its 
atomic-scale enhancement mechanism. Through microstructural strain analysis and theoretical 
calculations, we demonstrated that pronounced tensile strain at the coherent t/o-AFE interface 
softens a key phonon mode in the t-phase, directly driving the dielectric enhancement. We also 
identified the optimal crystallographic orientation between the phases for maximum 
performance. 
Therefore, the title “Atomic-scale mechanism unlocks thermal-stable high-κ performance 
in HfO2 via coherent interfaces” accurately reflects our core contributions: 
1. Shift in MPB paradigm: It highlights our move from the conventional, thermally unstable 
t/o-FE MPB to a new, stable t/o-AFE MPB system. 
2. Mechanistic insight: It emphasizes our foundational discovery of the atomic-scale (strain-
phonon) mechanism responsible for the enhanced performance.

3. Key outcome: It clearly states the achieved goal: thermal-stable high-κ performance, which 
addresses a critical limitation in the field. 
We believe this title succinctly conveys that our work redefines the approach to designing 
high-performance MPBs in fluorite-structure dielectrics by providing both a new structural 
strategy and a fundamental mechanistic understanding. 
 
2. Further details should be provided about the quenching technique, since it is central to the 
results. The authors claim that the crystals were quenched within ~2 seconds. How sensitive to 
variations in the quenching process is the stabilization of the desired MPB? 
 
Fig. R1. (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Schematic diagram of the optical floating zone method. 
 
Response: Thanks for these constructive comments. The quenching process was primarily 
employed to stabilize the t-phase in as-grown bulk crystals, as the presence of the t-phase is 
vital for preparing t/o-AFE MPBs. Following the formation of t/o-AFE MPBs, rapid quenching 
effectively suppresses the subsequent t-to-o phase transition. Due to the sharp vertical 
temperature gradient near the peak temperature point (>1000 °C/cm) during crystal growth 
[Cryst. Growth Des. 23, 2609-2618, (2023)], careful consideration of the quenching initiation 
point was required. 
Regarding the quenching procedure details, Figure R1 illustrates the optical floating zone 
(OFZ) crystal growth process: the feed and seed rods are connected by a molten zone sustained 
via surface tension. As growth proceeds, both rods move downward at a coordinated rate,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

enabling continuous crystal growth onto the seed rod. Upon completion of crystal growth, seed 
rotation ceases. The as-grown crystal is lowered a specific distance d from its uppermost 
position to the highest heating point. The quenching process then begins: the pull-down rod, 
attached to the seed rod, rapidly descends approximately 50 cm (within ~2 seconds), 
transferring the crystal into the ambient room-temperature environment to achieve quenching. 
Following this rapid transfer, the system power is linearly ramped down to zero over 2.5 hours. 
The xenon lamps are then switched off, and the crystal is retrieved after the equipment cools 
and powers down. 
Concerning the influence of quenching, the significance of this process is directly 
demonstrated by our position-dependent growth of Lu:HZO bulk crystals (10 at.% Lu doping), 
as evidenced by Fig. 1d. The as-grown crystals at three different distances d prior to quenching 
exhibit distinct t/(t+o) phase interface positions, corresponding to a time interval of 15 minutes 
(at a growth rate of 20 mm/h). This indicates that quenching must be executed rapidly under 
such significant temperature gradients and high growth rates. Consequently, we strictly 
controlled the dimensions of the as-grown crystals. Upon completion of growth, the as-grown 
bulk crystals were transferred to room temperature within approximately 2 seconds.
