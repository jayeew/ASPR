Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, authors employed cryo-EM to resolve the structures of human GAT3 in inhibitor-bound, substrate-bound, and
substrate-free states, revealing key molecular determinants for inhibitor selectivity and substrate recognition. The study
provides valuable insights into the mechanism of GABA transport inhibition and offers a structural basis for rational drug
design. The manuscript is recommended for publication after addressing the following revisions. 
1. It is recommended to introduce mutants of key residues (e.g., E66 in GAT3 vs. Y60 in GAT1) could validate their
contribution to SR-THAP selectivity. For example, mutating E66 in GAT3 to tyrosine (Y) and observing its impact on
inhibitory activity would strengthen mechanistic conclusions. 
2. The apo structure of GAT3 is poorly resolved, as the model appears to rely heavily on the inhibitor-bound state for
refinement, which may affect the accurate interpretation of TM1a conformational changes. Consequently, the current build
may not accurately represent the true apo conformation, and structural differences between the two states could be
overlooked. Given these limitations, I recommend prepare apo GAT3 sample to perform data collection and cryo-EM data
processing to get high resolution substrate-free state map or exclude this low-quality structure from the main figures and
advise against its deposition in the PDB. 
3.The author discussed about the similarities of substrate binding mode among NSS families as well as the key residues
involved in diversified GABA recognition in GAT1 and GAT3, please provide more detailed figures illustrating the points.
Particularly the difference between hGAT1 and hGAT3 in GABA recognition. 
4. To elucidate the inhibition mode of SR-THAP, IC50 values measured with or without pre-incubation are not enough. To
support the hypothesis, it is recommended to evaluate the effect of SR-THAP on GAT3 kinetics (Vmax and Km). 
5. In Fig1 a-e, please determine and report the error values for IC50. 
6. In Fig3 c-e, the distance between the inhibitor's nitrogen atom and F308 exceeds the permissible range for a cation-π
interaction. Additionally, hydrogen atoms should be removed at this resolution. Meanwhile, the current figure lacks clarity in
depicting key molecular interactions. 
7. “In higher resolution maps of inhibitor-bound and GABA-bound GAT3, we obsereve a clear density for a water molecule
that mediates an interation between S472 (TM10) and D301 (TM6a) similar to those observed for inward-facing structures of
GAT1 bound to inhibitors and substrate. 23,35, 28”. It seems only the structure by Yan’s group (Zhu et al. NSMB. 2023) can
see water molecules.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript describes the determination of the structure of wild type hGAT3 by cryo-electron microscopy. The last years
have witnessed a tremendous progress in the determination of the molecular structure of membrane bound transport
proteins, including the GABA transporters. Thus, meanwhile three papers have been published describing the structure of
hGAT1, the first from the Gati group in 2022 (ref.22), one from Penmatsa et al. in 2023 (ref. 28) and one from Yan et al. in
2023 (ref. 35). 
The present paper now presents the long-awaited structure of the GABA transporter subtype hGAT3. Whereas for hGAT1
many potent and selective inhibitors are known including Tiagabine, a drug used for the treatment of epilepsy, attempts
directed toward the development of hGAT3 inhibitors with reasonable potency and selectivity met so far only very limited
success. 
The hGAT3 structures published by the authors show the protein in an unliganded form and with bound inhibitor (SR-
THAP), both in the inward-open conformation, as well as with bound substrate GABA in closed conformation. The gained
knowledge on the conformational states, in particular with bound inhibitor (SR-THAP) and the molecular details of binding
can be expected to significantly contribute to and speed up the development of new hGAT3 inhibitors. 
As hGAT3 inhibitor a compound, SR-THAP, is used, that had been described by one of the corresponding authors in a
preceding paper (ref 17). This development had been inspired by literature known compounds analogous to SNAP-5114.
One of these is DDPM-1457 differing from SNAP-5114 by exhibiting a stable N-substituent. The polar head of SR-THAP is
derived from (2S,2’S)/(2R,2’R)-TS1 (compound name in ref. 17) and its racemic diastereomer (2R,2’S)/(2S,2’R)-TS1 from
Steffan T, et al. (Bioorg Med Chem. 2015; 23,1284–306; https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bmc.2015.01). 
SR-THAP being a hybrid of literature known DDPM-1457 and of the racemic diastereomers TS1 has been well outlined in
the preceding ref 17, but less clear in the present manuscript. 
Prior to the hGAT3 structure determination the authors characterized SR-THAP in a hGAT3-uptake assays once without and
once with pre-incubation (120 min), which revealed a distinct increase of the inhibitory potency in the latter case (decrease
of IC50 value). Here the authors miss to present data, that show how the potency of their inhibitor changes with the time for
pre-incubation leading them to finally choose 120 min for this purpose. 
The change of the inhibitor potency as a result of the pre-incubation conditions (without and with pre-incubation of 120 min)
is thought to reflect a dual inhibition mechanism based on two binding sites at the transporter, located at the extra- and the
intracellular side. A similar phenomenon has e.g. been described by Gati et al. (ref. 23) for the binding of tiagabine to
hGAT1. These authors assume tiagabine to reach the intracellular binding site of the transporter through the transport
protein, as for entering the aforementioned binding site from the cytosol after crossing the cell membrane, tiagabine appears
as too polar. 
In contrast, the authors of the present manuscript conclude that their inhibitor binds to the inward facing state of hGAT3, the
intracellular binding site from the cytosol after crossing the plasma membrane (see e.g. legend of Figure 4). Conclusive
arguments for this assumption are, however, missing – and have to be provided. 
When the authors performed docking experiments based on the SR-THAP bound hGAT3 structure, (S)-SNAP-5114 and
DDPM-1457 matched well with the binding mode of SR-THAP, but not RS-THAP, the enantiomer of SR-THAP. These
inhibitors bind as zwitterions, with their amino groups by being protonated giving rise to an additional center of chirality. The
authors should comment whether both diasteromeric forms of the individual inhibitors have been studied in the docking
experiments. Furthermore, for a reasonable judgement of the quality of the docking experiments the binding energies of the
individual ligands should be provided. 
The disclosed hGAT3 structures distinctly broaden the general understanding of the GABA transporter proteins and will be
of great value for medicinal chemist when designing new and more potent hGAT3 inhibitors. Hence, the manuscript is
recommended for publication provided its revision according to the comments given above and the minor points listed in the
