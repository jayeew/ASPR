Reply:We thank the referee for the helpful comments. In experiments, the observed correlation function

theory (χEFT) results and the HAL QCD lattice simulations.

we explain why extracting the “real” source function is essential, rather than relying on a roughly effective
source function, such as the one approximated by the Lednick´ y–Lyuboshits (LL) model in experimental

(1). Reconstructing the source function S(r) from measured correlation function C(q) via the form,

analysis in Ref. [1], this induces uniqueness of the recovered source within the admissible class. Empirically,
independent initializations converge to the same S(r) and yield indistinguishable fits, which is exactly what
a well-posed regularized inversion should produce. In fact, the choice of neural network architecture and the
number of parameters are arbitrary. However, when the network is deep enough, the reconstructed source
function S(r) is stable, and we have tested in our numerical computations.
Classical low-parameter Ans¨ atzecan fit current data, but at the cost of model bias: conclusions depend on

Reply:We thank the referee for this question. We realize that our previous reply may not have explained
the point clearly enough.
The result shown in your figure is from the Lednick´ y–Lyuboshits (LL) fit, which assumes that the radial
distribution of emission source is Gaussian shape with a width parameter r0, and that the interaction can
be fully characterized by two parameters—the scattering length f0 and effective range d0. These three
parameters are then fitted to the experimentally measured correlation functions. However, the approximations
underlying the LL model must be justified. First, the emission source in heavy-ion collisions is not Gaussian
but exhibits a long tail, as explicitly noted in our study and the experimental paper [49]. Second, only very
short-range interactions can be reliably reduced to the two-parameter ( f0, d0) representation. How good this
approximation is remains difficult to assess, and in principle the interaction is not short-ranged—especially
when considering, for example, the Coulomb potential between two protons. Although the LL parametrization
may appear to give a visually reasonable fit, this alone is insufficient—many different functional forms or
multi-polynomial expansions could also fit the data well without physical justification.
In contrast, our work does not rely on such approximations. The scattering wave function is obtained by
solving the Schr¨ odinger equation with realistic potentials such as Argonne-18 potential. In our study, the
transformation kernel (the scattering wavefunction) is fixed. As shown in our paper, the Gaussian source
does not provide an adequate description of the experimental data.
We would like to emphasize again: The three conditions—continuity, smoothness, and positivity—are
generally necessary (not sufficient) for a unique solution, while the Gaussian function or simple parametric

Report
The main claim of the paper is to present a method for extracting proton-emitting sources from
experimental measurements of correlation functions using a deep neural network.
The description of the problem addressed in the manuscript is very clear and easy to understand for
the reader. The authors used the experimental data obtained from pp collisions at √s = 7 TeV by the
ALICE collaboration [36 - reference number in the manuscript] to train the DNN model, shown
schematically in Fig. 2 of the manuscript, and to obtain the source function. 
The main results of the paper are presented in Figures 3 and 4, where the authors compare their
results with those obtained using the Gaussian source function (see Eq. 5). Although the approach
outlined in the paper looks innovative, I have strong doubts about its impact. In fact, the Gaussian
fit presented by the authors in Fig. 3. and Fig. 4. looks slightly worse than their DNN model, but if
one considers, for example, the femtoscopic fit presented in the ALICE document [36] (see Fig. 3 in
the  ALICE document,  which  I have also included in my report below), one  can  see that  the
femtoscopic fit is also very good.
This raises the question of whether it is really possible to uniquely determine the source function
from these experimental data, or whether it is possible to find a whole family of functions that lead
to fits of similar quality.  Looking at the ALICE femtoscopic fit, one gets the impression that a fit of
similar quality can be obtained with a small number of parameters, so it is not clear what is gained
by using a DNN with a much larger number of parameters (by the way, what exactly is the number
of parameters of the DNN used in the study?). It is also interesting that with a much larger number
of parameters, the authors failed to get a better fit for small k values. What is the reason for that?
My final comment concerns the reprehensibility of the results presented in the paper. I do not think
it is possible to reproduce the results presented in the manuscript from the information in the
manuscript.  Publishing the DNN code in a publicly accessible repository would help. 
In summary, because of the problems described above, I am not convinced that the result obtained
will be of much interest to others in the community.
