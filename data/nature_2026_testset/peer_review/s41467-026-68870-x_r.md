Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In MSA research, the mystery of αSyn aggregation in oligodendrocytes, which are originally thought to express very low
level of αSyn, is being actively investigated. In this study, the investigators are trying to demonstrate the propagation of αSyn
from neurons to oligodendrocytes under in vitro and in vivo conditions. 
First, using an adenovirus vector to overexpress αSyn in SHSY5Y cells and exposing the conditioned medium to
oligodendrocytes, the oligodendrocytes showed immunopositivity for αSyn, suggesting propagation from neurons to
oligodendrocytes. Next, in Tg mice overexpressing the A53T mutant SNCA under the PrP promoter, αSyn-positive
oligodendrocytes were observed, implying that αSyn propagation from neurons to oligodendrocytes occurs in these Tg mice
as well. 
Since NM-101 (a TLR2-antagonizing antibody) treatment reduces αSyn in oligodendrocytes, it is suggested that NM-101
suppresses the propagation of αSyn from neurons to oligodendrocytes. After administering αSyn preformed fibrils to the
striatum of wild-type mice, NM-101 treatment led to a reduction in αSyn aggregates observed in oligodendrocytes of the
cerebellum, suggesting that NM-101 suppresses the propagation of αSyn aggregates from neurons to oligodendrocytes. 
Using scRNA sequencing analysis, the impairment of oligodendrocyte differentiation and maturation in MSA was
investigated, and the data are consistent with previous reports. 
The propagation of αSyn from neurons to oligodendrocytes and the exploration of its mechanisms are highly intriguing.
Moreover, suppression of propagation by NM-101 is a translatable finding.. 
However, there are several concerns that need to be properly addressed as follows..: 
1. In Fig. 1, αSyn was overexpressed in SHSY5Y cells using an adenovirus vector (serotype Ad5, CMV promoter), and the
conditioned medium (CM) was exposed to oligodendrocytes. The invetigators states that the immunopositivity of αSyn and
p-αSyn in oligodendrocytes suggests propagation from neurons to oligodendrocytes. However, if the viral vector was present
in the conditioned medium,these αSyn positivity could be caused by viral vector transduction. It is necessary to demonstrate
that the conditioned medium from the virus-transduced SHSY5Y cells does not contain adenovirus vectors at all. The
conditioned medium has been concentrated, and viral PCR should be performed on this concentrated medium. Instead of
performing RT-PCR of cells 2 hours after CM exposure, as shown in Fig. S3 in reference #8, the concentrated CM should be
examined directly. 
2. In Fig. 2, it is suggested that exposure to CM containing αSyn leads to the uptake of αSyn into oligodendrocytes, which
was reduced by NM-101. However, it is unclear from this figure whether αSyn is incorporated into MBP-positive
oligodendrocytes: It is likely that these sticky aggregates are only attached to the cell membrane. Since αSyn fibrils tends to
attach to membranes (Kaji S et al. Pathological Endogenous α-Synuclein Accumulation in Oligodendrocyte Precursor Cells
Potentially Induces Inclusions in Multiple System Atrophy. Stem Cell Reports. 2018), it is necessary to showthe images
along the Z-axis to demonstrate that αSyn is being internalized. 
3. In Fig. 3, the investigators claim that αSyn propagates from neurons to oligodendrocytes, because of the presence of p-
αSyn-positive cells in oligodendrocytes of mice overexpressing αSyn under the PrP promoter. Although PrP promotor
basically drive the expression in neuron-specific manner, it also promotes the protein expression potentially in
oligodendrocyte lineage cells and astrocytes. The following references provide additional evidence for non-neuronal

expression of PrP-driven genes.: 
• Castle et al. Physiological Functions of the Cellular Prion Protein, Front. Mol. Biosci., 2017 
• Bribian et al. Role of the Cellular Prion Protein in Oligodendrocyte Precursor Cell Proliferation and Differentiation in the
Developing and Adult Mouse CNS, Plos One, 2012 
• Moser et al. Developmental Expression of the Prion Protein Gene in Glial Cells, Neuron, 1995 
To demonstrate the propagation of αSyn from neurons to oligodendrocytes, it is necessary to prove that human αSyn mRNA
is not expressed in oligodendrocytes through gene expression analysis of PrP-αSyn Tg mice. 
4. In Fig. 3j and Fig. S3, the formation of αSyn aggregates in oligodendrocytes of PrP-αSyn Tg mice and in oligodendrocytes
of wild-type mice injected with αSyn-PFFs is reduced by NM-101 treatment. This suggests that the propagation of αSyn from
neurons to oligodendrocytes via TLR2 can be inhibited. While this is a potentially intriguing finding, there are the following
concerns: 
1. A paper from the same research group suggests that TLR2 is involved in autophagy: Kim et al. Antagonizing Neuronal
Toll-like Receptor 2 Prevents Synucleinopathy by Activating Autophagy. Cell Rep 2015. The reduction of αSyn aggregates
in neurons and oligodendrocytes may be due to enhanced autophagy. 
2. The statement in the manuscript, "NM-101 treatment led to a dose-dependent reduction in αSyn in the treated mice
(Supplementary Fig. 3b, c)," is not accurate. Double staining for TPPP and p-αSyn showed very scarce co-localization,
suggesting that most p-αSyn-positive aggregates are located in the axons of neurons, not oligodendrocytes. To assess αSyn
aggregates in oligodendrocytes, it is necessary to count the area of αSyn aggregates in the regions co-localized with
oligodendrocyte cell markers.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Regarding the points raised, it appears that the authors have addressed most of the comments appropriately. However, two
points may still warrant further consideration. Moreover, I have identified three critical points that I did not raise in the
previous round of review and have now listed them as points 3, 4 and 5. I apologize for these late comments, but I expect the
authors to address them appropriately. 
1. (Regarding Review Comment #4-1) 
In the revised discussion, the authors stated that it remains unclear whether TLR2 is involved in autophagy in
oligodendrocytes. Given this uncertainty, drawing the conclusion that intervention targeting TLR2 blocks the propagation of
αSyn and subsequently ameliorates pathology may require further justification. The potential involvement of autophagy in
this mechanism warrants further clarification. 
2. (Regarding Review Comment #4-2) 
In Supplementary Figure 5b, it appears that p-αSyn co-localizes more strongly with DAPI than with TPPP. Therefore,
describing this as co-localization with TPPP–positive cells may be misleading. A more appropriate figure is recommended
to better support this point. 
3. It has come to my attention that neuron-to-oligodendrocyte propagation of alpha-synuclein has previously been reported in
the following article: Uemura N, et al. (2019) Slow Progressive Accumulation of Oligodendroglial Alpha-Synuclein (α-Syn)
Pathology in Synthetic α-Syn Fibril-Induced Mouse Models of Synucleinopathy. J Neuropathol Exp Neurol 78:877–890. doi:
10.1093/jnen/nlz070. In this study, Uemura, et al. demonstrated that WT mice injected with mouse α-Syn PFFs developed
neuronal α-Syn pathology within weeks after injection, whereas oligodendroglial α-Syn pathology appeared only after longer
intervals of several months. This important report should be cited and discussed, with attention to both the differences and
the commonalities compared to the model described in this manuscript. For example, at what time point did oligodendrocytic
aggregates emerge following α-Syn PFF inoculation in the model reported by Bae et al.?

4. The extension of survival and the improvement in grip strength of M83 mice by NM-101 treatment, as shown in Figures 3t
and 3u, cannot solely be attributed to the attenuation of oligodendrocytic pathology and neuroinflammatory responses, but
may also result from an improvement in neuronal pathology. However, no data are presented regarding changes in neuronal

antibody treatment.

1. (Regarding Review Comment #4-1) 
In the revised discussion, the authors stated that it remains unclear whether TLR2 is involved 
in autophagy in oligodendrocytes. Given this uncertainty, drawing the conclusion that 
intervention targeting TLR2 blocks the propagation of αSyn and subsequently ameliorates 
pathology may require further justification. The potential involvement of autophagy in this 
mechanism warrants further clarification. 
 
Response: We agree with the reviewer that the role of autophagy in synucleinopathy represents 
an important and relevant topic. To evaluate the potential involvement of autophagy in TLR2-
mediated neuron-to-oligodendrocyte propagation of α-synuclein, we examined the expression 
of autophagy-related genes in oligodendrocytes. Based on our transcriptomic analysis, in which 
oligodendrocytes were isolated using laser capture microdissection, the expression levels of 
autophagy-related genes in oligodendrocytes from wild -type (WT) mice were comparable to 
those in transgenic (Tg) mice. Furthermore, treatment with NM-101 did not result in significant 
alterations in the expression of these genes (Figure 1A, provided for reviewers only). 
To further assess whether TLR2 inhibition affects autophagic activity in oligodendrocytes, we 
performed co -immunofluorescence staining for LC3IIB, a marker of autophagic induction, 
together with TPPP, an oligodendrocyte marker. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, 
NM-101 treatment produced only a slight reduction in LC3IIB,  and LC3IIB levels in TPPP -
positive oligodendrocytes were not significantly affected (Figure 1B, for reviewers only). 
Although previous studies have shown that TLR2 activation can impair autophagy and promote 
protein accumulation in neurons and astrocytes,  our data suggest that a direct link between 
TLR2 signaling and autophagic degradation in oligodendrocytes remains inconclusive in the 
current experimental system. We agree that this question warrants further investigation in 
future studies.
