Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
COMMSCHEM-25-1215-T 
In this manuscript, the authors investigate the structural and dynamical properties of 22A-nanodiscs using an integrated
approach combining solution NMR spectroscopy, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), small-angle neutron scattering
(SANS), and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The major claim is that the central region of the 22A peptide forms a
relatively rigid, ordered belt around the lipid bilayer, whereas the terminal regions, particularly the C-terminal K22 residue,
remain conformationally disordered and solvent-exposed, potentially providing accessibility for interaction with LCAT and
thereby offering a structural rationale for the functional competence of 22A nanodiscs. 
Peptide-based nanodiscs are of broad and growing interest as alternatives to protein-based systems, and the multimodal
methodology presented here will likely be valuable for studying other membrane mimetics. Overall, the conclusions are
largely supported by the data, and the combined use of NMR, scattering, and simulations is a strength of the study, although
a clearer discussion of NMR observables (e.g., peak intensities), inclusion of additional NMR data in the supplementary
information, and comparison with previously characterized peptide nanodiscs would further strengthen the manuscript. With
these refinements, the work has the potential to influence thinking in the field by improving understanding of how peptide
sequence and terminal flexibility govern nanodisc structure and function, with implications for the rational design of peptide-
based nanodiscs. 
I have a few minor comments for the authors 
1) The introduction and discussion would benefit from citing more recent literature on various peptide nanodiscs. 
2) In the HSQC spectra, two distinct sets of cross-peaks are observed, differing substantially in intensity. Do these intensity
variations reflect specific peptide-lipid interactions or heterogeneous dynamics within the nanodisc? Such intensity
modulations have been reported for peptides interacting with binding partners (https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-018-0063-1). 
3) As the authors mentioned, “…room remains for improvement in experimental agreement…”, the results are not very
conclusive. Given that structures and properties of several peptide nanodiscs have been reported previously, the discussion
should explicitly compare how peptide sequence variations influence structure, rigidity, and orientation relative to the plane
of the lipid bilayer (https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.M511475200). 
4) The methods state that TOCSY and NOESY spectra were used for resonance assignment. These spectra, along with the
complete chemical shift assignment table, should be included in the supplementary information. Additionally, have the
authors compared NOESY spectra of the free peptide versus the nanodisc-bound peptide to probe peptide-lipid contacts
and peptide orientation relative to the plane of the bilayer, as reported for other nanodisc-forming peptides
(https://doi.org/10.1074/jbc.M511475200 & https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcis.2023.09.162)? 
5) In Figure 2A, the L21 (& L14) cross-peak appears overlapped with another signal. The presence of an additional peak is
unexpected, as there is no metabolic scrambling for synthetically prepared peptides. Authors should comment on this. It
would be helpful to include individual HSQC spectra of Leu-only-labelled and R7-A16-K18-labelled samples in the
supplementary information, along with 1D projections of the relevant 2D cross-peaks.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Manuscript by Nouri et al. reports the integrative structural study of peptide nanodiscs formed by 22A peptides. The authors
suggest an interesting approach, in which several MD simulations are used to back-calculate the average parameters, such
as NMR relaxation rates and scattering curves, and compare these parameters to the experimental values. Based on the
good agreement of experimental and simulated data, the authors conclude on the composition and size of 22A nanodisc,
and describe the parameters of 22A peptide packing in the rim of the particles. 
Although the authors claim their motivation was to facilitate the design of novel therapeutics, I believe the proposed
approach and the results are more interesting to structural biologists CryoEM microscopists, and NMR spectroscopists,
especially those who may consider using peptide nanodiscs as a membrane mimetic for their proteins of interest. Altogether,
the paper presents a novel approach and data that may provide a substantial improvement in the field of structural biology of
membrane proteins. In my opinion, the paper should be published in Communications Chemistry after some
corrections/additions. 
Nevertheless, I have several comments: 
1. I would suggest describing the whole concept of the approach in the first paragraph of the results question. Then it would
be clear why the authors perform these particular experiments. As it is written now, the motivation for doing the experiments
is sometimes unclear. 
2. Line 105, SEC retention time of ~13 ml, what is the molecular weight (or hydrodynamic diameter) that this volume
corresponds to? 
3. On line 107 authors claim: "but we also observed heterogeneity". How was this heterogeneity observed? According to the
authors, both DLS and SEC results correspond to the homogeneous solutions. This needs to be clarified. 
4. line 116: Why were these particular three residues N/C labeled? This should be clarified in the text. It would also be
helpful to have a sequence of the peptide in the results, at least in Figure 2. 
5. line 121: How would the authors estimate the nanodisc size from T1/T2, using the model-free approach? Would the
rotational correlation time from T1/T2 correspond to the rotational dynamics from the MD simulations and to the size
distribution from DLS? Can one also derive a size distribution from the TEM images? 
6. line397. What was the mixing time in the TOCSY experiment? 
7. As one can see, the authors measured a 1H-NOESY experiment. In this regard, it would be interesting to look at the
peptide-lipid contacts. Even if the lipids were deuterated, the headgroups are most likely still protonated and the profile of the
peptide-lipid contacts could additionally support the model of the nanodisc, proposed by the authors. 
8. line 353. The deuteration of DMPC (d54?) should be indicated in the materials and methods. 
9. Based on the MD simulation, what could be said about the lipid packing in the nanodisc, compared to the bilayer? Can
one observe a phase transition in 22A nanodisc? For example, by the 31P NMR, as described in the work
(https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.langmuir.7b03610).

Reviewer 1  In this manuscript, the authors investigate the structural and dynamical properties of 22A-nanodiscs using an integrated approach combining solution NMR spectroscopy, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), small-angle neutron scattering (SANS), and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The major claim is that the central region of the 22A peptide forms a relatively rigid, ordered belt around the lipid bilayer, whereas the terminal regions, particularly the C-terminal K22 residue, remain conformationally disordered and solvent-exposed, potentially providing accessibility for interaction with LCAT and thereby offering a structural rationale for the functional competence of 22A nanodiscs.  Peptide-based nanodiscs are of broad and growing interest as alternatives to protein-based systems, and the multimodal methodology presented here will likely be valuable for studying other membrane mimetics. Overall, the conclusions are largely supported by the data, and the combined use of NMR, scattering, and simulations is a strength of the study, although a clearer discussion of NMR observables (e.g., peak intensities), inclusion of additional NMR data in the supplementary information, and comparison with previously characterized peptide nanodiscs would further strengthen the manuscript. With these refinements, the work has the potential to influence thinking in the field by improving understanding of how peptide sequence and terminal flexibility govern nanodisc structure and function, with implications for the rational design of peptide-based nanodiscs. I have a few minor comments for the authors: 1) The introduction and discussion would benefit from citing more recent literature on various peptide nanodiscs. We have now added more recent literature to the introduction and discussion regarding peptide-lipid nanodisc formulations (lines 53, 55, 388-390).
