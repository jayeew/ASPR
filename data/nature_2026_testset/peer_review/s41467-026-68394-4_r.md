Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an interesting story that the zinc-based hybrid metal halides (ZHMHs), C4N2H12ZnBr4 and
C4N2H12ZnCl4, could be used as zinc ion solid electrolytes, because of their analogies to the halide lithium ion
electrolytes. The electronic structure analysis of these hybrids is ok to confirm the electronic insulation for the solid
electrolyte. However, I have some doubts about the migration mechanism for the ionic conduction and the electrochemical
process of the fabricated cells. To allow the publication of this manuscript without severe problems, I think the authors
should clarify the zinc ion conduction mechanism and real electrochemical reactions by providing sufficient evidence. 
Here are my specific comments: 
1. For the proposed concept of an atomic substitution strategy, I do not think it accurately reflects the central idea that the
authors intend to present. The modification of the halide solid electrolytes involves using the organic cations to replace some
inorganic species, which can not be conceptualized as “atomic substitution”. This inconsistency would make the readers
very confused about how advanced the proposed atomic substitution strategy. 
2. A heavy, uneasy concern comes from the ambiguous ionic migration analysis presented in this manuscript. Firstly, the
proposed Zn²
⁺
 migration occurs primarily through interstitial sites rather than the experimentally identified stable Zn-ion site
(4c site), which raises doubts about the validity and connectivity of these migration paths. Furthermore, the authors'
proposed mechanism of "defect-facilitated ion conduction" should be supported by more comprehensive data, either
computationally or experimentally. Lastly, given the presence of abundant halogen defects, the authors should
consider/examine the possibility of halogen ion conduction. 
3. The Arrhenius plot of PipZnBr4 in Fig.4e exhibits significant deviations from linearity, indicating a poor correlation
between the conductivity and temperature. Consequently, the derived activation energy of 0.11 eV (an unusually low value
for solid-state electrolytes) appears questionable. This anomalous result may suggest poor thermal stability of the material
and therefore additional temperature-dependent characterizations (e.g., thermal gravimetric analysis) are recommended. I
also suggest that the authors repeat this experiment with tighter temperature control (e.g., 5
℃
 intervals). If the nonlinearity
persists, a reasonable explanation for both the low activation energy and non-Arrhenius behavior should be provided. 
4. Besides the concern about the conduction of ionic species, the electrochemical process in the fabricated cells needs
careful analysis and more convincing evidence. It is necessary to identify the real conducting ions in the reported
electrolytes. A Zn2+ block layer for the impedance test should be employed. In addition, chloride based electrolytes should
also be tested in the symmetric cells and full cells. In particular, the charge/discharge curves of chloride-type electrolyte
based full cell can reveal the details of electrochemical reactions in the cell. 
5. There are many minor errors in the manuscript. For example, Fig.2j lacks the DOS data for Cl 3p; Line 231: an incorrect
full name of CI-NEB; Line 453: duplicate "PipZnBr4"; Line 463: incorrect activation energy value. These errors could be
avoided if the authors prepare the manuscript in a well-trained and multiple-checked way.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Hu et al. proposed an interesting design rule for Zn-based metal halide electrolytes by replacing inorganic atoms with
organic clusters. The hypothesis is that organic clusters may provide more abundant defect properties and thereby enhance
the kinetics of zinc-ion transport. They investigated the hypothesis both computationally and experimentally. Guided by
computational results, they synthesized PipZnBr4, which shows high conductivity. Overall, I found the work interesting and
relevant for the battery community but there are a few technical concerns that need to be addressed before being considered
for publication in Nature Communication. 
Technical concerns: 
1. Applicability of BVSE can be limited and material-dependent, the authors need to provide a discussion on the limitation of
the approach and how it can still be used. 
2. The author assumed that Pip is one “component” like Zn and Br/Cl when predicting chemical phase diagrams and defect
formation energy. But this assumption becomes questionable when the results of defect formation enthalpy for point D1/D2
were shown, especially the formation enthalpy is very negative (at the order of 1 eV) 
3. Related to point 2 above, the formation energy of C,H,N vacancies can be used to test if the assumption is valid. In
addition, what about C/H/N interstitials? Those can be charged defects and move the equilibrium Fermi energy. 
4. Since the band edge/gap correction is not included in the defect formation enthalpy, the uncertainty of the predicted
values is unknown and thus not strong enough to fully support the discussion. The authors need to either add the correction
to the results or discuss the limitation. 
5. One of the main conclusions from computational results is that PipZnBr4/PipZnCl4 can be good insulator based on the
large band gap and defect calculations. 
While 
the results of equilibrium Fermi energy being at least about 1 eV away from
band edges can support the argument, a more quantitative analysis on the number of electron/hole carriers as a function of
synthesis temperature can be a stronger support. 
6. For NEB calculations, how are the initial path determined? In other words, how can we know this is the minimum energy
path among all the possible pathways. 
7. “The broken tetrahedra leaves a low migration energy barrier for Zn ions in the material,…” It is unclear whether broken
tetrahedra is included in the NEB calculations. If not, the statement is not fully supported 
8. For XRD results, do the authors identify competing phases? This can provide a hint whether the material is near the
phase boundary, i.e. close to energy of the A,B,C points shown in Fig 2b. 
9. For the measured stability window, how does this compare to prediction (Fig 2b)? 
Related to point 2 above, this seems
the indicate the breakdown of the assumption that treats pip as one component. 
10. Why is the measured activation barrier (~0.11 eV) much lower than the calculated migration barrier? 
11. Here is a general question since I see some connection between this work and hybrid perovskite. Can the authors
comment on the stability of PipZnBr4 against water/moisture? 
Readability/Presentation: 
1. In abstract, the importance of Zn-based battery is unclear 
2. In abstract, the statement, “low energy barriers between organic groups and halogen tetrahedra”, should be more precise.
I understand this statement only after seeing Fig 3 i/k. I originally thought this means Zn diffuse between organic groups and
halogen tetrahedra. 
3. In abstract, minor grammar issue for the last sentence 
“… a new approach to designing ….” 
4. One general comment, the readership of nature communication is not limited to chemist. Some of the statements might be
straightforward to chemist, but further elaboration can be helpful for a broader audience. 
5. In introduction, “Zinc ion possesses a high valence charge and mediocre electronegativity, making the study of solid
electrolytes that utilize zinc ion transport as the primary conduction mechanism particularly challenging” 
the connection is
not fully clear. 
6. In introduction, “These two materials show good electronic properties…” The statement is vague 
7. For FigS2 and S3, the meaning of red and blue dots is not defined. 
8. “The localized charge of ionic bonds suggests that Li-based halides can conduct lithium ions” 
elaboration is needed. 
9. On the related topic to the point 8 above, Crystal Orbital Bond Index (COBI) analysis could be a good way to quantify the
covalency. (optional) 
10. How the authors created the interaction diagram is not fully clear in current format 
11. For many figures, color scheme for each atom is currently missing. 
12. On line 171, “The valence band maximum (VBM) mainly consists of the nonbonding states of Br 4p orbitals” 
This
statement can only be drawn with further details discussed later in the paragraph. 
13. “For PipZnCl4, due to the electronegativity of the Cl atom is more negative than that of the Br atom, leading to a
significantly higher anti-bonding orbital between Zn 4s and Cl 3p. The case results in a larger band gap (6.31 eV) for
PipZnCl4 than that of PipZnBr4 (5.37 eV)” This is a correct statement but might not be intuitive. 
14. Likely a typo in figure 2j, it should be Cl instead B 
15. “The Fourier transform spectra of the extended XAFS (FT-EXAFS) curves in R-space show that the bond length of Zn−Br
in PipZnBr4 is shorter than that in pure ZnBr2 (Fig. 6b), which suggests a relatively weaker Zn−Br bond in PipZnBr4.” Like a
typo since Zn-Br is longer in Fig 6b. 
16. In the Discussion, there is a typo “low activation energy (0.01 eV), ...” since the value is different from ones in the Results
section.

separator. I think it is better to clarify the Zn2+ ion conduction mechanism rather than showing the battery cycling under
limited areal capacity.

10. Why is the measured activation barrier (~0.11 eV) much lower than the calculated migration 
barrier? 
Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for  this valuable comment. We agree that this

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 53 -->

difference warrants careful discussion, and we appreciate the opportunity to elaborate on the 
potential reasons behind it. This difference may arise from fundamental distinctions between 
experimental and theoretical methods, as well as material-specific factors.  
Firstly, the calculated migration energy barrier is estimated using an idealized crystal 
lattice under periodic boundary conditions. This approach is standard for isolating the 
fundamental migration mechanism and its intrinsic energy barrier. However, in real materials, 
especially at elevated temperatures where diffusion occurs, the presence of multiple vacancies, 
vacancy clusters, or other extended defects is common. Secondly, the theoretical migration 
barrier was calculated by using the first-principles method at 0K.
 In contrast, the experimental 
activation energy is measured at finite temperatures . The temperature-dependent entropy and 
other factors are also one of the reasons for the inconsistency between theoretical and 
experimental values. Consequently, while the calculated barrier serves as a qualitative indicator 
of a material’s ion migration capability, it should not be directly compared to experimental 
activation energies in absolute values.  
In addition, t his phenomenon aligns with reports in solid- state lithium-ion electrolyte 
literature. For example, Li 2.75Sc0.75Zr0.25Cl6 exhibits a calculated migration barrier of 0.47 eV 
but an experimental activation energy of 0.33 eV (ACS Energy Letters, 2024, 9(3): 1043-1052), 
further supporting the discussed origins of such discrepancies.
