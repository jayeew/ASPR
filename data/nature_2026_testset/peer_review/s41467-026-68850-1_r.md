Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
While the study does show an interesting case of viral mimicry associated with colitis, a very interesting topic, there are a 
couple of concerns which prevent greater enthusiasm. 
In particular, what the authors term viral mimicry is a co-expression of transposable elements and other repeats with 
interferon and in the presence of demethylating agents. Its unclear whether the agents expressed are capable of engaging the 
pattern recognition receptors of the innate immune system. The agents most capable of forming dsRNAs are inverted repeats, 
which as Alus in humans and a subset of SINE elements in mice, or CpG rich satellite repeats such as HSATII. The authors 
should provide some more detail as to whether the specific repeats they see upregulated are likely to form innate agonists such 
as in here: 
https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.11.04.467016v2
Likewise the authors would need to show the interferon is induced by there repeats. In and of itself it is unsurprising that 
demethylating agents lead to repeat upregulations – elements such as active LINE-1 are known to be CpG rich. But there needs 
to be a clearer demonstration of causality over correlation. 
For what its worth repeat upregulation was found to be associated with HCMV induced colitis here: 
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30626867/ 
So in summary to move forward the authors would have to show the repeats upregulated likely engage PRRs and that such 
repeats directly lead to IFN upregulation to establish causality. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Larsen et al establishes a link that epigenetic changes (DNA demethylation) results in the ectopic 
expression of transposable elements (TEs) which otherwise would be remain silenced. They further propose that such 
ectopic TE expression induces a cell autonomous viral mimicry response through IFN signaling which inhibits cancer cell 
stemness and tumorigenesis. 
Using pharmacological and genetic in vivo mouse models as well as in vitro organoid cultures the authors show that 
experimentally demethylating DNA and thus TE elements by epigenetic drugs or genetic models increases TE expression and 
reduces tumor formation in mouse models of colitis associated cancer. 
The results in this manuscript are timely and most claims well supported by control experiments to exclude confounding effects. 
Moreover, the authors show, using multiple models, both genetical and pharmacological, a convincing link between DNA 
methylation, transposable elements and colonic tumor burden. However, it is the opinion of this reviewer that the link between TE 
expression and viral mimicry response, mediated by IFN is not sufficiently backed by data. The authors are requested to 
strengthen this claim.

Major points: 
 The general working hypothesis of the manuscript is unclear. The authors show that inflammation /colitis increases 
transposable elements (TE) expression, while at the same time the manuscript shows that TE expression serves as a tumor 
suppressive mechanism. However, inflammation (and thus TE expression) is strongly associated with tumor initiation and the 
authors themselves leverage this fact in all their models. (see also minor comment (6). The authors are therefore asked to 
clearly state their hypothesis and discuss why tumors, despite having abundant TE expression escape suppression via viral 
mimicry response. 
 While the sequencing data linking demethylation to increased TE expression is convincing, the direct link between 
increased TE expression and cell autonomous viral mimicry response (IFN) need to be strengthened, in this context 
in depth interrogation of JAK-STAT pathway activation in 5-AZA treated mice or the genetic models described (DNMT 
K.O.) is needed.

instead of absolute counts, as sequencing depth might be different among samples

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reported that activation of transposable elements elicits a viral mimicry response to foster a tumor 
suppressive function in inflammation. This viral mimicry, induced by DNA hypomethylation, inhibits tumorigenesis by 
regulating the stemness of cancer-initiating cells. This study is interesting and convincing, however, there are several 
fundamental questions need to be addressed or thoroughly discussed, such as, how to reconcile the results reported 
in this study with the inflammation-induced tumorigenesis model? What is the relationship between inflammation and 
TEs activation? How to distinguish the tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive functions of TEs? Specific questions or 
comments are as follow: 
1. The authors discovered patients and mice with colitis express TEs that lead to a viral mimicry response, inducing TEs by 
DNA hypomethylation in inflammation inhibits tumorigenesis. However, many studies have also found that TEs are activated 
in patients with colorectal cancer and promotes cancer progression (DOI: 10.1158/2159-8290.CD-21-1117,DOI: 
10.1038/s41467-022-31197-4). Does TEs play different roles in the process of tumorigenesis and tumor progression? Or

does TEs exhibit an inhibitory effect specifically on patients with colitis-associated cancer? 
2. Related to above, using a mouse model with AOM/DSS administration, the levels of TEs in the colonic tissue of C57Bl6/J 
mice treated with different concentrations of DSS and the vehicle, as well as in the tumor tissues, should be detected. 
3. How can we explain the phenomenon that patients who experienced IBD are more prone to developing 
colorectal cancer? When comparing patients with active IBD without TEs activation to those with TE activation, does 
the latter group exhibit a lower incidence of colorectal cancer? 
4. In Fig. 1 and Fig. S1, although the majority of TEs are upregulated, the expression of some TEs is 
downregulated in patients with active IBD. What accounts for the selective activation of a subset of TEs? And how 
do the expression of these TEs in another dataset, GSE230524? 
5. Compared with control patients, is there an enrichment of genes associated with viral or interferon signaling 
pathways in patients with active IBD from GSE230524 as determined by GO term pathways analysis? 
6. Is there an overlap between the TEs that are upregulated in patients with active IBD compared to control 
patients and the TEs that are downregulated in patients with IBD-associated dysplasia compared to patients with 
IBD without dysplasia? And why? 
7. The selection criteria for interferon response gene expression in Fig. 1 is unclear. Were these genes identified 
based on established databases? Why do the genes shown in Fig. 1A and Fig. 1C differ? In addition, why is there 
an inconsistency between the TEs and interferon response genes detected by the authors in Figures 2, 3, and 5? 
8. The authors have shown that DSS can induce the expression of TEs. Then, why is it necessary to use 5-AZA to 
further activate TEs? Does 5-AZA inhibit tumorigenesis by promoting inflammation to activate TEs? Is there a difference 
in the degree of inflammation between C57Bl6/J mice treated with DSS and those treated with 5–AZA in Fig. 2? 
9. The authors utilized multiple mouse models to demonstrate that DNA hypomethylation activates viral 
mimicry and suppresses colitis-associated cancer. However, the rationale for administering 5-AZA before DSS 
treatment remains unclear. 
10. The authors found the level of IFNα increases in the colonic tissues of Dclk1CreERT2;Apcf/f mice treated with 
5-AZA (Fig. 2B), while it decreases in the tumor tissues of Dnmt1f/f mice (Fig. 3H), What could be the underlying 
reason for this divergent phenomenon? 
11. the authors induced colitis in C57Bl/6J mice by treating them with 2.5% DSS in Fig. 2. 
Dclk1CreERT2;TdTomato;Apcfl/fl;Dnmt1+/+ mice were treated with 0.5% DSS in Fig. 3, and 1.5% DSS was used 
to in MAVS KO mice in Fig. 5. Why the DSS concentrations vary across mouse models? Any explanation? 
12. The authors found global 5-mC levels were significantly reduced in colonic epithelial cells from mice treated with 
5-AZA versus vehicle, which supports 5AZA suppresses DNA methylation. To further confirm this hypothesis, the 
percentage 5-mC (5-mC/total DNA) in colonic epithelial cells from Dnmt1+/+ and Dnmt1f/f mice treated with 
tamoxifen should also be examined. 
13. In Fig. 2A, it is indicated that DSS activates the expression of TEs. However, in Fig. 4B, it is found that DSS has 
no effect on DNA methylation. So, how does DSS influence the expression of TEs? 
14. Although 5-AZA caused DNA hypomethylation of repeat elements, the methylation in other regions is also regulated 
by 5-AZA (Fig. 4F). Therefore, the suppression of tumorigenesis by 5-AZA cannot be attributed to the activation of TE. 
15. The authors found that treatment with 5AZA reduced AOM-DSS induced tumorigenesis of wild-type mice in Fig. 
2. However, in Fig. 5, the authors discovered that there was no difference in the survival of wild-type mice with or 
without 5AZA treatment. Why? 
16. MAVS gene expression is increased upon 5-AZA treatment in WT mice (Fig 5F). Does the methylation level 
of the MAVS promoter decrease with 5-AZA treatment? 
17. In Fig. 3 and Fig. 5, the mRNA of DNMT1 can still be detected in the tumor tissues of DNMT1fl/fl mice. Why

18. The authors generated colonic organoids from Dclk1CreERT2;Rosa26TdTomato;Apcf/f and

dissociate the colonic DCLK1+ cells for organoid culture? 
19. The authors observed that the number of lineage traced crypts per colonic section is lower in 
Dclk1CreERT2;Rosa26mTmG;Apcf/f mice treated with 5-AZA compared to those treated with the vehicle in Fig. 6. 
Could this phenomenon be attributed to the inhibitory effect of 5-AZA on the proliferation of DCLK1+ cells? Please 
add the number and size of DCLK1+ cells per organoids over time treated with 5-AZA and vehicle. 
20. Can other drugs such as HDAC inhibitors that activate TEs also suppress the colitis-associated cancer? 
21. The authors found that DNA hypomethylation triggers a viral mimicry response to inhibit stemness of cancer-

In this revised manuscript by Larsen et al, the authors have addressed multiple aspects of the concerns raised in relation to 
the first version. In this paper, the authors demonstrate that colitis leads to activation of transposable elements which act as 
viral mimetics and then are reduced as the inflammation progresses to dysplasia. The central hypothesis is an interesting one

viral mimicry mediated by MAVS. In particular, more information regarding the type of TEs induced in human and in mice in 
colitis and the demonstration that the induced TEs are the cause of the interferon response. The authors addressed these

identifying the expression of those that are old and have degenerated compared to those which are young and mobile would be of 
interest.

inconsistency?

many subclasses, while others have unique patterns. If the patient samples are segregated based on TE expression – i.e.

The results in this manuscript are timely and most claims well supported by control experiments to 
exclude confounding effects. Moreover, the authors show, using multiple models, both genetical and

additionally provide new data showing that overexpression of TEs, namely LINE1 or SINE B2, in

/colitis increases transposable elements (TE) expression, while at the same time the manuscript shows 
that TE expression serves as a tumor suppressive mechanism. However, inflammation (and thus TE 
expression) is strongly associated with tumor initiation and the authors themselves leverage this fact in

We thank the reviewer(s) for these comments and apologize for the lack of clarity of our hypothesis. We 
have now revised our manuscript to make this clearer. To clarify, our hypothesis is that upon injury (i.e. 
colitis) TEs become re-expressed leading to a viral mimicry response that suppresses tumor initiation. 
Thus, for tumors to arise, we postulate that cells must overcome this tumor suppressive response (see 
model below). 
As previously proposed by others (i.e. see review by Lindholm et al. 2023, Trends in Cancer), our data 
are consistent with the idea that expression of transposable elements work as alarms for disruption to 
homeostasis. Thus, we see upregulation of TEs in patients with IBD and mice with colitis, whereas in 
those patients with IBD that have dysplasia, we see reduced expression of TEs and interferon signaling. 
Hence a viral mimicry response is beneficial in that it serves to prevent tumor initiation. In line with this 
hypothesis, we also show that MAVS-KO mice display increased tumor initiation again highlighting that 
activation of viral mimicry inhibits tumor initiation. Indeed, when we further activate a viral mimicry 
response by DNA hypomethylation, we observe inhibition of tumor initiation, and this is reversed by 
knockout of MAVS.

We thank the reviewer for these questions. The relationship between inflammation and TE activation is 
clearly not straightforward or well understood. Previous reports suggest that inflammation is often 
associated with epigenetic changes in methylation and/or histone modification, both of which are
