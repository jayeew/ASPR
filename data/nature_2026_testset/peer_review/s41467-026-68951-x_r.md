Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript introduces a nanotechnology-inspired strategy that combines ultralow CNT (0.1 wt.%) heterostructures with
gradient crystallinity engineering to produce scalable PCFs, and these fibers exhibit exceptional synergy between thermal
storage density and adaptive thermal regulation capabilities. The integrated experimental and MD studies elucidate three
synergistic strategies to optimize PCFs performance: 3D-IPNs confinement engineering, CNT-induced structural
modulation, and a scalable manufacturing paradigm. Furthermore, these fibers can be cut and sewing to PCM-based
thermoregulatory clothing that outperforms traditional polyester fabrics in thermal regulation, maintaining stable thermal
conditions under dynamic environmental temperatures, while demonstrating transformative potential for wearable systems in
extreme-environment applications and medical thermotherapy applications. Overall, the methodology is reasonable and
logical. Below are a few comments on your manuscript. 
1. In this manuscript, authors obtained C22/CNT fibers from primary fibers that underwent 3 stretching cycles, so how to
balance the relationship between the number of stretching cycles and mechanical properties of fibers? Will increasing the
number of stretching cycles further enhance the final mechanical properties of the fibers? 
2. On line 207 of page 9, “single-roll C22/CNT3 fibers (with a diameter of 200 mm and inner width of 56 mm)”, the diameter
should be length. 
3. On line 335 of page 13, “Free energy analysis revealed that CNT-induced nucleation barrier reductions in nucleation
barriers were attributed to decreases in interfacial energy and strain energy minimization”, could the author further visually
demonstrate how the interfacial energy and strain energy changes? 
4. Please check the reference citation format in the manuscript, for example, line 355 of page 15. 
5. Part of the description in Fig. 4 shows unclear font display. Additionally, what stage is the RDF distribution in Fig. 4i? 
6. What is the set stress strain rate in MD simulation? Is there any experimental verification? 
7. In the supporting documents, I think that the introduction to the subsections in section 1.6 is redundant. Additionally,
relevant references and reasons should be provided for the rationality of the selection of the force field. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript reports phase-change fibers by incorporating ultralow CNT loading into an interpenetrating polymer network
(HDPE/SEBS). The authors demonstrate high latent heat storage, enhanced mechanical robustness, and improved thermal
conductivity in the fiber. The work is interesting, combining experiments and molecular dynamics simulations, and
demonstrates potential applications in wearable thermal management. However, there are several concerns that should be
addressed before the manuscript can be considered for publication. 
1. In Fig. 2c, the authors assign the observed lattice spacing of 0.32 nm to the HDPE. Could the authors clarify which
crystallographic plane of it corresponds to? The reported d spacing is 0.32 nm, but the (110) and (200) plane of orthorhombic
PE have strong diffraction at d-spacings of around 0.41 nm and 0.37nm, respectively (e.g., Polymer, 2021, 212: 123164.). Is
it possible to be something else? 
2. The MD simulations in Fig. 3 focus on the interaction between n-docosane and CNTs, which demonstrates the
heterogeneous nucleation and crystallization behavior. However, the actual fiber system also contains HDPE and SEBS,
which may significantly influence molecular confinement and crystallization dynamics. Could the authors explain why these
polymer components were not included in the simulations, and discuss whether this might limit the accuracy or

completeness of the MD interpretation? 
3. The manuscript claims that the melt-spinning and multi-stage stretching processes largely influence chain orientation and
crystallization behavior of the fibers. However, the DSC analysis only compares fibers with different CNT loadings, without
showing the thermal response of the unspun precursor and the fibers without stretching. Including a comparison of enthalpy
before and after spinning and stretching would strengthen the mechanistic interpretation. 
4. While the manuscript claims 0.1 wt.% CNT as the optimal loading for performance (thermal stability, latent heat, and
mechanical strength), the orientation degree (Fig. 4d) and elongation at break (Fig. 4b) are highest with 0.05 wt.% CNT. It
would be helpful if the authors could further discuss why the optimal CNT content differs depending on the performance
metric. 
5. There are some typos and grammatical errors (e.g., “blew 40%” should be “below 40%”). In addition, the fonts in some
figures are too small (e.g., Fig 4a-d, etc.).

corresponding unambiguously to the inter-wall distance between the graphitic layers of 
the carbon nanotube. Therefore, the lattice fringes measuring 0.32 nm in Fig. R2c

4. While the manuscript claims 0.1 wt.% CNT as the optimal loading for performance 
(thermal stability, latent heat, and mechanical strength) , the orientation degree 
(Fig.4d) and elongation at break (Fig.4b) are highest with 0.05 wt.% CNT. It would 
be helpful if the authors could further discuss why the optical CNT content differs 
depending on the performance metric. 
Our response: We do appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful and constructive comments. 
The ideal loading is a relative concept, intrinsically dependent on the targeted property. 
This observed variation is fundamentally a manifestation of the competition between 
performance metrics in a multi-objective design space, where each property is governed 
by a distinct microstructural evolution pathway and reinforcement mechanism. 
Under lower CNT loading conditions (0.05 wt .%), CNTs are primarily individually 
dispersed, which facilitates their uniform distribution within the polymer matrix and 
allows then to be sufficiently wetted by the polymer. Thus, these individually dispersed 
CNTs can more readily align along the direction of shear or tensile flow during melt 
processing. The stretching process further facilitates the alignment of these well -
dispersed, low -content CNTs along the stretch direction, yielding a high degree of 
orientation. This high orientation, combined with good dispersion, is pivotal for 
efficient stress transfer.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 23 -->

When the CNT content increases to 0.1 wt.%, the intensified van der Waals interactions 
between CNTs promote the formation of local aggregates. This promotes the formation 
of a robust percolating network, acts as enhanced physical cross -links and thermal 
barriers within the composites, contributing to enhancing thermal stability and 
mechanical strength (e.g., higher tensile strength and modulus). Furthermore, the 
denser CNT network provides a sufficient number of uniform nucleation sites, 
promoting more perfect overall crystallization. As suggested by Valentini 1, the 
heterogeneous nucleation efficiency of CNTs does not increase linearly with their  
content but rather exhibits a saturation behavior. An insufficient amount (e.g., 0.05 
wt.%) offers too few sites to maximize this effect, while an excessive amount may 
hinder crystal perfection due to spatial confinement. Thus, although some local 
aggregation occurs, slightly reducing local orientation  degree, the network effect 
outweighs the detriment, delivering the best balance of strength, thermal properties and 
latent heat.  
In short, the 0.05 wt .% CNT loading represents the microstructural threshold for 
optimal dispersion, maximizing the “single-fiber toughening effect”, whereas 0.1wt.% 
activates the “three-dimensional network enhancement effect”, optimizing the trade-off 
between strength, thermal stability, and laten heat. 
For our target application – phase change fibers requiring reliable thermal performance, 
energy density, and load-bearing capacity – the 0.1 wt.% CNT loading was selected as 
the system -wide optimum. This choice reflects a design  strategy that prioritizes 
multifunctional performance over a single metric.
