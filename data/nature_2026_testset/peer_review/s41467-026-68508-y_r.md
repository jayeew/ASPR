(Remarks to the Author) 
Review Ncomms-25-56171 
Jia and colleagues report in this study on a Mycobacterium that was highly enriched in an acidic sulfide-removing bioreactor.
The authors report on simultaneous sulfide oxidation for energy generation and assimilatory sulfate reduction for
rechanneling surplus redox equivalents that would otherwise be available for toxic ROS synthesis in this organism. To proof
this, the authors employed a combination of controlled bioreactor operation, amplicon sequencing, metagenomics,
metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics, and chemical analytical analysis. Using these different methods, the authors show
along multiple evidence lines that Mycobacterium MAG 116 likely catalyzes this bidirectional sulfur metabolism under highly
acidic conditions (pH 1.0-1.5) and provide a logical explanation for this. These results are novel and relevant for
understanding principles of sulfur cycling. Nevertheless, the authors need to state already in the title and the abstract that
they observed this phenomenon under highly acidic conditions. I would also ask for a brief discussion that would rule out
abiotic sulfur conversions under the highly acidic conditions of the system studied. More room needs to be given already in
the introduction for how oxidative sulfur metabolism and ROS generation are interconnected. In parts, the manuscript would
benefit from more clarity in writing. Sometimes, it was hard to follow the lines of arguments.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Simultaneous sulfide oxidation and sulfate reduction for intracellular redox homeostasis 
Jia et al. presented the identification of a mytobacterial species MAG-M116, with the ability to perform sulfide oxidation to
sulfur and assimilatory sulfate reduction (ASR) under extreme substrate load. It is claimed that sulfide oxidation by bacterial
SQR supports sulfate reduction in a bidirectional manner: reduced quinone supplies electrons for the electron transport

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

chain, supporting energy production for ASR, and elevated QH2 also protomotes reverse electron transfer and thus
production of NADPH for downstream use as electron donor. It is concluded that the metabolic advantage of simultaneous
operation of sulfide oxidation (S-2 to S0) and sulfate reduction (S+6 towards S-2) lies in the management of elevated ROS
production from sulfide-induced RET and thus, maintenance of redox homeostasis under high-sulfide conditions. 
While the discovery of this bacterial organism with unique sulfur metabolic profile with redox implications certainly holds
value of scientific novelty, perhaps the study would be better suited for a more specialized readership than that of Nature
Communications. 
The experimental design is built around a bioreactor system which allowed exposure of the inoculated microbial material
with gradually raising, intense sulfide flux over a 235 day time period. H2S load was the only changing variable, which
extended from 40 to 230 g / (m3∙hour), corresponding to up to 1200 ppm of H2S, according to Fig. 1a. The authors should
have discussed the relevance of sulfide content of this magnitude, occuring in either natural or industrial settings. 
In any case, it was clearly demonstrated that with elevated sulfide flux increased in 3 phases, the exposed microbiome
underwent dramatic changes in speciation, shifting from Acidithiobacillus towards the Mytobacterium genus. Ultimately the
latter family entirely prevailed, suggesting the strong dependence of these organisms of high sulfide conditions and the
presence of functional advantages which help them cope with excess sulfide. MAG-M116 was isolated by its genomic
features to best represent the microbiome at Day 235. Most importantly, it has a full set of genes serving sulfate reduction,
but only SQR for sulfide oxidation (which is the first and rate determining enzyme of sulfide oxidation in mammalian cells).
Therefore, it is expected that the oxidative pathway halts at elemental sulfur, leading to its accumulation in the system
(confirmed by electron microscopy, X-ray and Raman-spectroscopy). 
Notes: 
• Fig. 2d does not appear in the Figure nor in the main text, but occurs in the legend. 
• Perhaps S8 would be a preferable denotion of elemental sulfur, S0 is widely used to label sulfane sulfur within reactive
sulfur species. 
Transcriptomic and proteomic screening implied that sulfide oxidation supports sulfate reduction through the production of
NADPH and channeling its electrons towards ASR enzymes. 
While the expression levels of the involved genes and proteins provide means to support the hypothesis of cooperating
sulfide oxidation and sulfate reduction pathways, in the lack of a viable culture and detailed knockout studies, the
experimental confirmation of the proposed mechanisms remains elusive. Furthermore, elevated cysteine production could
play a role in mitigating ROS, i.e. through increased glutathione synthesis or as a source for reactive sulfur species. This
angle should be addressed or at least discussed.

Journal scope and impact 
The authors presented compelling arguments on the justification of their choice of journal, highlighting the importance of
redox homeostasis on a cellular level, implications of ROS management (either from the production or the detoxification
angle) in disease conditions as well as industrial relevance in wastewater treatment. Furthermore, the added value in

Relevance of applied sulfide concentrations 
This was an important point and the authors supported their choice of the experimental range of sulfide load with adequate
literature. Perhaps a few of these references could be even included in the text. 
Minor notes were appropriately addressed. 
Purified culture studies 
I acknowledge the difficulties of the establishment of a mono-organismal culture of such extreme life-forms and the
application of appropriate experimental approaches to address this situation. 
Diminishing ROS and their connections with sulfide oxidation 
The authors made the deliberate choice to focus on the origin of ROS production in connection to sulfide oxidation rather
than post-production removal of detrimental amounts of ROS by sulfur species. They made an attempt to address both
angles in the manuscript. Although I wouldn’t find their added arguments (Lines 354-358 and 402-408) a comprehensive
discussion of the subject, considering the specific sulfur metabolic features of the investigated bacterial strain, it is
receivable that the production angle is deemed primary over cysteine mediated removal. 
The important point is that the sulfur metabolic network has multilayered interactions with oxidative stress, of which a
particular aspect is the prevention of detrimental (potentially lethal) ROS production.
