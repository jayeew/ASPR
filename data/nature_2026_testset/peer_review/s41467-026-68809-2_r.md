Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors use cryo-EM to uncover the precise details of interaction between the highly-conserved
bacterial transcription factor, MraZ (of Mycoplasma genitalium; Mge), and DNA. This is a significant advancement in the
field, and the new information fills a huge void in our knowledge. For context, the publication describing the crystal structure
of Mycoplasma pneumoniae MraZ was published over 20 years ago in 2004. Multiple reports have shown that MraZ of
multiple species recognizes a specific sequence (MraZ binding boxes) located on its own promoter sequence for
autoregulation. The specific residues needed for DNA binding have also been experimentally validated. Yet, how MraZ
binds to DNA has remained unknown. Given that MraZ is found in diverse bacterial phyla, the knowledge derived from this
work is likely applicable to other species as well. Thus, this manuscript is of broad significance. Overall, this is an easy-to-
read manuscript that has a logical flow. However, I have the following queries/concerns regarding data interpretation and
lack of alternative models - which dampened my initial excitement. 
Major Comments: 
1. Please include line numbers in the next iteration to make commenting easier. 
2. The authors suggest that MraZ needs to bind to all four binding boxes concurrently for efficient repression. However,
repression with only 3 boxes is still significant and a mutant unable to multimerize was shown to bind DNA and inhibit
transcription. Thus, why octamer needs to relax to bind DNA is puzzling. They make a point that octameric and nonameric
complexes cannot fit all four MraZ binding boxes. This may be true with synthetic setup with high concentration of MraZ that
has nM range affinity to DNA. Alternatively, this is due to authors prioritizing tetramer subtraction for refinement instead of
trimer. Additionally, this could be an artifact stemming from the length of DNA used in the experimental setup with multiple
high affinity MraZ binding sites in 2:1 stoichiometry. Basically 8 DNA binding sites (4 boxes in one DNA fragment)/protein.
Wouldn’t this ratio be the opposite in the cell? 
i. Why are there concatemers of DNA? Are there overhangs to promote end-to-end basepairing or different protomers of
MraZ from the same octameric complex are binding different DNA fragments? Based on the methods, it does not appear to
be the former. Please clarify. 
ii. Could they fit the octamer (or nonamer/decamer) with three boxes instead (using Fig. S4 data that they already have or re-
refining with trimer subtraction)? 
iii. MraZ-3BOX also forms the characteristic helical structure similar to 4BOX (Fig. S4). Repression is quite significant even
with 3 boxes (Fig. 2). Also, three boxes appear to be more conserved in many organisms (Fig. 1). 
iv. “Besides, the cryoEM complex structure maintains the interdigitated oligomeric helical interface between the four MraZ
subunits observed in the unbound MraZ octamer and nonameric rings.” Fig. S5 shows shift in side chains between chains A
and B. Is this shift identical between chains B and C as well? 
v. “In the final refined model, only three conserved DNA binding boxes could be correctly traced in the final cryoEM volume
after particle averaging, the fourth DNA box was not well defined, probably due to low local resolution or to volume
averaging of the DNA boxes containing non-identical sequences (Figs 5a,b).” Could this be because only three boxes are
used for binding? 
vi. “To further improve the map, we decided to focus on a single dsDNA molecule, corresponding to the 4-box promoter
region, binding a MraZ tetramer. To this aim, we generated masks to separate between the DNA ends in the helical
complex. Then, the tetramers present in the helicoidal segment in the map were extracted and merged in a single dataset.” Is
it possible the 4th MraZ extracted is binding to a different DNA fragment than the other 3?

vii. “Our experiments demonstrated that mutations in these boxes reduce binding affinity, indicating that each repeat
contributes to stable complex formation (Fig 2).” 
a) If the presence of 4 boxes is to simply increase the affinity of MraZ to the promoter sequence. Then monomer, dimer,
trimer, tetramer, and octamer species can all independently bind to the DNA and repress (as seen with the mutant that is
unable to multimerize). However, would all be equally efficient in blocking RNA polymerase (RNAP) when tested at
physiologically-relevant level in Mge instead of E. coli (in BL21 strain where MraZ and different mutants are highly
overexpressed)? 
b) In Fig. 2d, the gfp is under lac promoter and mraZ binding boxes allow MraZ binding. It is not clear where the -10/-35 sites
are with respect to the MraZ boxes. Could this be interpreted as the ability of MraZ to block RNAP? Simply the strength of
MraZ binding to only 1 box is insufficient to block RNAP. 
c) It is indeed surprising that the Kd is not severely impacted for the multimerization-minus mutant (still in the nM range).
What was the rationale for truncating the C-term alpha-6 helix? If this is due to “notable rearrangements” then perhaps that is
needed to sterically lock the octamer in place when DNA bound. The text in results section says partially, while discussion
says 14aa – which is the entirety of alpha-6 helix. If F36R/F52K mutant does not impair oligomerization (was this tested?),
perhaps removal of alpha-6 helix eliminates some sort of steric hindrance and allow multiple mutants to bind side-by-side to
the 4 boxes of the same DNA strand. Thus, when supplied at high enough concentration (Fig. 7b) this mutant is able to
saturate nearly all binding sites even in the absence of self-interaction. Is this what is happening? If so, is alpha-6 helix
promoting/allowing cooperativity. What happens with the truncation of alpha helices 5 and 6? 
d) Transcription rate of mraZ is controlled by MraZ protein level through autoregulation. Thus, instead of ON/OFF there is a
concentration-dependent tuning of this mraZ promoter. This can be achieved with different multimeric species of MraZ.
Could the authors calculate the Kd of WT MraZ with DNA containing 3 boxes, 2 boxes, and 1 box (similar to Fig. 7b)? Also
could the authors assess (or cite a publication if known) the intracellular concentration of MraZ in Mge? This may help put
things in perspective. 
e) MraZ is highly stable in M. pneumoniae (PMID: 33320415). Perhaps multimerization protects MraZ from proteolysis. If this
is also the case in Mge, it could be speculated that MraZ exists as an octamer to bind only to 3 boxes and not all 4 to weakly
repress when cells are actively growing to allow for ftsA-Z expression to support growth. And when cells need to halt
growth/cytokinesis - as authors speculate maybe accumulation of “cofactors or environmental signals” may then relax the
octamer/nonamer/decamer (or some nucleoid-associated factors bend the DNA?) to bind all 4 boxes to tightly repress the
promoter. 
3. Could the authors expand their work by say fitting the Alphafold model of Staphylococcus aureus MraZ to its promoter
DNA sequence (which lacks the fourth box)? This would be highly informative. 
4. This work convincingly shows that Mge MraZ inhibits transcription using a reporter assay in E. coli. However, could the
authors explain how MraZ binding to promoter boxes help transcription *activation* in Mycoplasma species? “Nonetheless,
in M. gallisepticum, it has been reported to function as an activator upon overexpression [14].”

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors determined the crystal structures of the MraZ octamer/nonamer and cryo-EM structure of MraZ-DNA complex at
3.36 A resolution. The crystal structures demonstrated the multimeric assembly of MraZ, while the cryo-EM structure
revealed specific interactions between the MraZ DNA-binding motif and DNA, in which residues K13/R15/R86 are
indispensable for recognition of the consensus sequence within its cognate promoter. MraZ is a DNA-binding transcriptional
regulator, highly conserved across most bacteria, and plays important roles in controlling cell division through transcriptional
regulation. Elucidating its transcriptional mechanisms is therefore of considerable importance. The experiments are
thoughtfully designed and competently executed. However, several aspects outlined below are insufficient for publishing in
Nature Communications. In particular, more definitive evidences are required to demonstrate that the cryo-EM structure
actually reflects the in vivo context. The reviewer recommends submission to a more specialized journal.

### Major concerns ### 
1. p.5/Fig2c 
The authors described "Moreover, when a longer spacer is introduced between the 4-boxes, MraZ is still able to bind
efficiently the DNA at high concentrations (Fig. 2d)." It would be preferable to clarify the mechanistic basis of this
observation. Does the DNA adopt a looped conformation, or do MraZ molecules assemble into multimers exceeding four
subunits? 
2. p.5/Fig2c 
The band intensities of the 4BOX and long-spacer constructs at 400nM do not appear equivalent. How were these
quantified? 
3. p.7/Fig4a 
In the cryo-EM grid, how were the 43-bp DNA fragments connected exactly in concatemers? Was the sequence precisely as
assumed? 
4. p.7/Fig4a 
Generally, DNA-transcription factor complexes dissociate readily during cryo-grid preparation. For instance, even
nucleosomes dissociate in a cryo-grid without crosslinking. In this study, the grid was prepared without crosslinking, yet the
DNA-MraZ complex was observed. This finding implies that DNA-MraZ interactions may be (artificially) stabilized through
filament formation, raising concerns about their physiological relevance. The authors should address this issue and provide
additional experiments to exclude this possibility. 
5. p.7/Fig4a 
The MraZ protein was finally purified in a buffer containing 250mM NaCl. While such conditions may be suitable for protein-
protein complexes, they are often unfavorable for protein-DNA interactions. Did the authors modify the buffer for DNA-
binding experiments or cryo-EM grid preparation? If so, this should be clearly described. 
6. p.7/FigS3 
In the panel showing masks, 11 subunits are divided into groups of 3 (cyan), 4 (green), and 4 (magenta). Does the green
mask truly correspond to BOX1-4? Given that 3.36 A resolution is insufficient to unambiguously resolve nucleotide
sequences, assigning BOXes to specific volumes appears uncertain. The cryo-EM map surrounding nucleotide bases
should be shown. Fig4c and 5d do not adequately support this discussion. 
7. p.7/FigS3 
The reported map resolution of the helical and tetrameric assemblies changed from 3.3 A to 3.36 A. Did this affect the map's
appearance? In the Method section, the authors described "This map showed significantly enhanced structural details of the
complex compared to the Cryosparc map of the larger helix, which seemed to have an overestimated resolution, despite a
similar global resolution." If so, the local resolution of the helical reconstruction should be presented. 
8. p.7/Fig4c 
For the cryo-EM structure of MraZ-DNA complex, how was the initial model of DNA built? Please describe the procedure. 
9. p.7/Fig4c 
Since the reviewer cannot access the map/cif, it is difficult to verify, but at 3.36 A resolution, distinguishing nucleotide
sequence is generally challenging. If sequence assignment is ambiguous, it would be more appropriate to denote base as
"N" rather than A/T/G/C. If the nucleotide sequence is indeed resolved, please provide the map and model around the
nucleotide bases. 
10. p.8/Fig6 
In the cryo-EM structure, recognition of nucleotide bases by K13, R15, and R86 is reasonable. However, the reported
interactions between the oxygen atoms of E80/D82 and the phosphate backbone oxygen atoms appear unusual. The
authors should carefully address this point. 
11. p.10/Fig7,FigS6 
The ΔCTD_F36R/F52K mutant was prepared to disrupt oligomerization. The reviewer is concerned that this mutation might
instead destabilize the tetramer. It would be preferable to assess molecular weights directly using SEC–MALS. 
12. p.10/Fig7 
The authors described "Interestingly, the ΔCMraZ-F36R/F52R mutant is also capable to bind DNA specifically". To
convincingly demonstrate specificity, it would be more appropriate to test protein-DNA interactions with point mutations in
the DNA sequence. 
13. p.10/FigS7a 
The plots suggest that the 2BOX interaction is weaker than the 1BOX interaction. The reviewer believes such experiments
are prone to variability. Repeated experiments and calculation of standard deviations are recommended. 
14. p.12/Fig8a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

The authors propose a mechanism in which MraZ oligomerization regulates DNA binding. For oligomerization to occur, a
minimum concentration is required. Is the intracellular concentration of MraZ sufficient to promote oligomerization? 
15. p.12/Fig8a 
For the helical state, repetition of 4BOX elements appears necessary. However, within cells, only a single 4BOX is present.
Please clarify how MraZ extends from a tetramer into a more elongated assembly under physiological conditions. 
### Minor comments ### 
p.4, l.11 
The authors did not clarify why "the mechanisms by which MraZ interacts with DNA promoter region and regulates

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a structural study of MraZ, a transcriptional regulator that controls the dcw operon, highly
conserved in most bacteria. The authors obtain the cryo-EM structure of the MraZ complex with the four-box promoter region
in dcw operon of Mycoplasma genitalium and complement the data with biochemical and functional analyses. But there are
several comments and questions that I would like to clarify. 
1) Promoter sequence specificity: The structure is shown for the promoter region of the four repeat boxes from M. genitalium.
Although this is a conserved pattern across species, the text should more clearly distinguish which conclusions can be
generalized to other bacteria, especially given the differences in promoter architecture between Mollicutes and model
organisms (E. coli, Bacillus, etc.). The discussion section should emphasize the limitations of extrapolation to a broad
spectrum of bacteria.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

2) In previous works MraZ has been shown to be a transcriptional repressor in E. coli and transcriptional activator in M.
gallisepticum. In this study most of the functional data were obtained in vitro (EMSA, GFP reporter in E. coli). The authors
demonstrate that MraZ from M. genitalium works as a transcriptional repressor in E. coli in genetic construct with Lac
promoter. How this finding corresponds to the function of MraZ from M. genitalium in cells of M. genitalium and its promoter
of DCW cluster? Can the particular function of MraZ depend on the relative position of MraZ boxes and core promoter boxes
(-10 and -35)? What factors can influence the dynamics of oligomerization in vivo? 
3) Although the Lys13/Arg15/Arg86 mutants result in loss of binding, the relationship between specific contacts and
functional activity in the mycoplasma cells requires further study. It would be useful to compare the effects of individual
mutations in the context of dcw regulation in vivo, not just in the GFP predictor system in E. coli. 
4) Authors demonstrate a remarkable and elegant mechanism of DNA recognition by MraZ ring-shaped oligomer. If an
octameric MraZ complex binds DNA as one monomer per one binding box. The core binding box of MraZ is very simple and
can randomly occur in multiple genomic loci. Is it possible to MraZ complex to bind more than one DNA sequence at once
and form DNA loops by cross-linking distant loci containing the respective boxes? 
5) On the Supplementary Figure 4 the authors demonstrate chain-like oligomers of ring-like MraZ complexes with DNA. How
long are DNA molecules used for these experiments and are they of the same length in 4-box, 3-box, 2-box and 1-box
experiments? How do these structures correspond to the model of side-binding of MraZ ring-like complex to DNA?
According to the side-binding model one 4-box DNA molecule bound to one ring-like MraZ complex. The data on
Supplementary Figure 4 clearly demonstrates that specific DNA (4- and 3-box) promotes formation of chain-like oligomers
from multiple ring-like complexes. How is it possible, if all boxes on the same DNA molecule are already bound by one MraZ
ring and no boxes remain to interact with other MraZ ring? 
6) EMSA images on Figure 2b and c, Figure 5f, Figure 7a show visible (sometimes bright) bands at the beginning of the
lanes. It seems that they are not formed by soluble nucleoprotein complex, but represent aggregates of (partially) denatured
proteins that stick to the well bottoms (and are unable to enter the gel). These aggregates appear frequently on EMSA
images. True nucleoprotein complex should form a clear band below the well bottom. There are some faint bands on some
images that can be interpreted as nucleoprotein complexes since they seem to enter the gel. However if the authors rely on
EMSA method to demonstrate nucleoprotein complex formation they should provide images with better resolution where the
bands formed by soluble complex can be clearly distinguished from the well bottoms. 
In addition, the numbering of figures 2c and 2d is reversed. 
7) CryoEM data analysis: the map reached 3.36 Å, which is reasonable for such a complex; however, there are areas of
incomplete localization of the DNA contour, including the third/fourth boxes. The impact of limited localization and possible
errors in model building should be discussed, as well as the potential for hybrid imaging (e.g., combining cryo-EM with X-ray
structure for individual subunits). 
8) The authors should more clearly formulate how oligomerization affects binding cooperativity, perhaps proposing an
experimental test of cooperativity with different lengths of the promoter region and in the presence/absence of DNA strands. 
9) Supplementary Figure3 in poor resolution. 
10) As a recommendation for improving the quality of the article, we would like to suggest: 
• Investigating the dynamics of MraZ interaction with DNA under different cellular stress conditions, including stresses that
affect the regulation of the dcw cluster. 
• Understand cooperativity: experimentally test the dependence of binding on the number of boxes and the distances
between them in different promoter constructs, possibly using single-molecule or FRET methods. 
• Consider evolutionary aspects: compare the structure and functionality of MraZ in different Mollicutes and non-Mollicutes
and discuss possible paths of evolution of the regulatory mode at the dcw level.
