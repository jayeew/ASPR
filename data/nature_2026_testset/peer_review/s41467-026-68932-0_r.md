Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work presents the design of a legged-aerial robot in which the legs are recruited to enhance jumping capabilities. 
The proposed design incorporates two two-link legs with parallelogram structures on each side and four vectorable thrusters
attached to the body. The legs are equipped with actuated wheels at the end, which assist during the acceleration phase of
jumping. 
The authors demonstrate experimental validation of the platform across several scenarios, including walking on rough
terrain, jumping, and wheeled locomotion. 
However, there are significant concerns regarding the control framework and the actual contributions of the mechanical
design: 
There is minimal evidence supporting the implementation and effectiveness of MPC. For instance, it is unclear how well the
prediction model captures the dominant dynamic behavior of the actual system. The authors do not present any comparison
between the model's predictions and experimental results—an essential step to justify the use of MPC. Without this
comparison, it is unclear why MPC was considered appropriate in the first place. 
A key technical question remains unanswered: What is the actual contribution of the legs to the jumping performance? Are
the legs actively contributing to jump thrust, or are they merely synchronized with body motion during lift-off without
meaningful mechanical impact? In other words, is this a legged robot that jumps, or is it essentially a drone that lifts off while
extending its legs? The manuscript provides little evidence to clarify this distinction. 
The paper is saturated with jargon, starting with the title. Phrases such as 'regulating aerial-phase dynamic precision' are
vague and difficult to interpret. 
Several pieces of data appear to be inaccurate. For example, Table 1 incorrectly states that Salto has no aerial regulation
capability, which is not correct. Salto regulates its roll dynamics using thrusters. 
Mathematical equations are difficult to follow due to missing symbols and unconventional notations. For example, in
Equation 20, variables are defined with arrows, which is not a standard. 
The figures are excessively dense and cluttered, making it hard to extract clear insights. Moreover, the manuscript includes
too many figures, which dilutes the clarity of the key messages. 
Overall, the paper does not provide sufficient evidence to evaluate whether the design enables meaningful collaboration
between the legs and thrusters to enhance jumping performance. The main contributions of the work are unclear and get lost
in the volume of information presented. 
Clarification and focused validation -- particularly around the role of the legs and the justification for using MPC -- are
essential.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a control method for robotic leaping in a hybrid robot that integrates quadrotors with jumping legs.
The core contribution lies in the design of a model predictive controller (MPC) for generating Time-Coupled Parabolic
Trajectories (TCPTs) during the aerial phase of motion. The manuscript includes a variety of experimental demonstrations,
including extremely high vertical leaps, lateral jumps with directional control, and traversal of moving obstacles. These
results are presented clearly and effectively. 
The paper is generally well written and easy to follow. The experimental section is particularly strong, with clear descriptions
and visualizations that effectively communicate the robot’s dynamic capabilities. The explanation of the controller design is
also easy to understand, making the manuscript accessible to a broad robotics audience. 
That said, the novelty of the work appears limited in both the hardware design and control methodology. From a hardware
standpoint, the concept of combining legged systems with thrusters is not new. Several prior works have proposed and
demonstrated similar hybrid locomotion platforms. Notable examples include: 
• Songnan Bai et al., An agile monopedal hopping quadcopter with synergistic hybrid locomotion, Science Robotics, 2024 
• Kyunam Kim et al., A bipedal walking robot that can fly, slackline, and skateboard, Science Robotics, 2021 
These works already established the feasibility and benefits of integrating aerial propulsion with legged mobility. Therefore,
the hardware presented in this manuscript does not constitute a significant advancement beyond existing designs. 
With regard to control, while the authors highlight their approach for enabling TCPTs as a key contribution, the
implementation essentially follows standard model predictive control techniques that track pre-planned reference trajectories
over time. The dynamics considered during the aerial phase are fundamentally those of a conventional quadrotor, and the
control problem is equivalent to trajectory tracking in existing aerial robotics literature—a problem that is well studied and
considered mature. Thus, the manuscript does not present a novel control formulation or fundamental advancement in
control theory. 
Moreover, while the authors claim that their controller enables reduced impact forces, higher jump heights, and more
predictable trajectories, these outcomes are largely expected when employing quadrotor control during flight. The
experimental maneuvers—although visually impressive—could likely be executed with quadrotor control alone, without
requiring the use of the jumping leg. 
In summary, while the manuscript offers a set of compelling experimental demonstrations with a hybrid aerial-leg robot, it
lacks a clear technical contribution in either hardware innovation or control methodology. For this reason, the reviewer
believes that the work does not meet the threshold for publication in Nature Communications, where novelty and
fundamental insight are expected. 
Minor Comment: In the vicinity of Equations (3), (4), and (5), the definitions of variables such as I, v, and Fb appear to omit
R(real numbers), which should be clarified for correctness and completeness. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary 
The paper introduces GravOff, a bipedal jumping robot that combines leg actuation with quadrotor thrust assistance to
simulate hypogravity conditions. It proposes time-coupled parabolic trajectory (TCPT) tracking for aerial regulation and
claims significant improvements in leap height and accuracy. While the work presents an ambitious hardware system and
demonstrates several high-performance jumping tasks, the experimental design and claims raise significant concerns. In its
current form, the work lacks rigorous comparative justification and overstates the contribution of the legs in what is effectively
a drone-aided aerial robot. 
Strengths 
- Interesting mechanical design: Inspired by bush crickets, the direct-drive leg mechanism is thoughtfully implemented with a
high leg extension ratio and a compact jumping mechanism. 
- Impressive leap feats: The robot demonstrates high jumps (up to 6.9 m), predictive leaping through moving windows, and
robust aerial tracking. 
- A comprehensive set of experiments includes wind disturbances, confined landings, and dynamic targets. 
Weaknesses and Major Concerns 
1. Unjustified Energy Comparison (Fig. 2C, 2D) 
The robot achieves high leaps by combining leg actuation with constant vertical thrust from four rotors. Yet it compares its
“leg energy” with other jumpers that use only leg power. 
This is misleading: the constant lift from rotors (simulating hypogravity) drastically reduces the energy required from legs. 
There is no reporting of total energy consumption, which is crucial to assess the full system's energy efficiency. 
2. Is this really a legged jumper or a drone with legs? 
During the aerial phase, the robot behaves essentially as a quadrotor with minor leg involvement. It tracks a trajectory using
MPC and controls attitude with PID, similar to drones.

The legs are largely dispensable after takeoff. This raises the question: what is the role of legs in overall motion beyond
launching? 
The introduction and discussion should clarify the necessity and contribution of legs—especially since many tasks could be
achieved with a tilt-controlled quadrotor. 
3. Core claims about hypogravity need deeper physical justification 
The paper claims that reduced gravity reduces the take-off speed and hence energy demand. While kinematically true (same

Since the robot must apply vertical thrust over the full duration of the aerial phase, this introduces substantial energy draw,

4. No comparison to existing quadrotor-jumper hybrids or multi-modal drones 
Prior work on drone-leg hybrid systems (e.g., flapping-wing assisted jumpers, or quadruped drones like FLaSH) should be

5. Overclaiming precision and control performance

There is no noise or uncertainty modeling in MPC nor any robustness bounds in the presence of actuation delay or ground

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The readers of this work expects to see two substantiated results, as I also stated in my first review: (1) if MPC is used, what
is the accuracy of the prediction model? and (2) what is the contribution of the legs (i.e., what makes this robot different from
a quadcopter). 
I specifically looked for these results throughout the draft but was unable to locate them. 
My first concern relates to the prediction model. Fig 5-b shows reference (commanded) versus predicted trajectories during
the flight phase, while Fig 5-g shows reference and trials but not prediction model versus trials. The prediction model error
has to be directly compared with the trials. The point of this comparison is to demonstrate MPC effectiveness, since MPC
relies on the prediction model to control the actual system. A straightforward way to establish this is to record open-loop
trajectories of the physical robot and compare the robot states with the prediction model states. 
My second concern relates to the role of the legs. Fig 3 shows energy-based analyses of leg and thruster actuators, but this
analysis alone cannot reveal meaningful insight. For example, leg actuators can be high energy in terms of being high
torque at low speed (as is typical of off-the-shelf servos) while still being incapable of producing the high-torque, high-speed
movements needed for dynamic jumping maneuvers. Future readers of this paper will expect to see clear evidence that this
robot is not merely a quadcopter. At present, no technical results demonstrate whether the system is capable of jumping
without thrusters. 
A simple experimental setup could address this: remove the propellers (not the brushless motors), constrain the robot’s
motion in all directions except Z using a simple jig, then perform trials with leg activation only and record the resulting jump
trajectories. If the legs contribute meaningfully, the robot should achieve some measurable jump. Leg versus thruster
contributions could then be quantified, for example, by comparing the body-length height achieved with legs alone versus
legs plus thrusters. 
My other concerns remain as well too. The terminology needs careful reconsideration. Phrases such as “vector thrust MPC”
or “vector-thrust-based jump” are not standard. Perhaps the intended terms are “thrust-vector-based” or “thrust-vectoring-
based.” There are several other examples throughout the manuscript where terms are unclear or unconventional, and these
should be revised.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
While the authors have added an extensive energy analysis, the fundamental issues remain unresolved. The claim that
E_leg is less than 10% of the total energy and thus the legs are "efficient" is misleading. This overlooks the indirect impact of
the legs on the system. 
The legs significantly increase system mass, aerodynamic drag, and inertia. These factors directly increase the energy
consumption of the rotors (E_rotors), which are required to generate and maintain hypogravity. Therefore, E_leg cannot be
interpreted in isolation. A small direct energy input from the leg actuators does not imply a small contribution to overall
system energy consumption. 
Moreover, the LP/GP formulation (where LP + GP = 1) is not a valid partition of energy. LP is derived from normalized takeoff
velocity squared, and GP is defined as normalized gravity reduction. These are not additive energy terms and cannot be
used to infer energy tradeoffs in the way presented. 
The energy comparisons with drone-only flight are also not properly controlled. The authors compare against drone modes
with different masses and thrust configurations. A fair comparison would require same-mass, same-hardware baselines with
and without legs. 
Finally, the justification for the specific leg design remains weak. There is no evidence that this complex 3-DoF architecture
provides benefits beyond simpler alternatives. Most control and trajectory tracking are performed by the rotors, and the role
of the legs beyond takeoff is limited. 
In summary, the energy analysis is conceptually flawed and does not support the claimed efficiency or necessity of the leg
design. A valid system-level energy breakdown, with controlled comparisons, is still missing.

(or height) upper bound is restrained by the limit launching velocity resulted from the propulsion of the leg
mechanism. To gain broader leap range and conquer larger obstacles, the legs should be designed with satisfactory
launch speed. The design of the legs draw inspiration from the low-inertia leg mechanism of bush crickets, who
feature high extension ratio (e.g., in literature [1], the leg extension ratio (hip-foot length at extension / hip-foot
length at contraction ) is estimated to reach more than 400 %) and powers their jumps with a long acceleration phase
via the direct action of muscles acting on long levers[1]. For long acceleration duration, the presented robotic legs
were designed with an extension ratio of 340%, driven by a 2-DOF compact actuator where the majority of the mass
are near the hip(Fig. 1D, 2A), to ensure low inertia of the legs. The two DOFs in each leg actuator enables the foot
trajectory to be arbitrarily assignable (within its working space).
T o enable both longitudinal and lateral jumps, the robot is designed with a bipedal configuration. In the longitudinal
direction (y
J-direction in Fig. 4B), the bipedal configuration provides two supporting points such that the launch angle
rotates only in one axis (i.e., around x J), while for jumps in the lateral direction (x J-direction), the two legs can provide
phase difference during the controlled extension process and enables the robot to jump sideways (as shown in lateral
jumping experiments in Fig. S8 ).
Role of legs beyond takeoff:
For robotic jumping locomotion, we agree that the role of the legs beyond takeoff is meaningful, since effective
jumping motions should also pay attention to the landing phase, which is crucial for mechanical safety and stability
upon reaching the target regions. Beyond takeoff, our work also demonstrates legs’ active activation upon the landing
phase. Specifically, we used a contraction speed control scheme estimated from jumping apex height of each parabola
(see active damping methods presented in supplementary section S8 ). This is involved in our experimentation with
inspiration from the leg contraction movement observed in various biological examples, such as humans, non-human
primates, cats, and toads, who have demonstrated that pre-activation of limb muscles is an essential part of the
neuromotor control strategy to ensure safe landing(e.g., [2], [3],[4]).
Still, legs activation upon landing is insufficient in literature studying robotic jumping. Many existing jumpers rely
their legs solely for takeoff, e.g., catapult-based jumpers such as Tribot[5], JumpRoACH[6], Moobot[7], EPFL
jumpers[8], TAUB[9], MSU jumper[10], Flea-inspired catapult jumper[11], or Pogo-leg hybrid jumpers such as
Hopcopter [12], Flapping-wing-assisted hopper[13], PogoDrone[14], PogoX[15]). These jumpers exhibit direct
mechanical impact upon the landing phase, stable yet bounce-free landing using leg activation is rare in robotic
jumping literature.

Rationale of the 3 DOF design and application benefits over simpler designs:
When we design the leg mechanism, several requirements and goals of motion for field operations were considered:
(1) The robot must be able to locate a desired launch position with adjustable heading direction, when overcoming
large obstacles.
(2) The robot should be able to adapt to some degree of une ven obstacles or terrains if not obstructed by large
barriers.(e.g., locomotion on grassland, cobbles, etc), with omnidirectional locomotion capability, similar to those that
can be overcome by Wheg[16] mechanism.
(3) On simpler terrains, like flat ground, the robot is expected to be wheelable for better efficiency than jumping.
There exists several simpler alternatives of jumping mechanisms and they can be usable for the hypogravity jumping
modality. E.g., pogo-stick (for rebound-based jumping)(used by Hopcopter[12], flapping-wing-assisted hopper[13],
PogoDrone[14], PogoX[15]),1-DOF screw-slide mechanism or 1-DOF four-bar planar mechanism, as demonstrated in
Figure R13.
Figure R13. Potential simpler alternatives for the leg design of the GravOff robot.
While simpler leg designs may be adequate for unidirectional launching, these lower-DOF configurations are
insufficient to meet our locomotion objectives in field operations without the incorporation of additional devices (e.g.,
actively driving wheels for wheeling on flat ground, or whegs[16] for crawling). For example, JumpRoaCH[6] utilizes
six additional whegs to endow the jumping robot with crawlin g capability, in addtion to its jumping mechanism (1-DOF
four-bar leg); SNU jumping-crawling robot[17] similarly a dds four whegs to enable crawling capability along with 1
DOF six-link jumping leg; Moobot[7](1-DOF four bar leg) uses extra eccentric motor to adjust heading direction.
Harvard multimodal jumping-walking robot[18] employs four additional legs for walking capability along with its
catapult jumping mechanism.
In our design, we aimed to realize the locomotion objectives (1)-(3) by fully exploiting the bipedal configuration with
minimal additional devices. The robot realizes the crawling and wheeling motions by only adding one active DOF (for
hip rotation; driven by a servo weighs 5.8 grams) per leg.
