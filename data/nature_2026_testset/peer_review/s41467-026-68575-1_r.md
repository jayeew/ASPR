Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study focuses on the identification of ClBarrel, an outer membrane protein from Candidatus Liberibacter (Cl), the
bacterium responsible for Huanglongbing (HLB), a devastating citrus disease. Cl is transmitted to plants through insects,
particularly psyllids such as Diaphorina citri, which deliver the pathogen into the plant phloem. The bacterium disrupts
assimilate transport, leading to wilting in the host plant. However, less is understood about how the vector insects
themselves recognize bacterial surface patterns. While extensive research has explored insect innate immune activation,
particularly in Drosophila, the mechanisms by which vector insects respond to pathogens are still poorly understood. 
In this study, the authors present a rare example of direct recognition between ClBarrel and the ectodomain of a newly
identified Toll-like receptor (TLR), TLR8, from D. citri. This discovery demonstrates that insects can activate innate immune
responses to plant pathogens that they transmit, facilitating the pathogen's journey into host plants. Upon ligand binding,
TLR8 undergoes dimerization and recruits the co-receptor MyD88, initiating a signaling cascade. This leads to the activation
of IκB kinase IRD5, which in turn activates the transcription factor NFAT. This signaling pathway mirrors canonical innate
immune responses, and in this study, the authors identify the D. citri homologs analogous to those found in Drosophila. 
The most compelling part of this research lies in the second key finding: the bacterial effector SDE3230. This effector is
produced by Cl and functions to suppress the insect immune response. It does so by ubiquitinating and degrading MyD88
through the proteasome, preventing the activation of immune responses that could otherwise harm the pathogen. This
discovery highlights an intricate interplay between immune activation and suppression as the pathogen moves from the
insect vector to the plant. 
As a plant immunologist, I am particularly excited by the extensive data supporting the proposed model and the quality of the
research. While I am less familiar with the literature in insect innate immunity, based on standards in our field, this study
represents an outstanding contribution to the scientific community. I have no significant critiques but would suggest
simplifying the writing slightly to improve accessibility for readers less familiar with the topic.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The reviewers raised several concerns that the authors addressed with additional experiments, clarification, and revisions to
the manuscript. Reviewer 1 provided the most extensive and technically detailed critique. One of the main questions
centered on why only Toll8 was studied, rather than other members of the Toll family. In response, the authors conducted
yeast two-hybrid assays for Toll1, Toll6, and Toll7, demonstrating that none of these interacted with Barrel. This additional
work supported the specificity of the Toll8-Barrel interaction. Reviewer 1 also requested broader screening for potential
CLas membrane proteins that might interact with Toll receptors. The authors expanded their assays to include nineteen
CLas membrane proteins and confirmed that Barrel alone binds to Toll8. 
Another larger issue concerned the immunogold localization shown in Figure 1e, which the reviewer found unclear and
lacking proper controls. The authors repeated this experiment, providing new micrographs that clearly demonstrate
membrane-specific labeling and including appropriate negative controls. Reviewer 1 further questioned the mechanism of
Toll8 activation, suggesting that ligand-independent activation should be considered. The authors revised the Discussion to
acknowledge this possibility but emphasized that their data support Barrel-induced activation as the biologically relevant
mechanism. The reviewer also asked why only MyD88 was examined among potential adaptor proteins. In response, the
authors justified their focus on MyD88 as the universal Toll adaptor in insects and expanded the discussion to address its
evolutionary and functional significance. 
Several reviewers sought additional detail on downstream signaling components, including MyD88 and IKKE. The authors
added two supplementary tables listing all identified interactors and clarified their decision to focus specifically on IKKE and
NFAT as central regulators of Toll signaling. Reviewer 1 also requested transmission assays to evaluate the functional
consequences of gene silencing. The authors performed new experiments showing that silencing of IKKE, Reeler, or NOS
increased CLas transmission by adult psyllids, but I will address this in more detail below. 
Reviewer 2 expressed support for the work, noting that the study was of high quality but could benefit from improved
readability for a broader audience. The authors responded by reorganizing the Results section for clearer logical flow and
simplifying complex sentences. Reviewer 3 requested several minor clarifications and corrections. These included
questions regarding gene nomenclature, which the authors addressed by clarifying phylogenetic relationships and adopting
the more accurate name IKKE in place of IRD5. Reviewer 3 also noted inconsistencies in NFAT localization, prompting the
authors to replace the relevant figures with consistent and better-quality images. Concerns about potential alternative
ubiquitination sites on MyD88 led the authors to perform additional mutagenesis experiments, confirming that lysine 48 is the
principal site of modification. 
In my view, the authors have successfully addressed the primary concerns raised by the reviewers through the inclusion of
new experiments, more detailed methodological explanations, and targeted clarifications. They also improved figure quality,
standardized terminology, and revised the manuscript to enhance clarity and readability. However, upon reviewing the
revised version, one additional issue has come to my attention—an issue that was not present in the original draft but
appears to have emerged during the revision made in response to Reviewer #1. 
In the initial round of review, Reviewer #1 noted that although the authors had measured IRD5 (also referred to as IKKE)
expression, phosphorylation, and CLas titers following gene silencing, they had not conducted transmission experiments to
assess whether these molecular changes affected the psyllids’ capacity to transmit the bacterium to host plants. The
reviewer emphasized that such transmission assays are essential, as they provide the most direct functional evidence
linking the identified candidate proteins to CLas transmission and should therefore be included in the study.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

In response, the authors agreed with the reviewer’s point and explained that they subsequently performed transmission
assays using adult D. citri, which are the primary vectors of CLas under natural conditions. They noted that although nymphs
efficiently acquire the bacterium, their limited mobility makes them less relevant to transmission dynamics. Citing previous
studies showing that adults transmit CLas more effectively than nymphs, the authors justified focusing on the adult stage.
They reported that, in these new experiments, silencing IKKE expression with dsRNA significantly increased CLas
transmission efficiency by infected adult psyllids. This result, according to the authors, supports the conclusion that IKKE
plays an important role in the psyllid’s immune defense against CLas. They also indicated that full methodological details
and results have been incorporated into the revised manuscript in the Results, Materials and Methods, and supplementary
figures. 
I commend the authors for addressing reviewer comments and for conducting additional inoculation assays. However, I
remain skeptical that ‘standard’ cited methods, based on the citation of Ammar et al. (23), would have been sufficient to
accomplish this experiment. In all published transmission assays to date involving CLas and D. citri, whole-plant
inoculations are employed, and leaf tissue is typically analyzed at 1, 3, 6, and 9 months post-inoculation. Depending on the
methods used and specific investigation, plants generally begin to show signs of CLas infection around 3 months after
inoculation as detected by qPCR. 
Several laboratories have attempted to use single-leaflet assays, as described in this manuscript, to accelerate data
collection; however, to my knowledge, such efforts have not been successful and/or previously published. The DNA yield
from a single leaflet is generally insufficient to reliably detect CLas DNA just 7 days after a 7-day inoculation access period
by CLas-infected adult psyllids when using “standard” extraction and qPCR methods such as those described by Ammar et
al. or in similar published studies not cited here. 
Therefore, I recommend that the authors provide a more detailed description of their DNA extraction and subsequent qPCR
methods associated with the transmission assays rather than just citing ‘standard methods’ for rapid single leaflet assays.
While the manuscript reports several notable findings, a genuinely rapid method for evaluating CLas inoculation within just
two weeks would itself be a significant contribution to HLB research and thus warrants a more thorough explanation.
