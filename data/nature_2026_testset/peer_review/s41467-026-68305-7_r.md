Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Wei et al describe the first single cell sequencing atlas for S. japonicum—the human-infective Asian blood fluke. This is a
really impressive study; with approx. 100k cells it’s the largest single cell atlas produced in schistosome research—creating
a rich new resource for understanding schistosome development. 
The high cell coverage and inclusion of intermediate stages of development has enabled the authors to produce high
resolution data on the sexual-tissue development, uncover distinct neural lineages and reveal novel biological insights into
sexual signalling/development in schistosomes. 
A key highlight of the work is the discovery of a male-specific neural circuit that directly mediates female development. The
discovery builds upon previous research in S. mansoni demonstrating that nrps in males produces the pheromone BATT,
which is essential for male-stimulated female development. The authors report a neural cell type (“delta-3”) that expresses
nrps, and the S. japonicum homologue of lsamp, which expresses a neural surface protein in other species. The authors
functionally characterise lsamp, confirming its expression in delta-3 cells and showing that it is required for BATT production
but affects neither delta-3 development nor nrps expression. They further show that lsamp expression is closely colocated
with the axons and synaptic junctions of a ventral neural network. 
Knocking down lsamp disrupts this network. 
The authors
suggest that lsamp is 
required for males to produce or maintain neural fibrils. 
Overall, this is exciting work that deepens our understanding of sexual signalling and development in these obligate
dioecious parasites. The work is highly significant within the parasitology field and will be of broader interest for
developmental biologists. 
It is a large body of work and, in places, the manuscript is currently hard to navigate. There are some sections that may
benefit from simplifying/streamlining, including some analyses that didn’t seem strictly necessary. We also felt that there
were aspects of the functional characterisation, where the authors have over-reached in their interpretations. 
*Specific comments - Major points* 
To validate clusters (S. Fig 3), some of the hybridization patterns are unclear. Presenting evidence for the more diffuse
tissues is difficult. 
Nevertheless, ctsb, tpm2, syn2 do look very similar. 
Describing the proportions of cell types is quite prominent in the manuscript (incl. Figs 1 c and d), but how informative is this
analysis, beyond an extremely crude look? 
Sorting, library prep etc. creates bias for certain cell-types (e.g. more round cells
like stem cells are favoured by sorting) and can distort recovered cell numbers. 
In previously published works, actual cell
abundance vs cell captured by the scRNAseq process differ wildly, affecting differing cell types unequally. 
Some of the
numbers make sense, like the expansion of vitellaria in females, but others make less sense like the much higher
abundance of flame cells than gut cells. There’s a vague/superficial statement, which could be removed to save space,
about there being ~20% neural and neoblast cell types when the numbers actually differ quite markedly between timepoints.
Abundance across the whole dataset is shown in S Fig 5; yes it does broadly confirm some expectations but it doesn’t really

shed any new light. And some of the findings seem counter-intuitive: like the proportion of muscle cells from males. 
The authors performed sub cluster analysis on sexual tissues and neoblast populations, from which they define 17 sub-
types. 
The rationale for their naming scheme is not clear and abbreviations need to be explained. A discontinuous series
from the Greek alphabet, plus some numbering (i.e. alpha, beta, gamma-1, gamma-2, delta, epsilon, kappa, nu) is used.
Previously, a similar scheme has been used in published descriptions of S. mansoni stem cells (see
doi.org/10.1016/j.pt.2021.03.005). 
It’s not clear if published delta, epsilon, and kappa sub-types are equivalent to those
reported here. Distinguishing between 17 populations in the figures (S Fig 6a and Fig 2) is also challenging. Perhaps a
numerical key could be added to help readability? 
RNA velocity analysis was performed on the sex related subsets, revealing trajectories (Fig 2b) that made sense like the
GSC -> GSC progeny in both sexes and the S1 vitellocytes ->->late vitellocyte. There are also a lot of arrows converging on
the centre (SSC beta?) that aren’t mentioned. Further subsetting (Fig 2c) reveals trajectories from GSC to gametes in males
but the equivalent analysis is not at all convincing in females (contrary to the main text). In Fig 2c the four panels show a lot
of redundant information and could be reduced to two panels. 
There’s quite a long section on clustering male and female specific genes. The authors reveal a cluster of genes with
opposite temporal patterns between the sexes. It’s also interesting to note that for the majority of sex-specific genes, the
temporal pattern is different between the sexes, with male genes reducing and female genes increasing. However, some of
the methods and figures in this long section are difficult to navigate and it’s disappointing that the section is left quite open-
ended with no further analysis of the 47 C1 genes. The overall conclusion is that the process is complex and involves
modules of genes. 
Without the composition of the modules being described, it doesn’t really provide anything new. 
The main text (L201+) and legend for Fig 7 really need signposts to the relevant section of Methods because it’s not
immediately obvious that trajectories are the input for the sex-specific analyses. Even after the gam R description is found,
it’s still unclear exactly what the inputs are and how the lists of 693, 750 and 156 relate — are they mutually exclusive? If not,
overlaps should be indicated. In Fig 7b, pseudotime=0 is presented in the centre, so that one panel reads right-to-left and the
other reads left-to-right; that seems counter-intuitive. How were the datasets clustered? It looks like the cluster number was
set to 5 in each case, but it’s not clear why, when several clusters could have been further divided and some possibly
merged. What is the dotted line on each cladogram? 
After the module analysis, the narrative returned to trajectory analysis using RNA velocity. The authors were unable to
connect stem cell development to vitellaria development but were able to show trajectory from S1 vitellocyte to S1 progeny,
with the latter marked by esg-2a expression. The paragraph finishes with a rather bland statement that the work aligned with
previous findings and provides new insights. 
Vf1 expression was known, but the esg-2a expression later in differentiation is
new. 
What else switched on later? 
Having established a convincing trajectory, was it not possible to mine further information
on genes expressed early vs late? 
Likewise, the analysis of Mehlis gland is interesting but only two strong markers are
shown in Supplementary Figure 6. 
Could no other markers be found? 
The authors subset the neuronal data and recluster into a highly connected UMAP showing at least 5 lineages. This is an
important part of the reference resource created by the project. However, the authors should maybe avoid using
nomenclature based on Greek letters, given that it is already used for stem cells, including within the same paper. 
How
about just numbering them? Over time, more functional names will be applied but, for now, it might suffice to name them as
neural lineage 1, etc. 
Or maybe use N1, etc? 
The more granular names like delta-3 would then become N4.3. 
The authors use Slingshot to define lineages and derive pseudotime from their neural UMAP. How well does the
pseudotime derived from these Slingshot trajectories agree with the sample-time metadata? (does a UMAP coloured by
sample show differential enrichment for earlier or later parts of the trajectories?). 
The subsequent RNA velocity analysis
agrees well with their pseudotime predictions, and Cell Rank is used to define terminal states (fully differentiated cells?).
Similar to the earlier section on gamete development, the signposting to the methods is not very clear in the text - it’s not
immediately clear the purpose of each analysis until reading further into the paper. Cell Rank is not mentioned in the legend
to S Fig 10 and “dpt pseudotime” is not explained. The PAGA analysis, resulting in a network of pie charts (Fig. S10e and
11e), isn’t clear. What exactly is this showing? 
Is it really needed? 
Regarding the section on male-specific neurons: in schistosomes, a large number of genes are present in sex-specific
regions (~900 in S. mansoni) and, given their incomplete dosage compensation between sexes, those sex-specific genes
can skew the relative abundance of all other transcripts. Did the authors check whether the clustering was influenced by
genes in those regions? 
If it is, there could be distinct clusters that are expressing the same genes but just differ in the
expression levels across their sex-specific genes. 
Beta-2, gamma-2 and delta-3 neurons are described as ‘male-specific’ (Line 291), but the markers for two of these
populations (beta-2 and gamma-2) were then detected in the heads of females (Line 304). Was this a sensitivity issue of sc-
RNASeq vs. WISH? Is it really appropriate to describe beta-2 and gamma-2 neurons as ‘male-specific’? They have also
included beta-2 and gamma-2 neurons on the ‘Female anterior’ schematic in Fig. S16, suggesting they are not male-specific
in the authors’ model. This needs some clarification in the text. 
Navigating through some of the results on markers for the male specific clusters is confusing due to the different types of
terminology. The markers are initially referred to by their cell type and systematic gene id (in the text and Supplementary
Figure 13) but in the WISH validation gene names like nrps and ddc appear but without their systematic names.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, You et al. identified a key gene required for male schistosome-triggered female reproductive maturation in
Schistosoma japonicum
. Specifically, they applied single-cell transcriptomics, RNA velocity and pseudotime analysis, and
RNA in situ hybridization to reveal the cellular and molecular dynamics underlying this process. By focusing on four key time
points, they uncovered putative developmental trajectories of male and female cells, focusing on germ cells and various
neuronal cell types. Notably, the authors found that one of the three male-specific neuron subtypes, delta-3 neurons, relies
on 
lsamp
 for normal axonal growth and ventral nerve network formation in the gynecophoric canal of male parasites.
Through RNA interference experiments, they demonstrated that defects in this male nerve network due to 
lsamp
 knockdown
reduce pheromone BATT production and subsequently impair female sexual development. Overall, this study provided a
comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic dataset of both male and female 
S. japonicum
 during the important male-female
pairing process and discovered a male nerve network critical for female reproductive development. The paper is well-written
with high-quality figures that present solid experimental evidence. Beyond the important discovery of the role of 
lsamp
 in
BATT signaling and its contribution to understanding this new signaling molecule, this work provides a useful resource for
understanding schistosome development and reproductive biology. Thus, it is well suited for publication here. However,
there are a few places where results are over-interpreted or alternative interpretations are not considered. Some suggestions
for issues the authors should consider addressing in a revised manuscript are listed below. 
1. 
Some of the language used to describe cell states identified via single-cell sequencing overinterprets what has actually
been learned. For example, male gametes are sperm and female gametes are oocytes. Since transcripts defining the
“gamete” clusters are not presented in high-magnification FISH images, it is not clear whether these clusters reflect mature
gametes or earlier stages along the differentiation pathway. Similarly, the cluster referred to as neural stem cells has not
been validated in a way that allows them to be called stem cells. No evidence is presented that they self-renew, one of the
defining features of stem cells. This could be a large pool of neural progenitors. In the absence of evidence that these cells
are actual gametes or neural stem cells, the authors should soften their language. 
2. 
The authors conclude that “LSAMP is essential for nerve fiber growth by stabilizing microtubules” (line 410). Since lsamp
does not appear to be expressed in the affected neurons, it is unclear how it would act to stabilize their microtubules. Based

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

on what is known about LSAMP function, it seems much more likely that the affected neurons are not able to extend their
axons to the appropriate targets (or maintain them there), so any impact on microtubules would be secondary (i.e., there are
no axonal microtubules because there may be no axons, not because the microtubules are less stable). 
3. 
Understanding the phenotype of lsamp RNAi parasites requires the authors to provide much more detail about the RNAi
protocol and the subsequent phenotypic analysis. As written, it is unclear when the male parasites treated with dsRNA were
initially harvested from the host. If they were harvested at 4 weeks or later, then lsamp is required to maintain the structure of
the neural network (because it would have developed already). If they were harvested earlier, it is important to know when,
so the reader can place the phenotype in the context of the developmental time course shown in Fig. 5e. The authors should
also clarify the time points used for the subsequent analysis of the lsamp RNAi phenotypes. Were all assays conducted at
21 days post-pairing, or were some phenotypes examined at other time points? The graph in Supp. Fig. 22 overinterprets
what is known. It is not clear that LSAMP guides the ventral neural network. Their RNAi experiment shows a single
undefined endpoint from an undefined starting point. 
4. 
The conclusion that the affected axons transport BATT precursors is not supported by the data; other interpretations are
possible. The authors begin their argument with the claim that “synaptic vesicles also transport other small molecules, such
as the precursors for BATT ( -alanine and tryptamine)38, 39.” As far as I could tell, neither of these references mentions  -
alanine or tryptamine. It remains an open question whether these compounds are transported via synaptic vesicles, released
via solute carrier proteins, or if some other mechanism is at work. Furthermore, although the authors note that ddc (aadc) is
required for ovary formation in planarians, they do not mention that it is also required for BATT production and is co-
expressed with nrps in planarians, where it is likely producing tryptamine from tryptophan in the BATT-producing cells
(PMID: 38889152). The lack of a phenotype in the ddc RNAi parasites could easily be due to insufficient knockdown (Supp.
Fig. 18). Because ddc (aadc) is co-expressed with nrps in schistosomes, it seems most likely that the tryptamine is produced
locally, rather than transported. If not, why is ddc (aadc) expressed in these cells? 
Minor comments 
1. 
To help readability and the interpretation of the figures and text, wherever possible, informative gene names should be
used in place of the Sjc numbers. To provide just one example, the in situ in Fig. 3b used Sjc numbers that are not shown
elsewhere in the figure and are marked in colors that do not correspond to any others in the figure, so it was unnecessarily
difficult to keep track of which neuronal subclass they were marking. 
2. 
The authors may wish to change the nomenclature of their neuronal subtypes to avoid confusion. Stem cell subtypes in
schistosomes have already been defined as delta, using the Greek letter (δ) to define them (PMID: 29988015), so this may
add unnecessary confusion to the literature. Furthermore, because delta genes are conserved across animals and play
critical roles in the development of many organ systems, defining subclasses as delta-# (i.e., without the Greek lettering) may
lead readers to expect that they express a specific delta gene. 
3. 
Line 148-9: “This finding indicates that neural progenitor cells are active during the juvenile stage but gradually
degenerate in adulthood.” There is no evidence that these cells degenerate. It seems more likely that they simply generate
their progeny. 
4. 
Since most readers of this journal will be unfamiliar with details of schistosome biology, instead of noting that they find
female germ cell markers in males with no additional context (Supp. Fig. 6d), the authors should point out that
hermaphroditism is occasionally observed in schistosomes and provide appropriate citations. It is not clear from the images
shown in Supp. Fig. 6d that female germ cells are found in the testes because the testes are not visible at the magnification
shown. The authors would need to show higher magnification views that show these cells in the testes to support this claim. 
5. 
Figure 1C, S5, and Data S5 showed changes in cell type compositions across different time points. These measurements
are relative, meaning a significant expansion of one cell type may cause a noticeable reduction of other cell types in
proportion. Please provide a plot or table with absolute cell counts. 
6. 
Line 170: eled is referred to as a germline cell marker, but it is also expressed in vitellaria and neoblasts in the posterior
growth zone in juveniles. Can the authors comment on the eled+ cells in males that appear to be in vitellocytes at 22dpi (Fig.
1d)? 
7. 
In Figure 3E, lsamp and ddc expression seems to be partial in the delta-3 neuron cluster. Is this a gene coverage artifact
from the scRNAseq or a reflection of the heterogeneity of delta-3 neurons? Quantifying their double FISH studies should be
able to clarify this question. 
8. 
Unpaired two-tailed parametric t-tests were widely used for the statistics. Have all the data passed a normality test? 
9. 
Besides the top three genes, nrps, lsamp, and ddc, what other genes and associated pathways are specific to delta-3
neurons? Can the authors elaborate on this further? 
10. 
For IF images, please add quantification and experimental replications in the figure legends. 
11. 
Line 347: “worms treated with lsamp dsRNA instead of ddc dsRNA lost their ability…” This statement would be more
effective omitting mention of ddc dsRNA here. First, state what happens after lsamp dsRNA treatment, then say that ddc

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

dsRNA treatment did not affect female sexual development. 
12. 
Line 398: “Acetylated alpha-tubulin is a protein that stabilizes microtubules” is an awkward statement. Acetylation of
alpha-tubulin (a subunit of the microtubule polymer) is a post-translational modification of microtubules (on alpha-tubulin)
associated with stable microtubules. 
13. 
Line 469: “We… characterize gene regulatory programs…” This work did not characterize gene regulatory programs; it
identified changes in gene expression associated with dimorphic sexual development. 
14. 
Line 507: “…no active neurogenesis at the adult stage.” Single-cell sequencing does not suffice to make this claim. The
population of neural progenitors may just be smaller than can be detected, given the number of cells sequenced here. 
15. 
“Gender” is a social construct, so the authors should omit that word and refer simply to the parasite’s sex.

The authors have made extensive changes to the manuscript and have addressed all our previous points comprehensively.
The new version of the manuscript has been greatly improved, including a streamlined analysis of vitelline cell
developmental trajectories, neural lineages, and germline gene expression dynamics. 
We remain enthusiastic about its
publication and just have some minor suggestions to consider: 
1. Proportions of cell types 
Analyses describing the proportions of cell types are still really prominent in the manuscript but we still don’t really feel that
they add any new insights. The context for this comment is that the paper is huge with many figures, so we feel that making
some reductions would further improve readability. 
The authors have swapped some figures between supplementary
material and main text but we’re unclear how this improves the manuscript. figure 1c does confirm a couple of known things;
for instance, it clearly shows that when vitellocyte cells are combined across the datasets, most come from mature females,
as expected. 
The figure (1c) legend text “…showing the relative proportions of key cell types in S. japonicum at different developmental
stages” is a bit misleading. 
Each bar represents all cells of a given type from across multiple stages. It’s not the proportion of
that type within the parasite. A better description might therefore be “…showing the relative proportions of cells from different
stages within the cell types of the combined S. japonicum dataset”. 
The composition analysis also doesn’t consistently support the narrative. 
For instance, it is true that “male … differentiated
neural cells (e.g., non-ciliated neurons and ciliated neurons) continuously expanded” but the authors don’t mention that in
females this is not the case. 
2. Convergent RNA trajectories

multipotent progenitor pool that functions as a developmental hub capable of giving rise to multiple downstream cell types in
both males and females.” They conclude that “This finding offers new insight into the hierarchical organization of gonadal
cells in this organism, suggesting the presence of a common—rather than sex-specific—early developmental regulatory
mechanism.” 
This seems 
like a lot to infer from RNA velocity alone, without additional validation. 
Some reference to
published precedents from other systems is needed 
3. The last sentence of the results (“These results provide new insights into male-female interactions during sexual
development in schistosomes and may inform future strategies for combating schistosomiasis”) is more of a
discussion/conclusion statement. 
It’s also very similar to that last sentence of the Discussion, so I suggest removing it from
Results.

Reviewer #1: 
  
Wei et al describe the first single cell sequencing atlas for S. japonicum—the human-
infective Asian blood fluke. This is a really impressive study; with approx. 100k cells 
it’s the largest single cell atlas produced in schistosome research —creating a rich 
new resource for understanding schistosome development. The high cell coverage 
and inclusion of intermediate stages of development has enabled the authors to 
produce high resolution data on the sexual -tissue development, uncover distinct 
neural lineages a nd reveal novel biological insights into sexual 
signalling/development in schistosomes.  
 
A key highlight of the work is the discovery of a male -specific neural circuit that 
directly mediates female development. The discovery builds upon previous 
research in S. mansoni demonstrating that nrps in males produces the pheromone 
BATT, which is essential for male -stimulated female development. The authors 
report a neural cell type (“delta-3”) that expresses nrps, and the S. japonicum 
homologue of lsamp, which expresses a neural surface protein in other species. The 
authors functionally characterise lsamp, confirming its expression in delta -3 cells 
and showing that it is required for BATT production but affects neither delta -3 
development nor nrps expression. They further show that lsamp expression is 
closely colocated with the axons and synaptic junctions of a ventral neural network. 
Knocking down lsamp disrupts this network. The authors suggest that lsamp is 
required for males to produce or maintain neural fibrils.  
 
Overall, this is exciting work that deepens our understanding of sexual signalling 
and development in these obligate dioecious parasites. The work is highly 
significant within the parasitology field and will be of broader interest for 
developmental biologists.  
 
It is a large body of work and, in places, the manuscript is currently hard to navigate. 
There are some sections that may benefit from simplifying/streamlining, including

2. Convergent RNA trajectories 
In discussing the convergent RNA velocity trajectories, the authors now 
hypothesise that the convergence point is “a shared multipotent progenitor pool 
that functions as a developmental hub capable of giving rise to multiple downstream 
cell types in both males and females.” They conclude that “This finding offers new 
insight into the hierarchical organization of gonadal cells in this organism,

precedents from other systems is needed 
 
Thank you for this insightful comment. We agree that inferrin g shared progenitors from 
RNA velocity alone requires caution. Our hypothesis was proposed to explain the 
convergent pattern observed in both sexes, and it is strongly supported by precedent: in S. 
mansoni, Wang et al. identified δ-neoblasts—somatic stem cells defined by high nanos-2, 
fgfrA, and fgfrB expression—as multipotent progenitors for both sexes (PMID: 29988015). 
The convergent SSCs -2 cluster in our data shows a highly similar molecular signature. 
Therefore, our RNA velocity results likely reflect a conserved, shared multipotent progenitor 
pool analogous to δ-neoblasts, extending this model to S. japonicum. 
 
We have cited the relevant paper (PMID: 29988015) and revised the Discussion section in

similar, shared multipotent progenitor pool, capable of giving rise to multiple 
downstream cell types in both males and females, which  requires further 
experimental validation.”
