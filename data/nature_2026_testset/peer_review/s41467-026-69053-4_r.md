Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This article reposts some interesting results on the effect of titanium oxide nanoparticles in the catalyst layer to improve the
performance of non-PGM based AEMWE with pure water operation. The local pH value was measured to verify the effect of
titanium oxide to increase the pH for better HER and OER in the operating cell. I am supportive of this article in Nature
Comm, however, there are technical concerns that have to be well-addressed before the final decision can be made. 
Major concerns: 
1) Although the authors claim that the AEMWE exhibited comparable performance with PEMWE under identical conditions,
it is not mentioned how pure water was supplied. I believe that pure water was supplied to the anode side only since in the
PEMWE water is not supplied to the cathode in order to obtain pure (or less humid) hydrogen. In AEMWE, dry cathode
operation is also desirable but much more challenging since water has to be permeated through the membrane to the
cathode. Effect of titanium oxide nanoparticle in the dry cathode reaction should be discussed, comparing with that of wet
cathode reaction (water is supplied to both electrodes). 
2) Effect of titanium oxide nanoparticle on the mass transport (in particular, gas diffusivity) is concerned. Since one of the
major advantages of membrane-based water electrolyzers is high current density operability, run the WE at least up to 4 A
cm-2 of the current density (or 2.4 V of the cell voltage) in Figure 4c, as were done in Figure 1a. 
3) On page 11, why did membranes affect the electrochemical performance of the electrodes? If the authors mean AEMION
and FAA were used as ionomer (binder) in the catalyst layer, it should be mentioned so (but I do not believe that) and the
structural differences of those ionomers should be taken into account for the discussion. 
4) In addition to the catalyst materials, post-stability test analysis should be done for titanium particles (size and distribution,
etc.). 
Minor concerns: 
1) On page 2 last line, the authors mention that alkaline water is used in most AEMWEs to improve the alkaline stability, but
this is not very true. The alkaline water is needed rather to facilitate the electrochemical reaction and lower the overpotential.
2) On page 19 line 3, specify the ionomer material used in the catalyst ink. Was it the same anon exchange polymer as the
AEMs? What was the ionomer/catalyst weight ratio for each electrode?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Guo et al. report a ‘solid base’ strategy that enriches local hydroxide (OH–) concentration within the catalyst layer for high-
performance pure-water AEMWE. They introduce TiO2 nanoparticles on both the anode and cathode catalysts, and then
employ pH ultramicroelectrode and surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy to in situ probe the interfacial microenvironment
at the catalyst–electrolyte interface. The results reveal a TiO2-dominated localized alkalinity, accelerating the Volmer step at
the cathode and water dissociation at the anode. They also evaluate electrolyzer and stack performance and estimate the
levelized cost of hydrogen; collectively, these cross-scale investigations underscore the concept’s device-level potential. In
parallel, the manuscript presents several notable weaknesses, particularly concerning (i) the definition and adaptation of the
‘solid-base’ concept and (ii) the mechanistic evidence demonstrating its operation. Before the manuscript can be considered
for publication in Nat. Commun., the authors should systematically address the following issues. 
1. The primary issue lies in using the core concept of ‘solid base’. In heterogeneous catalysis and solid acid–base
chemistry, a solid base denotes a solid that features surface basic sites, namely Lewis (e.g., O2–/M–O
⁻
) and Brønsted (e.g.,
–OH) sites, with representative materials such as alkali and alkaline-earth oxides and layered double hydroxides (LDHs). Its
mechanism involves Lewis-type electron-pair donation and Brønsted-type proton abstraction at surface basic sites, thereby
facilitating adsorption and activation of acidic substrates, which in turn promotes deprotonation, transesterification, and
condensation, and regulates the interfacial microenvironment. 
However, TiO2 is generally amphoteric and often weakly acidic; in surface chemistry, it is more commonly regarded as a
solid acid rather than a solid base. In addition, based on the authors’ experimental results, TiO2 primarily acts by enriching
OH
⁻
 and modulating the local interfacial environments, thereby assisting the reaction kinetics rather than serving as the
principal catalytic active site. This differs, in terms of terminological scope, from classical solid acid–base chemistry, where
surface basic centers activate substrates via Brønsted proton abstraction or Lewis electron-pair donation. Borrowing the
concept here risks implying that intrinsic basic sites of the material cause alkalization, whereas a more faithful explanation is
local pH regulation under applied bias, driven by OH
⁻
 generation and ionic redistribution in the electric double layer. I
recommend that the authors reconsider using the ‘solid-base’ concept and adopt a more precise conceptual framing aligned
with their experimental evidence.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

2. The mechanistic role of TiO2 nanoparticles has not yet been systematically established. Although extensive in situ
characterizations were performed, the data indicate that introducing TiO2 correlates with accelerated OER at the anode and
HER at the cathode, and with enhanced near-interfacial OH
⁻
 concentrations in the catalyst layer (i.e., an elevated local pH).
However, the pathway by which TiO2 leads to OH
⁻
 enrichment remains insufficiently evidenced, and the molecular-level
mechanism is still unclear. The XPS results show predominantly Ti4+ with no discernible Ti3+ features, suggesting a low
concentration of oxygen vacancies. According to established surface chemistry, stoichiometric TiO2 surfaces typically
adsorb water molecularly, whereas dissociative adsorption leads to surface –OH being more favorable when oxygen
vacancies are present. I recommend defect-sensitive measurements (e.g., EPR) and first-principles calculations to contrast
water adsorption/dissociation on defective versus stoichiometric TiO2, thereby clarifying the specific mechanism, explaining
the performance gains, and avoiding overly speculative interpretations. 
3. In Figures 3h and 3i, the strong band at ~1900 cm–1 is assigned to *H. Given its high intensity, this assignment warrants
careful verification. I recommend isotope substitution experiments; if the band arises from a surface metal–H vibration, the
wavenumber should exhibit a characteristic red shift. This control would help determine whether the band corresponds to *H
rather than other adsorbed species.

Original comment 2-1-1: 
In Figure 1c, pH detection on the electrode surface is achieved by introducing microelectrodes and 
scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM). What is the principle of this test? Can detailed 
descriptions and more intuitive models be supplemented in the Supporting Information (SI)?  
Response: 
We thank the Reviewer for this insightful comment. Our method (Fig. R5a, b) is based on the Nernstian 
response of iridium oxide (IrOx) to hydrogen ion activity. Specifically, the IrOx/IrO(OH) redox couple 
exhibits a linear open-circuit potential (Eoc) dependence on pH (Eoc ∝ -0.059 pH), according to the 
Nernst equation (Fig. R5c, d).  
To implement this principle for spatially resolved local pH under operating conditions, we 
established the following experimental configuration. An ultra-micro pH sensor was fabricated by 
electrodepositing an IrOx film onto a platinum microelectrode tip (10 μm diameter). A 1 × 1 cm2

observation window was machined into the bipolar plate of the AEMWE (Fig. R5a), and the pH 
microelectrode was positioned ~1 μm above the catalyst layer surface using the high-precision 
positioning module of the SECM. During AEMWE operation, changes in Eoc were recorded and 
converted into local pH values using the pre-calibrated Eoc–pH curve (Fig. R5c, d).

Original comment 2-1-3: 
The paper mentions that pH detection with micrometre-scale spatial resolution can be achieved (Page 
4, Line 79). I have a question regarding this: why can OH⁻ generated in the catalyst layer (on the 
nanometre scale) be detected in the micrometre-scale solution region?  
Response: 
We thank the Reviewer for this valuable comment. The process can be described as follows: the 
generated OH⁻ first becomes enriched within the nanoscale electrical double layer (EDL) and then 
diffuses outward through the microscale diffusion layer, where it is detected by the pH microelectrode.  
We note that in our method, the pH microelectrode probes not the interior of the nanoscale EDL, but 
rather the resulting steady-state OH⁻ concentration within the diffusion layer. This spatial resolution 
limitation arises fundamentally from the physical dimensions of the microelectrode tip, which has a 
diameter of approximately 10 μm. Nevertheless, as addressed in our response to the Comment 2-1-4, 
our method offers a reliable semi-quantitative assessment of the local microenvironment in AEMWEs.

Original comment 2-1: 
The authors must clarify whether the reported local alkalinity effect is unique to TiO2 or can also occur 
on other metal oxides. Comparative experiments are essential to substantiate whether this phenomenon 
represents a general property of oxides or merely an artifact specific to TiO2. 
Response: 
We thank the reviewer for raising this insightful comment. According to classical Lewis acid -base 
theory, hard acids tend to strongly interact with hard bases (such as OH⁻), making materials with strong 
Lewis acidity more conducive to creating a locally alkaline environment on the catalyst surface. To 
verify this, we selected another strong Lewis acid oxide, Cr2O3, whose Cr3⁺ ion has a pKa value (~2.05) 
similar to that of Ti 4⁺ in TiO2 (~2.20). In situ local pH measurements  in neutral electrolyte (0.5 M 
Na2SO4) show that the surface pH of the Cr 2O3-modified electrode increases with potential, reaching 
up to 14 at  1.7 V RHE (Fig. R2a ), confirming the formation of an alkaline microenvironment. This 
observation is further supported by performance tests (Fig. R2b): Cr 2O3 modification significantly 
enhances the OER activity of the NiFe anode, achieving performance comparable to that in 1.0 M 
KOH. Together, these results confirm that strong Lewis acid metal oxides can generally induce local 
alkalization. Notably, although Cr 2O3 can induce local alkalization, it faces the risk of oxidation to 
soluble CrO4²⁻ at high potentials. For this reason, we selected TiO2 that remains stable within the OER 
potential range to construct a durable alkaline microenvironment in this work.

Original comment 2-3-1: 
In Figure R12, the peaks at 1010 and 1015 exhibit a shift in 18O, so the authors assign them to OOH.

Following your suggestion, we conducted comparative measurements in H2O and D2O. As shown in

it does not affect the conclusion of this work: local pH measurements confirm the presence of a strongly 
alkaline microenvironment, and the observed OER performance aligns closely with that achieved in 
1.0 M KOH. These results collectively support that the OER mechanism under our conditions follows 
an alkaline pathway.
