Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In order to elucidate how the T4 genome is kept inside the capsid during the later stages of assembly despite the strong
internal pressure, Han et al. have studied the in vitro assembly of the gp13-gp14-gp20 portal neck complexes. They observe
the unexpected presence of an additional protein that they could identify as the host protien Hfq. They determine
reconstructions of the entire and partially assembled complex and show that this Hfq protein stabilizes the neck and
facilitates its correct docking to the phage head. This work provides the first detailed mechanism of viral genome retention in
tailed bacteriophages. 
Major point : The authors have shown here that Hfq can bind to gp14, which leads to a closure of the channel. However the
experiments on hfq-minus E.coli strain only show a 50% reduction in burst size even if it is significant. A larger effect could
have been expected if Hfq was essential for DNA retention. Moreover, given that Hfq is involved in several major bacterial
processes (transcription regulation, genome stability, mRNA decay), it is possible that impairing these functions would have
an effect on the phage biology and thus on the burst size. 
The authors should thus discuss this apparent contradiction
between the structural data that suggest a major role of Hfq in genome retention and the in vivo data that suggest a not that
important role. In particular, is there a significant conservation of the gp14 aminoacids that are binding to Hfq ?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a well-written paper describing the assembly of the T4 portal protein complex and the function of an endogenous
factor, identified as a plug, which prevents genome leakage at the end of packaging before tail assembly. The paper is clear
and easy to understand. Although not groundbreaking, the authors present an interesting discovery that could have
implications for other T4-like phages. Also, is a ~2-fold decrease in phage yield in hfq-plus E. coli sufficient to conclude that
Hfq is physiologically important? What would the phage yield be if a common chaperone were deleted in E. coli? Overall, I
like the paper but find its scope and significance somewhat limited to stand alone as a full article in Nature Communications.

I. A 50% reduction in phage burst size when grown on E. coli  Hfq mutant appears 
modest if Hfq were an “essential” function. However, Hfq is not an essential function. 
Rather, it is an accessory function.  
Multiple structures of the neck assemblies described in our manuscript demonstrate 
that Hfq is not the primary genome gate. It is a secondary gate that reinforces the 
primary genome gate formed by the phage-encoded gp14 protein. We should have made 
this distinction more explicit in the original manuscript. 
The gp14 gate is the one to contact the genome and stop it, while the secondary 
Hfq gate merely supports it. In principle, the gp14 genome gate might be sufficient 
(mutants of gp14 are lethal) but having a second gate would make it fail -safe. Hence, 
Hfq is an accessory to the phage neck.   
Consistent with its accessory function, Hfq’s role is transient as it leaves the neck 
complex once the tail approaches for docking onto the neck through interaction with 
gp15 situated at the top of the tail structure. Clearly, Hfq is not found in the virio n as 
demonstrated by Western blotting and structural analyses. Our energy calculations 
given below in response to another comment (another insightful comment) show that 
the gp14-Hfq interaction is weak (ΔGdiss =13.6 kcal/M) and driven by modest enthalpic

In response to this comment  (and a comment by Reviewer #2 below) , we have 
developed a new assay to test the ability of our in vitro-assembled gp13/gp14/Hfq 
complex to bind to in vitro -packaged heads. Data from these experiments show, 
consistent with the energy calculations, that the gp13/gp14/Hfq complex binds to 
packaged heads demonstrating its functional significance. Additionally, the data show 
that Hfq stoichiometry varied and lower than expected 1:1 ratio indicating that Hfq 
interaction might weaken when attached to pressurized head and some of it dissociates 
even when the tail is not attached. 
Finally, it is also worth noting that a 50% reduction in phage yield is significant, 
given that the yield for the wild -type (WT) phage is only ~100 phages/infected cell. A 
2-fold enhancement by Hfq therefore does provide a significant survival advantage in  
the highly competitive phage universe.  
In fact, curiously, phage T4 evolved by incorporating many such accessory 
functions that provide modest ~50% enhancement of phage yield. Examples include 
MotB, gp5.4, gp45.2, and so on (Mattenberger et al., 2025; Patterson-West et al., 2018); 
(Fokine et al., in preparation). Referred to as “nonessential” genes, such accessory 
functions constitute a significant portion of the T4 genome. What is novel and 
interesting here is co-opting a host protein for enhancing genome retention and virion 
assembly with no genome or metabolic cost to phage itself. This to our knowledge has 
not been observed in any other phage system so far. 
Therefore, our structural, genetic, biochemical, and binding/dissociation energy 
analyses are consistent with Hfq serving in an accessory role through transient 
association with the neck complex for maximizing the efficiency of neck assembly and 
genome retention.

in which two metastable components converge to generate a hyperstable structure. This 
strategy ensures that a strong driving force for assembly is unleashed only upon correct 
formation of the functional unit, thereby preventing premature or erroneous interactions.

when associated, form a robust complex. Notably, the cooperative mode of assembly, 
rather than the intrinsic stability of the individual components, underlies the fidelity and 
stability of the final structure.

I. About the dissociation energy and the stability of the gp14-Hfq complex: 
 Quantitative analysis using PDBePISA  provides a clear mechanistic explanation 
for how the tail protein gp15 triggers Hfq displacement and the stable docking of the 
tail. The gp14–Hfq complex exhibits both a low TΔSdiss and low ΔGdiss, indicating that 
the complex is not stable and  it can dissociate easily. By contrast, the ΔGdiss of the 
gp14/gp15 complex is approximately 25 times greater than that of gp14/Hfq, reflecting 
markedly higher stability. This large difference in stability indicates that, from a 
thermodynamic perspective, gp15 binding to gp14 is strongly favored. 
This is consistent with Hfq’s role, which is restricted to the neck intermediate and 
dissociates upon tail attachment. Hfq binds gp14 ( ΔG_diss: +13.6 kcal/M), forming a

This is a well-written paper describing the assembly of the T4 portal protein complex 
and the function of an endogenous factor, identified as a plug, which prevents genome

We thank the reviewer for stating that our paper reports an “interesting discovery“, 
and for an overall positive assessment of our work and its presentation.  
We should note that this paper reports the first atomic structures of portal -neck 
assembly intermediates, and the first complete viral genome retention mechanism of 
any tailed phage. This is a major advancement because the post -genome packaging 
mechanisms, particularly the highly vulnerable genome retention in a pressurized head, 
remained elusive despite decades of intensive research. Additionally, the paper reports 
the discovery that a host component, Hfq, is coopted by T4 phage to enhance genome 
retention and phage production. 
Also, is a ~2-fold decrease in phage yield in hfq-plus E. coli sufficient to conclude that

We appreciate the reviewer’s concern. We apologize for not clearly explaining the

I. We understand the reviewer’s point that a 50% reduction in phage burst size when 
grown on E. coli  Hfq mutant appears modest, if Hfq were an “essential” function.

However, Hfq is not an essential function. Rather, it is an accessory function.  
Multiple structures of the neck assemblies described in our manuscript demonstrate 
that Hfq is not the primary genome gate. It is a secondary gate that reinforces the 
primary genome gate formed by the phage-encoded gp14 protein. We should have made 
this distinction more explicit in the original manuscript. 
The gp14 gate is the one to contact the genome and stop it, while the secondary 
Hfq gate merely supports it. In principle, the gp14 genome gate might be sufficient 
(mutants of gp14 are lethal) but having a second gate would make it fail -safe. Hence, 
Hfq is an accessory to the phage neck.   
Consistent with its accessory function, Hfq’s role is transient as it leaves the neck 
complex once the tail approaches for docking onto the neck through interaction s with 
gp15 situated at the top of the tail structure. Clearly, Hfq is not found in the virion as 
demonstrated by Western blotting and structural analyses.  
Our energy calculations given above (Supplementary Table 2) in response to 
Reviewer #1’s comment show that the gp14 -Hfq interaction is weak ( ΔGdiss =13.6 
kcal/M) and driven by modest enthalpic and entropic changes, indicative of a transient 
or regulatory association rather than a core structural component. It also dissociates 
easily when the tail attaches. 
Furthermore, as describe below, we have developed a new assay to test the ability 
of our in vitro-assembled gp13/gp14/Hfq complex to bind in vitro-packaged heads. 
Data from these experiments show, consistent with the energy calculations, that the 
gp13/gp14/Hfq complex binds to packaged heads demonstrating its functional 
significance. Additionally, the data show that Hfq stoichiometry is variable and  lower 
than expected indicating that Hfq interaction might weaken when attached to the 
pressurized head and some of it dissociates even when the tail is not attached. 
Finally, it is also worth noting that a 50% reduction in phage yield is significant, 
given that the yield for the wild -type (WT) phage is only ~100 phages/infected cell. A 
2-fold enhancement by Hfq therefore provide s a significant survival advantage in the 
highly competitive phage universe.  
In fact, curiously, phage T4 evolved by incorporating many such accessory

functions that provide modest ~50% enhancement of phage yield. Examples include

II. With regard to Hfq -minus E. coli metabolism, it should be noted that E. coli has 
redundant regulatory systems that compensate for Hfq. The most impact Hfq has is 
when E. coli is grown in nutrient-deficient and other stress conditions. Under nutrient-
rich conditions, the main effect observed is that the E. coli rods become longer because 
Hfq does inhibit cell division. If anything, this phenotype might actually skew our 
results towards higher phage yield because equal number of cells were used for 
infection in our experiments to determine the phage yield. Bulkier cells may provide 
more nutritional resources to support phage infection.  
Furthermore, phage T4, unlike most other phages, completely shuts down host 
machineries immediately after infection and is not much dependent on E. coli 
metabolism. T4 then repurposes the existing intracellular resources to maximize its 
replicative capacity and phage production. Therefore, there is no evidence that Hfq -
minus E. coli metabolic state as such will have an impact on phage T4 yield.
