Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Huang et al. investigated how dietary conditions, such as time-restricted fasting (TRF) and diet-induced
obesity (DIO), affect protein arginine methylation and how mitigation of PRMT activity impacts the metabolic flexibility of the
fat tissues. Specifically, they focus on the role of PRMT3 in the visceral adipose tissue (vWAT) of mice. First, they observed
that the global arginine methylation level fluctuates in correlation with the feeding cycle and higher methylation levels are
observed in mice fed with high-fat diet and in obese individuals. Second, they selectively focus on PRMT3, whose
expression pattern mirrors the methylation changes, and showed that inhibiting PRMT3 activity, using a small molecule
inhibitor SGC807, reduces DIO by enhancing adipocyte glycolysis. Along this line, they also revealed that the insulin-AKT
signaling pathway acts upstream to regulate the levels of PRMT3 protein expression and arginine methylation. Third, they
focus on SLC25A1, a citrate transporter located in the mitochondria, and showed that SLC25A1 interacts with PRMT3 and
likely is methylated by PRMT3 and that its expression also follows the pattern of the feeding cycle. Fourth, they
demonstrated that time-restricted fasting together with PRMT3 inhibition greatly reduces diet-induced obesity. Lastly, they
showed that adipocyte-specific deletion of SLC25A1 could reduce high fat-diet induced obesity by enhancing glucose
metabolism, indicating that modulating SLC25A1 expression, maybe through PRMT3-mediated pathway, could promote
adipocyte glucose metabolism and prevent diet-induced obesity. 
Overall, this manuscript made a few interesting observations, such as 1) global changes of arginine methylation in response
to the feeding cycle; 2) mitigating of HFD-induced obesity by small molecule inhibitor SGC707; 3) correlation of SLC25A1
expression with the feeding cycle; and 4) reduction of HFD-induced obesity by adipocyte-specific knockout of SLC25A1.
Although these individual conclusions seem to be well supported by data, they are not logically connected due to the lack of
mechanisms. At this stage, the manuscript is deemed too descriptive and preliminary. 
Major critiques: 
1. Regarding the global changes of arginine methylation in response to feeding. The authors suggest that changes in
PRMT3 protein level are the cause. However, this conclusion is not well supported: 1) gene expression changes under the
feeding cycle, fasting, and high-fat diet could contribute to the changes of methylation signal on western blot gel. It might not
be related to altered methylation on a given protein. 2) PRMT1 is the major PRMT that catalyzes more than 90% of ADMA,
however, it was excluded at the very beginning of this study. The authors mentioned a similar study (it was not cited), which
reported a strong induction of PRMT1 upon HFD. They should determine the extent to which the alternation of global
arginine methylation is contributed by PRMT1 vs PRMT3. 3) PRMT3 has only a few reported substrates. It is unlikely that
PRMT3 is responsible for global methylation changes. In addition to using SGC707, they should also knockdown or
knockout PRMT3 in eWAT to complement the results with the inhibitor. 4) The alternation of certain metabolites involved in
the methylation pathway could lead to tissue-specific and global changes of methylation. Because the feeding cycle, fasting,
and high-fat diet could have significant impacts on metabolism, this possibility should be tested. 
2. How PRMT3 expression is regulated by the insulin-AKT pathway is not clear. The authors implicate that PRMT3 could be
phosphorylated, although the data supporting this claim is weak. This needs to be thoroughly characterized, such as by
which kinase, at which site, what is the impact of methylation. Additionally, if insulin elevates PRMT3 expression, what is its
impact on methylation? Does it restore methylation under fasting or in the presence of SGC707? 
3. The impact of PRMT3 inhibition, specifically by the small molecule inhibitor SGC707, on lipid metabolism has been
reported. This study was not cited or discussed (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-04524-w). Importantly,
although the specificity of SGC707 was initially characterized against other methyltransferases in vitro, its specificity in vivo

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

is not clear. The in vivo study using SGC707 (Fig. 3) should be complemented with adipocyte-specific PRMT3 knockout. 
4. Regarding the regulation of SLC25A1 by PRMT3. The expression pattern of SLC25A1 in response to the feeding cycle is
interesting. Although this change mirrors the changes of PRMT3, whether there is a direct causative relationship is not
addressed. Also, the rationale for focusing on SLC25A1 as the downstream target of PRMT3 is not strong. They mentioned
a previous publication regarding SLC25A1 interaction with PRMT3 and PRMT5. This paper was not cited. The authors
suggested that PRMT3 might methylate SLC25A1 or at least regulate SCL25A1 methylation. This needs to be thoroughly
characterized, including the sites of methylation on SLC25A1, and how methylation may directly affect SLC25A1 protein
level. Additionally, the authors suggest that SLC25A1 expression is also regulated by the insulin-AKT pathway (Fig. 4p). Is
this through PRMT3? Lastly, what contributes to the tissue-specific regulation of PRMT3 and SLC25A1 in eWAT, but not in
other adipose tissues? 
5. Although PRMT3 inhibition by SGC707 and adipocyte-specific knockout of SLC25A1 both reduce diet-induced obesity,
the extent to which PRMT3-mediated SLC25A1 methylation contributes to this process is not clear. For example, will
supplement the expression of SLC25A1 in PRMT3 KO restores some of the metabolic features? Similarly, what happens if
applying SGC707 on SCL25A1 conditional knockout mouse model? It is more convincing that SLC25A1 expression
changes in response to feeding, whereas changes in PRMT3 are rather minor. 
6. Page 9, section title “TRF reduces PRMT3-SLC25A1 interaction…” was not supported by the data shown in Fig. 5. 
Other critiques 
1. The authors use recombinant proteins to test the specificity of PRMT3 antibody. This method is not appropriate. They
should use PRMT3 knockout cell lines to confirm that the signal they see with the antibody is indeed PRMT3 (PRMT3
knockout cells are viable). In many cases, the changes of PRMT3 protein levels are very marginal (eg. Fig. 2b, 2f, 2j, 2l, Fig.
3r, Fig. 5a). 
2. The correlation between methylation levels and BMI is weak. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript the authors study how different dietary approaches change levels of PRMT3 and MMA/ADMA in white
adipose tissue (WAT). In WAT, the authors firstly show a circadian regulation of MMA/ADMA levels, which seems to be
abrogated upon HFD. Furthermore, inhibition of PRMT3 by SGC707 reverts weight gain on a HFD as well as it accelerates
the decrease of MMA/ADMA levels during a 12h fast to levels similar to a 36h fast, on an insulin dependant way. The
authors also claim that SLC25a1 is implicated. Besides the role of PRMT3 in the levels of MMA/ADMA, the authors study the
changes in these proteins using a Time Restricted Feeding (TRF) paradigm and draw a parallel between TRF and SGC707
action upon long term HFD consumption. 
Former studies have implicated PRMT3/SGC707 in weight loss and ameliorated phenotype from liver steatosis, also
showing an improvement on overall WAT morphology. Overall, literature on PRMT3 or its inhibitor is lacking in models of
HFD, and its effects on body weight, although known, are still quite understudied. 
This adds to the novelty of this present
study. 
Regarding the study itself, in my opinion, some major considerations are as follows: 
1. In figure 1c the authors claim that animals on HFD showed a “marked increase” in proteins containing MMA and ADMA.
HFD feeding is known to alter several parameters that have a circadian component to it as well as feeding patterns. As such,
one can wonder whether the time of collecting the tissue can have an impact on MMA and ADMA levels, and whether
repeating the experiment done on figure 1a would help to get a better understanding on whether this changes in protein
level are continuous during a 24h period or shifted in comparison to NCD. In my opinion, this experiment has relevance
towards the claim of the paper, since understanding the dynamics of protein levels both in NCD as in HFD could influence
the timing of fasting for improved outcomes. 
2. In figure 2, the authors show that a 12h fast in addition to the administration of SGC mimics the results of a 36h fasting,
regarding MMA and ADMA levels. This experiment is central to the claim of the paper, since one of the main premisses is
that by inhibiting PRMT3 during a 12h the observed decrease in MMA and ADMA levels is similar to a 36h fast. It is of my
opinion that is highly important to also show that, in this particular experiment (figure 2h and 2i) the levels of ADMA and
MMA in ad libitum animals administered the drug remain unchanged. In this way its possible to ensure that proper controls
are in place. 
3. The authors present data on the effects of PRMT3 inhibition in overall metabolism and body mass. The authors claim that
no changes in food intake nor energy expenditure were observed on a 24h period. 24h readings can be misleading as small
incremental changes can be observed when measures are taking for extended periods of time. A prolonged measurement
entailing 5-7 days would allow for a better overview and a more convincing claim that the weight loss is not due to small
cumulative changes in feeding amounts/energy expenditure. A longer assessment of the RER could also provide better
proof that this delta RER changes are repeated daily in prolonged periods and not on a particular single isolated event. 
4. It has been published that TRF changes body weight without affecting total caloric intake, and the authors are able to
replicate this finding. Furthermore, in figure S9m, we can observe an increase in locomotion during the night cycle, which
also goes with current literature. In this present study, we observe changes in body weight in HFD animals under a TRF
paradigm, without alteration of food intake or energy expenditure (Figure S9). It would be important for the authors to adress

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

this particular data set, where increased locomotion but unchanged energy expenditure and feeding behaviour are
observed. An important suggestion would be to look at cumulative energy expenditure during a longer period of time, or
even calculating it using the two days presented in figure S9. 
5. Overall, and related to the previous points, the authors claim in different parts of the manuscript that weight loss is
observed with no changes in food intake or energy expenditure. It would be of importance that the authors could address
better this question, with (as suggested above) prolonged measurements and if the phenotype holds, maybe look into
absorption/intestinal issues that SGC707, for example, or any other model used might be inducing. 
6. In relation to the titles of the first section of the paper (as well as the text), the authors claim that “Mono and Asymemetric
(…) in correlation with feeding cycles” and in the second section the authors claim “PRMT3 Regulates Adipose ADMA
during Feeding in an Insulin-pAKT-Dependent Manner”. Both in figure 1 and in figure 2 there is no particular experiment that
shows that changes in ADMA or MMA levels are conneted to feeding behavior itself. Especially in figure 1, the experiments
done show that there seems to exist in fact a circadian variantion in the level of these proteins, that might in fact mirror or be
dependent on feeding/nutritional levels, but no experiments were done at this point to make such claims only with
correlational data. I would suggest changing the claim according to the data presented where in figure 1 its possible to
observe diurnal/nocturnal variations that might be circadian in nature and in figure 2 the changes with fasting might be
correlated with fasting/lack of nutrition and not “feeding” as the title implies. 
7. Also regarding the text on these sections, I would strongly recommend changing the first narrative, where the authors
claim that the patterns observed mirror the feeding patterns of mice. Although it is true, that these changes to mirror the
feeding patterns of animals on HFD, they also might mirror the sleeping patterns, locomotion patters, among other behaviors
that take place during night time when the animals are active. I totally understand that it is part of constructing the narrative,
but it is of my personal opinion that data should be the driver in these circumstances, and the data presented in the first
sections does not allow for any conclusion on what is driving the observations. 
And some additional comments that, although not major, are of some importance to the quality of the present work: 
8. Time restricted feeding lowers Slc25a1 levels in WAT while the levels in the liver remain unchanged. To tackle how the
changes in Slc25a1 affect the metabolic status of the animal, the authors created a mouse model that expresses the Cre
recombinase under the Adiponectin promoter, assuring that the expression of Cre is restricted to adipocytes. To ensure that
this deletion is specific to WAT (following the rationale of the paper where most changes in PRMT3 are seen in WAT), BAT
data on figure 6d should be added. 
9. Regarding figure 2, as it has been repeatidely described in the literature, that prolonged fasting decreases
phosphorylation levels of AKT, as shown for example in figure 2r. Unfortunately, in figure 2j and 2l the levels of pAKT
presented seem to be quite elevated for a 36h fast. On top, a ratio of pAKT/AKT is not present and AKT levels are not shown
in figure 2j. In figure 3 the legend claims that WB were performed in 12week old animals upon 36h of fasting. With all
conditions being equal, it would be important to address the differences in pAKT levels and also include a pAKT/AKT ratio. 
10. The overexpression model does not seem to answer any particular question and does’t seem to add any particular
insight into the question tackled in this current manuscript. If the authors want to use the overexpression model, it would be
ideal at least to show that the over expression worked, since it seems to have an effect on glucose levels homeostasis. 
11. A slightly minor comment (and surely irrelevant for the revision process) but, regarding some of the wording used, the
expression “time-restricted feeding” is more commonly used than the expression “time-restricted fasting”. 
12. I would strongly recommend further proof reading since some words seem to be missplaced, as for example in line 330
the authors write “primarily due to a decrease in liver mass”. Looking at the figure, I would imagine that the authors probably
were referring to lean mass.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the authors’ efforts in addressing my comments and revise the manuscript. The study has been clearly
improved. However, I still have some concerns about the characterization of SLC25A1 methylation by PRMT3 and extent to
which reduced SLC25A1 protein level is responsible for the phenotypes associated with PRMT3 KO. 
1. The sites of PRMT3-catalyzed SLC25A1 methylation were determined by using a pan-ADMA antibody. More rigor
experiments should be done to define the sites. They should perform in vitro methylation assay using recombinant proteins
and detect the methylation and loss of methylation with the mutants using an antibody-independent approach, such as
fluorography. 
2. The authors mutated the two candidate arginine methylation sites (R282 and R285) to Glycine (G) and Cystine (C)
respectively. The underlying rationale for this mutation was not mentioned. Arginine is positively charged, and it is generally
accepted that R-to-K (lysine) is the proper way to introduce arginine methylation deficiency while maintain the positive

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

charge of arginine. The G or C mutation may cause structural changes that could lead to protein misfolding, thus indirectly
affect methylation. 
3. As I mentioned in previous critiques, it is important to define the extent to which reduced SLC25A1 is responsible for
PRMT3 KO phenotype. The authors should supplement/add back SLC25A1 in PRMT3 KO background and assess if it
restores functional impact of PRMT3 KO.
