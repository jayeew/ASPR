Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Sears et al., conducted a series of behavioral and chemogenetic experiments to demonstrate that active avoidance
responses (AR) driven by safety signals exhibit sensitivity to devaluation. Furthermore, they observed that goal-directed
versus habitual ARs are mediated differentially by dorsomedial (DMS) and dorsolateral (DLS) striatum, mirroring the neural
substrates underlying instrumental action vs. habit associated with reward. 
Male and female rats underwent training in a two-way shuttle avoidance task involving a warning signal (WS) followed by
active avoidance of scheduled shock, and the presentation of a safety signal (FB cue) upon a successful avoidance
response (AR). After different training durations (5 vs. 20 sessions) aimed at promoting either outcome-driven or habitual
action, the safety signal was devalued through counterconditioning (in another context). Subsequently, animals were placed
back in the shuttle box and presented with 15 WS in the absence of FB or shock to assess the effects of devaluation on AR.
While males exhibited sensitivity to FB devaluation following the shorter training (reduced AR, but increased freezing to
WS), females failed to show this effect. 
Chemogenetic inhibition of the dorsal medial striatum (DMS), particularly the posterior region, impaired ARs (and increased
freezing to WS) during WS-only extinction sessions after moderate AR training. To demonstrate the disruption of habitual
responding with DLS inhibition, the FB evaluation procedure was now introduced after extensive AR training, and DLS
inhibited animals were found to be sensitive to devaluation. 
Finally, an unsignaled avoidance task revealed diminished responses in both sexes following contingency degradation,
highlighting the importance of the association between AR and FB. 
This is a compelling set of findings that potentially provide novel mechanistic insights into the behavioral and neural control
of instrumental active avoidance, and will likely be of interest to an audience interested in the neurobiology of learning,
anxiety, and function of the striatum. The statistical analyses are mostly appropriate, and the methodology is clearly written
and reproducible on the whole, although more clarification and details are warranted in some parts. I have also outlined
some major concerns including the need for further control experiments/groups that would need to be addressed for me to
fully assess the validity of the claims. 
Given the primary aim of the FB devaluation procedure is to assess changes in active avoidance behavior in response to the
devalued safety signal, the omission of the safety signal during the extinction test sessions is a major limiting factor in
evaluating the data. The claims would be greatly strengthened by the inclusion of the safety signal in the test. 
The authors noted that SalB produced non-specific effects including locomotor depression –a SALB only control group that
is typically included to control for off target effects is notably absent in this study, and should be justified, or included. 
I appreciate that the focus of the paper is on safety signals driving AR, but the paper would benefit from more consideration
and discussion of the contribution of the WS to AR acquisition. The level of AR is evidently much lower in the non-signalled
AA task – i.e., the absence of a WS (barely 50% in control males in Fig 6b), which speaks to the importance of WS in driving
AR as well. The authors imply that the WS elicits a competing behavior, such as freezing. However, it would be crucial to
demonstrate freezing behavior in response to the WS throughout active avoidance response (AR) acquisition, rather than

solely during extinction sessions. More importantly, it is also unclear if AR behavior is attenuated after pDMS inhibition due
to an impairment in goal directed AR driven by FB memory (as per the authors’ account) or due to an equally viable
possibility that WS processing has become disrupted (potentiated freezing). 
In general, the statistical reporting is minimalistic, and needs to be expanded to allow confirmation of the absence of effects.
In many places, F stats are reported, with no accompanying explanation of which factors are involved. Here, I provide
examples of such incidences: 
1) Figure 4D – F statistics for Block x Drug appears to be reported as being non-significant, but the F statistics for Drug
needs to be reported as there seems to be a difference in the number of shuttles between drug vs. vehicle conditions. 
2) Ext Figure 2 illustrates the inter-test reliability; however, the statistical analyses do not directly compare performance
between the two tests. A visual inspection of the performance in Tests 1 and 2 suggests potential differences that require
statistical validation or clarification with Test as an additional factor particularly for Block 1. For instance, the performance of
FB control rats in Block 1 in panels a and d, and performance of the males in Block 1 in panel b across the two tests are
notably different. Could repeated testing be contributing to some seemingly different vehicle group performance between
experiments (e.g., Ext Fig 3 h & m)? 
3) Fig 6 – there seems to be sex differences in avoidance shuttles in the unsignaled AA task, which could be investigated
further with statistical analysis. 
The concept of a safety signal serving as both a positive reinforcer and conditioned inhibitor is complex, and not very well
differentiated in the introduction and discussion. The study presents evidence of the inhibitory properties of the tone safety
signal, as demonstrated by the retardation of a new tone-foot shock pairing and the observed summation effect
(freezing(context + safety signal) < freezing(context)). However, it is important that the authors clearly articulate how their
study supports the claim that the tone safety signal serves as a positive reinforcer to the animals.

1. Given the primary aim of the FB devaluation procedure is to assess changes in active 
avoidance behavior in response to the devalued safety signal, the omission of the safety signal

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

during the extinction test sessions is a major limiting factor in evaluating the data. The claims 
would be greatly strengthened by the inclusion of the safety signal in the test. 
In the early stages of this project, we agreed with the reviewer on this point. However, after 
consulting with experts on devaluation like Andrew Delamater, we were persuaded that this is 
incorrect. Observing an avoidance decrement when the devalued reinforcer is omitted is more 
compelling evidence that animals are using a cognitive strategy. If the devalued reinforcer is 
presented after each avoidance response, animals may stop responding due to punishment and 
S-R mechanisms cannot be ruled out. This is explained more clearly in Dickinson & Balleine’s 
influential 1994 paper Motivational control of goal-directed action:  
“…it is important to note that the test was conducted in “extinction”; neither outcome was 
actually presented during the test. If Adams and Dickinson had actually presented the food 
pellets contingent on lever-pressing during the test, this devaluation effect could have been 
explained in terms of the direct suppressive effects of the now-aversive positive outcome. By 
testing in extinction, however, they ensured that the differential performance of the two groups 
must have reflected the interaction of knowledge of the instrumental contingencies acquired 
during initial training with some representation of the status of the outcome as a goal following 
devaluation treatment.” 
 
We added the following text to the Methods section to make our rationale for extinction tests 
clearer: “Note that feedback stimuli were omitted during the final tests to ensure that avoidance 
reflected a memory of the outcome value rather than reinforcement/punishment during the test 
phase1.” 
 
2. The authors noted that SalB produced non-specific effects including locomotor depression –a 
SALB only control group that is typically included to control for off target effects is notably 
absent in this study, and should be justified, or included. 
This is an important point that we did not adequately discuss in the original manuscript. We 
address this in the revised manuscript in two ways. First, we tested a subset of rats from the 5-
day DMS and 20-day DLS KORD experiments in the open field after counterbalanced Vehicle or 
SalB injections using a within-subjects design (n=17 Males). This was the last test conducted 
before brains were removed for histology. We left this out of the original submission because 
some rats were ultimately excluded from the avoidance analyses (poor avoiders, poor bilateral 
KORD expression, mistargeted KORD expression). However, the data show no indication of 
impaired locomotion after a 10-minute delay, suggesting that occasional suppressive effects of 
SalB after i.v. injections are transient and expressed mainly in the homecage. Second, we also 
included data in the initial submission showing many null effects on avoidance and freezing after 
SalB treatment (now Figs. 6, 7, and Extended Fig. 6). Although we occasionally observed fewer 
shuttles during the acclimation or ITI periods of avoidance tests, these were inconsistent and did 
not show a pattern that could explain avoidance/freezing effects. For instance, SalB reduced 
acclimation shuttles in only two of six experiments (KORD in pDMS vs aDMS after moderate

training). Of these two, SalB affected avoidance and freezing only in the pDMS experiment. The 
opposite profile was also observed; SalB affected avoidance and freezing in overtrained rats 
with KORD in DLS after devaluation (Fig. 8) but had no effect on acclimation shuttles. A similar 
story can be told about ITI shuttles. Together, these results strongly suggest that non-specific 
effects of SalB on locomotor behavior are transient and cannot explain the patterns we observe

present the acclimation, ITI and Open Field data to address this issue more directly (Extended

5. The concept of a safety signal serving as both a positive reinforcer and conditioned inhibitor 
is complex, and not very well differentiated in the introduction and discussion. The study 
presents evidence of the inhibitory properties of the tone safety signal, as demonstrated by the 
retardation of a new tone-foot shock pairing and the observed summation effect 
(freezing(context + safety signal) < freezing(context)). However, it is important that the authors 
clearly articulate how their study supports the claim that the tone safety signal serves as a

We agree that this topic is complex and made several revisions in the Introduction and 
Discussion to add some clarity. We added text emphasizing that prior studies already support 
the hypothesis that response-produced safety signals increase avoidance behavior (generic 
positive reinforcement). However, many of these studies assumed positive reinforcement of 
stimulus-response associations that mediate habits. Our contribution is the demonstration that 
avoidance can be goal-directed, that the goal is safety, and that devaluation of safety signals 
can differentiate between goal-directed and habitual ARs. Impairing avoidance by devaluing a 
response-produced outcome is additional evidence for positive reinforcement, but it also goes 
further to demonstrate goal-directed control by an action-outcome association. Summation and 
Retardation tests were used only to confirm that feedback stimuli were safety signals 
(conditioned inhibitors) after avoidance training. Currently it is unknown how fear-inhibitory 
properties of safety signals relate to positive reinforcement.  
 
In the Introduction: A less studied hypothesis is that ARs are positively reinforced by response-
produced safety signals that are generated via their inverse relationship with the warning 
stimulus and shock during avoidance training7,10-12. Explicit feedback cues that immediately 
follow ARs are known to develop conditioned inhibition (safety) and reinforcing properties that 
promote avoidance10,13-16. Here, “reinforcing” is used in a general way, though many early 
studies assumed strengthening of stimulus-response associations. It is unknown, however, 
whether safety signals behave as valued outcomes supporting goal-directed avoidance via 
action-outcome associations, akin to food in a barpressing experiment. To our knowledge, no 
active avoidance studies have attempted to devalue response-produced safety signals. To 
examine this, we designed a devaluation protocol where AR feedback cues are 
counterconditioned by pairing with shock. We also present data on a novel contingency 
degradation test, where non-contingent safety signals are presented during avoidance.  
In the Discussion: Although negative reinforcement theories dominated during the heyday of 
active avoidance research, the idea that safety signals may positively reinforce avoidance was 
introduced as early as 1936 by Konorski and Miller. This was echoed by Soltysik, who called

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

avoidance feedback a “reward” that “stabilizes avoidance behavior and makes it virtually 
independent of the noxious [unconditioned stimulus]”8. Studies since then are consistent with 
the notion that response-produced safety signals improve or “reinforce” avoidance but were not 
designed to implicate goal-directed behavior9-14. Our finding that devaluation impairs moderately 
trained avoidance during extinction tests (no shock or feedback) is the first direct demonstration 
that response→feedback associations can control avoidance.  
 
In the Discussion:Our results provide evidence for positive reinforcement in avoidance, but we 
acknowledge several limitations. First, “positive reinforcement” is an umbrella term that has 
been used to describe response-produced outcomes that promote goal-directed and/or habitual 
instrumental behavior. We implicate safety signals in goal-directed avoidance, but it remains 
unclear if these also provide the teaching signal that stamps in stimulus-response associations 
underlying habitual avoidance. One recent study suggests they do15. We also cannot rule out 
additional roles for WS-termination and/or US-omission in the negative reinforcement of ARs, 
especially early in training before feedback cues develop inhibitory properties. However, 
conditioned inhibition likely develops faster than is currently appreciated (Fig. S1 in reference16) 
and we suspect that ARs become safety-directed within a few trials. Also, for safety signals to 
be considered true positive reinforcers, they should support new response learning in the 
absence of shocks. One study failed to find evidence for this17, but more work is needed to 
determine if this is generally true or if the task needs optimization. Studies in rodents and 
humans show that response-produced safety signals during avoidance induce dopaminergic 
prediction error signals and pleasant feelings like other positive reinforcers18-21.

Reviewer 2 
 
This manuscript by Sears and colleagues reports an interesting set of findings. The authors 
have designed a clever method for devaluing a safety signal (that signals successful avoidance) 
by pairing that safety signal with footshock. In addition, the authors adapt the classic 
contingency degradation procedure by providing non-contingent safety cues, although the 
training for this second procedure is distinct from the first design in which a discriminative 
stimulus signaled the opportunity to avoid shock. There are some interesting findings here that 
will be of interest to specialists in avoidance learning, as well as to the broader fields concerned 
with aversion and fear, animal learning, and functions of striatal circuitry. These findings 
additionally have relevance for understanding behavioral disorders in humans related to fear

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

and avoidance. While the concept and experimental design are potentially of interest, and the 
topic is highly significant, the manuscript was somewhat burdensome to read because of the 
acronymns and the lack of clear presentation of the statistical analyses.  
Some of these issues are detailed below. 
We have reduced the use of acronyms and moved key statistical analyses into the Results 
section. We have also added Results-style text to the extended figures to improve the reading 
experience.   
 
1. There are aspects of data analysis and presentation that are not clear, missing, or confusing. 
First, the authors divide subjects into good and poor performers. There is no justification for 
such a stratification. Do these subjects improve following overtraining? 
Poor avoiders sometimes improve with additional training; however, we have never attempted to 
overtrain them. For this project, we were interested in how neural circuits may change when 
animals who acquire avoidance at similar rate continue to encounter the threat and emit the 
response. The simplest way to do this was to study the large segment of the population that 
readily acquires avoidance responses. We are also hesitant to expose animals to repeated 
shocks when they show little evidence of learning the task. This is not an issue for the good 
avoiders as they experience few shocks after acquiring the response. We are very interested in 
the poor performer population and study them in a separate project that does not involve 
overtraining. For instance, it appears that many in this group learn the instrumental contingency 
but are unable to avoid due to excessive freezing reactions. We can “rescue” avoidance 
responding after moderate training with central amygdala lesions2,3,22 or propranolol23. Studies 
of the precise neural mechanisms of poor avoidance are ongoing24 and we view this as an 
important, but separate, question.    
 
2. Second, stats reported in the figure legends do not give the complete information. For 
example F-values are reported without significance levels. I recommend moving the statistical 
results to the results text so that appropriate text can be used around the statistical values to 
assist the reader. For example, some of the figures might suggest that there are significant 
interactions that would be of interest, but I can’t tell from the text, therefore I can not 
appropriately evaluate some of the findings. 
We have added more complete descriptions of the statistical analyses to the Results section as
