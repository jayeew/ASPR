Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors reported a new solid additive (SA), namely 3-allylrhodanine (3-AR) to precisely regulate the ASM-OSC active
layer morphology. They found that 3-AR can effectively narrow the film formation window, suppressing lateral over-
aggregation and crystallization, while promoting vertical donor-acceptor homogeneity within the active layer. Consequently,
the 3-AR-processed MPhS-C2:N3-based ASM-OSCs achieved a high efficiency of 18.43%, certified as 18.16%. Although
the authors have achieved credible device performance metrics, several technical issues require resolution prior to publish
in Nat. Commun. after major revisions. 
1. The purportedly commercially sourced solid additives appear critical to device performance and blend morphology. The
authors should provide certified purity validation through supporting analytical characterizations (e.g., NMR spectroscopy,
mass spectrometry). 
2. The chemical structure of 2PACz and H75 should be provided. 
3. A recent publication reports 18.51% device efficiency (https://doi.org/10.1039/D5EE01162K), rendering the current work
(18.4%) no longer the highest values. Relevant performance claims require immediate revision per community-established

benchmarking standards. 
4. The control and SA-modified devices show comparable EQE spectral ranges, they display different JSC and EQE
amplitude responses. To clarify the underlying charge transport kinetics, the authors should provide internal quantum
efficiency (IQE) and PL quenching measurements for these devices. 
5. The carbonyl stretching region (1660-1760 cm
⁻
¹) exhibits characteristic vibrations in both pristine additives and blend
films. The 1734 cm
⁻

(Figure S10) all align with C=O vibrations. Crucially, the putative 1734 cm
⁻
¹ signal in blends may belongs to spectral noise,
precluding definitive assignment of additive elimination. We therefore suggest quantitative NMR validation to
unambiguously determine additive removal efficacy from the complex matrix. 
6. Binding energies of 3-AR with MPhS-C2 and N3 register as -33.21 and -35.35 kcal/mol, respectively. These results
remain mechanistically inconclusive regarding solid-additive working mechanism. We suggest two-dimensional NMR
spectroscopy (specifically NOESY or ROESY) to resolve spatial configurations and quantitatively delineate the operative
mechanisms at molecular resolution. 
7. Given the prospects for large-scale application, although ASM OSCs based on this system have achieved notable device
efficiency, an observed efficiency gap persists when compared to state-of-the-art polymer-based OSCs exceeding 21%
PCE. Therefore, it is critical to investigate: (1) whether this additive enhances operational stability; and (2) how is the
performance regarding maximum power point tracking (MPP-T80) and thermal stability under heating for corresponding
devices? 
8. What is the application universality of this SA? Does its efficacy exclusively pertain to all-small-molecule systems, or
alternatively, is it functionally limited only to the present MPhS-C2:N3 blend framework? Cross-system validation across
diversified active layers is warranted to ascertain its adaptability spectrum. 
9. For the photovoltaic parameters presented in the Supporting Information tables that are not derived from a single-device
measurement, the corresponding standard deviations must be rigorously provided to ensure statistical significance. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Cao et al. regulated active layer morphology of all-small-molecule organic solar cells (ASM-OSCs) by
using a solid additive 3-allylrhodanine (3-AR). 3-AR narrows the film formation window, suppressing over-aggregation and
crystallization, while promoting vertical donor-acceptor homogeneity within the active layer. As a result, the 3-AR-processed
MPHs-C2:N3-based ASM-OSCs showed a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 18.43%. The major issues of this research
are lack of innovation and insufficiency of data and analysis, and this reviewer can't recommend publication in Nature
Communications. Below are some detailed comments: 
1. First, the strategy of employing solid additives (structurally analogous to certain groups in the donor or acceptor) to
regulate active-layer morphology for enhanced photovoltaic performance has been extensively documented in the literature.
Second, the authors only proved that 3-AR (a structure similar to the donor terminal group) can improve performance. While
numerous similar donor terminal groups exist, can all enhance performance? If not, what specifically distinguishes 3-AR?
Third, the PCE of 18.43% does not represent the state-of-the-art value for ASM-OSCs (18.51%, Energy Environ. Sci., 2025,
10.1039/D5EE01162K). 
2. The transient absorption characterization and analysis require further refinement. The generation of photoexcited species
occurring around 1 ps appears unexpectedly slow. Experimental validation is needed to determine whether a GSB signal
emerges when exciting the acceptor material at 400 nm. Additionally, the rising kinetics of the N3 GSB signal requires
detailed analysis to determine the timescales for both exciton dissociation and exciton diffusion. More critically, hole transfer
dynamics is typically the rate-limiting step in OSCs compared to electron transfer/energy transfer. A comprehensive study of
hole transfer kinetics is essential to conclusively understand exciton and carrier dynamics within the active layer because
focusing solely on electron transfer/energy transfer lacks persuasiveness. 
3. The mechanistic explanation states that 3-AR’s interaction with MPhS-C2 shortens the Stage II duration of MPhS-C2.
Why does its stronger interaction with N3 prolong the Stage II duration of N3? Moreover, 3-AR enhances crystallization in
single-component systems but suppresses crystallization in bulk-heterojunction blends, necessitating mechanistic
clarification. 
4. Sun's group developed an IC-FI solid additive, structurally analogous to the end group of N3, enhancing the molecule
self-assembly of N3 (J. Mater. Chem. A 2021, 9, 2857–2863), while 3-AR (structurally analogous to end group of MPhS-C2)
enhances acceptor N3 crystallization rather than MPhS-C2, why? 
5. The authors mention trap-assisted recombination. Further data on the trap density should be provided. 
6. The photo, thermal and operational stability of the device needs to be provided.

In this work, Cao et al. designed 3-allylrhodanine (3-AR) as a solid addition, structurally analogous to the MPhS-C2 donor
terminal group, control active layer morphology. As a result, the 3-AR-treated MPhS-C2:N3 device achieves a remarkable
power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 18.43% (certified18.16%), which is the highest reported for binary ASM-OSCs.
Regarding the reviewer #3's response, I consider some of the replies to be reasonable, and the manuscript has been further
improved. However, certain responses still present significant issues requiring further revision. 
1. It is recommended that authors retain only the highest value for third-party certified data. 
2. The author claims that current understanding of the working mechanism of additives is still primarily empirical and lacks
systematic design principles for ASM-OSCs. And then, the author designs the new solid addition to control active layer
morphology. The manuscript does not establish a rational design principle or validation for the solid additive. The approach
of using additives with functional groups similar to the donor or acceptor appears to be an empirical optimization strategy
rather than a principled one. The authors must provide a convincing explanation of the design principle and its verification to
establish the scientific significance of their work. 
3. The normalized stability decay curves in Supplementary Fig. 9 do not accurately reflect the absolute degradation of
device performance. These should be replotted using the absolute values of key parameters (such as PCE, FF, JSC) versus
time to clearly show the actual degradation. 
4. In Supplementary Fig. 20c, the time interval for adding or not adding 3-AR in N3 is imprecise during the solution-to-film
conversion stage. I propose that 3-AR has a negligible effect on the film formation time of N3, which remains constant at
approximately 0.1 seconds. Its primary influence is on shortening the film formation time of the donor, MPhS-C2. This
observation suggests a processing sequence in which the acceptor (N3) aggregates first, followed by the donor.
Consequently, the authors are requested to re-analyze the relevant data, provide a reasonable explanation for this observed
mechanism, and furnish detailed parameters for each stage of the process, including the precise film formation times and
aggregation order.

3. The mechanistic explanation states that 3 -AR’s interaction with MPhS-C2 shortens 
the Stage II duration of MPhS -C2. Why does its stronger interaction with N3 prolong 
the Stage II duration of N3? Moreover, 3 -AR enhances crystallization in single -
component systems but suppresses crystallization in bulk -heterojunction blends, 
necessitating mechanistic clarification. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for the comments. The two seemingly contradictory 
phenomena you pointed out are precisely the most interesting and important findings 
in our work.  
First, the prolonged duration of Stage II for N3 due to its stronger interaction with 
3-AR is a matter of nucleation kinetics. For MPhS -C2, 3 -AR likely functions as a 
nucleating agent: its relatively weak interaction with MPhS-C2 is sufficient to provide 
effective nucleation sites, significantly reducing the nucleation energy barrier. This 
enables MPhS -C2 molecules to rapidly attach and arrange in an orderly manner, 
resulting in a "shortened phase transition duration" (Adv. Funct. Mater. 2025, e14342 
https://doi.org/10.1002/adfm.202514342). However, when 3 -AR interacts with the 
acceptor N3, the stronger interaction between them effectively enhances their 
compatibility. This improved compatibility suppresses the premature aggregation 
behavior of N3 during the  early nucleation stage (Adv. Funct. Mater. 2025, 2503009 
https://doi.org/10.1002/adfm.202503009). As a result, we observe a "prolonged phase 
transition duration" for N3 (Supplementary Fig. 20). This retardation of the kinetic 
process facilitates the forma tion of finer, thermodynamically more stable crystal 
structures in N3, which aligns with the GIWAXS characterization results indicating 
improved crystallinity in the 3-AR-treated N3 film.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 41 -->

Second, the phenomenon where 3 -AR promotes crystallization in single -
component systems but suppresses it in blend systems underscores the complexity of 
the bulk heterojunction (BHJ) system. The core of this behavior lies in the mutual 
constraints between the donor MPhS -C2 and acceptor N3 molecules, and how the 
introduction of 3-AR alters this dynamic balance. In the single-component N3 system, 
the molecular environment is homogeneous, with no competitive interactions. The 
strong interaction between 3-AR and N3 directly promotes the orderly arrangement of 
N3 molecules, thereby unequivocally enhancing crystallization. However, in the BHJ 
blend system, the interpenetration and mutual dilution of MPhS-C2 and N3 molecules 
significantly restrict their free movement and orderly packing. Our in -situ UV-Vis 
results (Fig. 3a, b) reveal that MPhS-C2 tends to nucleate and crystallize preferentially 
and rapidly, driving initial phase separation in the blend solution. At the same time, the 
strong interaction between 3 -AR and N3, along with the resulting improved 
compatibility, suppresses the early nucleation of N3. This extends the time interval (Δt) 
between the final crystallization of the donor and the onset of nucleation for the 
acceptor from 0.38 s to 0.48 s  (Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2025, e202515280 
https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202515280). As this Stage I time window lengthens, the 
solvent evaporates more thoroughly, subsequently enabling N3 to undergo a faster 
phase transition in the following stage (Δτ shortened from 0.190 s to 0.097 s). This 
effectively prevents N3 from forming excessiv ely large phase domains due to over -
crystallization, which are detrimental to device performance.  Thus, while 3 -AR 
moderately suppresses the overall crystallinity of the blend film on a macroscopic scale 
(as supported by GIWAXS data), it promotes the formation of more numerous, smaller-
sized crystallites through precise kinetic regulation and guides the construction of a 
more ideal nanoscale phase -separated morphology (consistent with AFM and TEM 
results). This optimized microstructure features a larger don or/acceptor interface area 
and more continuous charge transport channels, ultimately leading to significant 
improvements in device performance (as confirmed by SCLC, TPC, PL, and J -V 
characterizations).

prolongs the nucleation process and regulates phase transition kinetics. While

orderliness, ultimately achieving an ideal active layer morphology (Angew. Chem. Int. 
Ed. 2025, 64, e202501816 https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202501816).
