Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, the authors explore the intriguing phenomenon of C. elegans which are naturally deficient in DNA methylation.
They point out that the methyl-binding domain protein 2/3 (MBD2/3) ortholog, while losing the methyl-binding domain (MBD),
is still present, and they ask if this protein has any conserved functions in worms. They show that worm MBD2/3 retains its
conserved role as a scaffolding component of NuRD, and demonstrates new functions in recognizing H3K27me3. Overall
the study is very interesting, well written, and the figures are well made. I do not have any suggestions for improvement, and
congratulate the authors on a very interesting and well designed work. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The article “Dual genomic localizations and functions of MBD-2 with and without NuRD in DNA methylation-free C. elegans”
presents an informative case study in the evolution of chromatin regulation. The authors tackle a clear outstanding question
“what role does MBD-2 have if not through 5mc”? This question is particularly interesting given that 5mc is (parsimoniously)
ancestral, yet mbd-2/3 is maintained in several metazoan lineages that have lost 5mc. The authors perform a suite of genetic
and biochemical experiments to address this question, and conclude that mbd-2 in C. elegans retains NuRD binding and
functon(i.e., phenotype), however, it also has apparently gained NuRD independent chromatin binding (and possibly
function). The two different categories can be distinguished by the presence/absence of repressive (non-NuRD) and
activating (NuRD +) histone modifications. They end by presenting a model in which H3K27me3 replaces 5mc as a
mechanism of recruiting MBD-2 to genomic loci. 
Overall, this was an exciting paper that used mechanistic experiments to address a broadly interesting evolutionary
question. The data quality, reproducibility and writing were all appropriate for the field. Their result of mbd-2 binding outside
of NuRD raises interesting questions about what roles it evolved since the loss of 5mc - and how it is recruited in its
absence. I look forward to seeing this work in press, however, I have a few major-revision requests that if completed would
substantially strengthen their manuscript and interpretations. 
Major comments: 
1. The authors note that the CC-deletion phenocopies the mbd-2 knockout phenotype. This suggests that the interactions
with GATAD2A/B and CHD-3/4/5 are the dominant cause of infertility and vulva development. This is somewhat surprising
in light of the large number of non-NURD peaks in the Chip-Seq data (93%), and the large number of genes which are
differentially expressed but do not overlap with hda-1 and dcp-66 differentially expressed genes. If the CC-deletion
phenocopies the null, what is the role of non-NURD mbd-2 binding? The authors note in the Discussion that coiled-coil
mutants are too sick to do ChIP with, and I am receptive to that. However, an indirect but possible way to get at this question
would be RNA-seq of their coiled-coil mutant. The differentially expressed genes relative to wildtype, and comparison to
mbd-2 knockout, should yield insight into the role of NuRD vs. non-NuRD mbd-2 binding. Given that the dual roles of mbd-2
is a major feature of this paper, I would argue that this is a necessary (and again relatively easy) experiment for the authors

to do. 
2. The authors emphasize that the non-NuRD MBD-2 ChIP-signal is enriched over H3K27me3. However, in Fig. 5D, the
data shows that 45.7% of peaks overlap with H3K27me3 peaks. Just slightly less enrichment (41.1%) can be seen with
H3K9me2. Moreover, as the authors are likely aware, H3K27me3 covers almost half of the entire worm genome (Liu et al.,
2011). Thus, overlap with H3K27me3 is not that different from overlap with all non-actively transcribing regions. When
inspecting the genome tracks presented in Figure 5F, there does not appear to be any particular correspondence of MBD-2
peaks with the broad domains of H3K27me3. When inspecting the chromosomal patterns in Fig. 7A, there is clearly a bias of
MBD-2 to chromosome arms, but what is the evidence for specific enrichment with H3K27me3 compared to generally
repressed chromatin (also see Major Comment #3 below). 
Looking at Fig. 5C, it is true that the most distinguishing corollary of NuRD vs. non-NURD MBD-2 binding is the absence or
presence of H3K27me3. While I agree that this is an interesting pattern, the inference is limited to the few histone
modifications that were assessed. Without more mechanistic evidence, or at the least, statistical support for enrichment, the
authors should omit this emphasis in their abstract, and probably minimize its emphasis in their Discussion. 
3. 
In general, the bioinformatic enrichment analyses lack statistical support. This is especially important for their
interpretations regarding overlap with histone modifications. For example, is the ChIP-seq peak overlap of MBD-2 with
H3K27me3 significant relative to broad repressed domains? I would also appreciate more methodological information for
RNA-seq, ChIP and IP-MS in the main text: sample size, p value and fold-change cut off, and replicate numbers. Most of
these are documented in the methods, but as they are critical for interpretation, in my opinion they should be incorporated
into their Results section as well.

7. Figure 6 has some formatting issues (possibly just due to .pdf conversion).

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their manuscript “Dual genomic localizations and functions of MBD-2 with and without NuRD in DNA methylation-free C.
elegans”, the authors characterize C. elegans mbd-2, including detailed characterization of its expression pattern, mutant
phenotypes, structure-function analysis (role of coiled-coil domain), protein interaction partners, and roles in gene regulation.
Despite its name (Methyl - CpG - Binding - Domain - 2), mbd-2 lacks the methyl-cpg binding domain, but has conserved idr
and coiled-coil domains, which are important for interactions with the NuRD complex in other organisms. Whether mbd-2 is
part of a C. elegans NuRD complex, and how mbd-2 is recruited to chromatin (in absence of CpG methylation) remained
open questions. The authors find that mbd-2 is broadly expressed and required for proper development and fertility, as is its
coiled coil domain. Through IP-MS, they find that mbd-2 is in a complex with most known NuRD components and with
known NuRD-interacting transcription factors. RNA-seq in mbd-2 mutants revealed many misregulated genes (~1/4 of
protein-coding genes), with a bias to up-regulation, Notably only a minority (~7%) of mbd-2 ChIP-seq peaks were co-
occupied by other NuRD components. Rather, the majority of mbd-2-occupied sites are concentrated on chromosome arms
and often co-localize with H3K27me3 (and H3K9me2), which leads the authors to conclude that mbd-2 has NuRD-
dependent and NuRD-independent roles. The authors propose a model wherein H3K27me3 functionally substitutes for
methylated CpG in recruiting mbd-2 to chromatin.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

This work provides new insight into a conserved and important chromatin modifying complex, which will make it of interest to
a broad readership. The authors make use of a broad toolkit of approaches (genome engineering, IP-MS, genomics). They
convincingly show that mbd-2 is in a complex with NuRD factors, but that the majority of mbd-2 does not colocalize along the
genome with NuRD factors. The work highlights the multi-tasking nature of many chromatin regulators, which can have
different effects depending on the genomic context and/or varying sets of cofactors. The authors speculate that repressive
histone modifications, and in particular H3K27me3, may provide an alternative mechanism for recruitment of mbd-2 in
animals without 5mC. While the study is overall well done, this last point is somewhat weak as it is based on correlation and
there is a lack of statistical analysis or additional experiments to support this conclusion. Overall, the rigor and statistical
analysis of the genome-wide analyses as well as the organization of the latter half of the manuscript/figures should be
improved prior to acceptance for publication. 
Major Points: 
1. A more thorough analysis of the “non-NuRD-overlapping” mbd-2 peaks is needed. There is an emphasis on overlap with
H3K27me3, but it appears that the overlap with H3K9me2 is similarly high (Fig. 5D), or even higher (Fig. S6D). In addition,
there does not seem to be much evidence for overlap with H3K9me3, in contrast to statements in the manuscript (e.g. Line
556) and the model figure. The analysis of the overlap of the “non-NuRD-overlapping” mbd-2 peaks with H3K27me3 or
H3K9me2/3 (or other histone modifications) should be more robust, and minimally include correlation of the signals, analysis
of the ‘converse’ relationship (e.g. peaks of those histone marks overlapping mbd-2), and preferably a statistical analysis of
the overlaps. 
2. There is an implication that the overlap with H3K27me3 may be ‘specific’ to genomes without 5mC – it is difficult to
evaluate this without an analysis of the relationship between H3K27me3 and mbd-2 (at “high 5mC site”) in mammals – has
this been analyzed? Is there data that could be used to investigate if mbd-2 also co-localizes with H3K27me3 in other
organisms? 
3. The proposed model that H3K27me3 and/or H3K9me2/3 are involved in recruiting mbd-2 to genomic sites is based on the
ChIP-seq correlations. As noted by the authors (line 806), experiments to address causation (and the directionality of this
relationship) would strengthen the conclusions. 
4. Organization of the manuscript, errors, and figure presentation: 
a. Ordering: From Fig. 5 onwards, please ensure that the order of the text and figures (and panels within figures) correspond
to one another. Currently, the figure content is not discussed in sequence, and some panels are not discussed in the text at
all. 
b. Errors in display panels: In Fig. 6D, it appears that the incorrect data have been plotted in either the left or right panel,
since the upper and lower clusters should have identical numbers of rows in the right and left panels. Of note, this panel
(6D), is not discussed in the text and the trend is different than what is observed for the P0 data (Fig. 6C). In Fig 8D and Fig
S8B, it appears that panel labels are swapped (the “up”/n= 2262 and “no change”/n=6763 labels on the clusters). 
c. There is unnecessary redundancy in the ChIP-seq heatmaps. For example, the left panels of Fig. 5B, 5C, and 7B (mbd-2
ChIP-seq in 2 clusters) are identical to one another, and the left panel of Fig. 5E is shows 2 clusters that are likely also very
similar to these other 3 panels. These same data are also shown in Fig S6B/C as one cluster instead of 2 clusters. A more
efficient grouping of heatmaps is warranted. 
d. Please simplify the labels on all multi-cluster heatmaps so that they are easier to understand and a legible font size can
be used. 
e. Fig. 7A the y-scale may need to be adjusted for the sta-1 whole genome tracks, as they are dominated by 3 “peak” sites. 
Minor Points: 
5. Title – consider rephrasing “in DNA methylation-free C. elegans” – as written, this could be misinterpreted to mean that C.
elegans without DNA methylation were “made”, as opposed to that they naturally lack 5mC methylation 
6. Introduction Line 106 – “convergent evolution of 5mC loss” - Please clarify the meaning of and evidence for “convergent
evolution” in this context. 
7. Line 397, Figure 2 – I think that “bagging” is C. elegans-specific jargon. Please explain or replace with a term that is more
broadly understandable. 
8. Expression pattern (Figure 1 and S1) – is described as “ubiquitous” in all stages. It would be helpful to include images of
other larval stages (in addition to embryo and adults which are shown). There also appears to be minimal expression in the
distal germline. The Fig S1 caption also indicates young adults, but some of the animals have many embryos and do not
appear to be “young”. The HDA-1::GFP in particular have abnormal looking oocytes/embryos as well. 
9. Role in fertility – the mbd-2 mutant animals have very few progeny, however it is unclear how much contribution the egg
laying defect has to this outcome, especially in the generation with maternal contribution. An analysis of the
proliferation/health of the germline and presence of gametes (e.g. by DAPI staining or another method) in these animals
would more clearly show the essentiality in fertility.

10. Line 446: ~half of differentially expressed genes are bound by mbd-2. The significance is not clear, since mbd-2 targets
~50% of protein-coding genes. Does this overlap represent an enrichment over what is expected by chance? 
11. In the section discussing the RNA-seq results, e.g. Line 553 – “there is not a huge difference in …” , line 568 “seem to be
dysregulated to a larger extent” – these descriptions would be improved by more formal language and quantitative analysis/

12. Line 528 – citations should be included providing evidence of the histone modification reader activity of the mentioned

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The revised version of the manuscript addressed Major Comment #1: Does the cc-mutant exhibit a distinct transcriptional
effect from the full deletion. The authors conducted the requested additional experiment and came to the conclusion that the
transcriptional profiles are highly similar between the cc-mutant and the full mbd-2 knockout. These data put some cold
water on the possibility of non-NuRD MBD-2 function, unless these functions are also mediated by the cc-domain. Both
possibilities are addressed in the Discussion section. I found these data and analyses useful, and believe the field will
appreciate it, even if they are somewhat confusing. 
Regarding Major Comment #2: The model of H3K27me3 and non-NuRD recruitment; the authors adequately toned down
the discussion of this model given the lack of direct evidence for it. I would argue that it still gets too much coverage, but they
appropriately indicate that it remains speculative at this point. I have one remaining sticky point here though, the title of the
relevant Results section is "Transcription factor-Independent recruitment of mbd-2 to repressive chromatin". Logically
however, all they have shown is an absence of evidence for transcription factor recruitment, which is not evidence of
absence. Indeed, the authors conclude in the Discussion that other proteins like TFs are likely responsible for chromatin
recruitment - I recommend that the authors modify the title of the Results section to reflect that. 
Regarding Major Comment #3: The authors did a great job addressing the presentation and description of bioinformatic
analyses. The manuscript is much stronger as a result. 
In summary, I am satisfied with the revised manuscript and think it will be a valuable addition to the field.
