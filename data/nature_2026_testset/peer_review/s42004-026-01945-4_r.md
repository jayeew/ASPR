Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a well-structured and methodologically sound study that addresses the challenging problem of multi-
target drug discovery through an integrated evolutionary optimization framework. The work innovatively combines latent
representation learning with synthesis-aware prioritization, demonstrating clear improvements over existing methods in both
pharmacological efficacy and synthetic feasibility. The experimental evaluation is thorough, with compelling case studies in
Alzheimer’s disease and ovarian cancer. I recommend acceptance after major revisions, pending clarification and extension
of the following points: 
1.The paper states that EvoSynth is built upon the core architecture of MolSculptor, but the nature of their differences is not
systematically articulated. It would strengthen the contribution of EvoSynth if the authors could explicitly summarize the core
conceptual and architectural distinctions between the two frameworks. 
2.The paper describes two fine-tuning approaches (contrastive vs. predictor-based) but does not fully explain why the
contrastive method outperforms the predictor-based one in terms of stability and coherence. A more detailed discussion on
the sensitivity to labeled data scarcity and the inductive biases of each method would strengthen the methodological
justification. 
3.The study focuses on dual-target optimization as a proof of concept. While this is reasonable, the authors should discuss
how EvoSynth could be extended to true polypharmacology (≥3 targets) and what computational or representational
challenges might arise (e.g., latent space dimensionality, multi-objective Pareto front complexity). 
4.The framework relies heavily on docking-derived affinity labels, which may not always correlate with experimental binding.

The authors should acknowledge this limitation and suggest potential mitigations, such as ensemble docking, experimental
fine-tuning, or integration of binding affinity predictors trained on larger chemogenomic datasets. 
5.While SPARROW is effectively integrated, it is applied after evolutionary optimization. The authors should discuss
whether and how synthesis-aware objectives could be incorporated during evolution (e.g., via multi-objective optimization
including synthetic cost), and what trade-offs this might involve. 
6.The comparison with MolSculptor and CMD-GEN is appropriate, but the field of multi-target AI design is rapidly evolving. A
brief discussion of other recent approaches (e.g., pharmacophore-based, fragment-based, or reinforcement learning
methods) would better contextualize EvoSynth’s contributions. 
7.The paper highlights successful cases but does not discuss scenarios where synthesis planning failed or produced
prohibitively expensive routes. A short reflection on the limitations of ASKCOS/SPARROW in the context of novel scaffolds
would provide a more balanced perspective.

ABSTRACT7
We thank the editors and reviewers for their thoughtful and constructive feedback, which has substantially improved the clarity,
rigor, and scope of this work. In response, we expanded the related work to better contextualize EVOSYNTH among recent
multi-target affinity prediction and drug design methods, clarified key methodological distinctions from prior frameworks, and
improved the consistency of terminology and figure organization. We further strengthened the methodological discussion by
analyzing optimization robustness, elucidating the stability advantages of contrastive fine-tuning under limited supervision, and
expanding the discussion on synthesis-aware optimization, polypharmacology extensions, and practical limitations of docking-
and retrosynthesis-based evaluations. We sincerely hope that these revisions address the reviewers’ concerns and that the
revised manuscript will now be suitable for consideration for publication.

Response to Reviewer #110
1. Comment: Please discuss and cite MMatt-DTA which is an advance deep learning based multi-target binding affinity11
predictor. They also have webserver for real time prediction of drug-target affinities across multiple drugs and targets.12
Response: We thank the reviewer for this helpful suggestion. We agree that advanced deep learning–based multi-target13
binding affinity predictors such as MMatt-DTA can substantially improve both the efficiency and reliability of affinity14
supervision in frameworks like EVOSYNTH . In particular, such predictors can provide faster and more consistent multi-15
target affinity estimates than docking alone, reducing noise and computational cost while enabling scalable evaluation16
across multiple targets. We have expanded the Discussion section to explicitly acknowledge this direction and to discuss17
how EVOSYNTH could integrate or be guided by learned binding affinity predictors trained on large datasets, including18
MMatt-DTA, as a complementary or alternative supervision signal to docking-based affinity estimation. We have also19
added the corresponding citation to better contextualize this extension.20
2. Comment: Abbreviations for JNK3 and GSK3 β are used before they are defined. The same issue applies to the21
abbreviation OC for ovarian cancer in the Introduction.22
Response: Thank you for pointing this out. We have revised the manuscript to avoid introducing specific target names23
and abbreviations in the Introduction, where we now refer only to the two disease-level case studies. All target names24
and abbreviations (JNK3, GSK3β, PI3K, PARP1) are introduced and defined at their first occurrence at the beginning of25
the Results section, where each case study is described in detail.26
3. Comment: Some figures are cited in earlier sections but placed in later sections (e.g Figure 5 and 6). This makes it27
difficult to track figures. Please place figures next to section where these are cited or discussed.28
Response: Thank you for pointing this out. We have reorganized figure placement so that each figure appears immediately29
after its first mention and discussion.30
4. Comment: Evolutionary algorithms are known to get stuck in local minima/maxima; how did you tackle this issue?31
Response: We mitigate premature convergence through a combination of population-based multi-objective optimization32
and explicit diversity preservation. Specifically,EVOSYNTH employs NSGA-II with constraint-aware Pareto dominance33
to maintain multiple competing solutions, while crowding-distance–based selection 1 favors candidates in sparsely34
populated regions of the objective space, preventing population collapse around a single local optimum. In addition,35
stochastic perturbations introduced during candidate generation provide continual exploration of the search space.36

exploration of multi-target trade-offs. These design choices and their implementation have been added and described in38
detail in the revised Methods section.39
Response to Reviewer #240
1. Comment: The paper states that EVOSYNTH is built upon the core architecture of MolSculptor, but the nature of their41
differences is not systematically articulated. It would strengthen the contribution of EVOSYNTH if the authors could42
explicitly summarize the core conceptual and architectural distinctions between the two frameworks.43
Response: We thank the reviewer for this helpful comment. While the original manuscript already described the44
key contributions of EVOSYNTH , we agree that it did not explicitly frame these contributions in direct comparison45
to MolSculptor. We have therefore revised the Discussion section to clearly highlight both the shared backbone and46
the key conceptual and architectural differences between the two frameworks. In particular, we now emphasize that47
although EVOSYNTH builds upon MolSculptor’s latent evolutionary optimization framework, its score-informed latent48
alignment, integrated pharmacological and synthetic constraint handling, and synthesis-aware candidate prioritization49
collectively distinguish EVOSYNTH as a functionally guided and synthesis-aware extension for multi-target drug50
discovery.51
2. Comment: The paper describes two fine-tuning approaches (contrastive vs. predictor-based) but does not fully explain52
why the contrastive method outperforms the predictor-based one in terms of stability and coherence. A more detailed53
discussion on the sensitivity to labeled data scarcity and the inductive biases of each method would strengthen the54
methodological justification.55
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. We have revised Section 2.3 (Analysis of Score-Informed56
Latent Space) to provide a more detailed explanation of the differing inductive biases and data sensitivity of the two57
fine-tuning strategies. In particular, we now clarify that the contrastive objective enforces relative, pairwise alignment58
based on similarity of multi-target binding profiles, which yields a more robust and stable latent organization under59
limited and noisy labeled data. In contrast, the predictor-based objective directly regresses absolute affinity values and60
is therefore more sensitive to label scarcity and noise, leading to higher variability in latent space organization. This61
expanded discussion explains why contrastive fine-tuning consistently produces smoother and more coherent latent62
representations in our dual-target setting.63
3. Comment: The study focuses on dual-target optimization as a proof of concept. While this is reasonable, the authors64
should discuss how EVOSYNTH could be extended to true polypharmacology (≥ 3 targets) and what computational or65
representational challenges might arise (e.g., latent space dimensionality, multi-objective Pareto front complexity).66
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. We have expanded the Discussion section to explicitly67
describe how EVOSYNTH can be extended to polypharmacological optimization by augmenting the multi-objective68
affinity vector to include additional targets. We further discuss the key challenges introduced by this extension, including69
increased Pareto front complexity, reduced selection pressure in higher-dimensional objective spaces, and increased70
evaluation cost. Potential mitigation strategies, such as staged optimization, reference-point-based selection, and objective71
scalarization, are now discussed to clarify how these challenges could be addressed in future work.72
4. Comment: The framework relies heavily on docking-derived affinity labels, which may not always correlate with73
experimental binding. The authors should acknowledge this limitation and suggest potential mitigations, such as74
ensemble docking, experimental fine-tuning, or integration of binding affinity predictors trained on larger chemogenomic75
datasets.76
Response: We agree with this concern and have explicitly acknowledged this limitation in the Discussion section.77
We now discuss several potential mitigation strategies, including ensemble docking and rescoring, fine-tuning with78
experimentally measured binding data when available, and integrating binding affinity predictors pretrained on large-scale79
datasets to provide more robust and transferable supervision beyond docking alone.80
5. Comment: While SPARROW is effectively integrated, it is applied after evolutionary optimization. The authors should81
discuss whether and how synthesis-aware objectives could be incorporated during evolution (e.g., via multi-objective82
optimization including synthetic cost), and what trade-offs this might involve.83
Response: Thank you for this valuable suggestion. We have expanded the Discussion section to address how synthesis-84
aware objectives could be incorporated directly into the evolutionary optimization process, for example by augmenting the85
multi-objective formulation with surrogate estimates of synthetic cost or route complexity. We also discuss the associated86
trade-offs, including increased computational overhead and the need for efficient synthesis feasibility predictors, to87
provide a balanced perspective on this extension.88

7. Comment: The paper highlights successful cases but does not discuss scenarios where synthesis planning failed or99
produced prohibitively expensive routes. A short reflection on the limitations of ASKCOS/SPARROW in the context of100
novel scaffolds would provide a more balanced perspective.101
Response: We thank the reviewer for this valuable comment. We recognize that retrosynthesis tools such as ASKCOS102
can fail to identify feasible routes or may propose long and prohibitively expensive pathways, particularly for highly novel103
scaffolds. This limitation was a key motivation for integrating SPARROW into our framework. Rather than evaluating104
synthesis feasibility at the level of individual molecules, SPARROW operates over a population of candidate solutions105
and formulates candidate selection as a synthesis-aware optimization problem that explicitly balances therapeutic reward,106
reaction reliability, and material cost. As a result, candidates associated with empty routes or unfavorable cost–reliability107
trade-offs are naturally deprioritized, while synthetically tractable and affordable solutions are prioritized. We have108
clarified this motivation and mechanism in the revised manuscript, with explicit discussion added to the Introduction109
section, to provide a more balanced and transparent perspective on retrosynthesis planning in EVOSYNTH.110
