Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors report precise quantification and synthetic control of ligand composition by using a biphasic ligand exchange
technique. Controlling precisely the surface chemistry of nanoclusters is certainly a challenge and guidelines to do so will be
of significant interest to the synthetic nano community. This is especially true for use in biological applications where
homogeneity is critical for not only for quality control in their eventual application, but also to ensure the safety of those
eventually being treated with these materials. 
Specifically, the authors report using a biphasic ethyl acetate/water solvent system that is capable of controlling the yield of
exchange reactions while limiting undesired etching side reactions. Reaction kinetics and product distributions were
assessed via UV-Vis spectroscopy and ESI-MS, respectively. They additionally determined that increasing the polarity of the
non-aqueous solvent decreased the products of the etching side reactions and rationalized that the increased viscosity of
solvents limited thiolate diffusion rates in solution, though this also resulted in a lower ligand exchange yield overall. A
variety of feed ratios were also assessed to determine the concentration dependence of the incoming thiol on the reaction
products, and they were able to push the exchange reaction to near complete coverage at a high feed ratio, while protecting
the cluster core integrity by minimizing the etching side reactions. This is a very exciting result towards controlling ligand
surface coverage via exchange reactions. Further, the method was extended to include cationic and neutral ligands in
addition to the anionic pmba. Finally, the biodistribution of a variety of mixed ligand preparations were investigated in mice,
which showed that anionic clusters accumulated preferentially in the liver, and that by increasing the positive charge of the
ligand layer towards a zwitterionic distribution, the clearance pathway could be shifted towards the kidneys instead, which is
generally the preferred clearance mode. 
Overall, the study is significant in scope, findings and offers a new, straightforward strategy towards common synthetic goals
to generate nanoclusters for use in biological applications. 
The provided data (UV-Vis, ESI-MS) in the main text and SI are appropriate to address the questions asked and support the
conclusions drawn. The synthetic methodology is generally straightforward and employed both typical exchange reaction
conditions and resulting product analysis. In general, there appears to be enough information to reproduce the results. My
recommendation is the article is fit for publication in Nature Communications, but there were a few areas in the main text and
SI where clarification or additional information would be helpful to researchers looking to employ or build on this work. 
For example: 
• My only major point of confusion in the main text is lines 168 – 169, which I presume is a typo. “we determined it to be 0.3
— yLE =0.3— substantially” – are these minus signs? Or dashes? If this is an equation yLE =0 not 0.3 as stated elsewhere
in text and SI. Clarification needed here. 
• SI Line 45 states that after the addition of CO, the solution is sealed and “stored properly” with no indication of what
‘properly’ means, clarification would be helpful for anyone who wants to reproduce the work. 
• SI line 64: I am not clear if this is saying that three times the volume of 1 M NaCl was added (in addition to the 3-fold
alcohol) or if the final salt concentration was 1 M. Clarification would be helpful. 
• SI Line 202-203/Figure 9 I assume this is also at a ratio of 1:1? Not explicitly stated here, though it is stated as 1 elsewhere
in the SI.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments on “Atomically precise ligand engineering of gold nanoparticles via interphase mass transfer”. 
This paper reports on an elegant strategy for controlling the chemical composition of the organic layer adsorbed onto gold
nanocluster (Au25). The modification of the chemical composition of the organic shell can be performed by ligand exchange.
However, this method which appears easy to implement is not so trivial because the stoichiometry is not guaranteed, and
etching is generally observed. Since the interest for the gold clusters rests on their very precise composition at the origin of
their outstanding luminescent properties, the etching is clearly detrimental because it induces a non-controlled modification
of the composition and therefore of their properties. The authors demonstrated that a rigorous control of the chemical
composition of the organic shell can be obtained while limiting the etching of the gold core. For achieving this, they
developed an interphase-assisted ligand exchange method. In other words, the exchange of the ligands adsorbed onto the
gold cluster dispersed in water by incoming ligands from the organic phase was performed at the interphase between the
aqueous and the organic phases. The study seems to be carefully performed. Although the results appear interesting, main
issues must be addressed for rendering the results and their interpretation more convincing. 
1. Since the biodistribution experiments are focused on zwitterionic gold Au25 clusters (AuNC) obtained by a partial
replacement of pMBSA by ATP through the interphase-assisted ligand exchange method, the reader can be surprised by the
fact that the interphase-assisted ligand exchange method was mainly validated with the exchange of pMBSA by pMBA. It
appears more suited that the study was focused on the exchange of pMBSA by ATP and then extended to other ligands for
generalization or for a better understanding of the process by varying the incoming ligands and eventually the ligands of the
organic shell. 
2. The interphase-assisted ligand exchange reaction which is in fact a two-phase ligand exchange reaction was compared
to a single-phase ligand exchange reaction. This comparison clearly shows the advantages of the interphase-assisted
ligand exchange reaction for ensuring a better control of the composition of the AuNC after the ligand exchange. 
However, the single-phase ligand exchange was performed by mixing the AuNC with the total amount of ligands used for
the interphase-assisted ligand exchange reaction. It should be more relevant for the comparison if the required number of
incoming ligands was added during the single-phase ligand exchange process through very small fractions with a well-
defined interval of time between each addition. 
3. pMBSA, pMBA, MPA, MHA, ATP are organic molecules whose charge depends on the pH. The study was carried out at
pH 7 in the aqueous phase. How was controlled the pH in the aqueous phase? Information on the control of pH are lacking
in the manuscript and in the Supporting Info. Moreover, the effect of the pH in the ligand exchange deserves to be
investigated because the impact on the solubility and therefore on the process is expected. 
4. The partial replacement of pMBSA adsorbed onto the AuNC by incoming ligands with a weaker hydrophilicity should
generate an alteration of the colloidal stability. The authors are encouraged to extensively study the colloidal stability over
the time, as a function of pH around the physiological pH (e.g. between 4 and 9) and of ionic strength. Ionic strength should
be extensively studied because it exerts a great influence on the colloidal stability when the nanoparticles are electrically
charged. Since the biological fluids and eventually the suspension prepared for injection (in order to fix the osmolarity)
contain ions, undesirable precipitation can occur after in vivo administration. 
5. Is the fluorescence intensity modified by the variation in the chemical composition of the organic shell of AuNC? This
issue should be addressed since the biodistribution study is based on the fluorescence imaging. 
6. From the in vivo and ex vivo imaging, it seems difficult to conclude on the consequence of the modification of the chemical
composition of the organic shell. First a color scale is missing. It makes the comparison between images difficult. Moreover,
the comparison of the in vivo and ex vivo images acquired 20 minutes after the administration is astonishing. The amount of
fluorescent AuNC in the liver 20 minutes after intravenous injection appears higher in the case of Au25(p-MBSA)18-n(ATP)n
(1/150) than in the case of non-modified AuNC (Au25(p-MBSA)18) according to Figures 6a and 6b. But Figure 6d clearly
shows that the fluorescence intensity is very high in the liver after intravenous injection of Au25(p-MBSA)18 and moderate
after intravenous injection of Au25(p-MBSA)18-n(ATP)n (1/150). There is an apparent contradiction between the results
displayed in Figures 6a,b and Figure 6d. 
The fluorescence in the bladder and in the kidneys confirms that the AuNC described in this manuscript are removed from
body by renal clearance. However, the quantification of the renal clearance from measurements in the kidneys and in the
bladder is not appropriate. The presence of AuNC in the kidneys does not prove a renal clearance because the AuNC can
be trapped for a long time in the kidney’s tissues. Such a renal clearance is effectively confirmed by the presence of AuNC in
the bladder. But the quantification of renal clearance from measurements in the bladder is difficult to compare between the
animals because the filling of the bladder is very different for each animal and because the imaging session can be
performed with full or empty bladder. It is not easy to control this situation. The comparison is therefore difficult to achieve. To
remove any doubt, it is necessary to analyze urine and feces. It implies the use of rodent metabolic cages which permits to
regularly collect the urine and the feces. Their analyses will provide interesting and more reliable data for the removal of the
AuNC from the body. 
Finally, the results must be confirmed by a complementary analysis. For instance, the chemical titration of gold element in
the different organs by ICP-MS analysis should permit to enrich the discussion and to strengthen the conclusion. 
7. The authors emphasized in the introduction of the manuscript on the advantages of the small size of AuNC in biological

applications. Effectively ultrasmall nanoparticles are generally well suited for renal clearance but it depends also on the

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
It is well known that an excess of free ligands in solution can effectively initiate ligand exchange on the surface of molecular
Au nanoclusters (NCs). However, excessive ligands can also promote undesired structural transformation or decomposition
of the cluster core through etching, making it challenging to obtain clusters with the desired ligand composition. In this work,
the authors develop a novel interfacial mass transfer strategy that enables atomic-level precise ligand exchange on Au
₅₂
nanoclusters. This method significantly slows down the etching reaction while minimally affecting the ligand exchange
kinetics, thereby achieving high ligand exchange efficiency while preserving the structural integrity of the Au
₅₂
 core. 
As a potential application, the authors demonstrate that the surface charge density of Au NCs can be finely tuned via ligand
exchange—specifically, by controlling the number of cationic and anionic ligands—leading to organ-selective accumulation
for NIR II imaging. 
Overall, the ligand exchange strategy presented in this work is a significant advancement, providing a means to efficiently
achieve well-defined ligand variation on AuNCs while minimizing structural degradation. Nevertheless, it should be noted
that similar Au clusters can still be synthesized by conventional methods, albeit with lower yields (typically one-half to one-
third). Additionally, the reported findings on NIR-II imaging and organ-selective accumulation may parallel previously
reported phenomena for small molecules and quantum dots (e.g., Nano Lett. 2009, 9(6), 2354–2359; Acc. Chem. Res. 2016,
49(9), 1731–1740; Nat. Photon. 2024, 18, 535–547). The most critical limitation appears to lie in the general applicability of
the strategy, particularly regarding the selection of starting compounds and solvents. Based on the current data, the method
seems applicable only to a subset of water-soluble clusters with specific ligand structures. 
The manuscript is well-structured and supported by detailed experiments, but its overall impact may be limited to the fields of

Fundamentally, thiol-induced core etching refers to free thiols actually stripping gold 
atoms from a cluster or larger particle, often fragmenting it into smaller clusters or 
Au(I)-thiolate complexes. The electronic effect of the thiol ligands (i.e., electron 
withdrawing or electron donating) plays a critical role in this process. E lectron-
withdrawing end- groups at the para sites of aromatic thiols (e.g. –COOH) pull 
electron density away from the thiolate  group. The thiolate group becomes less 
electron-rich (the negative charge is delocalized or stabilized by the substituent),  
which reduced S to Au donation and enhanced Au to S donation. The dual effects

make Au-S bond strength remain high, but its nature becomes more ionic. It means 
the high electronegativity of such ligands likely stabilizes Au in the +1 valence state

(Nat. Commun., 2023, 14, 3374). In contrast, an  electron donating end aromatic 
thiol (e.g. -NH3) (more electron-rich sulfur) may prefer to stabilize the Au site in a

oxidatively etched into Au(I)-SR complexes.
