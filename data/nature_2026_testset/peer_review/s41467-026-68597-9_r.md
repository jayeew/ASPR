Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports high-performance red LEDs based on a 0D organic-inorganic antimony halide (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8,
achieving an impressive EQE of 19.4%. The authors attribute the improvement to the use of a semiconducting carbazole-
functionalized organic cation. However, the core concept and material design were already published in Advanced Materials
in 2023 (reference 16 in the manuscript), where a nearly identical compound, TPPcarzSbBr4 (the same as
(TPPPhCz)2Sb2Br8 in this work), was introduced and demonstrated as a red emitter. The present study very much reused
the same strategy, but claimed a record EQE of 19.4% for (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 based devices and ~17% for
(TPPPhCz)2Sb2Br8, substantially higher than the ~5% reported for the same material in reference 16. The manuscript does
not address or explain this discrepancy, raising serious concerns about novelty, clarity, and consistency. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors present a comprehensive study on antimony halide hybrid systems with carbazole-
functionalized organic cations. Their work demonstrates the critical role of carbazole-based TPPEtCz+ organic cations in
facilitating high-quality film formation, suppressing non-radiative recombination, and enhancing electrical conductivity.
Furthermore, the fabricated antimony halide-based LED devices achieve a record-high external quantum efficiency and an
extended half-lifetime, representing a significant advancement in the metal halide LED field. While the manuscript is well-
structured and merits publication in Nature Communications, some conclusions require additional clarification and
experimental evidence. 
1. The authors attribute the slowed crystallization rate of (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 films to TPPEtCz+ cations. However, the
underlying mechanism remains unclear. Could the authors provide further discussion or experimental evidence to elucidate
how these organic cations modulate crystallization kinetics? 
2. A red shift is observed in the electroluminescence spectra (Supplementary Fig. 5) compared to the photoluminescence
spectra (Fig. 2e). What causes this discrepancy? 
3. The turn-on voltage (more than 6 V) for large-area devices based on (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 appears relatively high
(Supplementary Fig. 19a). This phenomenon is also observed in the other carbazole-derived LEDs. What underlying factors
contribute to these high turn-on voltages in the large-area devices and carbazole-derived systems? 
4. The authors state that “For the (TPPEt)2SbBr5, the calculated projected density of states (pDOS) show that the top of
bandgap consists mainly of the valence band maximum (VBM) of Sb 5s and Br 4p orbitals, while the bottom of bandgap is
composed of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of TPPEt+ (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 7a).” However, it
should be noted that both the VBM and LUMO are calculated for the properties of the entire (TPPEt)2SbBr5 system and
cannot be meaningfully assigned to individual fragments such as TPPEt+ or SbBr52-. Therefore, the statement of “the lowest
unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of TPPEt+” and HOMO, LUMO, VBM, CBM labeled in Fig.2a-d are very confusing.
The authors need to clarify this statement and ensure that the type I or type II semiconductor of these hybrids are consistent
with the definition in the heterostructure semiconductors.

Referee’s Report 
In this study, Zhuangzhuang Ma et al. present a report on promising solution processed zero-dimensional hybrid antimony
(Sb) based emitters for highly-efficient LEDs, demonstrating a promising approach towards lead-free high-performance
optoelectronic devices. This study represents a significant and important breakthrough in the field of zero-dimensional
organic-inorganic hybrid LED systems and is therefore highly relevant and interesting in this field. However, the reviewer
suggests the authors consider the following points for strengthening the manuscript prior to publication in Nature
Communications. 
1 As shown in Fig. 1f, there has been a several reports on LEDs employing hybrid metal halide emitters in recent years. In
addition to the Cu, Mn, and Sb-based systems presented by the authors, numerous studies on other metal halides (e.g., Sn-
based) have also been reported. It is therefore suggested that such examples be included in Fig. 1f of the main text or in a
supplementary table. 
2 In the device operational stability data shown in Fig. 1h, the measurement intervals during operation appear to range from
several minutes to several tens of minutes. Such intervals may not be sufficient to accurately assess the true operational
stability of the devices. In addition, the measurement intervals for the control device and the target device also appear to
differ. For greater clarity and reliability, the operational stability results should be presented with consistent and clearly
defined measurement intervals. In addition, the current densities of the control device and the target device at each
luminance value (specified in the main text as 100 and 30 cd/m2) are also expected to differ, and these values should be
clearly presented as well. 
3 Regarding the storage stability data shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, was this measurement conducted on large-area
LEDs? It would also be beneficial to present the actual device parameters alongside the relative luminance values: for
example, the measurement voltage, the corresponding luminance, EQE, and other parameters. 
4 From the PL spectra of each film, the FWHM of the target sample appears to be broader than that of the control sample.
Could the authors provide a possible explanation for this observation? In addition, the target sample seems to exhibit a
larger Stokes shift. It would be helpful if the authors could provide an explanation for these observations. 
5 In the in-situ PL data shown in Fig. 2h, the PL intensity of TPPEtCz appears to be very strong. Is this peak attributable to
the inherently strong PL emission of the organic material itself? Furthermore, according to the experimental section, the spin-
coating time is reported as 60 sec with an acceleration rate of 500 rpm/s, whereas the data presented in Fig. 2h only covers
the first 8 sec. Information on the subsequent time period is also important. How do the crystallization process and optical
properties evolve beyond this point? In addition, please provide the optical properties before and after annealing for a more
complete understanding. 
6 The overall flow of the study regarding the development of organic cations for Sb-halide emitters to LED application is
appropriate; however, the transition to the final section on “Universality characterization” could be made more seamless. If
the authors wish to present this part in the main figures, it is recommended that they provide a detailed explanation for
introducing the additional analogous derivatives, or alternatively, consider addressing this content in the supplementary
figures and notes. Furthermore, it is advised to include a detailed discussion on the observed differences in PLQY and
conductivity among the derivatives, as well as a comprehensive explanation of the performance variations of devices
incorporating each carbazole-functionalized organic cation. 
7 Several minor comments 
i. The red labeling in Fig. 1h, shown as (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br5, appears to be incorrect. It seems more likely that it should be
(TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8. The authors are kindly requested to verify this point. 
ii. The emission and absorption spectra of the two emitters appear to differ (Fig. 2e). Could the authors provide the EL
spectrum of (TPPEt)2SbBr5? If the bandgaps of the two emitters are indeed different, then the energy level diagram
presented in Supplementary Fig. 17 should also reflect this difference. The authors are therefore requested to clarify this
point. 
iii. On page 19, in the Device Fabrication section (line 312), the text states “onto the substrate at 4000 rmp for 60s.” This
appears to be a typo and should be corrected to “4000 rpm” In addition, it would be helpful for clarity and reproducibility if the
defined device area is explicitly specified in this section. 
iv. In the Device Characterizations section, it is stated that LED performance was measured using an integrating sphere and
a QD-Pro spectrometer, and further double-checked with a PR705 spectrophotometer. Could the authors clarify whether the
device performance data presented in the main text are those obtained from the integrating sphere measurements? In
addition, it is recommended that the double-checked data also be included in the supplementary Figure, as this would
provide stronger validation of the reported device performance. 
We hope the authors find these comments constructive and helpful in further improving the manuscript.

1)  Theoretical insights into π−π interaction at (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8/TPBi interface 
We systematically investigated the mechanisms underlying the enhanced charge transport 
attributed to the π−π interaction at the (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8/TPBi interface. From a molecular 
point of view, the molecular structures of (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 and TPBi have planar 
π−conjugated aromatic rings of carbazole and benzimidazole, respectively. Electrostatic 
surface potential (ESP) analysis reveals that the central regions of these aromatic rings form 
electron-rich (negative) centers, while the hydrogen atoms at the peripheral positions exhibit 
electron-deficient (positive) characteristics (Fig. R4). These π−conjugated aromatic rings 
with complementary charge distribution create favorable conditions for intermolecular π−π
stacking between (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 and TPBi, which provides an effective pathway for 
enhanced charge transport ( Adv. Mater. 37, e05597, 2025 ). In contrast, the ethyl-terminated 
surface of the control (TPPEt)2SbBr5 lacks extended conjugated planes, making effective π−π
interaction with TPBi highly improbable.

To further elucidate the π−π interactions, we theoretically studied the interaction between the 
TPBi layer and (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8. We calculated the adsorption energy (Eads) of TPBi on the 
(TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 (001) surface to identify the thermodynamically favorable stacking

2)  Experimental demonstrations of π−π interaction between (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 and 
TPBi 
We conducted the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Fourier transform infrared 
spectroscopy (FTIR), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements to reveal the 
mechanisms of π−π interactions between (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 and TPBi. In the high-resolution 
XPS spectrum of N 1 s (Fig. R6a), the pure (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 films exhibit a single C−N 
peak (400.11 eV) from carbazole moiety, while the pure TPBi films not only show C−N peak 
(400.60 eV) and C=N peak (398.48 eV) from benzimidazole moiety, but also display a 
characteristic π−π satellite peak (405.82 eV) of N-containing aromatic compounds. 
Importantly, in the TPBi-treated (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 films, all relevant N 1 s peaks, including 
the C−N peak of carbazole, the C−N peak and C=N peak of benzimidazole, undergo 
discernible shifts compared with their positions in the pure TPBi and (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 films, 
indicating an electronic interaction between (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 and TPBi. Moreover, the π−π
satellite peak is significantly enhanced and broadened in the TPBi-treated (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8
films, confirming an existence of the parallel stacking structure dominated by π−π
interactions.

The FTIR spectra shows that the C−N and C=N stretching vibrations of TPBi-treated 
(TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 films shifted higher wavenumbers relative to those in pure TPBi films (Fig. 
R6b), consistent with enhanced bond polarization resulting from π−π stacking between the 
conjugated benzimidazole and carbazole rings.  
Further confirmation of this π−π interaction is provided by proton 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 
R6c). The 1H chemical shift of benzimidazole moieties in the pure TPBi was located at 
7.45−7.09 ppm (labeled as H1−9), while these signals shift downfield to 7.42 −7.02 ppm in the 
TPBi-treated (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 sample. This shift arises because the protons on the planar 
conjugated rings involved in π−π stacking enter the shielding region generated by another 
aromatic ring, indicating that the induced magnetic field from the ring current of the aromatic 
ring opposes the external magnetic field, offsetting part of the external field and requiring a 
higher external magnetic field to induce proton resonance. In contrast, the protons of the 
carbazole moieties in (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 (labeled as H 10–13) show the opposite trend, with 
their 1H chemical shifts move upfield from 8.17–7.85 ppm to 8.23–7.90 ppm, indicating the 
formation of a shielding region generated by the carbazole-conjugated moieties. Collectively, 
these 1H NMR results confirm that π−π interactions form via the stacking between 
benzimidazole (from TPBi) and carbazole (from (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8) moieties.

3)  Influence of π−π interaction on charge transport 
We supplemented kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) and ultraviolet photoelectron 
spectroscopy (UPS) measurements to characterize the charge transport properties of two 
samples. As shown in Fig. R7a, the average contact potential difference ( VCPD) of the pure 
(TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 films was 302.6 mV, which increased significantly to 330.4 mV for 
TPBi-treated (TPPEtCz) 2Sb2Br8 films. This increase in VCPD indicates a reduced work 
function (WF) in the TPBi-treated (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 compared to the pure (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8, 
promoting more efficient electron injection from TPBi to the emitter layer ( Appl. Phys. Lett. 
120, 243501, 2022 ). According to the cutoff region of the UPS spectra, the WF of 
TPBi-treated (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 and (TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 films is estimated to be 4.83 and 4.88 
eV , respectively (Fig. R7b). The reduction of the WF caused by π−π stacking interaction 
favors the electron transport of emitter layer, which is consistent with previous reports ( Light 
Sci. Appl. 14, 285, 2025; Adv. Funct. Mater. 34, 2402522, 2024 ). In addition, the VBM of 
(TPPEtCz)2Sb2Br8 films is increased from −5.82 eV to −5.66 eV , and the corresponding CBM 
is increased from –3.26 eV to −3.10 eV (Fig. R7b). The overall upward shift of the energy 
level of the emitter layer matched better with the energy levels of the transport layers, thus 
reducing the injection barrier and charge accumulation at the interface, which is conducive to 
the faster injection of both hole and electron (Fig. R7c). On the contrary, the VCPD, WF and 
energy level structure of the control (TPPEt) 2SbBr5 films and TPBi-treated (TPPEt) 2SbBr5
films remained almost unchanged due to the lack of π−π stacking interaction between the 
(TPPEt)2SbBr5 and TPBi (Figs. R7d−f).
