Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Balch et al. report multiple cryo-EM structures of the human telomerase during the nucleotide addition cycle. As expected
from the Nguyen lab, the here presented biochemical and structural data is of high quality. The findings are important for our
general understanding of telomerase biology and contribute an important next step in further elucidating the fundamental
mechanism of nucleotide addition. The manuscript is well written and the figures are clear. I recommend publication of this
manuscript in Nature Communications. 
The authors should, however, extend their methods section. Description of the telomerase reconstitution and purification is
extremely short. It becomes extremely tedious to follow all cited references in this section and the way this paragraph of the
method section is written obfuscates reproducibility. Therefore, the authors must write an extended methods section to
adequately describe the state-of-the-art experimental procedures for the reconstitution and purification of the telomerase
including precise DNA sequences used. Additionally, the authors should also make the code available used for plotting the
conformational changes. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports three new cryo-EM structures of human telomerase (3.2, 3.5, and 3.8 Å), catching the
enzyme/primer/incoming nucleotide complex at three stages of catalysis: initiation, elongation, and pre-termination. The
structural analysis is of high quality. One important new finding is that the TRAP-wedge and the TEN N-terminal helix bind
the DNA and RNA. However, in light of previous structural analysis of human telomerase from this group and others,
decades-long biochemical and biophysical analyses of human and Tetrahymena telomerase, and the resolution (medium
resolution at best) of the presented structures, the increase in knowledge of the telomerase reaction mechanism from the
remaining experiments is not significant. 
Major comments 
1. It is not clear what new knowledge is gained from the cartoons and 3D structures in Figures 1 and 2. These figures
contain information available from previous cryo-EM structures at similar or even better resolution from this lab, Juli Feigon’s
lab, and Ming Lei’s lab. 
2. Figure 3 describes how a 4-5 bp template-primer complex forms regardless of the sequence of the duplex region, which
has been observed in previous structures of human telomerase and Tetrahymena telomerase, and suggested by previous
telomerase activity and single molecule studies of both human and Tetrahymena telomerase (example: Julian J-L Chen
lab’s studies). 
3. The reason for how a constant RNA-DNA hybrid is maintained has been demonstrated previously from the human cryo-
EM structures at similar resolution from the Lei lab. In a study published in Cell Research (Wan et al, 2021, Zipper head
mechanism of telomere synthesis by human telomerase), the authors describe how TERT L980 “zipper head” acts to unzip
old RNA-DNA base pairing when new bases are added to the 3’ end of DNA. There is a figure panel in that study that is
similar to Figure 3 (c, e and g) panels in this manuscript and Wan et al also propose this as part of their final model in the
paper. So, in addition to visualizing the incoming nucleotide, Figure 3 of the manuscript under review doesn’t add much new
insight.

4. Figure 4 gives a name for the regions flanking the RNA template as 5’ and 3’ linkers, but the importance of these regions
and the model for elongation and contraction of these regions as nucleotide addition proceeds is well appreciated already,
as is noted by the authors. It might be argued that this study provides a structural basis for the model. But is this a strong
argument, given the low to medium resolution of the structures and the heavy processing (DRRAFTER modeling) needed to
bring out these linker regions? The map-model fit for the linkers - as shown for the RNA-DNA in the fifth supplementary
figure - should be shown for the reader to assess the quality of the modeled linkers. 
5. In a recent publication from this lab, dimerization of telomerase is suggested to be important for assembly of the enzyme
but the authors did not rule out contributions to activity. Did the authors pick dimeric particles in their nucleotide bound
structures to ask if the dimer is compatible with nucleotide binding? What changes occur in the dimer upon nucleotide
binding that would favor, disfavor, or not affect the active site configuration? These seem like important follow-up questions
to address for that publication that the current study is well suited to address. 
6. TPT increases telomerase processivity. Comparative analysis of dNTP-bound telomerase and telomerase-TPT should be
performed to differentiate between features coming from telomerase and those from bound TPT. 
Other suggestions: 
1. Consider moving figures 1-3 to the supplement based on comments above. 
2. The manuscript uses new terminology for 5’ and 3’ linkers in the RNA. These need to be defined in the context of the
template boundary element (TBE) and template recognition element (TRE). TBE and TRE should be shown in Figure 4A for
example. 
3. The model in figure 6 is quite similar to the analogous model figure in the Cell Research cryo-EM paper of the Lei lab, with
the exception of the TRAP wedge. Consider moving this figure to the supplement unless the point of the figure is the TRAP
wedge (in which case the figure should make that clear). 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study presented by Balch et al represents a structure-focused study that contributes to the understanding of telomerase
function, most notably the steps involved in repeat addition processivity. The authors produce three high quality structures of
the catalytic core of the telomerase holoenzyme in complex with substrate DNA and incoming nucleotide at three different
stages of telomere extension: initiation, elongation, and termination. These structures demonstrate that the telomerase RNA
template binds to substrate DNA through a constant 4-5 base RNA-DNA duplex and confirms that L980 functions as a
"zipper head," maintaining the 4-5 base duplex as the substrate is extended. Additionally, the authors demonstrated
mechanisms through which the 3' and 5' flanking regions of the RNA template affect telomerase activity and the role of the
TEN domain in DNA substrate binding. In general, the study is well conceived, organized, executed, and communicated.
The manuscript does a particularly good job of integrating the new findings presented with published work to, collectively,
provide needed explanations and interpretations related to the nuances of telomerase structure and function. In our opinion,
the manuscript is acceptable for publication. However, we include some suggestions and comments for the authors to
consider when revising their manuscript.

We appreciate the reviewer’s concern regarding the resolution of the map at the linker regions. 
As stated in the manuscript, the flexible nature of the ssRNA 5′ and 3′ linkers limits the 
resolution of these areas, as indicated by the local resolution estim ates (Extended Data Fig. 
5d, i, n). 
 
In both cryo-EM and X-ray crystallography, it is standard practice to present and deposit one 
map and one corresponding model for each structure. Conventional cryo -EM workflows 
generate a single map filtered to a consen sus resolution determined by the gold -standard 
Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) criterion. Consequently, density for flexible regions typically 
appears weaker in this consensus map. When we generated a map filtered to local resolution, 
the densities for the  linker regions appeared clear and continuous, though with lower -
resolution features. To model these regions, we lowered the map threshold and applied map 
blurring in Coot, ensuring that the backbone density remained consistent with the map.  
 
The resultin g models were refined using standard model refinement protocols. During 
refinement, B-factors (temperature factors) are assigned to each residue, with higher B-factors 
reflecting greater flexibility, thus providing another representation of molecular dynamics within 
the model. 
 
While these procedures are routine, they inherently yield only a single structural representation 
of the ssRNA linkers. This static depiction cannot fully capture the conformational variability of

these regions. To better address thi s limitation, we collaborated with the laboratory of Rhiju 
Das, experts in RNA modeling. They generated an ensemble of the ten most probable 
conformations for the ssRNA linkers and the RNA template interacting with the DNA substrate 
(Extended Data Fig. 8 d–f). We believe this ensemble approach is valuable for depicting the 
intrinsic flexibility of the ssRNA linkers beyond the conventional one -map–one-model 
paradigm. 
 
Following the reviewer’s suggestion, we have now included map–model fits for the consensus 
model of the ssRNA region of hTR (nucleotides 38–63), encompassing the 5′ linker, template 
region, and 3′ linker, in Extended Data Fig. 8a–c.

In contrast, our study presents three experimentally determined structures of the human telomerase catalytic core 
bound to three distinct substrate permutations, each captured in the presence of an incoming nucleotide. These 
structures enable us to rationalise numerous prior biochemical and genetic observations and, importantly, reveal 
previously unrecognized regions within TERT and hTR that play critical roles in telomeric repeat addition. Given that 
telomeric repeat addition is central to telomerase function in vivo, our work substantially advances the mechanistic 
understanding of human telomerase activity.
