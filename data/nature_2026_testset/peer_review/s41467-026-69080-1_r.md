Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Zhu et al. describes a fully autonomous, artificial intelligence-based method to selective induce
molecular reactions in a molecule. Specifically, the group selectively induced carbon-bromine bond dissociations across a
molecule with multiple C-Br bonds. Without further human help, the algorithm determines the optimal tip position relative to
the molecule as well as the optimal pulse parameters to cause the chemical reaction. 
The work is clearly beyond the state-of-the-art and represents a major advancement in the field. The approach is (for the
most part) clearly described and reproducible, and the outcomes are intriguing and of very high interest to the field. We
recommend to publish this article subject to a few clarifications in the text. 
Major suggestions: 
Figure 3 is very extensive, but hard to read, and misses critical information. In particular, the number of attempts (i.e., a scale
bar for the number of attempts) is missing, making it difficult to assess how well the statistics are converged for smaller
sample sizes. Moreover, the grey area is said to contain all other cases – however, from a description of the green and the
red area, it is unclear to us what that could be. The clarity of the paper would clearly benefit if this was explained in more
detail. 
The statistics for the ideal parameters are difficult to assess from the provided figures. We would highly appreciate an
additional figure that depicts the success rate as function of the pulse parameters (e.g., voltage and current), for a fixed tip
position (i.e., the mostly used one for a specific debromination step). 
To evaluate the performance of their approach, the authors analyze only the final portion (the last 30%) of the learning
process. However, this appears to suggest that the algorithm was still engaged in exploration and thus operating with
suboptimal parameters during this period. It would therefore be informative to report the success rate obtained when
exploration is disabled, and the trained reinforcement learning agent executes manipulations using only the learned optimal
parameters. 
With respect to the reported success rates, the authors indicate that individual bond cleavages achieve success rates of 50–
60%, while a complete cleavage cycle still yields a success rate exceeding 40%. This description remains somewhat
ambiguous. If each individual cleavage has a success probability of 50%, the overall probability of completing a four-step
cleavage cycle would be expected to be approximately 0.5*0.5*0.5*0.5 = 1/16 (≈6%). It therefore appears that some
clarification may be warranted, perhaps regarding how “success” is defined for individual versus full-cycle processes. A
more precise and explicit definition of the success criteria for each stage would enhance the clarity of this section. 
Minor corrections: 
Please provide the range of parameters that the program was allowed to explore. How were these ranges chosen? 
What is the definition of an episode in this context. How does 948 bond-manipulation processes fall into 341 episodes? 
Could the authors comment on what, in the context of this paper, is meant by “molecular degradation”?

(Remarks to the Author) 
The development of autonomous site-specific chemical reactions in molecular systems is an area on intense research and
breakthrough works using STM have only appeared in the last few years. This work represents a highly interesting advance
on previous studies, and a key step on the way to fully autonomous molecular construction bond-by-bond. 
It is important to mention that ref. 41 already uses very similar computer vision and deep learning approaches (DRL, SAC
etc, originally introduced to STM control in 10.1038/s41467-022-35149-w) to selectively dissociate C-Br bonds in a molecule
on Au (111). As such, the importance of this work hinges on how selective and sequential the approach is when a single
molecule has multiple "manipulation" targets, and then how general the approach is when applied to other systems.
Particular highlights presented include a detailed understanding of the statistics of sequential C-Br bond removal and an
analysis of success rates vs. tip location for both single and multiple dissociation events. The development of the DRL
method with the introduction of IT-ER is also an impressive advance. It is clear that this work deserves publication in
general, but to reach the standards demanded by Nat. Comm. I would ask the authors to consider the following comments:

Minor corrections: 
1. Please provide the range of parameters that the program was allowed to explore. How were these 
ranges chosen? 
Our reply: The ranges of the bias voltage and tunneling current used by the reinforcement learning 
agent were determined based on both previous literature reports of STM tip -induced C -Br bond 
cleavage and our own pre -experimental characterization on the TPP -Br4/Au(111) system. Previous 
studies have shown that the activation threshold for C -Br dissociation varies substantially across 
different molecular systems and substrates (i.e., J. Am. Chem. Soc. 147, 1240–1250 (2025), ACS Nano 
19, 10255–10262 (2025)), with reported bias values spanning from approximately 2.2 V on insulating 
surfaces to above 4.0 V on metallic surfaces, and tunneling currents ranging from the pA to nA scale. 
Guided by these precedents, we conduct ed manual tip-induced dissociation experiments to identify 
the operational window within which selective single -bond cleavage can occur reproducibly. We

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

6 
 
found that when the applied bias and setpoint current exceed approximately 4.3 V and 1.6 nA, the 
molecule presumably undergoes irreversible degradation, often accompanied by abrupt changes in 
tip condition, whereas no dissociation is observed below approximately 2.3 V and 0.2 nA. 
Accordingly, to ensure that the autonomous agent explores a meaningful space, we restricted the 
parameter space to 2.4–4.2 V (Δ = 0.05 V) in bias voltage and 0.3–1.5 nA (Δ = 0.05 nA) in current. 
A similar consideration applies to the spatial action space: although we initially tested larger selection 
regions for tip position s, this significantly slowed training without improving reaction control. We 
therefore adopted the 13 discrete tip positions defined in Fig. 2e, which provide sufficient spatial 
resolution around the C-Br sites while preserving efficient policy convergence. 
Our action: We have added this clarification regarding the selection and justification of the 
parameter ranges to the Methods section 1.2 of the Supplementary information. 
 
2. What is the definition of an episode in this context. How does 948 bond -manipulation processes 
fall into 341 episodes? 
Our reply: In our reinforcement -learning framework, an episode refers to a complete reaction 
attempt on a single molecule following a predefined dissociation pathway. An episode begins when 
the agent first interacts with a molecule and ends when either (i) all four C -Br bonds have been 
successfully cleaved to yield TPP 4*, or (ii) an irreversible failure occurs, such as undesired multi -
bond dissociation, activation at wrong sites leading to loss of selectivity, or mole cular damage that 
renders the molecule unrecognizable . The RL agent continues attempting the same step until a 
successful transition or a failure occurs. Moreover, a molecule that experiences a failure event may 
still remain intact C -Br bonds and thus can be re-engaged in a new episode, starting from its new 
molecular state. Therefore, a single molecule can contribute to more than one episode. 
Our action : We have added a concise clarification of the episode definition to the Results and 
Discussion section of the manuscript to avoid ambiguity. 
 
3. Could the authors comment on what, in the context of this paper, is meant by “molecular 
degradation”? 
Our reply and action: In this work, “molecular degradation” refers to cases where the applied STM 
manipulation parameters are sufficiently aggressive that the molecule loses its recognizable structural 
signature in the STM image . For example, when the bias voltage exceeds appro ximately 4.0 V, 
multiple bonds beyond the targeted C-Br bond may be cleaved, leading to disruption of the porphyrin 
framework or complete molecular disassembly on the surface . After such events, the molecule can 
no longer be identified by our machine learning algorithm and thus cannot proceed along the designed 
reaction pathway. In our reinforcement learning implementation, these events are treated equivalently 
to cases of molecular disappearance, as both result in the autonomous system being unable to loca te 
and continue operating on the molecule. A clarification of the term “molecular degradation” has now 
been incorporated.

7. Fig. S10: how optimized is the reward function? is this general or specific to the particular 
molecule and reaction process being studied? 
Our reply: This is  indeed a very important and sometimes tricky issue in training RL agent. In 
principle, the reward function used in this work follows standard principles of reward shaping in 
reinforcement learning for physical control systems, where the goal is to provide the agent with 
informative and progressively structured feedback rather than a sparse terminal reward. The key idea 
is that tip-induced molecular manipulation is inherently a high-exploration, low-signal environment: 
random actions very rarely lead to selective single -bond cleavage, and purely sparse rewards (i.e., 
“getting positive reward only when the full sequence is completed”) would result in extremely slow 
or unstable learning . Therefore, the reward needs to be carefully designed to  encode directional 
chemical progress along the predefined dissociation pathway.  
Our reward function was designed with several considerations that balance generality with the 
specific demands of sequential single -bond activation. First, progression to a l ater intermediate is 
assigned a larger positive reward. This incremental weighting reflects the fact that later debromination 
steps require higher selectivity and tolerate a narrower region of action space, and thus should be 
reinforced more strongly. Conversely, unintended multi-bond dissociation or irreversible molecular 
degradation receives a large negative reward, as such failure modes terminate the reaction sequence 
and provide no useful learning signal. A small penalty is assigned to No -Reaction event s to 
discourage the agent from collapsing into a trivial “do nothing ” strategy, a common issue in 
continuous-action RL environments with safety constraints. Importantly, the structure of this reward 
function is general rather than molecule-specific. It does not rely on any symmetry of TPP -Br4, nor 
on hand -crafted molecular descriptors or visual templates. To adapt the framework to a different 
molecular system or reaction sequence, one only needs to redefine the ordered set of admissible 
reaction states. The same combination of (i) stage-weighted positive rewards for correct progression, 
(ii) tiered negative penalties for incorrect or destructive actions, and (iii) a small stagnation penalty. 
This design ensures that the reward function is transferable across  systems while still capturing the 
hierarchical nature of multi-step bond activation. 
Our action: An additional explanation of the reward function design and its reinforcement -learning 
rationale has now been included in the Supporting Information Section 4.
