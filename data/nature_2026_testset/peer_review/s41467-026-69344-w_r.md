Major Comments 
1. Linguistic Precision: Association vs. Causality 
The authors explicitly state in their rebuttal that the study design is inherently associational. However, this acknowledgment
is not yet fully or consistently reflected in the manuscript's prose. 
The frequent use of active, unidirectional verbs—such as "drives," "leads to," or "results in"—implicitly suggests a causal
relationship that the study design cannot formally support. Merely stating in a cover letter that causality is not claimed does
not suffice if the public-facing text invites such an inference. 
I urge the authors to conduct a comprehensive "linguistic audit" of the Abstract, Discussion, and Conclusion. Phrases
implying directionality or causal mechanisms should be replaced with more nuanced, associational terminology (e.g., "is
associated with," "is linked to," or "co-occurs with") to align the narrative with the study's evidentiary limits. 
2. Epidemiological Rationale for Model Selection 
While the statistical soundness of the modeling framework is no longer in question, the epidemiological justification for
specific structural choices remains underdeveloped. 
The current manuscript describes the implementation of covariates well, but it lacks a robust defense of why these specific
variables were prioritized over other biologically or ecologically plausible alternatives. A Nature Communications audience
expects a bridge between statistical selection (e.g., AIC/BIC or LASSO) and domain-specific theory. 
Please provide a more detailed rationale for the model structure. Specifically, explain how the chosen covariates align with
the current understanding of transmission ecology or the specific dynamics of the system under study. This context is vital for
multidisciplinary readers to interpret the biological relevance of the findings. 
3. Defining the Boundaries of Generalisability 
The authors position their framework as a broadly applicable tool for surveillance and early warning. While the framework is
indeed promising, the current discussion does not sufficiently delineate its boundary conditions. 
The response to previous concerns regarding spatial and temporal specificity felt somewhat generic. It is essential to
distinguish between the framework’s procedural generalisability and the resultant context-specificity of the findings. 
I recommend adding a dedicated subsection or a focused paragraph in the Discussion addressing "External Validity and
Scope." Clearly delineate which conclusions are likely system-specific and which aspects of the framework are expected to
hold across different ecological or regional contexts. 
4. Synthesizing Uncertainty into the Narrative 
Statistical quantification of uncertainty is now well-presented; however, the interpretation of that uncertainty remains
somewhat siloed within the results section. 
There remains a slight disconnect where the narrative conclusions occasionally sound more definitive than the underlying
data warrants, particularly where confidence intervals are wide or model agreement is modest. 
Ensure that the narrative interpretation in the Discussion explicitly reflects the quantified uncertainty. When identifying
hotspots or ranking risks, please use qualifying language that acknowledges the degree of confidence, thereby avoiding
overconfident policy-relevant statements that might misguide stakeholders. 
Minor Points 
Methodological Conciseness: To improve readability, certain highly technical or repetitive methodological descriptions

more effectively.

measures (e.g., credible intervals or variability across model runs). 
Harmonize color scales and legends across all panels to facilitate direct comparison between different scenarios or time
points. 
Terminological Consistency: There is still some overlap in the use of "hotspot," "high-risk area," and "cluster." Please

Reviewer #1: 
This paper presents a comprehensive analysis of HPAI-H5 transmission in wild birds in the United 
States, utilizing various epidemiological and machine learning techniques. The study addresses an 
important and timely topic, especially given the recent spillover events to mammals. The use of a 
multi-faceted approach, combining transmission capacity estimation, spatio-temporal analysis, and 
meteorological factor analysis, provides valuable insights into the dynamics of H5 avian influenza. 
The findings regarding the differing transmission capacities among bird orders, the strong 
seasonality, and the identification of hotspots are significant for public health and wildlife 
management. The application of machine learning to understand non-linear relationships with 
meteorological factors is also a strength. 
A significant finding is the varied transmission capacity among different wild bird orders. 
Specifically, Strigiformes showed the strongest transmission capability (R0 =3.164) while 
Anseriformes, despite being a primary concern for avian influenza due to their migratory patterns, 
had the weakest (R0 =0.992). This challenges conventional assumptions about transmission in 
migratory waterfowl and highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of avian influenza 
ecology. Additionally, the study clearly demonstrates a pronounced seasonal pattern in H5 infections, 
with autumn and winter exhibiting twice the number of infections compared to spring, and five times 
that of summer. This consistent seasonality provides valuable information for surveillance and 
control planning. The analysis reveals complex, nonlinear relationships between meteorological 
factors and H5 infections. Notably, average temperature, precipitation, and cooling degree days 
showed a monotonically decreasing relationship with infection counts, while drought indices (PDSI 
and PMDI) exhibited an inverted "U-shaped" relationship, and the Palmer Z Index a "U-shaped" 
relationship. This moves beyond simple linear correlations and offers a more realistic understanding 
of environmental influences. 
The work is of significant value to veterinary epidemiology, wildlife health, and public health, 
especially given the recent H5N1 spillover events to mammals, including cattle and humans. By 
linking HPAI-H5 transmission to various meteorological indices, the study contributes to 
understanding the broader impact of climate change on infectious disease dynamics in wildlife, a 
critical and evolving area of research. 
However, there are several areas where the paper could be improved for clarity, rigor, and impact.

This paper presents a comprehensive analysis of HPAI -H5 transmission in wild birds in the 
United States, utilizing various epidemiological and machine learning techniques. The study

of avian influenza ecology. Additionally, the study clearly demonstrates a pronounced seasonal

compared to spring, and five times that of summer. This consistent seasonality provide s 
valuable information for surveillance and control planning. The analysis reveals complex, non-
linear relationships between meteorological factors and H5 infections. Notably, average

inverted "U -shaped" relationship, and the Palmer Z Index a "U -shaped" relationship. This

The work is of significant value to veterinary epidemiology, wildlife health, and public health,
