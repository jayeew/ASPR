Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigates the plant fucosyltransferase AtFUT11 using glycan microarrays, crystallography, enzymatic
assays, and multiscale simulations. The enzyme is shown to adopt a GT-B fold with a distinctive N-terminal subdomain for
G0 N-glycan anchoring, while tolerating β1,2-xylose and α1,6 modifications. A central finding is that the catalytic base
Glu158 distorts the innermost GlcNAc, aligning it for nucleophilic attack and enabling an asynchronous mechanism with
transient oxocarbenium character. The work provides structural and mechanistic insights into plant-specific core fucosylation
and its evolutionary parallels with mammalian FUTs. 
Overall, the manuscript offers interesting insights into these enzymes and their catalytic strategies. The mechanistic
discussion of the borderline SN1/SN2 character of the reaction, and the appearance of a transient oxocarbenium ion, is
particularly relevant to the community. However, in my opinion, the paper requires a major revision before it can be
considered for publication. As a computational chemist, I will limit my comments to the computational aspects of the work: 
1. One of the most interesting aspects of the manuscript is the proposed borderline SN1/SN2 mechanism with a transient
oxocarbenium ion. I would have expected a clearer introduction to this long-standing debate, with explicit reference to
previous studies in the field. 
2. The conclusions regarding the mechanistic character may depend on the level of theory employed. The authors should
demonstrate the robustness of their results and to what extent they might change with different levels of theory. 
3. A substantial part of the work relies on the analysis of reactive trajectories, but very few details are provided on how these
were generated. How were the starting points selected? How was statistical independence ensured? How were initial
velocities assigned, and how were trajectories propagated in both directions, R and P? Under what conditions were these
simulations carried out? This information is essential for assessing the reliability of the results. 
4. It is also important to provide the structural evolution of key distances associated with bond formation and cleavage during
the reactive trajectories. Such structural information must also include torsional angles describing planarity around the
anomeric carbon. This would complement the electronic analysis and help clarify the nature of the transient species along
the reaction path. 
5. Another point concerns the conformational states of the reactants. The authors distinguish between a Michaelis complex
and a reactive state and they show their free energy difference in the Supplementary Material (about 5 kcal/mol). It is unclear
if the activation free energy reported in the manuscript (16 kcal/mol) includes or not that contribution. 
6. The terminology “hidden intermediate” does not seem ideal. The designation “transient species,” which the authors
themselves use in parts of the text, would be more appropriate and less ambiguous. 
7. The use of the term “Minimum Energy Path” to describe a pathway on the free energy surface (for example in Figure 6) is
confusing. The authors should clarify whether they computed a true minimum energy path on the potential energy surface, or
a minimum free energy path. 
8. Finally, data accessibility is an issue. It was not possible to retrieve the simulation data from the DOI provided
(https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16733350) at the time of review. This must be corrected to ensure transparency and
reproducibility. 
In summary, the manuscript contains important results and has the potential to make an important contribution to the field.
Nevertheless, the issues outlined above should be addressed through a careful revision before the paper can be accepted.

valid comparison with experiment.

Response: We have dedicated a subsection in Methods (“RE→TS→PR trajectories”) detailing how the trajectories were generated. While PBE has indeed been widely used to study enzymatic systems, the central question here is not the general validity of this functional but to what extent the identification of a carbocation-like intermediate might depend on the functional employed. In particular, PBE is known in some cases to over-stabilize dissociative pathways, which raises the legitimate concern of whether the transient carbocation observed could be, at least partially, a functional-dependent artifact. This specific point has not been fully addressed. The authors should explicitly discuss how the mechanistic picture—especially the presence and stability of the putative carbocation intermediate—might change when using alternative functionals, and provide evidence or arguments supporting the robustness of their conclusions against such methodological choices.  Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. We fully agree that the identification of transient carbocation-like species should be carefully examined with respect to the chosen level of theory. Our mechanistic picture is qualitative in nature, since we do not perform a conventional location and characterization of well-defined stationary points. Instead, we analyze the topology of the free-energy surface obtained from metadynamics simulations. From these data, free-energy minima and maxima are inferred and associated with the singular points of the reaction (reactant, transition state, and, if present, intermediates). Importantly, we do not claim the existence of a “carbocation intermediate” in the strict IUPAC sense, which would require a true local minimum on the potential-energy surface. Rather, we refer to a transient, short-lived, post-transition-state oxocarbenium-like configuration that does not become stabilized as an energy minimum, but nevertheless has a finite lifetime along the reaction coordinate. The “intermediate” discussed in our work should therefore be understood in this dynamic sense, not as a well-separated thermodynamic species. The use of different exchange–correlation functionals could indeed affect the lifetime of this transient species (though not its “stability,” since it is not a stationary point). This could, in principle, lead to different minimum-energy trajectories analogous to distinct IRC-like paths. In our work, this issue is addressed through the application of ELF analysis to a statistically significant ensemble of independent molecular-dynamics trajectories, whose reliability increases with the number of sampled trajectories. The observation of very similar bonding patterns in all trajectories, differing only in the temporal duration of the oxocarbenium-like configuration, and always displaying its presence, strongly supports the robustness of our mechanistic interpretation. If the mechanistic picture were an artifact of the functional, some trajectories would evolve toward a clearly stabilized carbocation minimum (if favored by the functional) or toward a purely SN2-like pathway. This is not what we observe. Instead, all trajectories consistently fall within the same SN1–SN2 continuum, supporting the reliability of the computational approach. The use of more expensive hybrid or meta-GGA functionals is not computationally feasible for a metadynamics study of this size due to the prohibitive computational cost. Nevertheless, for smaller model systems, where higher-level functionals have been employed, very similar mechanistic features have been reported. This indicates that, for the present problem, PBE reproduces the relevant features of the free-energy surface in a reliable way.
