Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors proposed a solvation structure engineering strategy by introducing a hydrogen bond donor co-solvent ANM to
construct adaptive hydrogen-bonding domains, aiming to optimize Li
⁺
 transport pathways and interfacial stability in high-
voltage LMBs. While the study presents promising electrochemical performance under high cut-off voltages and high
cathode loading, several critical concerns remain to be addressed or clarified before further consideration: 
1. The claimed novelty of modulating Li
⁺
 solvation structure via hydrogen bonding is not sufficiently justified. Similar
approaches, especially those involving Z-bond interactions and weak hydrogen bonding networks, have been extensively
reported in recent literature. The manuscript lacks a clear articulation of how this work surpasses the existing strategies in
terms of design principle or performance enhancement. 
2. The selection of ANM as a co-solvent is rather conventional. The –NH– and –CN functional groups are commonly
employed in high-voltage electrolyte systems in LMBs. However, the manuscript does not provide any systematic structure–
property correlation or design rationale that distinguishes ANM from previously reported additives with similar motifs. 
3. The strength and nature of hydrogen bonding were primarily evaluated using FTIR shifts and RDG analysis. However,
state-of-the-art methods such as NBO analysis and QTAIM are more appropriate for quantitatively assessing hydrogen bond
strength and electronic characteristics. The manuscript lacks rigorous theoretical analysis to validate and compare the
strength of the H-bonds formed by ANM. 
4. The concept of "oriented Li
⁺
 migration pathways" proposed in Figure 1d is attractive, but there is no direct evidence
demonstrating the directional transport or adaptive anchoring behavior of hydrogen bond domains. 
5. The authors use E-F as the baseline electrolyte throughout the study, but fail to include control formulations using
standard carbonate-based electrolytes (e.g., EC/EMC) or non-H-bond-donating cosolvents. This omission prevents a
conclusive attribution of the observed improvements solely to the hydrogen bond engineering strategy. 
6. While XPS and TOF-SIMS depth profiling are performed, the analysis remains qualitative. There is no quantitative ratio
analysis of organic/inorganic components, and the spatial distribution of inorganic species in TOF-SIMS appears non-
uniform. Therefore, the conclusion that CEI is predominantly inorganic in composition on NCM811 surfaces is not sufficiently
supported. 
7. The electrochemical stability window was evaluated by LSV, but the manuscript lacks complementary characterizations
such as Tafel plots, interfacial impedance evolution, or full-range EIS fitting to further support the proposed interfacial
improvements. Additionally, in the in situ EIS/DRT analysis of Li||NCM811 cells, the attribution of mid-frequency peaks
solely to RCEI is debatable, as RSEI can also contribute significantly in this region. 
8. The authors claim to maximize energy density by employing high-loading cathodes (20 mg cm
⁻
²), lean electrolyte (2.4 g
Ah
⁻
¹), and a thin lithium anode (80 μm, N/P ratio = 1.7). However, such a high N/P ratio is impractical for industrial-scale
production due to both economic and safety concerns associated with lithium metal. Therefore, the soft-pack pouch cell
validation in its current configuration lacks practical relevance and is of limited reference value for practical applications.

Comment 1. The claimed novelty of modulating Li + solvation structure via hydrogen 
bonding is not sufficiently justified. Similar approaches, especially those involving Z -
bond interactions and weak hydrogen bonding networks, have been reported in recent 
literature. The manuscript lacks a clear articulation of  how this work surpasses th e

Reply: We thank the reviewer for the constructive comments.  
In the current literature, several studies have utilized the nonclassical hydrogen -
bond (Z-bond) interactions to modulate electrolyte solvation structures of electrolytes 
in lithium metal batteries, thereby enhancing battery stability and performance. For 
example, Ren et al. proposed using molecular anchoring strategy to suppress side 
reactions of ether solvents at t he electrode interface, thereby achieving high -voltage 
performance in low -concentration electrolytes. The researchers selected fluorinated 
ethers containing the –CF2H group (e.g., 1,1,2,2 -tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-
tetrafluoropropylether, TTE) as molecular anchors. These molecules can form Z-bonds 
with the ether solvent 1,2 -dimethoxyethane (DME), reducing the electron density on 
the ether oxygen atoms, thereby enhancing the ether ’s oxidation resistance ( Nat. 
Commun. 2023, 15, 2033). Fan et al. proposed a molecular docking strategy, where Z-

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

bonds are formed between fluorobenzene (FB) or dichloromethane (DCE) and 1,2 -
bis(tri-methoxy methyl)ethane (BTE) or 1,3 -bis(tri-methylmethoxy)propane (BTP), 
altering the solvent docking configuration and reducing the desolva tion energy of Li + 
(Nat. Chem. 2024, 16, 1427-1435). However, these existing studies generally focus on 
adjusting the solvation structure and de -coordination mechanism of electrolytes 
through tuning a single Z -bond. The potential mechanisms governing the rational 
regulation of co -solvent–solvent H -bond and Z -bond interactions at the electrode –
electrolyte interface remain insufficiently understood.  
Specifically, the novelty of our present work is stated as follows: 
 The screening of electrochemically stable  hydrogen bond donor amides for 
modulating the size and stability of hydrogen-bonded domains. 
 For the first time, the contributions of H-bonds and Z-bonds to the hydrogen-
bonding domain were distinguished, and their presence was quantified 
through spectroscopic characterization and computational methods. 
 Hydrogen-bonded domains mediate highly stable inorganic -rich liquid-solid 
interfaces by remodeling the solvated structure of the interface. 
The atypical hydrogen bond (Z-bond) in our study not only acts independently but also 
enhances the formation of typical hydrogen bond (H -bond), with intermolecular 
interactions that are far more complex than those observed in traditional studies of Z -
bond. While the formation of Z-bonds and H-bonds can be directly determined through 
in situ  Raman/FT-IR spectroscopy, this adaptive hydrogen-bonded domains  still 
requires additional characterization methods. 
Our research indicates that Z-bonds are weak interactions formed between atoms 
with partial charges (e.g., Nδ⁻–Hδ+). Z-bonds are weaker than typical hydrogen bonds, 
with strengths approximately one -thousandth of coordination bonds. This substantial 
difference in bond energy raises intriguing questions: how can this weak Z -bond 
modulate stronger coordination bonds?  Can i ts effect be amplified by coupling to 
stronger interactions? Because of the relatively weak binding strength of the Z -bond, 
its ability to alter the solvation structure is limited and far less pronounced than that of 
strong H-bonds.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

Importantly, our study differentiates the interactions between H -bonds and Z -
bonds for the first time . When H -bond interactions dominate, the concomitant Z -
bonds create adaptive hydrogen -bonded domains that stabilize the solvation 
environment while facilitating rapid Li + transport. Notably, we performed DFT 
screening of a series of amide molecules as hydrogen -bond acceptors. The reason for 
selecting amides is their relatively high stability in hydrogen bond donors for lithium -
metal batteries (Figure S1-5). We successfully confirmed the differences in the size of 
H-bonds formed between amides and electrolytes with different side groups ( Figure 
3d-e; S17-26) through integrated infrared, Raman, and NMR spectroscopy techniques, 
accompanied with molecular dynamics simulations. Furthermore, to verify the role of 
this hydrogen -bonded domain s under charge -discharge conditions, we employed 
advanced in situ Raman and infrared spectroscopy at the anode and cathode interfaces 
to test the hydrogen-bonded domain’s effect on electrolyte solvent decomposition and 
its de-coordination mechanism (Figure 4b-c). We also performed DFT calculations to 
assess the impact of hydrogen -bonded domains on solvent dehydrogenation at 
NCM811 cathodes and their protective role in preserving the layered struct ure of 
NCM811 (Figure 5a-h).  
Overall, our research elucidates the fundamental principles distinguishing H -
bonds and Z-bonds. When both coexist, H-bond interactions dominate. Across kinetic 
studies and interfacial analyses, hydrogen -bonded domains stabiliz e the solvation 
environment, thereby enhancing high -voltage stability and facilitating rapid Li + 
transport in lithium-metal batteries.

Comment 2. The selection of ANM as a co -solvent is conventional. The  –NH–and –
CN functional groups are commonly employed in high-voltage electrolyte systems in 
LMBs. However, the manuscript does not provide any systematic structure -property 
correlation or design rationale that distinguishes ANM from previously reported

Reply: Thank you for your valuable comments. Although amide molecules exhibit 
high thermodynamic stability, the primary issue with the –NH– functional group in 
solvent molecules is its presence of “active hydrogen”. Lithium metal, known for its 
lowest electrochemical potential and the strongest reducing ability, is highly sensitive 
to proton sources. When solvent molecules containing –NH– groups come into contact 
with the lithium metal surface, the weakly acidic hydrogen atom in the N –H bond is 
readily reduced by lithium, generating hydrogen gas (H2) and the corresponding lithium 
salts (e.g., lithium amides). 
The reaction can be represented as: 2 R–NH–R' + 2 Li → 2 R–N⁻ Li+–R' + H2↑.  
A significant amount of active lithium is consumed in chemical reactions with the 
solvent rather than during electrochemical cycling, resulting in extremely low first -
cycle coulombic efficiency (CE) and rapid capacity fading.  The evolution of H2 leads 
to an increase in internal pressure within the battery, causing ser ious safety hazards, 
such as swelling or even rupture. Continuous gas generation compromises the integrity 
of the SEI, preventing it from effectively passivating the lithium metal surface. 
Researchers have improved the instability of amides towards lithium metal by 
introducing additives or adjusting the main solvent strategy (Joule 2024, 8, 2542-4351; 
ACS Energy Lett.  2024, 9, 3931 -3938). Additionally, the nitrogen atom in the –NH– 
functional group possesses a lone pair of electrons, making it a nucleophilic center that 
is susceptible to electrochemical oxidation. This oxidation process is catalyzed and 
intensified at high-voltage on the surface of cathode materials (Adv. Energy Mater. 2022, 
12, 2200139). In contrast to the one-sided disadvantages associated with  the –NH– 
group, the –CN (nitrile) functional group exhibits a certain “duality.” It has high 
oxidation stability and was once considered a candidate for high -voltage solvent s. 
However, the –C≡N triple bond i n nitrile solvents with low lowest unoccupied

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels (such as acetonitrile and propionitrile) is a 
strong electron-withdrawing group, which results in very low LUMO energy levels for 
these molecules, meaning that they are highly prone to electron gain and reduction (Adv. 
Mater. 2024, 36, 2404271; Adv. Funct. Mater. 2025, 35, 2421329). Existing strategies 
primarily focus on the molecular design of nitrile solvents, adjusting the regional 
electronic cloud and polarity to slow down side reactions with lithium metal (Adv. Funct. 
Mater. 2025, 35, 2421329; Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2024, 63, e202408902).  
In this study, we first screened different short -chain amide molecules and 
performed a comparative analysis of their H -bond stability using DFT calculations, 
combined with cluster stability  analysis. We found that molecules containing –NH2 
exhibited poor electrochemical stability due to the reactivity of their active hydrogen 
with lithium metal batterie s ( Figure S1 ). At the same time, introducing –CF3 or –
CH2CN substituents into the amide molecule effectively enhances the oxidation 
resistance of the amide ( Figure S2 ). Additionally, we have demonstrated 
experimentally that molecules containing –CH2CN exhibit good compatibility with the 
electrolyte and with lithium metal batteries (Figure S3).  
More importantly, the design principle proposed in this study is not merely based 
on the individual chemical characteristics of –NH– or –CN, but rather on the spatially 
coordinated arrangement  of these two functional groups within the solvation 
structure of the electrolyte  (Figure S4 -6). This arrangement allows them to play 
complementary roles in the solvation structure regulation. –NH–, as a typical hydrogen 
bond donor, can form a strong hydrogen bond (–10.8 kcal·mol–1) with the solvent's 
carbonyl oxygen, effectively stabilizing the solvation structure. –CN, as a Z-bond (Nδ⁻–
Hδ+) acceptor site, forms weak interactions (–2.5 kcal·mol–1) with the hydrogen of the 
adjacent solvent –CH2– (Table S1). While the interaction is weak, it can polarize and 
orient the surrounding carbonyl groups, thereby enhancing the stability of the main –
NH–···O=C hydrogen bond.  Furthermore, we established a structure -property 
relationship i n our work: by quantifying the strength of the H -bond and Z -bond 
interactions, changes in hydrogen bond stability, Raman/NMR shifts, and molecular 
dynamics simulations. We demonstrated that ANM, compared to other amide molecules,

can simultaneously optimize the solvation and desolvation processes and stabilize the 
electrode/electrolyte interface.

Comment 3. The strength and nature of hydrogen bonding were primarily evaluated 
using FTIR shifts and RDG analysis. However, state -of-the-art methods such as NBO 
analysis and QTAIM are more appropriate for quantitatively assessing hydrogen bond

Reply: Thanks for the careful review and constructive feedback. We employed 
FTIR and RDG analyses primarily to identify and characterize hydrogen -bond 
formation and properties. FTIR shift analysis can infer the strength of hydrogen bonds 
by observing the changes in the stretching frequency, while RDG reveals intermolecular 
interaction regions and strengths via the electron-density distribution. The QTAIM and 
NBO analyses mentioned by the reviewer are advanced tools for the quantitative

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 13 -->

assessment of hydrogen bond strength and electronic properties. QTAIM can precisely 
quantify the strength of hydrogen bonds by analyzing the electron density ( ρ) and 
potential energy density (V(r)) at the bond critical point (BCP), and is particularly suited 
for revealing the electron transfer and  interactions during hydrogen bond formation. 
Although NBO analysis can provide rich electronic structure information, it is not the 
most direct tool for describing solvation structures and ion transport. In our study, 
Raman peak fitting was conducted for a detailed quantitative analysis, which effectively 
characterizes the formation of hydrogen bonds and the solvation process. MD 
simulations demonstrate that ANM can form local hydrogen-bonded structural domains 
within a distance of less than 3.5 Å from the carbonate molecules (Figure S22a-b). The 
formation of these hydrogen-bonded domains  stabilizes the solvent molecules and 
results in a solvation structure featuring an anion -rich inner coordination shell and an 
outer hydrogen-bonded domains. Raman spectroscopy of both free and bound hydrogen 
bonds confirms that the hydrogen -bonded domains formed by ANM are more stable 
and more numerous, providing strong evidence for the exceptional stability of the E -
F@ANM electrolyte (Figure S23a-d).

Reply: We sincerely appreciate your valuable comments regarding the concept of 
‘directional Li + migration pathways ’ in our study.  In conventional dilute -salt
