Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled “Reversible bismuth reduction-driven dark photoelectrochemistry” reports a so
‑

system based on a BiOBr photocathode, which exhibits reverse photocurrent behavior (Idark| > Ilight) under air-saturated
conditions and is claimed to enable selective glutathione (GSH) detection. While the topic is within the scope of the journal,
the study suffers from major concerns in terms of novelty, mechanistic rigor, and data sufficiency. 
The core phenomenon – coupling of PEC activity with reversible valence changes of the electrode material leading to
modulation of photocurrent – has already been extensively documented in PEC–electrochromic systems (e.g., WO3, Bi-
based oxides) over the past decade. The present work does not introduce a fundamentally new physical effect but rather
labels an existing mechanism with new terminology. Furthermore, the dataset presented is not sufficiently robust to support
the proposed mechanism or the claimed high selectivity toward GSH. The reported effect only occurs under narrowly defined
oxygen concentrations, lacks comprehensive parametric study, and is supported mainly by indirect evidence. Without
stronger mechanistic validation and broader demonstration of robustness, the manuscript does not meet the novelty and
rigor standards of the journal. Based on the above concerns, I recommend rejection of this manuscript in its current form. 
1.Experimental scope is too narrow, with inherently weak photocurrent signals leading to poor robustness for sensing
applications. The effect occurs only within a narrow oxygen concentration window, raising concerns about reproducibility in
practical environments. 
2.The reverse photocurrent is only tested under air-, O2-, and N2-saturated electrolytes. Other critical parameters (pH,
applied bias, ionic strength, light intensity) are not systematically varied, limiting understanding of universality. 
3.No long-term cycling data (> hours) or batch-to-batch reproducibility tests are provided to assess electrode stability. 
4.Given that Bi(3-&)+ is chemically unstable, potential degradation pathways during repeated light–dark cycling are not
addressed. 
5.Application relevance is overstated. The GSH sensing range and detection limit are comparable to or weaker than existing
PEC sensors, while the operational constraints (air-saturation requirement) reduce practical applicability. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors report a novel “dark-enhanced” photoelectrochemical (PEC) system based on a BiOBr photocathode, where
reversible bismuth reduction drives an unconventional reverse photocurrent (|Idark| > |Ilight|) under air conditions. The work
demonstrates that glutathione (GSH) selectively interacts with BiOBr, enhancing the reversibility of the bismuth redox cycle
and amplifying the reverse photocurrent. This mechanism enables selective detection of reductant molecules at the
photocathode interface, addressing a key challenge of selectivity in PEC sensing systems. The study combines
electrochemical, spectroscopic, and surface characterization analyses to validate the mechanism and demonstrate practical
applications in GSH quantification in real samples. 
The manuscript introduces a compelling conceptual advancement in photoelectrochemical systems by shifting the paradigm
from light-enhanced to dark-enhanced PEC processes. The mechanistic insights into reversible bismuth redox cycles,
coupled with selective molecular recognition at the cathode, represent an original approach with potential implications for
selective PEC sensing. 
The experimental validation is thorough, with adequate characterization (XPS, TEM, EDS, Raman, Mott-Schottky)
supporting the proposed mechanism. However, there are points requiring further clarification, especially regarding the
reproducibility, scope, and broader applicability of the dark-PEC concept beyond the GSH model system.

I believe the manuscript has high novelty and significance, but several substantial issues need to be addressed before it can
be considered for publication in Nature Communications. 
1. Mechanistic depth – Role of oxygen concentration 
The proposed dark-PEC mechanism relies on a delicate balance between O
₂
 availability and Bi reduction/oxidation
dynamics. However, the authors should quantitatively assess the oxygen concentration threshold where the transition from
traditional PEC to dark-PEC behavior occurs. Could this be correlated with electrochemical O
₂
 reduction kinetics? 
2. Generality of the mechanism 
While the work demonstrates GSH selectivity, it remains unclear if this mechanism could extend to other thiol-containing
molecules or reductants capable of interacting with Bi species. Testing a broader set of analytes with similar functional
groups would help position this platform as a more general sensing strategy. 
3. Photocurrent reversibility and stability over time 
The manuscript lacks a detailed stability assessment. How does the reverse photocurrent behavior evolve over multiple
cycles or prolonged operation? Could there be degradation of the BiOBr surface or accumulation of irreversible Bi(0)? 
4. Signal amplification and sensitivity comparison 
The LOD reported (20 nM GSH) is excellent, but a benchmarking against state-of-the-art PEC sensors for GSH (or similar
reductants) should be included to contextualize the advancement in sensitivity and selectivity. 
5. 
Origin of the unconventional energy band (Bi(3-&)+) 
The description of Bi(3-&)+ as an intermediate oxidation state is central but speculative. The notation is non-standard and
chemically ambiguous. The XPS data (binding energy shift, new peak at ~157.5 eV) lacks deconvolution or comparison with
Bi⁰/Bi(I) standards, making the assignment uncertain. The authors should clarify the nature of this species and support its
existence with additional spectroscopic evidence (e.g., XANES, in-situ XPS) or, at minimum, a rigorous XPS fitting. 
Minor Comments 
1. Figure Quality 
Figures (e.g., Fig. 1, Fig. 4) would benefit from clearer labels and a more schematic visualization of the electron flow
directions. 
2. Terminology Consistency 
Throughout the text, the term “reverse photocurrent” is used. It might be helpful to explicitly define this term early on (i.e.,
reverse with respect to light-on condition). 
3. Supporting Information – Missing Spectroscopic Data 
In SI, including high-resolution XPS spectra for GSH-interacted BiOBr after long-term cycling (post-mortem analysis) would
strengthen the stability claims. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper proposes a “dark-enhanced” photocatalytic system based on a BiOBr photoanode, achieving a reverse
photocurrent (|Idark| > |Ilight|) through the reversible reduction of bismuth, and applies it to the selective detection of
glutathione, demonstrating a certain degree of innovation. However, the paper still has several shortcomings and requires
major revisions and improvements. The following are the revision suggestions: 
1. The discussion on selectivity in traditional PEC systems is overly brief. The manuscript lacks a systematic review of
recent advances in enhancing PEC selectivity, resulting in an unclear benchmark for evaluating the innovation of the
proposed system. Please supplement the introduction with representative studies from the past five years that focus on PEC
selectivity control. 
2. The functions of mannitol and PVP in the synthesis of BiOBr nanosheets are not clearly explained. Please elaborate on
their roles during the preparation process (line 265). 
3. The characterization of BiOBr's structural and optoelectronic properties is based on a single synthesis batch, without
replicates. To confirm reproducibility, results from at least three independent syntheses should be included, particularly
regarding the consistency of morphology, crystallinity, and photoelectrochemical performance (refer to line 61). 
4. In line 83, it is claimed that the reverse photocurrent is influenced by a “moderate O2 concentration,” yet no quantitative
data or correlation is provided. Please conduct a gradient experiment (0-100% O2) and plot the relationship between O2
partial pressure and photocurrent response to define the “moderate” range more precisely. 
5. The valence state change of bismuth is currently inferred only from XPS binding energy shifts and electrochemical
behavior, which is indirect. To convincingly demonstrate the reversible redox process and presence of intermediate
oxidation states (line 98), additional direct evidence such as in situ X-ray absorption fine structure and electron
paramagnetic resonance data is recommended. 
6. Line 151 states that Bi(3–δ)+ species may passivate ORR sites and reactivate O
₂
 oxidation in the dark. However, this
claim lacks in situ spectroscopic support. Please consider including data from in situ Raman spectroscopy or in situ AFM to
support this hypothesis. 
7. The manuscript does not discuss the impact of light parameters (e.g., intensity, wavelength) on the reduction degree of Bi
species, which may directly influence the generation of Bi(3–δ)+ and hence affect the reverse photocurrent. Please address
this in the discussion. 
8. The validation system is not rigorous enough. Only interference from five substances, including CY and UA, was
compared, but interference from other structurally similar reducing agents (such as cysteine and glutathione disulfide bonds)
was not ruled out (line 237). 
9. How does this detection system perform in terms of interference resistance in complex matrices such as serum? 
10. The recovery rate experiment for actual sample testing (243 lines) was not compared with other standard methods,
making it difficult to prove the accuracy of the method. At the same time, intra-day and inter-day comparison data need to be

(1) Experimental scope is too narrow, with inherently weak photocurrent signals leading to poor 
robustness for sensing applications. The effect occurs only wi thin a narrow oxygen 
concentration window, raising concerns about reproducibility in practical environments. 
Response: Weak photocurrent signals and sensing robustness are not synonymous. In 
photoelectrochemical systems, the robustness of “signal-enhanced” PEC sensing fundamentally 
relies on the inherently low blank photocurrent signal. As the concentration of the target analyte 
increases, the photocurrent response correspondingly intensifies. The research under different 
atmosphere conditions aims to explore the essential mechanism of reverse photocurrent generation, 
which does not mean that our detection application is limited to a narrow oxygen concentration 
window. This phenomenon is observed under ambient air conditions—a stable and well-controlled 
environment—that ensures high reproducibility. To validate this consistency, we synthesized three 
independent batches of BiOBr material and performed PEC measurements, which collectively 
demonstrated excellent reproducibility of the inverted photocurrent effect (Figure R1). In addition, 
intra-day and inter-day recovery tests were conducted to evaluate method reliability. The recovery 
rates varied between 98.8% and 107%, with relative standard deviations ranging from 0.52% to 
8.16%, indicating excellent repeatability and robustness in sample analysis (Table R1).

Response: According to the reviewer ’s suggestion, we have tested reverse photocurrent under 
other parameters (pH, applied bias, ionic strength, light intensity) . As pH increases, the reverse 
photocurrent signal gradually diminishes , attributable to the slowed kinetics of the ORR under 
alkaline conditions  (Figure R2a ). With increasingly negative applied  potentials, the reverse 
photocurrent intensifies as a result of enhanced electron injection and increased photocathode 
current driven by the external potential  (Figure R2b ). Regarding ion concentration, the 
mechanism of the reverse photocurrent primarily depends on the reversible equilibrium between 
oxygen concentration and the valence state of bismuth. Consequently, the signal intensity does not 
exhibit significant enhancement with rising ion concentration  (Figure R2c). Finally, an increase 
in light inten sity leads to a stronger reverse photocurrent signal, attributable to improved light 
absorption efficiency at the photoelectrode  (Figure R2d). In this study, the final experimental 
conditions were determined to be a pH of 7.0, an applied potential of -0.5 V, a 0.01 M Na 2SO4 
electrolyte solution, and a light intensity of 100 mW/cm², which were subsequently employed for 
the identification and quantitative analysis of reductant molecules.

(3) No long-term cycling data (> hours) or batch -to-batch reproducibility tests are provided to 
assess electrode stability. 
Response: According to the reviewer’s suggestion, we tested the long-term cycling and batch-to-
batch reproducibility of the reverse photocurrent . For the pristine BiOBr photoelectrode under 
prolonged on -off light cycling, the reverse photocurrent exhibited instability  (Figure R 3a). In 
contrast, upon the addition of glutathione, both the intensity and stability of the photocurrent 
response were markedly improved (Figure R3b). This behavior can be attributed to the underlying 
mechanism of reverse photocurrent generation . Under illumination, Bi 3+ is reduced, leading to 
decreased ORR activity . In the dark, the intermediate state Bi (3-&)+ is oxidized, r esulting in 
enhanced ORR activity.  Therefore, during the long -term cycling process, the pure BiOBr 
photoelectrode is difficult to maintain a reversible Bi redox reaction at equal time intervals (10 
seconds of light and 10 seconds of dark), resulting in reaction imbalance and photocurrent 
attenuation. However, after adding GSH, due to its reve rsible reaction characteristics, it can 
alleviate the above imbalance to a certain extent, thereby maintaining a more stable photocurrent 
for a longer time. Notably, since quantitative analysis of the target analyte is conducted within a 
relatively short t imeframe, maintaining photocurrent stability for approximately 100 seconds is 
sufficient to meet the experimental requirements of this study.  Furthermore, comprehensive 
morphological (Figure R4 ), crystallographic  (Figure R 5), and photocurrent ( Figure R 6) 
characterizations were performed on three independently synthesized batches of BiOBr, 
confirming consistent batch -to-batch reproducibility. In conclusion, the dark -PEC signal in this 
study exhibited excellent reliability with respect to stability and repe atability, thereby providing 
robust support for subsequent research endeavors.

(4) Given that Bi (3-&)+ is chemically unstable, potential degradation pathways during repeated 
light-dark cycling are not addressed. 
Response: As highlighted by the reviewer, Bi(3-&)+ exhibits chemical instability. To validate the 
presence of this intermediate species, we conducted comprehensive XPS measurements and in -
depth analyses  (Figure R7 ). The results indicate that the charge transfer (CT) satellite peak 
observed in the low binding energy region of the Bi 4f XPS spectrum following light exposure 
arises from the effective shielding effect of O 2p electrons on 4f holes, without inducing a 
significant alteration in the Bi3+ chemical state. During this process, Bi exhibits two distinct final 
state configurations: the primary peak corresponds to the [Xe] 4f 13 5d10 6s2 configuration, while 
the CT satellite peak is associated with the [Xe] 4f 13 5d10 6s2 6p1 configuration. In addition, the 
stable oxidation states of bismuth in reality are only three: 0, +3, and +5. It is difficult to correspond 
to the standard XPS characteristic peaks of +1 and +2 oxidation states. Accordingly, we designate 
the bismuth species with oxidation states between 0 and +3 as Bi (3-&)+. Furthermore, electron 
paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were employed to investigate the reversible 
behavior of BiOBr under alternating light and dark conditions. In comparison with the sample in 
the dark state, BiOBr after illumination exhibits a markedly enha nced EPR signal at g = 2.01  
(Figure R8). This enhancement can be attributed to an increased concentration of oxygen vacancy 
defects under photoexcitation, which is consistent with the appearance of the CT satellite peak in 
the Bi 4f XPS spectrum. 
 
The observation of the “dark-enhanced” reverse photocurrent during the light -dark cycle can be 
attributed precisely to the high instability of this intermediate state. During the illumination process,
