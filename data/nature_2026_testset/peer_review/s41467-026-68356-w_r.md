Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the article “Decoding RNA-RNA Interactions: The Role of Low-Complexity Repeats and a Deep Learning Framework for
Sequence-Based Prediction”, the authors aim to uncover the fundamental principles underlying RNA-RNA interactions
(RRIs). By analyzing several large-scale RRI datasets, they identify low-complexity repeats (LCRs) as key drivers of these
interactions. Their findings highlight LCRs as critical hubs within RNA-RNA interaction networks. Furthermore, RNA-
sequencing of the interactors of the Lhx1os lncRNA allowed us to validate the importance of LCRs in shaping interactions
potentially involved in neuronal development. In addition, the authors developed a deep learning-based model, RIME, which
predicts RRIs by leveraging embeddings from a nucleic acid language model. This work sounds interesting. However, there
are some concerns to be addressed: 
1 While the manuscript explains the use of Nucleotide Transformer for RNA embeddings, briefly comparing this model with
other embedding techniques would be useful. For instance, why were these models chosen over alternatives like RNA-FM
for RNAs? Additionally, a more detailed explanation of how embeddings capture meaningful structural/functional properties
(especially the LCRs) of RNA would strengthen the result. 
2 The selection process for negative samples appears reasonable, but it would be better if the authors could provide a more
detailed justification of whether this choice could significantly impact the model’s performance. 
3 The manuscript does not provide explicit details about the hyperparameter tuning process. It would be beneficial to add
information on hyperparameter optimizations. Was grid search or random search used to determine optimal values?
Similarly, it would be helpful to include performance metrics or validation loss curves during training to demonstrate how the
model converged and whether overfitting was effectively mitigated. 
4 A comprehensive analysis of the RIME model’s computational complexity and scalability, detailing the time consumption
and GPU memory requirements during training. 
5 The authors mentioned the principle of RRI on both human and mouse data. What species were included in the testing
datasets used to evaluate RIME? It’s better to offer a more in-depth analysis of the RIME model's performance differences
across species. 
6 The Methods section introduces the RIME architecture, where standard CNN-based layers and a final linear layer for
binary prediction follow Nucleotide Transformer embeddings. It would be helpful to provide an algorithmic workflow diagram
or pseudocode to illustrate the model framework more clearly. Additionally, why did the authors choose CNN-based layers?
Did the CNN layer capture the RRI features more effectively, particularly in the LCR regions? Did they explore alternative
architectures, such as transformers or LSTM-based layers? 
7 Could the authors provide more insights into the role of low-complexity repeats (LCRs)? Specifically, what do the LCR
regions represent in the context of RNA secondary and tertiary structures? A discussion on this topic would benefit a deeper
understanding of RRIs.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a beautiful work. I have a few questions:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

1) The authors have characterized the sequence context of RNA–RNA interaction (RRI) regions by analyzing the
enrichment of repetitive elements. While this provides useful insight, the analysis could be substantially strengthened by
considering additional molecular features known to influence RNA structure and function. For example, do RRI regions tend
to colocalize with binding sites of specific RNA-binding proteins (RBPs)? Furthermore, does the presence of low-complexity
regions (LCRs) affect the binding of RBPs to RRI patches? Exploring these aspects could offer a more comprehensive
understanding of the molecular determinants underlying RNA–RNA interactions. 
2) On a related note, the authors could assess the overlap between RRI regions and known adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I)
RNA editing sites. Since double-stranded RNA structures are the preferred substrates for ADAR enzymes, the presence of
RRIs could potentially influence local editing events. Additionally, it would be informative to examine whether any observed
association is modulated by the presence of LCRs, which the authors suggest may promote thermodynamically stable
interactions. This analysis could offer further insight into the functional relevance of RNA heteroduplexes in the context of
RNA editing. 
3) The current structure of the RIME software on GitHub requires users to manually launch multiple separate scripts and
manage the download of embeddings externally, which makes the workflow somewhat fragmented and unintuitive. I suggest
reorganizing the GitHub repository to provide a more streamlined and user-friendly experience. Ideally, the user should be
able to run the full pipeline by simply specifying the input query and target files, with all necessary steps,including the
download of embeddings,handled internally by the software. 
Providing a single entry point script or command-line interface that wraps the full workflow would greatly improve usability,
especially for non-expert users, and would help increase adoption of the tool. 
4) It is my understanding that RIME has been trained also on COMRADES data. One of the advantages of COMRADES
over similar techniques is that, in the COMRADES experiment, a control library is generated by first de-crosslinking and then
denaturing the RNA, prior to ligation. Did the authors take advantage of this to help defining false positive interactions? If
now, would it help? 
5) Is the algorithm only capable of predicting intermolecular interactions? I assume that the duplexes from intramolecular
interactions were all removed prior to training? What if they would be included? Can the algorithm be used for structure
prediction?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript explores RNA-RNA interactions (RRIs), with a focus on low-complexity repeats (LCRs) as key determinants
of these interactions. The authors used several transcriptome-wide RNA interaction datasets, performed analyses to identify
repeat-driven interactions, and developed a deep-learning model (RIME) to predict RRIs. They validate their findings
experimentally using specific lncRNA examples and demonstrate that LCRs correlate with stable, biologically relevant
interactions. 
There are several points of strength in the manuscript: 
Identification of LCRs as central to RNA-RNA interactions is a significant finding. 
A comprehensive analysis of transcriptome-wide datasets, combined with cross-validation using experimental techniques,
enhances its robustness. 
The development of a deep learning model (RIME) significantly advances the computational prediction of RNA interactions. 
The integration of diverse datasets (PARIS, SPLASH, RIC-seq, MARIO) provides broad support for conclusions. 
Complementary computational (enrichment, thermodynamics) and experimental (RNA pull-down, RNA-seq) approaches
validate their findings well. 
The deep-learning approach effectively captures complex sequence contexts not accounted for by traditional
thermodynamics-based tools. 
Figures are comprehensive, detailed, and informative, generally enhancing clarity and understanding. 
Supplementary figures adequately support the main text conclusions.

Incorporation of RNA localization in dataset preparation and modeling 
The authors present a compelling case for the role of low-complexity repeats in RNA-RNA interactions; however, one crucial
biological dimension appears to be missing from both the dataset preparation and the modeling pipeline: subcellular RNA
localization. RNA-RNA interactions can only occur if both partners co-reside in the same cellular compartment, yet the
manuscript does not appear to consider localization when constructing training or validation sets, nor when interpreting the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

biological feasibility of predicted interactions. This omission may allow for spurious interactions between transcripts that are
never co-localized in vivo and could artificially inflate centrality measures for broadly expressed but compartmentalized
RNAs. I strongly recommend the authors integrate publicly available localization data (e.g., APEX-seq, LncATLAS, RNA
Atlas) to either filter or annotate RNA pairs in the RIME training and evaluation pipeline. Incorporating such context-aware
information would enhance the biological relevance, specificity, and interpretability of their otherwise strong predictive
model. 
Role of RNA-RNA interactions in decay pathways 
While the authors briefly mention RNA decay as one of several consequences of RNA-RNA interactions, they do not explore
this mechanism or analyze it further. Given the observed enrichment of low-complexity repeats in duplex-forming regions—
and the known role of repeat-mediated RRIs in STAU1-dependent mRNA decay—the manuscript would benefit from a
deeper discussion or analysis of decay pathways. For example, are predicted LCR-mediated interactions enriched in
transcripts with known rapid turnover? Integrating RNA half-life data or decay factor binding profiles would substantiate the
functional importance of these interactions and broaden the biological impact of the study. 
Transcriptional regulation as a functional outcome of RRIs 
The authors note that many RNAs engaged in low-complexity repeat-mediated interactions encode transcription factors, as
identified via GO enrichment. However, the manuscript does not elaborate on how these RRIs might be functionally
impactful in transcriptional programs. 
Given the well-documented role of lncRNAs in transcription regulation, often through interactions with transcription factors or
chromatin-associated RNAs, it would be valuable to discuss this connection more explicitly. Do LCR-mediated RRIs,
particularly those involving transcription factors, play roles in specific cellular pathways? Could such interactions alter
transcript stability, nuclear localization, or translation efficiency for these transcription factors? 
A deeper investigation into these elements would offer clearer insight into the biological significance of the results and help
define concrete next steps for future work. 
Controlling for RNA expression bias in RRI datasets 
While the authors appropriately exclude small and highly abundant RNAs (e.g., rRNAs, snoRNAs), the manuscript does not
fully address expression-driven detection bias in transcriptome-wide RNA-RNA interaction datasets. High transcript
abundance can artificially inflate the frequency of detected RRIs and the apparent centrality of specific RNAs, particularly
those enriched in repetitive elements. This is especially relevant for network analyses and the evaluation of LCRs as
interaction hubs. I strongly encourage the authors to incorporate RNA expression levels (e.g., from matched RNA-seq data)
as a covariate in their analyses or to control for expression in matched random sampling procedures. Additionally,
expression-matched negative sets in RIME training could reduce overfitting to abundance-driven features. Addressing this
issue would improve the biological specificity and robustness of the conclusions drawn.

Potential Role of LCR-Mediated RRIs in Phase Separation 
The authors briefly speculate that LCR-driven RNA-RNA interactions might contribute to phase-separated condensates, but
this idea is not explored beyond a single sentence in the Discussion. Given the established link between low-complexity
sequences, multivalent RNA-RNA interactions, and biomolecular condensation, the manuscript would benefit from a more
thorough exploration of this possibility. For example, do LCR-enriched RNAs overlap with transcripts known to localize to
stress granules or nuclear speckles? Do they interact with RBPs containing intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs)?
Exploring this would broaden the biological relevance of their findings and potentially link their interaction networks to
spatial RNA organization in the cell.

Regulatory roles of RRIs 
While the manuscript effectively characterizes the sequence and structural features of RNA-RNA interactions, the functional
regulatory outcomes of these interactions remain underexplored. Beyond GO term enrichment and thermodynamic stability,

there is limited evidence that the LCR-mediated interactions identified influence gene expression, transcript stability,
splicing, or translation. Given the well-established roles of RRIs in these processes, the authors are encouraged to
investigate whether LCR-associated RRIs affect RNA fate. This could include integration with datasets on RNA decay,
alternative splicing, or RBP binding or through targeted experiments perturbing specific interactions (e.g., via LCR
mutagenesis or lncRNA knockdown). 
Doing so would substantially strengthen the biological impact of the work by moving from interaction cataloging to regulatory
function.

Additional Recommendations: 
Incorporate explicit RNA secondary structure analyses into results and discussion sections. Secondary structure elements
significantly affect RNA interaction formation. 
Include explicit consideration or hypothesis regarding why low-complexity repeats specifically have evolved as central hubs
for RNA interactions. 
Benchmarking against RIME can be broadened by including more recent deep-learning RNA interaction tools, explicitly
comparing strengths and weaknesses. 
A dedicated "limitations" subsection is strongly recommended to discuss potential biases, computational limits, repeat
annotations, and experimental constraints. 
This manuscript presents a significant and original contribution to the understanding of RNA-RNA interaction specificity and
modeling. 
The combination of large-scale transcriptomic data, experimental validation, and advanced machine learning is both timely
and impactful, however, several key claims—particularly those regarding the functional importance of LCRs—require
additional experimental evidence. 
Provided the authors address these concerns, this study has strong potential for publication in Nature Communications.
