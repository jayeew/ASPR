Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript from Yang et al. reports analyses of several publicly available large-scale datasets—including Neuropixels
recordings in mice and 7T fMRI in humans—in support of the claim made in the title: “Propagating cortical waves coordinate
sensory encoding and memory retrieval in the human brain”. The primary observation is that particular phases of a
previously described traveling wave process are preferentially associated with improved memory encoding or recall, as
operationalized according to encoding/decoding neural network models. The work builds on the group’s prior studies
describing these waves in humans and relevant behavioral results in mice. 
Overall, I found this to be a thoughtful piece of work, with a reasonable set of analyses that were carefully executed. It is a
valuable, novel contribution to the literature and will be of broad interest. I have no major technical concerns—just a few
comments on the framing that I hope will help clarify the authors’ message. 
1. There is a certain conceptual ambiguity at the heart of this study—namely, what specifically is the role of propagating
waves in promoting memory encoding and recall? Personally, I don’t see this ambiguity as a major flaw of the study, nor is it
obvious how to get to the bottom of it; however, I do think it is a limitation that should be more explicitly discussed. 
As the authors acknowledge, prior studies have separately demonstrated the existence of these waves, their
correspondence with indices of fluctuating arousal, and—in mice—modulation of sensory encoding according to
arousal/pupil size. The current paper is an important demonstration that these arousal-associated waves persist during task
performance, and that decodability from human brain images covaries with pupil size fluctuations. These are two valuable
findings—but I’m not sure that much more can be said beyond these points. In this sense, claims that “these waves in human
fMRI promote sensory information encoding and memory retrieval alternatively” (from the abstract) seem to sneak in causal
inferences that go beyond what the data can tell us. 
2. Related to this ambiguity, in certain places (e.g. the abstract and lines 145-148), the language slips into framing
“cascades” as the key regulator of memory in mice, and waves as the key regulator in humans. Are we sure this isn’t simply
because invasive electrophysiology was used in mice and brain-wide imaging was used in humans? In other words, very
many processes covary with arousal fluctuations; it’s not clear what can be specifically attributed to waves (or cascades)
versus any other correlate of these fluctuations (e.g., changes in pupil size, delta power, sharp-wave ripples etc.). 
3. Given this broader context, wouldn’t it be just as reasonable to contextualize the present observations within a broader
literature on arousal modulation of sensory encoding/recall rather than modulation by these waves per se (e.g., (Harris and
Thiele, 2011; Vinck et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2020); or in humans, e.g., (Goldinger and Papesh, 2012; Kucewicz et al., 2018))? 
a. To this point, it could be nice to present Fig. 3C-D (perhaps in the supplement) in a manner that aligns everything to pupil
size, rather than using the global fMRI signal as a reference in humans and firing patterns in mice. It would also be nice to
show such a representation without an estimated hemodynamic delay, as such a delay seems ill-defined in the presence of
these biphasic patterns. 
4. Related to this point, at present, the review of the rodent literature is very limited. The second paragraph of the Introduction
summarizes this literature through a total of three self-citations (each of these studies being performed on previously

published datasets). It would be nice to acknowledge a broader body of work in this space on which the present group’s
current and past works build. This includes some of the original studies reporting widespread coupling of neuronal firing
rates to pupil fluctuations (including their diverse delays and organization into two main clusters) (Okun et al., 2019; Stringer
et al., 2019), as well as prior arguments for the sort of exteroceptive-interoceptive (including memory encoding-retrieval)
switching that the present study is highlighting (e.g., (Buzsáki, 1989; Hasselmo, 1995)); see (Honey, Newman and Schapiro,
2017) for an extensive review of this theme). 
5. The question of whether spiking cascades and waves represent the same neurophysiological process is framed as a key
question addressed in this study. But to the extent that this question is (or even could be) answered by the current study—
namely, by interrelating phenomena observed asynchronously across not only different datasets, but different species—it is
already supported by prior literature. Specifically, although the connection between these waves and pupil fluctuations is
described here as the missing link, such a connection is apparent from prior works (Yellin, Berkovich-Ohana and Malach,
2015; Raut et al., 2021; Bolt et al., 2023; Podvalny, Sanchez-Romero and Cole, 2024). 
All this said, I believe it is an intriguing hypothesis that the propagating waves are directly involved in memory-related
processes, or that they may be a key, largely overlooked component of the arousal-related modulation of encoding/recall in
general. I hope that consideration of the above points will help the authors clarify their particular interpretations in relation to
a broader literature on arousal modulation of memory systems—and also clarify where those interpretations go beyond what
the data currently permit one to conclude.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Many thanks to the authors for edits/clarifications that largely address my original points and have improved an already
strong manuscript. There is one remaining point of clarification that I believe to be important, although relatively
straightforward to address. 
As raised in my initial point 3a., the interpretation of hemodynamic delay is complicated here. The authors provide
reasonable justification for their approach—namely, shifting all BOLD signals by 6 seconds to account for a presumed global
hemodynamic delay—although this does make certain conclusions dependent on this choice. For instance, the 6 second
shift is necessary to support claims regarding the temporal alignment of brain processes across datasets (e.g., in lines 359-
363). The cycle in question is ~11 seconds, meaning that not implementing a hemodynamic delay would lead to the
opposite conclusions regarding how things are aligned in time (i.e., in-phase relationships would become anti-phase). 
The reason this matters is because the concept of hemodynamic delay, and how exactly that is modeled, remains an
actively debated topic. The authors’ choice is reasonable to me, but I do feel it would be valuable to include an additional
Discussion/supplementary paragraph (referenced where relevant in the Results section—e.g. Figs 3 and 4, and around line
254 and onward) that (1) clearly notes which temporal relationships and interpretations depend upon the chosen
hemodynamic delay (e.g., coincidence of ripples and DMN activity, or relation between encoding performance and BOLD
activity in particular regions) and (2) discusses rationale for the global 6-s shift, and how conclusions might differ with
alternative choices (e.g., different global delay, regionally-optimized delays, or no hemodynamic delay). (Note: I saw that
Line 255 references a Methods section that supposedly discusses implementation of the hemodynamic delay, but I couldn’t
find said Methods section—so perhaps the authors accidentally omitted text speaking to the various points raised here.) 
All in all, I look forward to seeing this important contribution published.
