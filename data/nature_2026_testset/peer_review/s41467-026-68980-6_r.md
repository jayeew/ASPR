Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Wang et al. developed a strategy to modulate the local pH of peptide-based coacervates by incorporating different enzymatic
reactions. Using this system, the authors claim they can manipulate the pH within coacervates to enable reactions such as
PCR and in vitro transcription and translation (IVTT). Currently, the experimental evidence does not completely support the
conclusions. Many additional major/minor experiments and clarifications are needed before considering the potential of this
manuscript for publication. 
1) Line 67: The sentence “coacervate-based protocells are highly potential in revealing basic life mysteries” should be
rephrased. Consider more accurate terminology. 
2) Figure S1: Why did the authors calibrate the intracellular pH for the standard curve of SNARF-4F? Why not simply use in
vitro calibration with buffer solutions at defined pH values? Please justify. 
3) Line 122-128: The authors claim that CX-5461 disrupts nucleolar structure and also that deacidification of the nucleolus in
its presence is due to transcriptional inhibition. Could the structural disruption also (or possibly alone) contribute to the
observed pH changes? Please clarify whether the deacidification is exclusively attributed to transcriptional inhibition or if
structural effects also play a role. 
4) Figure S2: The differences between the pH with and without the treatment are negligible. Are they significant? Consider
adding error bars and statistics. 
5) Line 143: “in which coacervate droplets can be facilely generated, albeit with polydispersity” What does “facilely” mean? 
6) Figure 3C: Consider including representative bright-field microscopy images of the coacervates formed at different pHs.
This can help to confirm the turbidity data, and check the shape and size of the coacervates. It is important to rule out the
possibility that turbidity arises from non-liquid aggregates. 
7) Line 179-180: “The pH of the condensate and dilute phase of freshly prepared coacervates is slightly different, at 8.01 and
7.98, respectively.” Is this pH-difference significant? What is the variability across independent replicates, including
biological replicates? 
8) Figure S5: There is a typo with the concentration of HCl and NaOH. It should be “µM” instead of “mM”. 
9) Figure 3G: It is surprising that only 50 µM NaCl disrupts electrostatic interactions in a 10 mM peptide solution. This
concentration of NaCl is relatively low. Please comment on this observation and reconsider the interpretation. 
10) Figure 3L: The concentration of NaCl where coacervation occurs (from 0 to 260 mM) overlaps with the one used to
dissolve the coacervates in Figure 3G (50 µM). Can you explain the discrepancy between these two results? 
11) Major point: what percentage of the total enzyme concentration is inside the coacervates? And what is the volume
fraction of these coacervates? These values are critical, as the majority of enzymatic activity may be occurring in the dilute
phase rather than inside the coacervates. This clarification is key for interpreting the conclusions. 
12) Moreover, the volume fraction of the R10/D10 coacervates would give an indication on the accuracy of the micro pH
probe method in distinguishing the two phases. An image of the centrifuged sample would also provide additional validity to
the method. Based on Figure 3d there is no difference between the dense and dilute phase as no statistics are provided. It is
recommend to use the SNARF-4F dye previously employed to cross-validate the results from Figure 3d, e and f. 
13) It is suggested that the authors repeat the experiments of Figure 3 where they note a difference in the dense and dilute
phases in a system which is actually buffered (as per the Method section coacervates are prepared in water without an
additional buffering agent). Without a buffering agent, the observations of basification or acidification of the dense phase are
merely a partitioning of charged guest species as opposed to a true pH modulatory effect. Indeed the comparison made
between free enzymes and enzymes+coacervates the enzyme only solution is buffered in PBS. It is unclear if the coacervate
containing samples are also in PBS. If not, this needs to be performed to make the Michaelis Menten Kinetics truly
comparable. 
14) Can the strength of electrostatic interactions (KD in the nM range) between enzymes and R10, decrease the phase

coacervates in the presence and absence of the enzymes. 
15) How did the authors measure the reaction rates of GOx and urease within the coacervates? According to the methods,
the activity was not measured over time, just at one point of the reaction (activity in the supernatant after removing the

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this current study, Wang et al studies the differential pH between the dense and dilute phases of complex coacervates
across the condensate interface. They first show (confirm) previously reported results that such a difference in pH exists
between nucleoplasm and nucleolus in live cells. Then they use a simple model system, complex coacervates formed by
R10+D10, and study the differential pH across the interface and how that changes by the addition of salt and H+, and OH-
ions. Although the results are interesting, by no means, these coacervates formed by polyelectrolyte mixtures close to the
cellular structures. Very interestingly, from their cellular experiments, they observed that inhibition of rRNA production by
CX5461 disrupts the pH difference between the nucleolus and nucleoplasm, hinting at the role of active processes in
creating such a proton gradient. Such processes are absent in R10+D10 coacervates. Finally, the authors employ a few
common enzymes, such as Gox and urease, that can produce H+ or OH- ions as byproducts in these coacervate droplets.
They also attempted PCR reaction and IVTT in these droplets. 
The reported studies are highly qualitative in nature. There are at least a few recent reports(Ausserwöger et al. 2024; King et
al. 2024; Dai et al. 2024) that established that the concentration of H+ ions and other types of ions across the condensate
interface can be unequal, leading to an apparent difference in pH between the dense and dilute phases. However, it is
important to note that there is no “gradient” per se, since that would mean that the system is out of equilibrium. The
concentrations of the ions and/or protons are different across the condensate interface, similar to the concentration of
macromolecules that make up the condensate. This is also supported by the data showing CX5461 disrupts the pH
difference between the nucleolus and nucleoplasm. 
The model system (R10+D10) is interesting, but the characterization of such systems by the addition of salt and pH change
is not new. It is conceivable that as the system moves closer to the single phase regime by the addition of salt, the
differences in the H+ or OH- concentrations decrease between the dense and dilute phase since the density of the
macromolecules that make up the dense phase decreases as well. It will be good to establish quantitative phase diagrams
and how they relate to the differences in the pH between the dense and dilute phases. 
The addition of enzymes to these condensates is also interesting, but similar studies have been done at a much greater
depth(Jambon-Puillet et al. 2024; Testa et al. 2021). 
Finally, it will be great if the authors can focus on understanding the enzyme kinetics in condensates and how they are

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

impacted via differences in the intra-condensate pH. Such active systems may show emergent properties due to phase
separation-driven changes in the bulk pH, but to decipher that, more rigorous analysis is needed. 
Minor comments: the MD simulations and the use of microfluidics to generate coacervates are not really needed for this
study, are they? Some claims are not supported by the data, such as “bioreactors,” “protocells,” etc.; such terms should not
be used loosely. Finally, schematics like Fig. 1 are not helpful to occupy one figure in an article; rather, they may be better
reserved for SI. Describing how the enzyme reaction rates were measured at the section where the results were described
would be useful for the readers.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript "Coacervate Droplets as pH-Regionalized Protocells" aims to explore the potential mechanisms that govern
pH regulation in membrane-less organelles (MLOs). To this end, the authors first investigate differences in the condensed
phase nucleolus and nucleoplasm pH using a fluorescent indicator. The nucleoli report lower pH compared to the
nucleoplasm, with a less pronounced difference 
when RNA Pol I activity is inhibited or complete loss of a pH gradient when
they are disassembled upon treatment with actinomycin D. To capture a similar LLPS dependent pH gradient 
in vitro
, the
investigators design a peptide based complex coacervate formed by electrostatic complexation of decapeptides R10 and
D10. LLPS in this system is investigated as a function of pH, peptide concentration and presence of salt with this system
behaving in line with previously reported coacervates of similar nature. A small intrinsic pH gradient is noted with the
condensate phase being slightly more acidic, and coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations are used to explain the
preferential partitioning of the hydronium (H
3
0
+
) ions inside the peptide-rich droplets. Subsequently, enzymes are
encapsulated in these coacervates to build minimalistic protocell bioreactors. The enzymatic reactions of GOx and Urease
release products that influence the pH and therefore the substrate concentration serves as a handle to regulate enzyme
kinetics. 
The authors argue that this work provides potential insights into the mechanism for pH regulation in MLOs, but this is not
entirely evident as no similar examples are provided for sequestration of pH regulating enzymes in biological MLOs.
Nonetheless, the work provides an interesting demonstration of pH gradients in minimalistic peptide condensates with the
ability to regulate enzyme activity by tuning substrate concentration of the orthogonal pH reaction. The following concerns
should be addressed and clarifications provided prior to being considered for publication - 
1) In Figure 3 j-k and m-n, the RDFs indicate that with increasing peptide concentration or at lower salt-concentration, a
higher number of hydronium ion beads are recruited to the peptide-condensates. Similar RDFs for hydroxide (OH-) ions are
substantially lower (Figure S10) at g(r) ~ 7 vs. ~27 for H30+. The number of contacts measured from the simulations between
the peptides and ions does not appear to reflect this trend however with both H+ and OH- showing ~ 350 contacts within 0.6
nm with the peptides. This would require some clarification in the manuscript to explain the pH difference and preferential
partitioning of H+ into the condensates. 
2) Figure S5 (a-d): The caption reads 1 μM HCl/NaOH however the figure panels say 1 mM and should be corrected to be
consistent. 
3) Using the CG-MD simulations, the authors argue that the diffusion coefficients of the H+/OH- ions are reduced by
interaction with the coacervate network. In Figure S11, the H+ diffusion as quantified by the MSD always appears lower than
the OH- diffusion even at low coacervate concentration. H+ is known to have higher diffusion compared to OH- due to the
Grotthus effect and other mechanisms. Due the authors attribute this lower diffusion to preferential incorporation of the H+
beads in the peptide aggregates? A control simulation in the absence of peptides but presence of H+/OH- ions in similar box
volumes would be required to validate the MARTINI models' ability to capture the intrinsic differences in diffusivity of these
ions. 
4) The partitioning of enzymes was demonstrated to show electrostatic selectivity, with no significant binding and recruitment

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

noted between the D10 peptides and the FITC-GOx or other negatively charged systems (dsDNA/PAA). Although the
corresponding MD simulations for the favorable interaction are presented (GOx/Urease-R10), the manuscript/supplementary
information would benefit from a similar simulation with D10 indicating exclusion/poor interaction. Furthermore, this would
also rule out peptide-enzyme interactions as arising from excluded volume effects. 
5) The Michaelis-Menten plots in Figure 5 b-c show slower reaction rates inside the coacervates than in PBS. Condensates
that strongly partition the substrates and enzymes typically improve such reaction rates but the opposite effect can be noted
here. Is this due to lower diffusion inside the condensates or do some of the enzymes lose activity upon being bound by
peptides? 
6) The introduction and conclusions would generally benefit from a rewrite that emphasizes the pH differences, the ability to
regulate enzymatic reactions and consequently control IVVT or cascade reactions rather than the direct connection to pH
regulation in biological MLOs. The mechanisms of this are not entirely revealed in this manuscript, as the fundamental
reason of H+ enrichment in coacervates is not completely deciphered or explained but rather simply noted, observed and
exploited.

Introduction 
“Cellular functions rely on  numerous enzyme -catalyzed reactions that occur 
spatial-temporally correct under physiological conditions. The maintenance of enzyme 
activity is essential for metabolism, energy production, and the synthesis of various 
substances. A key factor that affects enzyme activity is pH, as each enzyme functions 
optimally within a specific pH range. Deviations from this range can impair the 
structure and function of enzymes, which even leads to diseases or disorders. It has 
been well established that various membrane  exchangers and transporters play 
significant roles in regulating intracellular pH. In contrast, membrane -less organelles 
(MLOs), which host diverse biochemical reactions and play essential roles in cellular 
regulation, lack such membrane -based control mec hanisms. Recent studies have 
revealed that MLOs can exhibit local pH variations, which may influence the kinetics 
and selectivity of enzymatic reactions. However, given the complexity of living cells, 
it is challenging to directly investigate the mechanisms of pH regulation in vivo by 
controlling conditions across the MLOs interface. 
In this study, we found that a natural pH difference exists between the nucleolus

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 45 -->

and nucleoplasm in several tested cell lines, which can be modulated by drug treatments 
to alter their structure and properties ( Figure 1a). Since coacervates are liquid -liquid 
phase separation (LLPS) systems driven by noncovalent interactions, and they share 
similar properties to MLOs such as molecular organization and compartmentalization, 
we then present peptide -based coacervate droplets as protocells to mimic the pH 
difference found in the nucleus.  We use microfluidics to prepare these coacervate 
droplets to ensure their uniformity in size, thus allowing for the generation of a similar 
pH difference across the LLPS interface under precisely controlled conditions. Through 
molecular dynamics simulations and quantitative experiments, we conclude that the 
dense phase of coacervates, rich in charged polymers, tends to attract a higher 
concentration of H+/OH- ions than the dilute phase. This results in an uneven apparent 
pH distribution between the interior and exterior of the coacervates. More importantly, 
such pH difference weakens or even disappears following destruction of LLPS, a 
phenomenon observed in both nucleolus and coacervate droplets. Based on these 
findings, we demonstrate that the pH -regionalized environment of the coacervate 
droplets can be custom ized by selectively accommodating enzymatic reactions (as 
shown in Figure 1b), and such feature can be further employed to control cascade 
reactions and perform polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and in vitro transcription and 
translation reaction (IVTT), thereby recapitulating various cellular activities (as shown 
in Figure 1c). Our findings suggest that coacervate -based protocells offer a valuable 
platform to explore how physicochemical compartmentalization influences 
biochemical reactions.” 
Discussion 
“This study employs coacervate droplet -based protocells as a minimal model to 
explore how phase separation can give rise to and utilize local pH differences for 
biochemical reaction regulation. We first identified a pH difference across the nucleolus 
in several tested cell lines and found that treatment with CX -5461 or Act D altered 
nucleolar structure and function, accompanied by changes in the pH difference between 
the nucleolus and nucleoplasm. We then developed an in vitro R10/D10 coacervate 
system to mirror such pH difference and examine its response to environmental changes.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 46 -->

Notably, uniform coacervate droplets are generated through the microfluidic technique, 
which allows precise control of the droplet composition as well as environmental 
parameters. We observe that a pH difference can be established between the condensate 
phase and the dilute phase of coacervates with the presence of acid or base added to the 
system. Through molecular dynamics simulations, we conclude that these pH 
differences likely arise from the enrichment of H +/OH- in the condensate phase rich in 
charged polymers. Intriguingly, we find that the pH difference no longer exists after the 
disassembly of the coacervate by adding salt, which mirrors the same phenomenon 
observed after the disassembly of the nucleolus. Based  on the pH-regionalized feature 
of the coacervate droplets, we further demonstrate a way of finely tuning the local pH 
by incorporating GOx - and urease -catalyzed reactions by leveraging the proteins’ 
partitioning affinity behavior. The acidic and alkaline byproducts of the enzymatic 
reactions can alter the local pH in dependence of the substrate concentration, which 
further impacts the activity of another, downstream enzymatic reaction. With this, the 
coacervate droplets can serve as protocells accommodating various biological-relevant 
processes such as PCR and IVTT. 
In conclusion, our work emphasizes how pH heterogeneity in coacervate -based 
protocells can be harnessed to regulate biochemical reactivity. Our findings also 
highlight the potential of coacervate droplets as pH-regionalized protocells to simulate 
cellular environments, study phase separation -related cellular activities, and perform 
complex bio(chemical) reactions.”
