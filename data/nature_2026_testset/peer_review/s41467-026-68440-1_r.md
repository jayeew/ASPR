Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study innovatively proposes the mechanism by which the "serine-S1P-Nrf2 axis inhibits ferroptosis to protect ovarian
function," with significant translational potential. However, several critical issues require attention. 
Main points: 
1. The clinical trial lacks descriptions of primary endpoints (e.g., AMH recovery rate, pregnancy rate) and the serine
administration regimen, reducing its translational value. Additionally, patients’ baseline characteristics (such as CTX
dosage, breast cancer stage) are not presented in the main text or supplementary tables. It is recommended to merge
Figures 1 and 2, particularly Figure 1e. 
2. In Figure 3K, growth curves for all groups increase before Day 27 but drop sharply between Day 27 and Day 30. Is there
an explanation for this phenomenon? Figure 3 omits a "serine-only supplementation (without CTX)" control group, making it
impossible to rule out serine’s direct effects on the ovaries. 
3. Figure 4 relies solely on transcriptomic inferences and requires protein-level validation. Key markers such as GPX4
protein levels/activity and ACSL4 expression should be detected. Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
images of ultrastructural changes (e.g., mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced cristae) are needed to fully establish the ferroptosis
evidence chain. 
4. Figure 5 does not use inhibitors like Ferrostatin-1 to directly block ferroptosis, weakening the specificity of conclusions. In
Figure 6, the claim that serine promotes S1P synthesis is inferred indirectly only via the inhibitor SKI178, without measuring
in vivo S1P level changes or performing exogenous S1P rescue experiments. It is recommended to add in vivo S1P
detection and rescue experiments using HO-1-KO cells. 
5. Regarding Figure 7: First, the sudden shift to discussing staying up late and sleep deprivation is confusing, and the
connection to the study’s core focus—serine inhibiting ovarian ferroptosis to maintain ovarian function—requires
clarification. Second, evidence for the mechanism of idiopathic POI is weak, and the causal relationship of "staying up late"
remains unproven: Sleep deprivation was assessed subjectively via questionnaires (106 responses) without objective
monitoring (e.g., actigraphy), and confounding factors (stress, diet) were not controlled, making the assessment insufficient.
While the sleep deprivation model shows iron accumulation, the animal model does not validate serine’s core role (e.g.,
verifying its rescue effect through supplementation), failing to demonstrate that "serine deficiency is the primary cause." 
Minor points: 
1. Please revise the title to reflect that serine inhibits ovarian ferroptosis to maintain ovarian function. 
2. "POI (premature ovarian insufficiency)" and "POF (premature ovarian failure)" are used interchangeably. It is
recommended to unify terminology as "POI" in line with the latest clinical guidelines. 
3. Figure 2e: Correlation coefficients (R² and p-values) between serum serine levels and AMH/E
₂
 need to be added. 
4. All tumor images, as well as immunofluorescence, H&E staining, and electron microscopy images, must include scale
bars. 
5. Key literature in the field is missing, including studies on the dual roles of serine and SLC1A4 in tumors, and crosstalk
mechanisms between ferroptosis, apoptosis, and senescence in POF. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors investigate how the breast cancer chemotherapeutic cyclophosphamide (CTX) induces

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

premature ovarian failure (POF). They demonstrate that CTX upregulates the amino acid transporter SLC1A4, leading to
reduced serum serine levels. Clinical correlations between serum serine and ovarian function markers (E2, AMH) support
the idea that serine depletion is linked to ovarian insufficiency. Importantly, dietary serine supplementation in murine breast
cancer models prevented CTX-induced POF without impairing CTX’s anti-tumor efficacy. 
Mechanistically, the study shows that CTX induces ferroptosis in ovarian granulosa cells (GCs), but not in breast cancer
cells. The ferroptosis regulator Hmox1/HO-1 is highlighted as a key mediator via iron overload, with upstream regulation
through serine-dependent S1P synthesis and Nrf2/HO-1 signaling. Furthermore, data from women with idiopathic POF
revealed similar reductions in serum serine, strengthening the translational relevance. 
This work is timely, clinically meaningful, and novel. However, while the descriptive evidence is strong, the mechanistic
claims remain underdeveloped. In particular, the causal involvement of SLC1A4, HO-1, and S1P signaling is not rigorously
validated. With additional functional experiments, the manuscript would make a more compelling mechanistic and
translational contribution. 
Major Comments 
Mechanistic validation is insufficient. 
The central role of SLC1A4 is proposed but not directly tested. Knockdown or knockout approaches (even in vitro, e.g.,
siRNA in GCs) are critical to establish causality. The authors note plans for SLC1A4 knockout mice in the Discussion, but
some functional SLC1A4 loss-of-function data should be included here. 
Similarly, the roles of S1P synthesis and HO-1 are inferred from correlative data. Silencing/rescue experiments (e.g., Sphk
inhibition + serine rescue; HO-1 inhibition or overexpression) would provide much stronger evidence for the proposed
pathway. 
Comparison of CTX effects in GCs vs. breast cancer cells is incomplete. 
CTX-induced ferroptosis is highlighted as GC-specific, yet the assays used are inconsistent between cell types. For
example, ferroptosis in GCs is demonstrated via MDA assay and HO-1 protein, whereas in breast cancer cells it is measured
by GPX4, Fth1, and Slc3a2 mRNA. Consistent assays across both systems are necessary for a rigorous comparison. 
In Figures 5a–b vs. 5g, differences in microscopy magnification and the lack of quantification in 5g weaken the conclusion.
Standardized imaging and quantification would strengthen this point. 
Generality beyond CTX. 
Because many alkylating agents are known to be gonadotoxic, testing whether other alkylators also reduce serine and
promote POF would broaden the clinical impact and generalizability of the model. 
Pathway cohesion (S1P → HO-1). 
The manuscript proposes a serine–S1P–Nrf2/HO-1 axis, but the experimental evidence remains fragmented. Integration of
up/downregulation studies (as noted in Major Comment 1) is needed to convincingly link these steps. 
Minor Comments 
Several figures are difficult to interpret. For example, the schematic in Figure 4f is overly complex, and some confocal
images are too small or unclear. Redesigning these for clarity would help readers. 
The incidence of ovarian dysfunction after just one CTX dose in patients (25/27 cases) appears strikingly high compared to
prior reports. Some discussion of this discrepancy is warranted. 
Terminology should be standardized: “POF” and “POI” are used interchangeably; the authors should clarify and adopt one
term consistently.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This study investigates the protective role of serine against cyclophosphamide (CTX)-induced ovarian damage by inhibiting
granulosa cell ferroptosis. The findings propose a novel mechanism involving the serine–S1P–Nrf2–HO-1 pathway, with

Major Comments:

8. The proposed regulatory relationship between Nrf2, SLC1A4, and HO-1 remains unclear. Experimental evidence or cited

(Remarks to the Author) 
Thank you to the authors for their thoughtful revision. The additional experiments and clarifications provided are appreciated.
Please find below specific comments regarding the authors’ responses. 
1. Mechanistic Validation Remains Insufficient 
The authors reference their previously published article, which demonstrates SLC1A4 silencing and its effect on CTX-
induced granulosa cell death. This prior work is appreciated and provides supporting evidence that SLC1A4 contributes to
CTX-induced granulosa-cell death. However, because this mechanistic validation lies outside the current dataset, it is
recommended that the authors incorporate a more detailed discussion of their prior findings in the main text.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

In addition to this, the experiments that the authors highlight from their Supplementary data, which examine S1P synthesis
and HO-1 function are valuable; however, they do not yet establish causality, which was the central concern. These
experiments rely exclusively on chemical inhibition without complementary genetic manipulation or rescue approaches.
Specifically, for S1P synthesis, the proposed link between serine availability and S1P production remains unclear, as serine
supports de novo sphingolipid synthesis rather than directly serving as an S1P precursor. Inclusion of a direct rescue
experiment (e.g., exogenous S1P supplementation) would substantially strengthen the mechanistic argument. 
Regarding HO-1, while ZnPP inhibition is informative, ZnPP has known off-target effects; without supporting overexpression
or genetic knockdown data, the conclusion that HO-1 is the primary driver of CTX-induced ferroptosis or POI remains
speculative. The authors are therefore encouraged to temper causal statements and present these findings as supportive
rather than definitive evidence of this pathway’s involvement. 
2. Standardized Imaging and Quantification 
The rationale for not quantifying Prussian blue staining remains unconvincing. Prussian blue detects ferric (Fe³
⁺
), not ferrous
(Fe²
⁺
) ions, the latter being directly involved in driving ferroptosis via the Fenton reaction. Accordingly, the interpretation of
“ferrous ion accumulation” should be corrected. Moreover, the absence of visible staining in tumor sections does not
preclude quantification; semi-quantitative image analysis could still reveal meaningful differences (e.g., near-zero signal
relative to ovarian tissue). 
The authors point to Hmox-1 mRNA expression changes as supporting evidence. However, Hmox-1 represents an indirect
marker of labile iron accumulation. Therefore, at a minimum, the authors should either provide such image-based
quantification or revise the discussion to frame these findings as qualitative observations rather than quantitative
conclusions regarding iron accumulation and ferroptosis. 
Other concerns have been adequately addressed.

Reviewer#2 and 3 
In this manuscript, the authors investigate how the breast cancer chemotherapeutic 
cyclophosphamide (CTX) induces premature ovarian failure (POF). They 
demonstrate that CTX upregulates the amino acid transporter SLC1A4, leading to 
reduced serum serine levels. Clinical correlations between serum serine and ovarian 
function markers (E2, AMH) support the idea that serine depletion is linked to 
ovarian insufficiency. Importantly, dietary serine supplementation in murine breast 
cancer models prevented CTX -induced POF without impairing CTX’s an ti-tumor 
efficacy. 
Mechanistically, the study shows that CTX induces ferroptosis in ovarian 
granulosa cells (GCs), but not in breast cancer cells. The ferroptosis regulator 
Hmox1/HO-1 is highlighted as a key mediator via iron overload, with upstream 
regulation through serine -dependent S1P synthesis and Nrf2/HO -1 signaling. 
Furthermore, data from women with idiopathic POF revealed similar reductions in 
serum serine, strengthening the translational relevance. 
This work is timely, clinically meaningful, and no vel. However, while the 
descriptive evidence is strong, the mechanistic claims remain underdeveloped. In 
particular, the causal involvement of SLC1A4, HO -1, and S1P signaling is not 
rigorously validated. With additional functional experiments, the manuscri pt would 
make a more compelling mechanistic and translational contribution.
