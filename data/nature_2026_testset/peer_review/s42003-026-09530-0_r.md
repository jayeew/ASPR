Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
AD is the most common cause of dementia in the elderly, but effective disease-modifying therapies remain unavailable.
miRNAs are dysregulated in the brain, CSF, and blood of AD patients, providing one avenue of research. In this manuscript,
the authors analysed the temporal superior T1 isocortex of control and AD patients, identifying and validating differentially
expressed miRNAs. The authors also found that some miRNAs were correlated with Braak stages and tau pathology.
However, there are some concerns: 
1. The NGS and RT-qPCR data of miR-151b and miR-3065-3p were opposite (Lines 248-249). In this study, the authors
used two different methods, and RT-qPCR was employed for verification. If the results are different using the same samples,
it would raise the question of whether the methods or experiments were correct. Can the authors explain? 
2. Differential expression of miRNAs was analyzed in the temporal superior T1 isocortex in this study. Table 2 showed other
studies reporting 13 miRNAs. Whether miRNAs in these reports were analyzed in the same subfield or other subfields in the
brain? 
3. In the Introduction part, the authors depicted the inconsistent results of miR-125b-5p (Lines 52-56). What is the data of
miR-125b-5p in this study? Is the result influenced by subfields in the brain or detection methods? 
4. The style of references in Fig. 1c needs to be the same with the main text for the convenience of readers. 
5. The references need to be cited in the main text as mentioned (Lined 191-192). 
6. The clinical application of differential expression of miRNAs needs to be stressed, such as diagnosis and replacement
therapy. 
7. How long is the first centrifugation in Fig. 7a?

9. The sentence (Lines 260-261) needs to be modified for grammar mistakes.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled “Differentially expressed miRNAs in the temporal cortex of Alzheimer’s disease patients and their
association to tau pathology” submitted by Nagaraj et al. presented a study on miRNA expression in the AD and control
brains. The authors found that several miRNAs were dysregulated in association with the Braak stages, and interestingly,
miR-146b-5p and miR-151a-5p seemed to modulate tau seeding in vitro. Although several studies on miRNA changes
associated with AD were already reported, the novel aspect of the current study relates to revealing miRNAs directly
correlated with tau pathology. Overall, the study was solid and presented clearly. However, there were also several issues
related to case selection and data analysis. Comments below may help clarify and strengthen the manuscript content. 
1. The authors filtered out significant differential expressed miRNAs by comparing expression levels in two Braak stage
groups (0-III vs V-VI). One important caveat of this comparison needs to be pointed out is that the brains with Braak V-VI
were at the end stage of AD disease, which had significant loss of cells and tissues. Therefore, the comparison of these two
defined groups is not exactly “apple to apple” in terms of tissue composition and cell types in these brains. 
2. Figure 5 presented correlations of miRNA expression and Braak stages for several miRNAs, for example miR-129-5p,
which shown seemly a decreasing trend along increasing Braak stage. Do any other miRNAs in the NGS dataset —those

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

did not meet the ‘significance criteria’—may demonstrate any ‘trending’ correlations with Braak stages, especially in the
control group? It is important to learn whether there miRNA changes associated within Braak stages 0-IV prior to full blown
pathology. 
3. AD is a highly sex-biased disease, which is more prevalent in women. In the current study, there were more females in AD
group but more males in control group (only 1 female in control group). It is unclear whether sex was stratified in the miRNA
analysis. Again, this is not exactly “sex to sex” comparisons. 
4. AD is a complex disease and often comorbid with other neuropathological conditions, such as Lewy body pathology or
cerebrovascular pathology. The authors should provide information for whether these cases were pure AD or had additional
pathologies. 
5. It is curious that while miR-146b-5p mimic enhanced the tau seeding in in vitro studies, yet this miRNA was decreased in
AD brains. The readers would appreciate an interpretation of these data. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study investigates the differential expression of miRNAs in the temporal cortex of AD in patients compared to controls.
Using a combination of NGS for initial screening and qRT-PCR for validation, the authors identify several miRNAs that are
dysregulated in AD. Importantly, the study aims to link these miRNA changes to tau pathology and seeding-activity and
further explores potential functional implications through target gene identification and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.
Overall, the manuscript is well-structured, methodologically sound and easy to read. Major and minor revisions are listed
below. 
Major revisions: 
1. The final analysis is done on a relatively small cohort (n=19), with a significant imbalance in sex distribution between
control and AD groups. This limitation, given its propensity to introduce confounding effects (and as sex differences are
known to influence AD pathology) should be discussed in more detail and consider statistical adjustments or sensitivity
analyses to account for sex as a covariate. Further the rationale for selection of a subset of cases for NGS should be
explained in the results. 
2. The criteria for selecting differentially expressed miRNAs could be more clearly justified. The rationale for these specific
cutoffs should be explained better, especially since no miRNAs reached significance after FDR correction at the
conventional 0.05 threshold. 
3. There are inconsistencies between NGS and qRT-PCR results for some miRNAs (like miR-151b and miR-3065-3p). The
authors mention this but should provide a more detailed discussion on possible technical or biological reasons for these
discrepancies. 
4. There are no correlation analyses done between miRNA levels and tau pathology (Braak). As shown in Figure 5, this is
just data stratified by Braak stage. Correlation analyses need to be done to state there are associations with specific
miRNAs. Further, controls are negative for PHF1, therefore correlation analysis with miRNA levels should be done only in
the AD cases and exclude controls from those analyses. 
5. It should be clear to the reader the rationale of why miRNA changes were evaluated with respect to tau (vs amyloid beta)
pathology as the hypothesis being pursued. 
6. The manuscript briefly mentions two novel miRNAs (71 and 122) but exclude them from validation due to low confidence.
The criteria for low confidence should be specified, and the potential biological relevance of these two novel miRNAs should
be expanded on. Except for these 2 novel miRNAs, the present study just validates previous findings. 
7. Identifying pathways relating to the differentially expressed miRNA are of interest, but it is unclear whether it is adding any
value to the overall hypothesis of the studies. Authors should better describe the implications of those findings if they want to
include them in the manuscript. 
8. There are mismatches between results and discussion. These should be carefully checked throughout. For example, in
the discussion it reads “we found no correlation between RIN and miRNA expression.”, but in the results we also see
“Among the 4 high abundance downregulated miRNAs 229 comparisons, all showed positive correlation, but only some
were statistically significant (miR-129-5p 230 vs. miR-146b-5p, miR-129-5p vs. miR-132-3p, miR-146b-5p vs. miR-1-3p,
miR-146b-5p vs. miR-132- 231 3p). 
Minor revisions: 
1. The authors should clarify criteria for significance when describing multiple comparison corrections, for some descriptions
of “trend” it is not clearly stated if they were significant under authors' own thresholds. 
2. While RIN and PMD are shown not to correlate with miRNA expression, other potential confounders should be
acknowledged if data are available. 
3. The discussion would benefit from a more critical and in-depth comparison/discussion of the findings to recent literature
(especially large-scale miRNA profiling studies in AD). 
4. The rationale for using RIPA soluble vs Sarkosyl-insoluble for studying pTau should be clearly stated. 
5. Figure 7 is missing scale bars 
6. A couple of improvements in biological interpretation could also be improved. For example, some miRNA roles are
described without adequate discussion of their known potential roles in neurodegeneration or even their relevance. In
addition, the functional relevance of miR-151a-5p and miR-145b-5p in tau seeding is intriguing, but the authors do not
provide a mechanistic hypothesis/connection/etc. to readers. 
7. For the statement “Additionally, our analysis demonstrated that cohort stratification showed no discernible 195 correlation
to either the brain banks assessed (Neuro-CEB brain bank vs ULB LHNN brain bank) or the 196 groups studied (control vs
AD) (Supplementary fig. 1b-c).” This supplementary figure does not address correlation of data with the different brain banks.

review and clarify the method of choice. While delta Ct may be of benefit when analyzing different housekeeping genes,
when comparing controls and AD, having a better sense of fold-change may be most relevant.

The reviewer raises an important point about the rationale for using RIPA soluble vs Sarkosyl-insoluble

soluble fraction is relatively easy to obtain and sufficient for quantifying phosphorylated tau (pTau) via 
Western blot. This fraction is prepared by centrifuging the sample at 20,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. 
However, the RIPA-soluble fraction contains only low levels of aggregated paired helical filaments  
(PHFs), which are necessary to generate a robust fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) signal 
in HEK tau biosensor cells.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 33 -->

For this purpose, in HEK tau biosensor cells , the Sarkosyl -insoluble fraction is more suitable, as it 
provides a higher concentration of aggregated PHFs required for tau seeding FRET assays. 
The procedure to obtain the Sarkosyl-insoluble fraction involves two centrifugation steps. The first step 
isolates the soluble fraction by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C, after which N -
lauroylsarcosine sodium salt is added. This mixture is then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 180,000 
× g for 30 minutes at 4 °C. The detergent effectively solubilizes natively folded proteins, and the 
resulting pellet contains an enrichment of detergent-insoluble protein aggregates, such as aggregated 
PHFs. These pellets are resuspended in PBS by vortexing and pipetting and are used as the Sarkosyl -
insoluble fraction.

Although we had complete diagnostic information for tau pathology (Braak staging), data on amyloid 
pathology (Thal staging) were not available for all samples in our cohort. Consequently, our analysis 
focused exclusively on evaluating the association betwe en differential miRNA expression and tau 
pathology, using Braak staging and phosphorylated tau quantification. Nevertheless, based on the 
available Thal staging data for amyloid pathology (13 out of 19 subjects used for sequencing), we 
observed a similar correlation between Thal staging and miRNA expression as seen with tau pathology 
(Supplementary Fig. 6). This observation aligns with previous findings reporting that miR-132-3p and 
miR-129-5p in the brain are associated with both amyloid and tau pathology (1). Given that miRNAs 
can target multiple genes and influence multiple biological pathways, we do not exclude the possibility 
that these identified differentially expressed miRNAs also contribute to amyloid pathology. Our study 
lacks experimental evidence directly linking the identified miRNAs to amyloid pathology. A literature 
search revealed that among the identified miRNAs, miR -132-3p has been associated with amyloid 
pathology in mouse models. One study demonstrated that deletion of miR -132/212 promoted A β 
production and amyloid plaque formation in the brains of 3xTg-AD mice (2). Similarly, in APP/PS1 mice, 
intracerebroventricular injection of antagomiR-132 increased amyloid plaque burden (3). Supporting 
this finding, another study showed that overexpression of miR -132-3p reduced amyloid plaque 
formation in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice (4). Further studies involving larger, well-characterized 
cohorts encompassing both pathological features, along with experimental models representing both 
combined and distinct pathologies, are needed to elucidate the associations and regulatory roles of 
these identified differentially expressed miRNAs.
