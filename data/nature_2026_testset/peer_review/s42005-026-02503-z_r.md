Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript investigates α-T3 nanoribbons under non-Hermitian boundary conditions and reports the emergence of
anomalous edge states (AESs), exceptional points, and “imaginary Landau levels,” with consequences for the Hall
conductance. The idea that boundaries alone can induce robust non-Hermiticity without bulk gain/loss is novel and
potentially impactful, and the α-T3 model provides a suitable testbed. The manuscript combines continuum derivations with
tight-binding modelling, and the connection to transport anomalies is timely. 
However, several conceptual and technical aspects need to be clarified and strengthened. 
1)The central mechanism remains qualitative. Please provide stronger microscopic justification (tight-binding/DFT results or
concrete literature) for anomalous dangling bonds (ADBs) that act as stable, directional non-Hermitian boundary couplings.
Discuss realistic platforms where such boundary-only non-Hermiticity could be engineered. 
2)The δ-substitution and the operator A_ad are introduced heuristically. A more systematic derivation—via self-adjoint
extension, complex absorbing potentials, or Lindblad formalism—would greatly improve rigor. 
3)Group velocities and edge currents should be defined biorthogonally, using left and right eigenvectors. Please re-derive
Eq. (7) accordingly and clarify the interpretation of finite currents for AESs. 
4)The derivation based on edge currents should be complemented or cross-checked with a proper non-Hermitian Kubo or
Keldysh formulation. The role of point vs. line gaps and spectral winding should also be discussed. 
5)The manuscript states that “exceptional points induce a topological transition in the complex energy spectrum from line
gapped to gapless.” A topological invariant supporting the claimed topological transition would be valuable. 
6)The implementation with large on-site potentials and non-reciprocal boundary hoppings seems ad hoc. Please justify this
construction, describe the parameter regimes of robustness, and ideally provide code or data to support reproducibility. 
7)To enhance the impact and broader relevance of the findings, the authors should examine how their key conclusions
apply to one or more specific experimentally reported systems. 
Minor issues: 
•Many typos (“flatten band” → “flat band,” “exception points” → “exceptional points,” etc.) and language issues should be
corrected. 
•Please provide intermediate steps between Eqs. (19)–(21). 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author)

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

The manuscript examines non-Hermitian solutions to nanoribbons of the alpha-T3 lattice, a lattice comprising 3 sites (A, B,
C) and two bonds (t1, t2), in the presence of a perpendicular magnetic field. The authors argue that armchair nanoribbons
are Hermitian in nature but some zigzag nanoribbons naturally are non-Hermitian. The authors appear to motivate this
physically by suggesting that depending on the termination, the broken bond at the edge may have surrounding
bonds/atoms that would prevent passivation, which leave behind what they refer to as anomalous dangling bonds (ADBs).
They then assume that these ADBs will have a specific non-Hermitian form and study solutions of resulting Hamiltonian.
They observe anomalous edge states that produce both flat and dispersing real spectra. They also calculate the Hall
conductance of the nanoribbons and find them to no longer be quantized. 
While I do not find issue with the mathematics contained in this manuscript, I do find the physical interpretations and
takeaways to be troublesome and misleading. The authors claim that a Hermitian system can be made non-Hermitian simply
by terminating the lattice at specific places. This is physically not motivated at all and the authors themselves show that this
does not naturally occur in their lattice model until they manually add a non-Hermitian term at edges of the nanoribbons.
Even when considering hopping to virtual states, the natural assumption is that the hopping would be Hermitian. The
authors, however, construct virtual states where the hopping is non-Hermitian and suggest that this is motivated physically
by being anomalous (a tautology it seems). 
There are many ways to physically motivate non-Hermitian gain/loss in a Hermitian system including external pumping or
connections to reservoirs in open quantum systems, but this particular approach I do not find compelling. Again, it is perfectly
acceptable to study this system mathematically, and the authors appear to be very thorough and rigorous in that regard. But I
think it is important to be careful in physically motivating any non-Hermitian contributions to our physical models. There are
far more non-Hermitian terms that can be added to a Hamiltonian than can be reasonably motivated physically. It is possible
I have misunderstood the motivation here, and so I am open to hearing the authors rebuttal. But given the current framing, I
cannot recommend this article be published in Communications Physics.

The Referee’s Comment:
1) The central mechanism remains qualitative. Please provide stronger microscopic jus-
tification (tight-binding/DFT results or concrete literature) for anomalous dangling bonds
(ADBs) that act as stable, directional non-Hermitian boundary couplings. Discuss realistic

Our Response:
This comment is highly insightful and constructive. The anomalous dangling bond (ADB)
mechanism and the resulting boundary-induced non-Hermiticity are grounded in a synthesis
of tight-binding formalisms, the theory of self-adjoint extensions, and the established concept
of complex absorbing potentials (CAPs). Below, we provide a systematic justification.
(1) Microscopic Justification: Tight-Binding Model and Boundary Condition Validity
The electronic structure of the α-T3 lattice, including its edge states, can be characterized
by a tight-binding model as shown in the main text. For zigzag nanoribbons, the boundary
conditions are non-arbitrary and determined by the lattice termination, which dictates that
a specific sublattice (A, B, or C) resides at the physical edge. It has been confirmed that
different types of zigzag edges in graphene can significantly affect the electronic structure
and induce edge states[1]. Importantly, the boundary conditions in present work represent a
natural extension of Brey and Fertig’s work on graphene[2], which is the limitation ofα = 0 in
our work. Within the α-T3 model, the distinct boundary conditions for different zigzag edge
terminations (A&C, A&B, B&C) have been explicitly derived, revealing that for non-B&B
terminations, the wavefunction on one sublattice does not vanish completely at the boundary
(i.e., ψA/B/C ̸= 0). To specifically characterize the behavior of such wavefunctions, localizing
at the boundary and decaying into the outside on the opposite side as required by the
continuity of the wavefunction, we introduce the Anomalous Dangling Bond (ADB), which
distinguishes itself from conventional unstable dangling bonds that are typically passivated.
(2) Bulk Impact and the Necessity for an Effective Description
Crucially, these boundary-localized ADBs are not mere boundary curiosities; they pro-

foundly influence the global electronic structure of the bulk. This is most prominently
manifested in the emergence of imaginary Landau levels in the bulk spectrum and the de-
struction of quantized Hall conductance within the ADB-dominated energy window (Fig.
5b). Such phenomena highlight a form of bulk-boundary correspondence, where boundary
physics dictates the key transport properties of the bulk. To capture such an effect within
a low-energy continuum theory, the Hermitian bulk Hamiltonian must be supplemented by
an effective term that encapsulates the dissipative channel afforded by ADBs.
(3) Delta Potential as a Limit of a Complex Absorbing Potential
A standard theoretical tool for modeling the loss of probability amplitude in an open quan-
tum system is the Complex Absorbing Potential (CAP), which is an imaginary potential[3,
23], −iw(r), where w(r) is Hermitian, added to the Hamiltonian to absorb outgoing waves.
In our physical picture, the ADB acts as a natural, atomically-sharp conduit for the elec-
tronic wavefunction to leak into the boundary and outside. To model this in the continuum,
we take the conceptual limit where the finite-width absorbing region is compressed into
an infinitesimally thin layer at the boundary. This limit naturally produces a boundary-
localized delta function, δ[g(x, y)], leading directly to the non-Hermitian operator Aad in
our manuscript. The strength of this potential is not a free parameter but is fixed by the
microscopic tight-binding parameters ( ∝ ℏvF /a ) via a regularization procedure, as shown
in our response to Comment 2.
(4) Mathematical Rigor via Non-Self-Adjoint Extension Theory
The introduction of this imaginary delta potential finds its rigorous mathematical jus-
tification in the theory of non-self-adjoint extensions[4]. The physical boundary condition
(Ψedge ̸= 0) defines an initial domain on which the momentum operator (and hence the
Hamiltonian) is not self-adjoint. The systematic procedure to find a physically consistent,
effective Hamiltonian involves extending this domain. As established in the seminal work of
Faddeev and others[5](e.g., in the context of zero-range potentials), such extensions can be
parameterized by singular, complex potentials, precisely of the delta-function form. There-
fore, our Aad term represents the specific non-self-adjoint extension required to consistently
describe the α-T3 system with ADB-mediated boundary occupation and dissipation. This
formalism unifies the CAP intuition with rigorous operator theory.
(5) Realistic Platforms
Boundary-only non-Hermiticity can be engineered by coupling a Hermitian bulk to

boundary-confined dissipative or gain reservoirs, or by utilizing the accumulation of bound-
ary charges.
(a) Photonic Waveguides and Metasurfaces[6, 7]: In a Hermitian photonic crystal or
waveguide array, the bulk can be designed to be lossless. By introducing lossy materials
or radiation channels only at the edges, the evanescent tails of the bulk modes couple to
these dissipative boundaries. This creates a non-Hermitian boundary condition precisely
through the leakage of evanescent waves, without altering the Hermitian nature of the bulk
Hamiltonian. This is directly analogous to the ”virtual sites” in our model.
It is worth noting that common dangling bond has been engineered in ‘photonic graphene’
and unconventional edge states have been found in Ref.[8].
(b) Cold Atoms in Optical Lattices[9, 10]: A cloud of ultracold atoms in an optical lattice
is an extremely clean Hermitian system. Selective boundary dissipation can be implemented
by projecting a weak, localized electron beam or a resonant laser exclusively at the edges
of the atomic cloud. This beam would ionize or excite atoms only at the boundary, ef-
fectively measuring them and projecting them into a lossy channel. The influence of this
measurement/localized loss on the bulk atoms is again mediated by the quantum-mechanical
evanescent coupling (tunneling) at the edge.
(c) Electrostatically Gated Quantum Materials[11]: In nanoribbons of materials (e.g.,
Hg1−xCdxTe quantum wells[12]), one can use local top gates or side gates to apply a strong
electrostatic potential exclusively at the edges. This will locally alter the chemical potential
and induce charge accumulation at boundary atoms, particularly where ADBs are present.
The resulting localized charge density mimics the boundary loss/gain in our model and can
be tuned in situ via gate voltage.
(d) Ion-liquid or Electrolyte Gating at Edges[13, 14]: In a solid-state device, confining an
ionic liquid or solid electrolyte to the sample edges allows one to induce strong electric double
layers and charge doping only at the boundaries. This is a bulk-preserving but edge-actuated
method to realize the non-Hermitian boundary condition via electrostatic accumulation.
