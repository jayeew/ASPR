Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Overview: 
This paper analyses landscape transformation in Northern Belgium, based on deep learning segmentation applied to
historical topographical maps from 1774, 1873, 1969 and a present day-land use (LU) map from 2022. For the four time
steps, 9 major LU classes where identified and mapped at a resolution of 10x10 meters. 
Differences in associations between soil types and LU classes, in autocorrelation for LU classes and in cumulative
distribution of the interspersion level are assessed and discussed in relation to the main drivers of LU change over the
assessed approx. 250 years. 
The authors find that the first period from 1774 to 1873 was characterized by land reclamation, the second period from 1873
to 1969 by agricultural intensification and the last period from 1969 to 2022 by urbanization. 
General comments: 
The applied deep learning segmentation to historical topographical maps is interesting and novel in the sense that, to my
knowledge, there are no other studies, which have applied an automized segmentation (or vectorization) of several (9) LU
classes from historical maps for a whole region (13,800 km²) over a time span of 250 years, and with 4 time spans.
Comparable studies have either been applied to much smaller study areas, fewer LU classes, fewer time steps and/or
shorter time spans. 
A major drawback is that the analysis of long-term changes is rather descriptive. The authors argue that the quality of
georeferencing of the applied maps is too low to allow for spatially explicit assessment of LU-transformations. This is a fair
argument. However, particularly in the discussion section, the authors describe transitions between LU-classes, e.g., from
heathland and dunes to arable and forest and from arable to pasture. It needs to be clarified that these are “visual”
interpretations of the maps. 
In the results and discussion sections, the authors frequently give explanations for trends in associations between soil types
and LU classes, in autocorrelation for LU classes and in interspersion. However, these are often somewhat esoteric and
need better explanation. 
Finally, I lack a more general discussion of how changes in mapping regimes (the purpose of producing maps) over time
might bias the definition of LU-classes and thus of LU-changes. E.g., was the definition of heathland and dunes the same in
1774 as in 2022? This should at least be reflected on. 
I recommend accepting this paper after a major revision

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This interesting study aims to quantify land-use changes over nearly 250 years (1774–2022) in northern Belgium using a
novel approach combining high-resolution historical maps and deep learning segmentation (GeoAI). By using artificial
intelligence, the evolution of land use can be mapped and analysed much faster and over a much larger geographical area
than was possible until a few years ago. These new techniques are not only time-saving but also enable innovative long-
term and comparative research, from various perspectives. The present contribution is a good example of the research
possibilities offered by these new techniques. Covering a 13,800 km² area, the authors analyzed four time slices (1774,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

1873, 1969, 2022) to trace the transformation of landscapes. The methodology involved the use of semantic segmentation
on tiled historical maps, allowing the authors to classify nine consistent land-use categories across all time periods. These
classifications were validated using historical map interpretation and field surveys, achieving high accuracy scores. Spatial
analyses focused on the association between land use and soil types, spatial autocorrelation, and land-use interspersion. 
The results indicate three successive phases of landscape transformation. First, between 1774 and 1873, land reclamation
reduced the area of natural and semi-natural land. In the second phase (1873–1969), agricultural intensification led to a shift
from arable land to grassland and orchards. The final phase (1969–2022) was marked by rapid urbanization and suburban
sprawl, weakening the historical link between land-use patterns and soil characteristics. As a consequence, previously
distinct landscape types became increasingly homogenized. 
This comprehensive reconstruction of land-use dynamics over nearly 250 years offers valuable insights into how human
activity, technology, and socioeconomic developments have progressively reshaped the rural and peri-urban environment of
northern Belgium. 
While the study offers a highly detailed and technically advanced reconstruction of land-use change, several conceptual and
methodological points merit critical reflection. 
The study describes agricultural intensification as a doubling of grassland and orchard areas at the expense of arable land.
However, this interpretation diverges from the widely accepted definition in agronomy, where intensification refers to an
increase in agricultural output per unit of input—whether land, labour, capital, or other resources. While the observed shift in
land cover may be a result of intensification, the term itself is used imprecisely and may mislead readers about the nature of
the agricultural changes. Moreover, a real agricultural intensification would only manifest itself from the 1950s-1950s thanks
to the widespread use of artificial fertilisers, crop protection productions, specialized seeds and plants and the introduction of
tractors and other farm equipment. 
A major omission in the study is the role of population growth, which significantly accelerated in the Low Countries from the
second half of the 18th century onward. This demographic expansion not only increased food demand but also made land
reclamation economically attractive and socially necessary. The linkage between population growth, land-use pressure, and
agricultural modernization is essential to understanding the drivers of landscape change. 
The study successfully demonstrates the use of GeoAI for historical landscape analysis, but its broader research aims
remain unclear. Is the primary goal methodological innovation, historical understanding, or policy relevance? The lack of a
clearly articulated research question weakens the interpretive framework of the analysis. If the authors intend to inform land-
use planning or biodiversity conservation, such implications should be made more explicit. 
The selection of 1774, 1873, 1969, and 2022 is not arbitrary. These years correspond to pivotal moments in the agrarian and
urban history of Flanders and Western Europe—pre-industrial society, the late 19th-century agricultural transition, post-war
modernization, and the current urban age. However, the study does not contextualize these periods sufficiently for the
reader, missing an opportunity to link cartographic data with broader historical narratives. 
While the article excels in clarity and visual analysis, it tends to remain descriptive. Explanatory depth—particularly
concerning the socioeconomic and institutional drivers of change—is often lacking. Several aspects could benefit from more
historical nuance: 
• Line 39–40: The text refers to "many research areas" interested in historical land use, but provides no concrete examples. 
• Line 204–205: The causal link between the potato blight and deforestation is too simplistic. Deforestation in Belgium had
deeper roots in long-term agricultural expansion and increasing demand for staple foods like grain and potatoes. 
• Line 214: The impact of synthetic fertilizers in the late 19th century is overstated. Their widespread use in Belgian
agriculture occurred only after World War I and particularly after WWII. 
• Line 221: The expansion of fruit cultivation in Haspengouw coincided with livestock farming. High-stem orchards often
served as grazing land, forming so-called "fruit meadows"—a form of mixed land use not visible on maps, leading to an
underestimation of grassland. 
• Line 235–236: Describing the region as a recent "hotspot of urbanization" is valid but overlooks its deeper history. The Low
Countries have been densely populated and urbanized since the Middle Ages, shaped by fertile soils and accessible
transport routes. 
To conclude, this study represents an important methodological contribution to landscape history and spatial analysis.
However, a more nuanced conceptual framework and historical contextualization would significantly enhance its interpretive
value. Incorporating demographic, economic, and social dimensions alongside cartographic and technological tools could
provide a more holistic understanding of landscape transformation in northern Belgium—and possibly offer valuable lessons
for other peri-urban regions in Europe facing similar developments and pressures today.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors provided detailed and technically sound revisions addressing nearly every point raised by Reviewer 1. While
the revisions demonstrate effort and engagement, several aspects still limit full acceptance: 
1. Many replies rely on brief clarifications rather than analytical expansion. The reflection on mapping regime bias was
limited to orchards. 
2. Despite rewriting, the causal reasoning behind some results (e.g., linkage between homogeneity/completeness and
socio-ecological interpretation) remains descriptive rather than analytical. 
3. In several places (e.g., historical land reclamation ratios or land-use transitions), the authors still present numerical
estimates derived from visual inference, which is conceptually weak given positional uncertainty. 
4. Although they explained why they avoided direct LU-transition overlays due to georeferencing errors, reviewers may
expect some sensitivity testing or quantified uncertainty to strengthen this argument. 
While the authors have made revisions that address some of the reviewers’ technical comments, the manuscript ultimately
lacks a clear theoretical or conceptual contribution beyond its regional case study. The application of semantic segmentation
and GeoAI techniques to historical maps, while competently executed, is not novel at this stage of the field. The study
remains primarily descriptive in nature and does not advance broader theoretical understanding or methodological
innovation in land-use change analysis, historical mapping, or GeoAI. Given the absence of a generalizable framework or
conceptual insight that extends beyond the specific regional context of northern Belgium, the manuscript does not meet the
threshold for publication in its current form.

Reply to comments by reviewer #2
This interesting study aims to quantify land-use changes over nearly 250 years (1774–
2022) in northern Belgium using a novel approach combining high-resolution historical 
maps and deep learning segmentation (GeoAI). By using artiﬁcial intelligence, the 
evolution of land use can be mapped and analysed much faster and over a much larger 
geographical area than was possible until a few years ago. These new techniques are not 
only time-saving but also enable innovative long-term and comparative research, from 
various perspectives. The present contribution is a good example of the research 
possibilities offered by these new techniques. Covering a 13,800 km² area, the authors 
analyzed four time slices (1774, 1873, 1969, 2022) to trace the transformation of 
landscapes. The methodology involved the use of semantic segmentation on tiled 
historical maps, allowing the authors to classify nine consistent land-use categories 
across all time periods. These classiﬁcations were validated using historical map 
interpretation and ﬁeld surveys, achieving high accuracy scores. Spatial analyses 
focused on the association between land use and soil types, spatial autocorrelation, 
and land-use interspersion.
The results indicate three successive phases of landscape transformation. First, 
between 1774 and 1873, land reclamation reduced the area of natural and semi-natural 
land. In the second phase (1873–1969), agricultural intensiﬁcation led to a shift from 
arable land to grassland and orchards. The ﬁnal phase (1969–2022) was marked by rapid 
urbanization and suburban sprawl, weakening the historical link between land-use 
patterns and soil characteristics. As a consequence, previously distinct landscape types 
became increasingly homogenized.
This comprehensive reconstruction of land-use dynamics over nearly 250 years offers 
valuable insights into how human activity, technology, and socioeconomic 
developments have progressively reshaped the rural and peri-urban environment of 
northern Belgium.
While the study offers a highly detailed and technically advanced reconstruction of 
land-use change, several conceptual and methodological points merit critical 
reﬂection.

areas at the expense of arable land. However, this interpretation diverges from the
