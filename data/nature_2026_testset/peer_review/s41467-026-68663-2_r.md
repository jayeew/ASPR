Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors developed a quantitative multivariate model that systematically integrates chemical structural, physicochemical,
and photophysical parameters with live-cell imaging sensitivity. This framework establishes statistical correlations between
fluorescent probe structures and their imaging performance. Furthermore, the authors reported the development of an ultra-
sensitive superoxide probe, whose performance was in strong agreement with the Sensight predictions. In addition,
Sensight successfully predicted probe sensitivity in two conceptually distinct systems: the tetrazine–BCN bioorthogonal
reaction and formaldehyde-selective probes. The establishment of a framework capable of predicting fluorescent probe
sensitivity in live-cell imaging represents a significant advancement in the field of molecular fluorescence imaging.
Nonetheless, further experimental validation is required to substantiate the robustness and generalizability of this approach.
A comprehensive analysis is presented below. 
1. In Supplementary Table 2, please clarify whether the descriptors were calculated based on the structures prior to
superoxide activation. In addition, please indicate whether quantum yield, Stokes shift, and Δλex were obtained from the
activated structures. If so, it would be important to explicitly state that the same approach was applied throughout all
subsequent sections where descriptors were calculated. 
2. In Supplementary Table 4, the normalized PCC value appears unusual, as it should range between 0 and 1. 
3. In Fig. 1D, the authors ranked the descriptors by simply summing the normalized entropy and PCC values. However,
descriptors such as nHD and NAR exhibited PCC values comparable to the final five selected descriptors, yet were
excluded due to their inherently low entropy. It is therefore important to explain why the ranking was performed by simple
summation of entropy and PCC, rather than by a weighted sum or product. 
4. In Supplementary Fig. 56, a clearer explanation is needed regarding how the optimal values of ClogP and tPSA were
determined. In Supplementary Fig. 55 (panels H and N), both tPSA and ClogP exhibit negative trends, yet the median
values were designated as optimal. Additional clarification on this choice is warranted. Moreover, rather than simply using
the median, alternative approaches such as fitting a Gaussian curve and selecting the peak should be considered.
Furthermore, as tPSA and ClogP are generally considered opposing descriptors, it would be important to provide additional
explanation for why they displayed similar trends in this analysis. 
5. In Fig. 2C, the authors present a pentagonal radar map, with each axis representing one descriptor. A more detailed
explanation is needed to clarify how the descriptor values were reflected in the radar plot. Given that the correlation
coefficients differ among descriptors, it should also be clarified whether these weights were incorporated into the radar map
construction and area calculation. 
6. In Figs. 3–6, please include the excitation wavelength information for the cellular imaging data. 
7. In the section “Validating Sensight through single-variable experiments,” validation was conducted for only three

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript is an extremely thorough examination of how key probe parameters can be used to predict biological
sensing ability. The authors present here a preliminary relationship, and very interesting and novel methodology, for making
such predictions. Selection of the tetrazine, which both acts as a superoxide sensor, and a biorthogonal reactive group,
enables the authors to demonstrate the utility of their algorithm on a second sensing system. They then further demonstrate
its use with an entirely different sensing group. While it will not be straightforward for others to immediately generalise these
results to their own compound libraries, nonetheless the work presented here is a big breakthrough in the field and I predict
that it will have significant impact. Changes required before publication: 
1. It would help to have a panel in Figure 1 showing the reaction of tetrazine with superoxide 
2. There is very little discussion of how the delta(Fc) parameter is calculated, even though this is the datapoint on which the
rest of the manuscript hangs. On what basis do you know that the optimal H2O2 concentration and incubation time were
selected? A titration (time and concentration) should be performed for at least a few of the compounds, to confirm that
plateau conditions are being studied 
3. It’s not clear why PCC is cited rather than R2 for the data shown in Figure 1C (and yet R2 is used in Figure 2D 
4. Figure 1 caption should explain what the red numbers signify in panel (B) 
5. Figure 2D – should the y-axis be Norm, not Nom? 
6. Supplementary table 2, footnote – stokes shift should be Stokes shift. And there is no footnote 16 despite the superscript
in the header row. 
7. DHE is not seen as a gold-standard probe these days – it is well established that it has poor selectivity, so it would is
necessary 
to perform the benchmarking experiments for another, better, superoxide sensor. 
8. What is missing in the discussion is probe localisation: does the analysis presented here only work because the probes
all remain cytosolic? Surely any sub-cellular localisation will greatly confound the results. For example, the Stokes shift is
heavily dependent on the polarity for many fluorophores, so differing organelle localisation will likely have huge impacts on
the Stokes shift – it is not clear that your model would address this. 
9. The statement in the conclusion “beyond activity-based sensing…” is not accurate – all the examples in this paper are
examples of activity-based sensing.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Chemical imaging probes have become indispensable tools in biomedical research. The development of high-performance
chemical imaging probes is crucial for visualizing low-concentration reactive molecules in cells. Currently, probe design
primarily relies on researchers’ empirical knowledge, which not only raises the barrier to probe development but also
significantly increases the workload, as optimization of probe performance can only be conducted after experimental data
are obtained. To address this, the authors proposed a five-dimensional model (Sensight) to predict probe performance,
aiming to guide rational probe design through quantitative calculations. However, Sensight and its ideal parameter values
were derived solely from a limited variety of probes and fluorescence modulation mechanisms, casting uncertainty on its
general applicability. More importantly, as key parameters of Sensight, ΔF
ₛ
 is clearly unpredictable, and Δλ
ₑₓ
 depends on
imaging instrument settings, substantially diminishing the practical utility of the model. Overall, the model offers limited
guidance for the design of entirely novel probes. Therefore, I do not believe this manuscript is suitable for publication in
Nature Communications. 
(1)The manuscript proposes ideal values for the five parameters of Sensight; however, these ideal values were derived from
a limited sample size and narrow variety of probe types. 
(2)The development of Sensight relied on tetrazine-based superoxide fluorescent probes and formaldehyde probes,
meaning only two fluorescence modulation mechanisms were considered. However, fluorescence modulation mechanisms
are diverse (e.g., TICT), which casts doubt on the general applicability of Sensight. 
(3)ΔF
ₛ
 (fluorescence turn-on ratio) is one of the five core parameters in the Sensight model and is inherently dependent on
the specific chemical mechanism of fluorescence modulation and the local cellular microenvironment, rendering it
unpredictable by computational methods prior to synthesis and experimental characterization. Since Sensight is designed to
guide the development of new high-performance probes, this fundamental limitation means that, in practice, only four of the
five parameters can be meaningfully calculated. The authors should explicitly state this limitation to help readers better
understand the actual capability of Sensight. 
(4)The unpredictability of ΔF
ₛ
 will affect the predictive accuracy of Sensight. Sensight functions more as an optimization tool
after probe synthesis and testing, rather than an initial design tool. 
(5)The authors designed a series of formaldehyde fluorescent probes and predicted their sensitivity in living cells, with
results consistent with the predictions. However, when using Sensight for prediction, ΔF
ₛ
 was set as a uniform value.
Importantly, would setting ΔF
ₛ
 to different uniform values lead to different prediction outcomes (for example, in the
manuscript, tPSA was set to 5, what if it were set to 50?)? What value or specific range should tPSA be set to in order to
ensure the predictive accuracy of the model? 
(6)Beyond sensitivity, the selectivity of probes is equally important. Sensight cannot predict probe selectivity, indicating that
it is only suitable for making rough predictions about the performance of fluorescent probes modified with known recognition
groups, and offers limited utility for the development of innovative probes. 
(7)The representation of the substituent denoted as R in Figure 1a is not sufficiently clear. Should "RTz" on the probe
structure on the left be modified to "RnTz" for clarity?
