Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript proposes an atomic layer design principle for fabricating high-performance, high-yield memristors. The
authors aim to elucidate the respective roles of the M1 layer—responsible for switching through filament formation and
rupture—and the M2 layer—which supplies oxygen vacancies (Vo)—using diverse device structures, material combinations,
and analytical methods. The comprehensive experimental data supported by advanced analyses such as STS and STEM
strongly demonstrate the value of this study for researchers in the related field. 
However, despite the importance of the presented information, the manuscript lacks sufficient details regarding the
experimental procedures. Furthermore, some parts of the discussion suffer from weak logical connections. Therefore, the
manuscript is not suitable for publication in its current form and should be reconsidered after a major revision. 
1.In the Introduction, the authors identify high switching speed, high endurance, high energy efficiency, and scalability as
key properties of memristors for neuromorphic computing. However, these features are generally requirements for high-
performance memories rather than specific to neuromorphic computing. Could the authors specify additional properties that
are particularly critical for neuromorphic applications? Moreover, the manuscript does not discuss energy efficiency or
scalability in detail. Are there potential strategies to enhance these properties from the perspective of atomic layer design? 
2. Throughout the manuscript, certain terms are used inconsistently. For example, in page 4, the phrase “a CF is formed via
diffusion of Vo through~” appears, but since the Vo movement is driven by an external electric field, “drift” would be a more
appropriate term than “diffusion.”

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This work presents a comprehensive study on atomically engineered sub-2 nm memristors fabricated using in vacuo atomic
layer deposition, emphasizing atomic-scale control of Vo distribution to tailor switching dynamics in MgO/Ga2O3/Al2O3
bilayer structures. Overall, this manuscript establishes a clear link between atomic-layer stacking design and memristor
dynamics, providing mechanistic insight into Vo-mediated switching at the atomic limit an essential step toward the future
neuromorphic circuits. However, the manuscript requires major revision, and several aspects need to be clarified further. 
1. How does the in vacuo ALD process differ from conventional ALD in terms of interfacial defect formation and surface
hydroxylation? 
2. Authors are requested to compare the sub-2 nm MgO/Ga2O3/Al2O3 memristors with traditional HfO2/TiO2-based devices
in terms of scalability and energy efficiency? 
3. The β-Ga2O3 crystalline phase typically requires post-deposition annealing above 600°C, how did the authors confirm
this phase? TEM demonstrates the amorphous nature of MgO/Ga2O3/Al2O3 bilayer structures. Is the simulation result

Major comments 
The manuscript (COMMSMAT-25-0835-T) titled “Coupling Atomic Layer Design with Switching Dynamics in Sub-2 nm
MgO/Ga2O3/Al2O3 Memristors” presents various multilayer thin-film structures fabricated using the ALD process and
demonstrates an optimized configuration that achieves a high on-off ratio and uniform distribution at a few-nanometer
thickness. 
I concur that reducing the thickness of the memristor’s insulating layer to a few nanometers is crucial, and further
investigations along this direction would be highly valuable. The topic is timely and relevant, with potential implications for
emerging memory, as it provides a detailed analysis of the physical properties of ultrathin insulating films fabricated via the
ALD process. However, there are several parts that need to address more details. Therefore, the manuscript requires major
revision before it can be considered for publication in Communications Materials, particularly in terms of scientific
understanding, technical rigor, and clarity of presentation. 
1. The manuscript is somewhat lengthy, and the main text could be streamlined to improve readability and focus on the key
findings. Condensing parts of the Introduction and Discussion may help emphasize the central message without loss of
scientific content. The authors are encouraged to move the redundant or repetitive content to the Supporting Information
section to improve the readability and conciseness of the main text. 
2. How did you measure LRS resistance? From Fig. S1, it appears that the filament formation was controlled by applying a

Reviewer #1: 
This manuscript proposes an atomic layer design principle for fabricating high-performance, high-
yield memristors. The authors aim to elucidate the respective roles of the M1 layer —responsible 
for switching through filament formation and rupture—and the M2 layer—which supplies oxygen 
vacancies (Vo)—using diverse device structures, material combinations, and analytical methods. 
The comprehensive experimental data supported by advanced analyses such as STS and STEM 
strongly demonstrate the value of this study for researchers in the related field.  
However, despite the importance of the presented information, the manuscript lacks sufficient 
details regarding the experimental procedures. Furthermore, some parts of the discussion suffer 
from weak logical connections. Therefore, the manuscript is not suitable fo r publication in its 
current form and should be reconsidered after a major revision. 
 
1.In the Introduction, the authors identify high switching speed, high endurance, high energy 
efficiency, and scalability as key properties of memristors for neuromorphic computing. However, 
these features are generally requirements for high -performance memories rather than specific to 
neuromorphic computing. Could the authors specify additional properties that are particularly 
critical for neuromorphic applications? Moreo ver, the manuscript does not discuss energy 
efficiency or scalability in detail. Are there potential strategies to enhance these properties from 
the perspective of atomic layer design? 
 
Response: We thank the reviewer for the comment s, based on which  we have revised the 
introduction to clarify properties that are particularly critical for neuromorphic computing, 
including analog tunability, multi-level conductance states, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity 
that enable faithful emulation of synaptic beh avior in hardware. In addition, we have added a 
discussion of potential strategies to enhance energy efficiency and scalability from atomic layer 
design perspective, such as optimizing oxide layer stacking, controlling oxygen vacancy 
distribution, and minimizing leakage pathways. These revisions are now included in the revised 
manuscript, Pages 2 and 3 respectively. 
 
On Page 2: 
“In addition, neuromorphic computing specifically requires devices that support analog tunability 
with multi-level conductance states  [6], uniform performance in large -scale arrays, as well as  
spike-timing-dependent plasticity in spiking neural networks, enabling faithful synaptic emulation 
for unsupervised learning as in biological neural networks [7,8].”  
  
On Page 3: 
Switching speed and scalability can be enhanced through atomic-layer engineering strategies, such 
as optimizing the stacking sequence of oxide layers, controlling oxygen-vacancy distribution, and 
minimizing leakage pathways through atomic scale control of defect formation .  ALD is also an 
extremely conformal method of depositing oxides over large wafers at high uniformity.  These 
approaches enable precise modulation of filament formation and rupture dynamics, allowing faster 
and more reliable switching with high device yield and uniformity.”

2. Throughout the manuscript, certain terms are used inconsistently. For example, in page 4, the 
phrase “a CF is formed via diffusion of Vo through~” appears, but since the Vo movement is 
driven by an external electric field, “drift” would be a more appropriate term than “diffusion.” 
 
Response: We thank the reviewer for pointing this out  and agree with our reviewer that the 
movement of oxygen vacancies (Vo) under an applied electric field is primarily via drift driven by 
the electric force rather than diffusion driven by oxygen concentration gradient. The manuscript 
has been updated accordingly to replace “diffusion” with “drift” wherever appropriate. 
 
3. In the discussion on switching speed, devices incorporating Ga₂O₃ in the M2 layer exhibited 
superior performance. Besides the Vo formation effect, could differences in dielectric constant —
leading to local electric fi eld concentration in the M1 layer —also contribute to the improved 
switching speed? 
 
Response: This is an excellent question and a short answer is that the effect of dielectric constant 
may be moderate. In fact, the differences in the dielectric constants () of the constituent atomic 
layers in M2 ( i.e. Al2O3, MgO, and Ga2O3) are fairly moderate based on our studies of ultrathin 
capacitors of oxide thickness in the range 3 -4 nm with a pair of Al electrodes . Specifically, a 
dielectric constant  up to 8.9 (bulk crystal value ~9.2) and EOT (effective oxide thickness) ~1.5 
nm have been achieved in ~3-4 nm thick Al2O3 capacitors (J. Acharya, J. Wilt, B. Liu and J. Wu, 
"Probing the Dielectric Properties of Ultrathin Al/Al2O3/Al Trilayers Fabricated Using in Situ 
Sputtering and Atomic Layer Deposition", ACS Appl Mater Inter 10 (3), 3112-3120 (2018)). On 
MgO capacitors of 3-4 nm in thickness, a seed layer of five Al2O3 atomic layers was found critical 
to reduc ing or eliminat ing the growth defects of the first few MgO atomic layers  on bottom 
electrodes and  ~ 8.82–9.38 (bulk crystal value ~9.8) and EOT~1.4 nm were obtained in MgO 
capacitors of 5C Al2O3/35C MgO (J. Acharya, R. Goul, D. Romine, R. Sakidja and J. Wu, "Effect 
of Al2O3 Seed -Layer on the Dielectric and Electrical Properties of Ultrathin MgO Films 
Fabricated Using In Situ Atomic Layer Deposition", ACS Appl Mater Inter 11 (33), 30368-30375 
(2019)). On Ga2O3 capacitors of similar thickness of 3-4 nm, ~up to 9.8 (bulk crystal value ~10) 
and EOT ~ 1.7 nm were obtained and no seed layer is necessary  (Aafiya, Angelo Marshall, Berg 
Dodson, Ryan Goul, Sierra Seacat, Hartwin Peelaers, Kevin Bray, Dan Ewing, Michael Walsh, 
and Judy Wu, “Probing Electronic and dielectric properties of Ga2O3/Al2O3 Atomic layer stacks 
made with in vacuo atomic layer deposition”, Journal of Applied Physics 136, 025104 (2024)). If 
the three constituent atomic layers of Al2O3, MgO, and Ga2O3 of M2 grow in similar ways as in 
the ultrathin capacitors, it is unlikely that the moderate difference in their dielectric constants 
would cause dramatically varied local electric fields . The machine-learning MTP simulation in 
Figure 8 suggests that the interface between constituent atomic layers, such as MgO and Ga2O3 of 
different crystalline structures, may facilitate much more efficient  Vo ionization and drift out of 
M2. Further research is certainly needed to pinpoint the mechanism. In order to clarify the role of 
the dielectric properties, a brief discussion has been added to the revised manuscript on Page 19.  
 
On Page 19:  
“It should be noted that the three constituent atomic layers of Al2O3, MgO, and Ga2O3 of M2 used 
in this work exhibit comparable dielectric constants in the range of 8.8 -9.8 measured on ultrathin 
capacitors of 3 -4 nm in oxide thickness  [51-53], which means that it is unlikely that the local 
electric field is affected dramatically by different dielectric constants as the three kinds of atomic

layers forming stack  differently in  M2. Instead, we argue the different stacking of the three 
constituent atomic layers may introduce interfaces that affect the Vo ionization in and drift out of 
M2 under Vset, which in turn impact the switching speed of memristors.” 
 
 
4. On page 12, the authors state that Eb and Vo concentration are inversely related. Please explain 
the physical reason behind this relationship. Additionally, the statement “Eg ≈ 2Eb” seems 
inconsistent with the known bandgaps of Al₂O₃ and MgO, as the mea sured 2Eb values differ 
significantly. Please clarify the origin of this discrepancy. 
 
Response: We thank the reviewer for highlighting this point. The inverse relationship between Eb 
and oxygen vacancy (Vo) or defects in general arises because Vo (or defects) introduce donor (or 
acceptor) states that locally reduce the conduction band minimum  (or/and valance band 
maximum). The Vo (or defects) introduction to intrinsic semiconductors  is reflected in the 
rounding of STS dI/dV curves from that of intrinsic semiconductors  which can be fit by two 
straight lines intercepting at Eb or the vertex of the dI/dV curve. As a consequence of the rounding, 
the Eb extracted from the interception of the two fitting straight lines is no longer coincide with 
the vertex of the  dI/dV curve. In addition, the Eb shifts to lower values . Regarding the apparent 
discrepancy between the measured 2Eb in this work and the reported bandgaps of crystalline bulks 
of Al₂O₃ and MgO, we note that our STS measurements were performed on ultrathin (<2 nm) oxide 
films. In this thickness regime, surface and interface effects significantly modify the electronic 
structure. Prior work by us in Ref. [21] showed that for ~1 nm thick ALD amorphous Al₂O₃ films, 
the measured 2Eb value of 2.5-3.4 eV agrees well with the bandgap of 1.3 nm thick epitaxial Al₂O₃ 
(C. Dietrich, H.-G. Boyen, and B. Koslowski, Characterization of ultrathin insulating Al2O3 films 
grown on Nb (110)/sapphire (0001) by tunneling spectroscopy and microscopy, J. Appl. Phys. 94, 
1478 (2003)), demonstrating that ultrathin films, both crystalline and amorphous, exhibit reduced 
effective bandgaps as compared to the crystalline bulk counterparts’ values. While further 
investigations are necessary to pinpoint the mechanism  responsible, we argue that the effect of 
surface and interface that may contain various defects may increase dramatically on semiconductor

Response: The Vo -mediated switching approach demonstrated here relies on the controlled 
formation and migration of oxygen vacancies in ultrathin oxide layers. In principle, this 
mechanism could be extended to other material systems, including 2D oxides or ferroelect ric
