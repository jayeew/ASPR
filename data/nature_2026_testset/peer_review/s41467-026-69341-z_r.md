Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Zhang, Tu and colleagues describe the mechanisms of how microbiota-derived indole propionic acid
(IPA) protects against colitis. IPA levels have been observed to be decreased in IBD, correlating with decreases in IPA-
producing microbes and increases in IPA-consuming microbes, and IPA has been shown to attenuate colitis in mouse
models 
(PMID: 39956891). These findings are confirmed in this study and the authors go on to investigate the mechanisms
of how IPA impacts colitis. Their novel findings include the interesting observation that the colitis-protecting effects of IPA act
via HMGCS2-regulated beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) production in intestinal epithelial cells. They show that IPA directly
binds the transcription factor PPARalpha, which activates increased transcription of HMGCS2 and increases BHB
production. BHB is known to mediate LGR5+ intestinal stem cell homeostasis and signal through HOPX (PMCID:
PMC6732196; PMCID: PMC9448510) and the authors show this pathway is also contributing to the protective effects of IPA
in colitis. Finally, the authors find that an IPA-producing member of the gut microbiota, Peptostreptococcus russellii, is
decreased in abundance in IBD patients and IPA/BHB supplementation or P. russellii colonization alone restored epithelial
regeneration capacity in colitis models. Overall, this is an interesting and important paper that makes significant advances in
our understanding of the molecular signals that underlie how the IBD-depleted microbiota-derived metabolite IPA
ameliorates colitis. The experimental approaches are generally well-designed and controlled, and the methods are well
described. However, several issues should be addressed before publication: 
Major comments: 
1) While this is a well-designed and executed study, there are concerns over a lack of clarity. Due to the sheer volume of
work presented in the study, it is extremely difficult to read and digest this study. This is exemplified by the volume of figures
and panels - Figure 1a-u; 2a-y; 3a-q; 4a-l; 5a-n; 6a-t and extended data 1a-m; 2a-w; 3a-w; 4a-e; 5a-m. While the authors are
commended for the incredible effort of trying to make this massive amount of data accessible with experimental model
summaries and well-written results, it was still extremely difficult to parse the amount of data presented, even as someone
very familiar with all aspects of this research area. There are a lot of mechanistic insights in this study that gets lost in the
volume of the data, and in its current state, my major concern is that this significantly affects readers’ ability to understand the
conclusions accurately. Can this manuscript be broken into 2 separate articles published concurrently? 
2) The statistical tests are not always appropriate and do not account for multiple testing corrections. I do not think this will
change any major conclusions of this study, but the statistics need to be updated when multiple comparisons are being
made to approach tests (eg. one-way ANOVA with a multiple testing correction such as Tukey). Any time more than 2 tests
are being made, this correction must be done such as Figs. 1f, 1g, 1h, etc. 
3) It is difficult to compare the effects of a complex microbiota colonization of GF mice (FMT of BL6 microbiota) versus mono-
colonization with an IPA-producing microbe P. russellii (Fig. 6K-Q). While IPA levels are increased in the P. russellii
colonized group, many other microbiota differences can result in exacerbation of colitis in the FMT-colonized condition. For
example, a known immune-inducing microbe such as SFB could contribute to the observed differences. Further, these data
suggest that a normal B6 mouse FMT does not engraft IPA producers (IPA levels much lower than conventional SPF mice in
Figure 1f). These limitations should be discussed more, especially in the discussion section (see lines 588-591), and future
directions should include colonization with defined microbial communities with and without P. russellii as well as the already
existing discussion of Wt and IPA knockout P. russellii comparisons (lines 624-626 of limitations of the study).

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments for authors: 
This study by Yanan Zhang et al. describes a role of indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) in mucosal repair via ketogenic
reprogramming of intestinal epithelial cells. Several intestinal inflammation models were used, such as DSS-induced colitis,
TNBS-induced colitis and a radiation enteritis model, which strengthen the importance of the findings. The authors
described a novel mechanism of action of IPA on the intestinal epithelium via the PPARa (Hmgcs2) – BHB – HOPx axis to
activate Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells (ISC). Of note, this axis was relevant only upon intestinal injury, as during steady state
conditions IPA was unable to activate this signaling pathway. In addition, the authors confirmed already known
observations, for example, the decreased levels of IPA in individuals with IBD. Furthermore, IPA was already shown to be
protective in DSS-induced colitis, although this was associated with the induction of IL-10R on intestinal epithelial cells. Its
impact on the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1b has also been demonstrated. Therefore, while the
protective role of IPA during intestinal inflammation via the regulation of the intestinal epithelium is not novel per se, Yanan
Zhang et al described a new molecular axis, which I believe is novel and important. However, I have some concerns as
outlined below. 
Major points 
In Fig. 2n, the authors show via single cell sequencing that IPA (200 mg/kg) treatment significantly decreased the frequency
of Hmgcs2+ cells within the Lgr5 high ISC (“CBC”) (day 3 post DSS). However, in Fig. 2p the authors show that IPA (also
200 mg/kg, 3 days after DSS) increased Lgr5+ ISC through Hmgcs2. These observations appear to be contradictory and
should be discussed. 
The experiments performed with conditional Hmgcs2 fl/fl mice used as a littermate controls the cre- mice (vs. the cre+). The
insertion of cre construct can impact the experimental phenotype, therefore the inclusion of cre+ x Hmgcs2+/+ vs cre+ x
Hmgcs2fl/fl groups would be important. This is not necessary for every time point, but I would suggest to include this control
on day 3 post DSS. 
The same is also the case for the experiments including Hopx fl/fl mice, and the PPAR fl/fl mice. Also, here littermate
controls lack the cre construct, and Cre+ controls should be included, if possible. 
The authors tested how the deletion of AhR (an IPA receptor) impacts the described phenotype. IPA has also been shown to
interact with the intestinal epithelium via the pregnane X receptor (PXR). Thus, it would be interesting to test, if the deletion
of PXR impacts the Hmgcs2 – BHB – Hopx axis. 
How relevant is the protective role of IPA via Lrg5+ ISC during the recovery phase after DSS removal? This is a key point,
since inflammatory bowel diseases are normally chronic relapsing conditions. 
IPA is a microbiota-derived metabolite. In line with this, the authors mention that “to exclude the possibility that the observed
phenotype was attributed to the interaction between IPA and the existing intestinal microbiota, GF mice were gavaged with
IPA before DSS induction”. In my opinion this experiment does not exclude a potential interaction between IPA and the
microbiota, but shows a microbiota-independent effect of IPA. I would discuss this more carefully. 
In the discussion, the authors mention that “…initiates a signaling circuit that does not activate unwanted LGR5 proliferation
to prevent oncogenic transformation.” The impact of IPA on carcinogenesis via the activation of ISCs could be evaluated
using the AOM-DSS mouse model. Alternatively, the authors could mention that this hypothesis needs further experimental
validation.

Please, review the text for typos and grammatical mistakes.

Manuscript 1 
Title: Microbiota-derived IPA protects against colitis by regulating intestinal HMGCS2-mediated ketogenesis to facilitate mucosal healing through PPARα 
Abstract: The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in sustaining intestinal epithelial homeostasis and barrier integrity. Here, we reveal a novel microbial-metabolic axis wherein indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), a tryptophan catabolite derived from commensal bacteria, orchestrates mucosal repair through ketogenic reprogramming of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). Mechanistically, IPA binds specific structural domains of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), potentiating transcriptional activation of the rate-limiting ketogenic enzyme 3-hydroxy-3-methylpentyl-hydroxyacyl A-convexylase 2 (HMGCS2). This drives β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) biosynthesis, which directly activates the LGR5+ intestinal stem cells (ISCs) post-injury. Using germ-free models and the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

understudied IPA-producing bacterium Peptostreptococcus russellii, we demonstrate the essentiality of dietary tryptophan and specialized commensals for sustaining intestinal IPA levels critical for mucosal healing. Crucially, IPA/BHB supplementation or P. russellii colonization restored epithelial regeneration capacity in colitis models, attenuating inflammatory cascades and barrier defects characteristic of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Our work delineates a microbiota-metabolite-stem cell signaling cascade that could be harnessed therapeutically to promote mucosal repair in IBD and other disorders of intestinal barrier dysfunction. 
Core Mechanism: This manuscript focuses on the pathway: P. russellii–derived IPA → PPARα activation in IECs → HMGCS2 upregulation → BHB production → epithelial regeneration and mucosal healing. 
Key Novelty: 
1) Identification of IPA as a novel endogenous PPARα ligand in IECs. 2) Demonstration that microbial tryptophan metabolism regulates IEC ketogenesis via HMGCS2, supporting LGR5⁺ stem cell function during colitis. 
Figure Allocation for Manuscript 1: 
1) Original Figures 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and Extended Data Figures 1-5 are retained. 2) Original Figure 3 (detailing the BHB-HOPX-rISC axis) has been removed and relocated to Manuscript 2.

Manuscript 2 
Title: A microbiota-IPA axis facilitates intestinal stem cell-mediated regeneration in colitis through a Hopx-associated program Abstract: The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal stem cell (ISC) homeostasis and epithelial barrier integrity. Here, we report that Blautia coccoides (B. coccoides) is significantly reduced in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients and dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis mice. Through an integrated approach combining single-cell RNA sequencing, metabolomic profiling, and ISC lineage tracing across multiple mucosal injury models, we demonstrate that B. coccoides colonization enhances β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) production in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), which activates HOPX⁺ reserve ISCs and promotes regeneration of the LGR5⁺ ISC pool, thereby accelerating epithelial repair. We further show that B. coccoides-derived indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), a tryptophan (Trp) metabolite, is converted into indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) by commensal bacteria such as P. russellii or C. sporogenes, stimulating IEC BHB synthesis. Using an engineered Escherichia coli strain expressing BC-derived phenyllactate dehydrogenase (fldH), we establish that both dietary Trp and bacterial fldH activity are essential for ILA/IPA generation and subsequent mucosal healing. Our findings reveal a microbiota-metabolite-ISC regulatory axis critical for epithelial regeneration and propose novel metabolite-based therapeutic strategies for IBD and other intestinal disorders associated with barrier dysfunction.

Core Discovery: Multi-omics screening revealed depletion of Blautia coccoides in IBD. We demonstrate its role as a primary ILA producer and its synergistic relationship with IPA-generating commensals to promote epithelial regeneration through HOPX⁺ rISC activation. 
Mechanistic Pathway 
B. coccoides (ILA) → IPA-producing commensal bacteria cross-feeding → IPA → BHB → HOPX+ rISCs activation → LGR5+ aISC replenishment → Mucosal healing. 
Key Advances: 
1) Identification of B. coccoides as a regenerative commensal and ILA producer depleted in human IBD. 2) Elucidation of a cross-feeding circuit between B. coccoides and IPA-producing bacteria that fuels BHB-mediated activation of HOPX⁺ rISCs. 3) Functional validation of fldH in ILA biosynthesis and mucosal repair. 
Figure Allocation for Manuscript 2: Includes original Figure 3 and new data further delineating the BHB-HOPX-rISC pathway.

Manuscript 1 establishes a novel microbiota-epithelial metabolic signaling axis centered on IPA and PPARα-driven ketogenesis, while Manuscript 2 unveils a distinct bacterial synergy that activates reserve stem cells to fuel regeneration.
