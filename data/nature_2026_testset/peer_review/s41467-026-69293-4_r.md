Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Review of the manuscript submitted to Nature Communications by Cui et al.: “Phyllosilicate adsorption limited phosphorus
bioavailability in early ferruginous oceans” 
In this manuscript, Cui et al. have investigated the roles of phyllosilicates in absorbing phosphate (P) in the oceanic
environment on early Earth and discuss how they function as an effective sink for P in early anoxic oceans. The manuscript
is well-written and comprehensive. I believe that the results presented by the authors are scientifically strong. I summarize
my comments below, but they are minor. The capital L represents the line number. 
General comments 
1. Throughout the manuscript, it was not clear how the P-absorption by phyllosilicates works (or doesn’t work) in the present
environment (ocean, river, crust, and sediments). Many parameters, including the different river and oceanic compositions
(e.g., low Fe(II) and silica concentrations), higher oceanic pH, and the composition of oceanic lithosphere, would affect the
long-term evolution of phyllosilicate P-absorption behavior. The authors have already summarized the environmental
evolution beautifully in Figure 5. A clear explanation of the long-term evolution of phyllosilicate P-absorption behavior from
Archean to the present and its impact on the present P cycle (if any) would be helpful for readers. 
2. The emerging geochemical proxy of the Archean P, including those based on P/Ca in shallow marine carbonate-
associated phosphate, suggests a P concentration higher than the present level during the Archean (Crockford and Halevy,
2022; Ingalls et al., 2022), which would be difficult to achieve without specialized conditions (Watanabe et al., 2024). How
does the P-absorption mechanism constrain the bimodal reconstruction of Archean P concentration?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Cui et al. present experimental and theoretical data showing the role of phyllosilicates in adsorbing phosphorus (P) from
ancient seawater and river water. The authors aim to assess how these processes may have regulated P bioavailability
during the Archean. This study makes an important contribution by showing that clay minerals, formed through weathering
and as alteration products of the seafloor, could have acted as a significant P sink during the Archean, particularly toward its
end. This is especially relevant given the limited number of experimental studies on these processes and the ongoing
debate about the different scenarios for P availability on early Earth. 
The adsorption experiments appear robust, however, my main concern is why the authors did not use actual seawater in
addition to the NaCl solutions. Previous P adsorption studies have argued that NaCl solutions do not capture the complexity

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

of the natural seawater matrix (Jones et al., 2015). The authors could have supplemented natural seawater with Si and
compared the results to those from the NaCl solutions. Should we expect differences when comparing the two? 
The effects of dissolved Si were not surprising and are consistent with previous studies, which have shown that an overall
increase in Si content leads to a decrease in P adsorption (Konhauser et al., 2007; Jones et al., 2015; Sabur et al., 2021).
However, it is still important to show that this mechanism is present under the studied conditions. 
Based on the experiments, it is clear that kaolinite and montmorillonite adsorb P, particularly in the presence of Fe(II).
However, the authors then link this process to P removal from seawater during the Archean. I believe there are important
aspects that need to be clarified in the text regarding the production of these clays during a time when there are still
uncertainties about when, and to what extent, continents were exposed. While the authors do touch on this at points in the
paper, it should be addressed more explicitly throughout. For example, if significant continental emergence only occurred
from ~3.0 to 2.5 Ga, it should be noted that P adsorption by kaolinite and montmorillonite may not have been a major
process before ~3.0 Ga. Additionally, the authors sometimes refer to the entire Archean and at other times specifically to the
end of the Archean; this distinction should be checked carefully and made consistent throughout the text. 
If present, could greenalite also have played a role in adsorbing P from seawater? If so, it might be worth addressing this in
the discussion. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigates the adsorption of orthophosphate onto kaolinite, montmorillonite, and lizardite under simulated
Archean and Proterozoic conditions. It integrates batch adsorption experiments, molecular modelling, and flux estimates to
assess phosphorus burial and bioavailability during early-Earth history, with implications for nutrient limitation and
atmospheric oxygenation. The study systematically explores Fe(II)-mediated adsorption and the competitive effects of
dissolved silica, providing a fresh perspective on the phosphorus cycle in anoxic oceans. Below, I outline several comments
that aim to strengthen the manuscript. In its current form, I recommend a major revision. 
Major comments: 
• The manuscript currently presents clays as the principal Archean P sink. However, recent studies argue that Precambrian
long-term burial is dominated by largely unreactive detrital P and that oxygenation hinges on the reactive/recycled fraction
(Guilbaud 2020 Nat Geo, Alcott 2022 Nat Geo, Canadas 2025 Nat Geo). How will you reconcile your claim that clays were a
principal sink with recent evidence that detrital P dominates preserved burial while reactive/recycled P controls productivity
and oxygenation? Would these studies reframe your conclusions to emphasize clays as modulators of short-term availability
and early diagenetic retention/release rather than the dominant long-term burial sink? Could you explain how a detrital-P-
dominated preserved inventory can still coexist with strong clay-surface control on seawater [P] and episodic oxygenation
(oxygen-oasis intervals)? 
• At the reported pH and Fe(II) concentrations, vivianite or Fe(oxyhydr)oxides phases may form and scavenge P, so part of
the apparent uptake could be co-precipitation. Readers might find it helpful if post-reaction solids were characterized in more
detail, for example, using XRD, TEM-EDS, FTIR…etc, to help distinguish surface-sorbed phosphate from newly formed Fe-
bearing minerals. Also, the discussion of possible secondary phase formation might benefit from 
speciation and saturation
index calculations for Fe–phosphate and Fe-(oxyhydr)oxide phases under the reported ionic strength, pH, and Eh
conditions. 
• The study assumes kaolinite/montmorillonite/lizardite to represent Archean products, but Fe–Si–O–H systems plausibly
yielded Fe rich 2:1 smectites (nontronite like) and 1:1 serpentines (greenalite) with heterogeneous mixtures, distinct layer
charge/cation exchange capacity, and redox sensitivity, which can change P uptake and binding mode. The manuscript
could benefit from a mineralogical sensitivity analysis that normalizes phosphate uptake to surface area and cations
exchange. When discussing seafloor sinks, it may strengthen the interpretation to consider that polymineralic alteration
(serpentine ± magnetite ± Fe-smectite) likely host mixed sorption and precipitation processes. Where stability fields,
mineralogical heterogeneity, and redox sensitivity of Fe-rich phyllosilicates are addressed, citing Dzene (2022, Clays Clay
Miner) could provide useful context and support.

strength, Ca/Mg ratio, alkalinity/DIC, organic ligands, and SO

General comments 
1. Throughout the manuscript, it was n ot clear how the P -absorption by 
phyllosilicates works (or doesn’t work) in the present environment (ocean, river,

Thanks for the suggestion. The P-adsorption by phyllosilicates plays important 
roles in mediating P cycling in the present environment. For example, clay 
particles serve as one major carrier of particulate P in terrestrial and freshwater 
environments such as soils, rivers, and lakes (Compton et al., 2000), due to the 
high sorption affinity of surface Al-OH sites towards dissolved phosphate. This 
remarkable capacity is now leveraged in geo -engineering applications, where 
specifically modified clays are deployed to mitigate eutrophicat ion in eutrophic 
lakes, offering a redox -insensitive P -binding alternative that supersedes 
traditional metal salts like alum and ferric salts (e.g., Ross et al., 2008). The 
subsequent fluvial transport of this clay -bound P delivers a major flux to 
estuaries, where the increased salinity and pH trigger significant desorption, 
amplifying the riverine P flux to the coastal ocean (Froelich, 1988; Hao et al., 
2021). The ultimate fate of clay -associated P in marine settings is mainly 
governed by redox conditions.  In oxic continental margin sediments, the iron 
oxide or aluminium (hydr)oxide coatings on clays continue to facilitate P 
retention. Upon the onset of sediment anoxia, the reductive dissolution of 
reactive iron phases leads to the release of P into porewat ers, which can be 
later released back to the water column (e.g., Slomp et al., 2002) or transform 
into clay -bound phases (Rasmussen, 2000). During burial diagenesis or 
metamorphism, clay-bound P could be further transformed into more stable and 
crystalline phosphate minerals (cf. Nriagu, 1976). Overall, this intricate interplay, 
from riverine transport to marine burial via either adsorption or precipitation 
pathways, enables phyllosilicates as an important control on P bioavailability 
and geological sequest ration in the Earth system throughout the geological 
history.  
 
We have now revise d the manuscript accordingly to point out the important 
roles of P-adosprtion by phyllosilicates in the present environment (lines 59-63, 
453-475).

Yes, you are right about the currently bimodal reconstruction of the Archean P 
level. Previous studies analyzed P contents in the Archean BIFs or shales and

the Archean ocean than the modern ( Bjerrum et al., 2002; Planavsky et al.,

appearance of oxygenic photosynthesis before the GOE (Anbar et al., 2008; 
Frei et al., 2009 etc.).
