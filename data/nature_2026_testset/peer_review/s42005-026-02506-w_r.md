Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript describes interesting localization effects of metallic nanoparticles in a one-dimensional chain. The
suppression of radiative heat transfer because of short-range evanescent components in contrast to far-field effects only is a
nice result. However, the setup is very theoretical, there are no comments if such chains can be fabricated and for example
what might be the effects of non-idealities in position, size and shape of such spheres. I also find this paper rather technical
and lengthy, maybe for a respected journal one could have tried to focus more on the main discovery. As this kind of
theoretical concept, it is only interesting to rather few scientists. There are a lot of Anderson localization studies published
recently. I find this one interesting, but it also contains lots of terms without explaining the meaning to a wider audience
(Cantor set, Chern numbers, dimer modes etc). I also find this Ohmic damping quite confusing, in theory one can of course
choose any number that makes results more striking, but I guess in real life that is not that much a free parameter. In
summary, I have my doubts if this is interesting enough for a wider audience and the main results could have been reported
in a much shorter format. Also Figure 2 could have been designed to demonstrate the main result in a more informative way.
I propose to reject this manuscript in this form. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, the authors theoretically investigate radiative heat transport in the near-field regime along quasi-periodic chains
of nanoparticles. They demonstrate that the heat flux carried by photons through these disordered systems can be
significantly reduced due to the onset of Anderson localization. 
From a theoretical perspective, this study is both novel and interesting, as the impact of localization in disordered systems
on radiative heat transport has not been, as far as I know, thoroughly explored in the literature. In this regard, the work makes
a meaningful contribution and merits publication. 
Overall, the manuscript is well written and the connection between localization effects and the suppression of heat transfer is
clearly established. However, several important questions and issues need to be addressed before the manuscript can be
considered for publication: 
-The study focuses exclusively on chains composed of metallic nanoparticles, with permittivity modeled using the Drude
model. How would the conclusions change for polar materials that support phonon-polaritons? 
-The dielectric function of InSb is generally not accurately described by a simple Drude model, even in the absence of an
external magnetic field (see, for example, E. D. Palik et al., Phys. Rev. B 13, 2497 (1976)). How might a more realistic model
affect the results? 
- Heat transfer is inherently a broadband phenomenon. Could a broader spectral localization—thereby enhancing the
thermal insulation effect—be achieved by tailoring the degree or nature of disorder within the nanoparticle chain? 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled ‘Ultralow radiative heat flux by Anderson localization in quasiperiodic plasmonic chains’
demonstrates the suppression of radiative heat transfer in one-dimensional arrays of nanoparticles due to Anderson
localization. The authors uncover this effect by computing the transmission coefficient as a function of particle spacing

disorder and Ohmic losses, including the influence of many-body interactions. The model is discussed in detail, and the
results are presented via illustrative and carefully prepared Figures. 
Anderson localization in disordered photonic structures has been identified not only theoretically but also experimentally in
previous work. In this sense, the work does not present unexpected or novel results. However, the work provides quantitative
analysis of the role of geometric parameters and damping rates into the strength of mode localization and heat transfer
suppression for an illustrative case of study. For this reason, in my opinion the work could be a good fit for a journal such as
Communication Physics. I would recommend publication of the work provided the authors address the following questions: 
1. Exceptionally small Ohmic damping rates are required to obtain a significant and potentially observable effect of
Anderson localization on the radiative heat conductance. For example, in Figure 6, conductance values smaller than the
high-damping limit are only obtained for extremely small damping rates and highly disordered distributions of particles. Are
those cases experimentally feasible in materials such as InSb considering the necessary presence of a supporting substrate
or the interaction with other dissipative media? If this is not possible, the authors should not present Anderson localization as
a strategy to manipulate the total heat conductance in metallic nanoparticle arrays. In any case, Anderson localization
seems to clearly limit the transmittance at specific frequencies even with relatively high damping rates as shown in Figure 3,
which might be useful for applications. In my opinion, the authors should critically revise the discussion and conclusions
related to this point. 
2. The authors discuss both the presence of near-field and far-field radiative heat transfer 
mechanisms. A significant fraction
of the bibliographic references in the introduction refer to near-field radiation. However, the relevance of near-field radiative
modes seems negligible. What is the relative importance of the energy transfer via evanescent modes in the situations with
the smallest interparticle distance? Is it possible to localize such modes, or the mode localization identified in this work only
refers to far-field radiative modes? If near-field is important in some cases, the authors should show the transmission spectra
for evanescent modes. If not, the authors should clarify why they consider a nanoscale geometry. Can the same effects be
observed at larger length scales? 
3. The authors consider cases where the particle size is comparable to the interparticle distance. In this case, the dipole
approximation might not be valid, and other modeling approaches such as the proximity approximation might be necessary.
Can the authors demonstrate that the dipole approximation holds for all the cases under study? Do the authors expect that
the phenomenon of Anderson localization might break down at sufficiently small scales, where the dipole approximation 
is not valid? 
4. In Figure 3, it is shown that the frequency of the surface plasmon resonance of an individual particle does not display a
reduced transmittance even in the case of extremely low damping and high disorder. I am curious about the physical
mechanism causing this effect. What is the physical relation between the collective localized modes and the resonant
plasmon frequency? Are there other frequencies that cannot be fully localized via strong disorder? Are resonances a
fundamental limitation preventing a complete suppression of radiative heat conductance via Anderson localization?

Response 3.4:
We thank the reviewer for raising this important concern regarding the validity of the dipole
approximation in our work. In general, the calculation of radiative heat transfer between a pair
of spheres can be categorized into three distinct regimes, based on the comparison between
the radius of sphere a, the gap between the two spheres d, and the thermal wavelength λT .
These regimes, which have been validated by multiple scattering theory [25], are as follows:
(I) Far-field regime ( d > λT ): In this regime, radiative heat transfer follows a far-field
behavior, with the thermal conduction scaling as σ ∝ d −2.
(II) Dipole approximation regime (a ≪ d < λT ): Here, the dipole moment of the particles is
the dominant factor in radiative transfer, resulting in radiative conductance scaling as
σ ∝ d −6.
(III) Proximity approximation regime (d ≪ a < λT ): In this regime, the conductance scales
as σ ∝ d −1.
This category is also applicable to radiative heat transfer among multiple spheres [26]. In our
scenarios, for all geometric configurations, the minimum distance dmin between any two
spheres is greater than 4a, placing them in the dipole approximation regime. If dmin < 4a,
for small particles, interactions are mediated through multipolar modes, rendering the dipole
approximation insufficient for describing radiative energy transfer. In such cases, multipolar
interactions should be incorporated into the Green’ s functions within the framework of many-
body thermal radiation theory [27]. Despite this, we still anticipate observing Anderson

shifts to other frequencies. This shift demonstrates the topological origin of the “anomalous”
