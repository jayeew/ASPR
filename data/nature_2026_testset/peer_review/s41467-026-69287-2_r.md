Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The control of holonomy has recently emerged as a highly active topic, attracting broad interest across various physical
disciplines. In this context, the manuscript entitled “In-memory multilevel control of generic SO(m) holonomy in photonics” by
Chen et al. presents a novel approach to implementing nonvolatile and tunable holonomic control by manipulating
degenerate photonic states. Specifically, the authors cleverly introduce a five-waveguide architecture incorporating a phase-
change material (PCM) waveguide, which enables the realization of two- and three-fold degeneracies. This in turn allows for
the demonstration of fundamental SO(2) holonomic transformations, as well as the prospect of extending to in-memory,
multilevel control of higher-dimensional SO(m) holonomies through cascaded designs. The proposed scheme is original
and timely, offering a potentially significant contribution to both the fundamental study of non-Abelian photonics and the
development of reconfigurable photonic devices. Since this work represents a big advance that is likely to stimulate further
research in this area, I would support the publication of the manuscript after the authors address my following comments. 
1. Please clarify whether the targeted degeneracies are well preserved in both the amorphous and crystalline states of the
five-waveguide system. Since the presence of multiple coupled waveguides can sometimes introduce unwanted
perturbations that affect the robustness of degeneracy (see, for example, Phys. Rev. A 105, 013507 (2021)), a more detailed
analysis would strengthen the work. In particular, simulations of the effective indices of the five modes as a function of gap
distance in the actual device model could help demonstrate that the two- or three-fold degeneracies remain well preserved
across both material states. Besides, the detailed parameters of reconfigurable two-mode braiding, including the evolution of
gap distances, coupling coefficients, and holonomic path, should be provided. 
2. Regarding fabrication, Supplementary Fig. 1 and the Methods section do not mention any planarization steps. Since
multilayer structures, particularly those involving braiding, typically require good planarity, it would be useful to clarify
whether a planarization process (e.g., chemical–mechanical polishing) was employed. For the PCM integration, the
manuscript presents SEM images of Sb
₂
Se
₃
 waveguides, but not of the silicon waveguides used in the holonomy structures.
Providing SEM figures of the silicon waveguides would improve the completeness of the work. In addition, while PCM layers
in photonic devices are often on the order of tens of nanometers, the PCM waveguide here is significantly thicker. Given the
limited penetration depth of visible laser light in PCM, this could lead to non-uniform phase transitions and refractive index
gradients along the thickness direction after laser writing. A discussion on how such effects may influence the reversibility of
laser switching and the stability of reconfigurable geometric phases would further strengthen the work. 
3. For the in-memory reconfiguration of SO(m) holonomy, two laser direct writing systems operating at 408 nm and 639 nm
were employed for amorphization and crystallization of the PCM, respectively. Why are two distinct laser systems used?
Also, the parameters of the specific control pulse for the phase switching process should be provided in detail. Further, the
laser-triggered phase transition in Sb2Se3 is characterized by grain morphology under microscopy and Raman
spectroscopy. While X-ray diffraction analysis would offer more direct observation of the lattice structure changes through
diffraction peaks, I would suggest the authors to add XRD results as Supplementary data. 
4. The demonstration of two-level SO(2) holonomy relies on the binary switching between the amorphous and crystalline
states of the PCM. It would be interesting to discuss how the system behaves when the PCM is in intermediate states (see,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

e.g., Nat. Commun. 15, 2786 (2024)), as this may introduce additional levels of control. Furthermore, the current design
assumes a uniform phase change across the entire waveguide. Considering the case where only a portion of the waveguide
undergoes a phase transition may open the possibility of generating multiple geometric-phase levels within a single device.
A discussion along these lines would strengthen the manuscript by highlighting the broader tunability and versatility of the
proposed approach. 
5. For the multilevel control of SO(2) holonomy, the authors demonstrate three discrete levels by cascading two identical
building blocks. While this provides a useful proof of concept, the scheme could potentially be extended further for a more
insightful discussion. In particular, cascading non-identical building blocks may enable a richer set of discrete levels and
more versatile control. 
Some small remarks for the authors’ consideration: 
1. Some typos should be double-checked carefully, i.e. in the abstract the word "reconfiguable" should be “reconfigurable”. 
2. In Fig. 1b, the annotation “Two degenerate subspace” is recommended to be corrected as “Two degenerate states” or
“Two-dimensional degenerate subspace”. Similar descriptions, such as “Three degenerate subspace” in the annotation of
Fig. 1b and “the number of degenerate spaces” in the caption of Fig. 1, are also recommended to be revised. 
3. In the sentence “This creates slight color contrasts between the recrystallized region and the original crystalline substrate,
as seen in Supplementary Fig. 2a,” it appears that the referenced image should be Fig. 2a in the main text rather than the
supplementary material. Please verify and correct this reference for clarity and consistency. 
4. The unit of the scale bar in Fig. 1d should be provided. 
5. The symbol “μ” in Supplementary Eq. (S3) should be well defined. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work demonstrates in-memory multilevel control of generic SO(m) holonomy using a multilayer silicon photonic platform
integrated with the phase-change material Sb
₂
Se
₃
. By optically switching the Sb
₂
Se
₃
 waveguides between amorphous and
crystalline states, the authors dynamically modulate the number of degenerate modes in the system, enabling reconfigurable
non-Abelian geometric phases without volatile external controls. They experimentally achieve high-fidelity, nonvolatile
SO(2) and SO(3) transformations, along with tunable two- and three-mode braiding operations, and showcase multi-level
programmability through cascaded SO(2) units and Givens rotations. This approach merges the inherent robustness of
geometric phases with the nonvolatile tunability of PCMs, offering a promising pathway toward fault-tolerant photonic
quantum processing, reconfigurable optical switching networks, and high-dimensional classical neuromorphic computing. 
In my opinions, this work demonstrates the first experimental realization of reconfigurable control over non-Abelian
holonomy, which exhibits notable innovation. However, several issues should be addressed prior to publication: 
1. First and foremost, regarding novelty, this work combines the concepts of "reconfigurability" and "non-Abelian holonomy,"
which brings to mind two related works also published in Nature Communications: Nat. Commun. 16, 7089 (2025) and Nat.
Commun. 16, 3650 (2025). Compared to the former, this study extends braiding operations to more general holonomic
scenarios and introduces a new refractive index tuning method; compared to the latter, it achieves dynamic control over the
SO(m) group. The authors should provide more detailed and specific comparisons with these relevant works, and include
necessary discussions in the manuscript to better highlight the originality and significance of their contribution. 
2. As a follow-up to the point above, different to Nat. Commun. 16, 7089 (2025), which employs the thermo-optic effect for
active tuning, this work utilizes the phase-change material, claiming advantages in lower energy consumption and improved
stability. The authors are encouraged to provide a detailed comparison of the advantages and disadvantages of different
active tuning methods to underscore the technical and systemic innovation of their approach. 
3. While the active tuning strategy presented is appealing, it remains limited to switching between SO(2) and SO(3) based
on the presence or absence of onsite index mismatch. It would be valuable if the authors could discuss whether their method
can be extended to design more complex or interesting functionalities. 
4. The caption of Figure 2e is overly brief. A more detailed explanation of how the SO(2) transformation is realized in
parameter space should be provided to assist readers unfamiliar with the process of non-Abelian holonomy. 
5. The multilayer quasi-3D waveguide platform constructed using deposited SiO
₂
 is a promising architecture. However, a
valid concern is whether next-nearest-neighbor coupling coefficients (such as κCD) can be neglected in such multilayer
waveguides, and whether they might disrupt the holonomy process. Supplementary data on coupling coefficients should be
provided in the Supplementary Information of this work to facilitate reader understanding. 
6. Several minor issues require attention: the parameter space vectors on lines 91 and 136 contain a square symbol on the
left-hand side of the equation, which may be a display error. The matrix alignment in Eq. (1) is also irregular. The authors
should correct these and carefully check for other similar issues throughout the text.

index matched), the configuration (κDX ≫ κAX, κBX, and κCX) leads to a collapse of the

In addition, while PCM layers in photonic devices are often on the order of tens of

Response: 
We would like to thank the reviewer for this instructive question. Indeed, in 
conventional PCM-integrated photonic devices, a thin PCM layer (typically tens of 
nanometers) is usually covered on top of the photonic waveguide to form a hybrid 
structure, interacting with light via the evanescent field, resulting in relatively weak 
refractive index modulation. In contrast, our work directly uses PCMs as the 
waveguide material itself, enabling significantly stronger modulation of the effective 
refractive index. This necessitates a relatively thicker PCM layer. To ensure effective 
refractive index matching between the Si waveguide and the PCM waveguide, our 
PCM waveguide is designed with a thickness of 100 nm. Low-loss PCMs with slow 
crystallization kinetics typically exhibit relatively good glass-forming ability, which 
allows them to achieve reversible switching at larger thicknesses. Laser switching of 
PCMs is a complex process involving light absorption, heat generation, and phase 
transition. Therefore, to verify whether the cross-section of the laser-switched PCM 
exhibits amorphous/crystalline stratification or obvious nonuniformity related to the 
penetration depth of the laser, transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X 
G2, Thermo Fisher Scientific) tests were conducted. The Sb₂Se₃ film is sandwiched 
between SiO₂ layers, and except for the absence of waveguide patterning, the 
structure is identical to that of the device in the manuscript. 
1) Annealed crystalline state: Figure R8a presents the TEM image of crystalline 
Sb₂Se₃ (Cry-Sb ₂Se₃) obtained through annealing, where the long-range ordered 
atomic arrangement is distinctly visible. A Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analysis 
reveals the orthorhombic lattice structure (Fig. R8b), and multiple sets of reflexes are 
observed due to the polycrystalline nature of the material with varying orientations. 2)
Laser amorphization: The amorphization is conducted using the commercial laser 
direct writing system, as mentioned in the manuscript. Fig. R8c and Fig. R8d show 
the TEM images of the top and bottom areas of the amorphized Sb ₂Se₃ (am-Sb₂Se₃) 
thin film. The ordered atomic arrangement is no longer observed, and no obvious

difference appears in the top and bottom regions of the film. The FFT of Fig. R8d 
shown in Fig. R8e displays typical amorphous diffraction rings. 3) Laser 
crystallization: Laser crystallization of an amorphous Sb ₂Se₃ thin film was achieved 
using our home -built system. Fig. R8g and Fig. R8h present the TEM images of the 
top and bottom regions of the laser -crystallized Sb₂Se₃, revealing lattice fringes with

Response:  
We are very thankful for the reviewer’s instructive comments. Indeed, both 
thermal turning and non-volatile tuning are important active tuning approaches. 
However, we believe that phase-change materials (PCMs) offer some advantages over 
conventional thermo-optic (TO) modulation in integrated photonic devices, 
particularly in terms of refractive index contrast, non-volatility, and no thermal 
crosstalk, as will be discussed in detail below. 
(i) Large refractive index contrast.  PCMs exhibit drastic changes in optical 
properties between amorphous and crystalline phases, with refractive index 
differences (Δn) exceeding 0.7 in the near-infrared regime. In contrast, TO effects 
induce only small index changes (typically ~10 -2). The large refractive index contrast 
facilitates significant onsite index matching or mismatching, enabling the complete 
and stable switching from a three-fold degeneracy to a two-fold degeneracy, and 
thereby supporting reconfigurable holonomic evolution paths.
(ii) Ultra-low power consumption and non-volatility. A key advantage of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 33 -->

PCMs is their non -volatile nature: once switched, the state is retained without power. 
Energy is consumed only during the transient switching event (fJ to pJ per bit). In 
contrast, TO devices require continuous power to maintain a state, resulting in high 
static power consumption and thermal crosstalk.
(iii) Absence of thermal crosstalk.  Thermal crosstalk is a notable problem of 
thermo-optic modulation in high -density programmable integrated photonics. The 
persistent heat diffuses laterally through the substrate and dielectric layers, altering 
the refractive index of neighboring waveguides. Especially for the non -Abelian 
holonomy with densely packed waveguides, thermal crosstalk makes independent 
control of nearest -neighboring waveguides challenging. Evident thermal crosstalk 
may influence the degeneracy of tunable SO(2) holonomy. As Fig. R14a shows, [Nat. 
Commun. 16, 7089 (2025)] also simulates the influence of thermal crosstalk in 
reconfigurable braiding, and the propagation constant of neighboring waveguide X 
slightly varies as the distance to the electrode. Fig. R14b shows the temperature 
distribution observed in an integrated thermo-optic modulation device, captured using 
a thermal imaging camera (FOTRIC 280+). Evident thermal crosstalk can be 
observed in the spot point (Sp1). In contrast, PCM is non -volatile with no static heat, 
and its switching heat is transient and highly localized, enabling crosstalk -free, 
independent tuning of individual waveguides within the holonomy, and preventing 
degradation of the degeneracy caused by thermal crosstalk.
