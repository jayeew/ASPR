Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study by Huang et al., focuses on the regulation of microglial responses to neurodegeneration, primarily in an optic
nerve crush model that displays a stereotyped pattern of neurodegeneration known as Wallerian degeneration. The authors
attempt to connect the regulation of phagocytosis, with a focus on the gene MerTK, to the expression of the transcription
factors PU.1 and IRF8, and the cytokine TGFB1. The authors also provide evidence, largely through genetic conditional
deletion experiments, that these genes play an important role in the regulation of the neuroinflammatory response to optic
crush, and potentially by extension, to other neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s. 
The authors begin by demonstrating that increases in PU.1 and IRF8 expression and CD11b+ cell number in this model are
significantly reduced when the phagocytosis regulating gene MerTK is deleted. The authors then continued to explore the
role of the phagocytosis-regulating genes MerTK and Axl, and attempt to connect the PLC signaling downstream of MERTK
signaling to the regulation of Irf8 and Pu.1 expression. To do this, the authors performed a series of in vitro manipulations of
FACS isolated microglia, and demonstrate that MerTK conditional mutant (cKO) microglia display reduced levels of IRF8 in
response to phagocytosis stimulation via phosphatidylserine-dependent phagocytosis. The authors go on to provide
evidence that this IRF8 expression is regulated by phospholipase C (PLC) signaling, which they demonstrate by treating in
vitro cultured microglia with a PLC inhibitor, which reduced IRF8 expression. 
The authors then perform an elegant experiment using Cre-dependent hM3D(Gq) (DREADD) receptor expression in
microglia to manipulate PLC activity in the uninjured optic nerve. These manipulations showed that hM3D(Gq) stimulation
(and not just CNO administration, an important control) increases PU.1 and IRF8 expression, in line with their model that
expression of these genes is regulated by PLC signaling downstream of phagocytosis. 
Then, the authors connect IRF8 and PU.1 transcriptional regulation to the expression of the cytokine TGFB1. They show,
using previously published ChIP-seq analysis of optic nerve microglia, that IRF8 and PU.1 both bind upstream of Tgfb1,
near the transcriptional start site. In a very elegant demonstration of the importance of this binding site, they specifically
mutated it using CRISPR-based mutagenesis, and showed that mutation of this site in vivo blocked the injury-induced
upregulation of Tgfb1. 
The authors then use a series of conditional mutagenesis experiments to delete Tgfb1, Irf8, Tgfbr2, or Tgfb1 using Cx3cr1-
CreER in the context of this nerve crush model. These experiments demonstrated that deletion of IRF8 completely blocked
the upregulation of neuroinflammation-related microglial gene expression in this model, in line with previous publications on
the role of IRF8 in the response to neuroinflammatory brain injury. Loss of Tgfb1, interestingly, and in contrast to previous
reports (McKinsey et al. 2024, Bedolla et al. 2024), did not appear to lead to a significant change in the homeostatic gene
expression in the uninjured optic nerve microglia. Loss of Tgfb1 did however, lead to profoundly reduced
neurodegeneration-induced response in optic nerve microglia, as well as a significant reduction in microglial proliferation.
Loss of either Tgfbr2 or Tgfbr1, again using Cx3cr1-CreER, appeared to phenocopy the loss of Tgfb1.in this model, at least
in terms of CD11b density, which the authors use as a surrogate marker for microglia throughout the manuscript. 
The authors then examine the 5xFAD mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease, and describe the upregulation of Tgfb1, PU.1,
IRF8 that in hippocampal microglia of these mice, in line with their observations in the optic nerve crush model.

Finally, the authors examine sn-RNAseq datasets derived from patients with Alzheimer’s, and find 
that the expression of Tgfb1, PU.1, IRF8 and MERTK are enriched in microglia. Finally, the authros examine pSmad2
expression in cortical tissue derived from control or Alzheimer’s disease patients, and find that there is a significant increase
in pSmad2 signal in IBA1+ cells, suggesting that TGFB signaling may be more strongly engaged in these cells in the context
of neurodegeneration. 
Overall, this study covers a lot of ground, the methodology is sound, and represents an ambitious attempt to link the
signaling pathways downstream of phagocytosis to the gene regulation of core microglial transcription factors, to the
regulation of a key microglial cytokine, Tgfb1. There is also some very interesting phenomenological discoveries in the cKO
mice that they describe, and the phenotypes that they display. This is complicated somewhat by the potential effects of
deleting these genes in circulating monocytes, which could be muting the response to injury. The genetic experiments
manipulating PLC signaling in uninjured microglia, and the generation of a novel DNA regulatory element mutant mouse
line are particularly elegant. These experiments, and the novel generation of Tgfbr2 mutant mice on the background of
Cx3cr1-CreER homozygosity, are a testament to the significant amount of labor that went into this study. Overall, this study is
of significance for the field, and while some of the results are contrary to what others have found in other models, this is very
interesting, as figuring out *why* these differences exist may reveal some very important truths about the role of microglia in
neurodegenerative disease and injury. A deeper discussion of these differing results, and potential explanations and/or
models would improve the manuscript. 
Regarding reproducibility, this model is easily accessible and is a robust model for studying neurodegeneration. However,
whether microglia in this optic crush model of white matter injury are comparable to microglia in the rest of the brain in other
neurodegenerative disease and injury contexts merits investigation (by the field at large, not in this study per se). Of note,
white matter microglia are known to display distinct gene expression patterns, which may be relevant in the interpretation of
these results. 
Below, I list some critiques and concerns that I had with the study. Of note, I don’t believe these critiques prohibit publication,
but do merit significant revision and some additional evidence for their claims: 
-One major caveat to this research is the possibility that the observed changes in PU.1 and IRF8 are due to infiltrating
monocytes, and that the cKO experimental results reflect a diminished inflammatory response from infiltrating monocytes.
This is exacerbated by the use of CD11b as a proxy for microglia, when this would also mark infiltrating monocytes. While
the authors state that “Importantly, our previous work combining the approaches of linkage tracing, parabiosis, and microglia-
specific genetic deletion demonstrated that such microglial responses in injured optic nerves would not involve circulating
peripheral immune cells 45”, an examination of the cited study does not show microglial-specific gene deletion studies, or
lineage tracing (I’m not sure if this is what the authors meant by “linkage-tracing”), and the parabiosis experiment results are
in a supplemental figure that is not accessible. 
A more definitive and much simpler strategy to examine the properties of
resident macrophages, would be to genetically label these macrophages prior to injury, using a CreER line that more
specifically labels microglia, such as P2ry12-CreER or Tmem119-CreER. 
-While the authors use increased PU.1 and IRF8 expression as a proxy for the microglial response to neuroinflammatory
injury, but it would be helpful if other markers could be shown as well, to better understand what exactly is happening in the
different models that the authors present. For instance, are levels of microglial homeostatic markers always inversely
correlated with the increases in PU.1 and IRF8, or is this independently regulated? Additional RNA-seq characterizations of
their different models would be an ideal for this. 
-The difference in the results of this study versus other recent reports (e.g. McKinsey et al. 2024 and Bedolla et al. 2024) are
interesting, and merit further discussion and some experimental investigation. One very useful internal control for these
studies would be to examine the brains of the various cKO mutant lines that the authors generated, and to examine whether
they observe a converse microglial phenotype, i.e. a loss of homeostasis. If this is the case, and they see converse effects in
the optic nerve versus other regions of the brain, then it might suggest that there are significant anatomical differences in the
requirement for TGFB signaling in the homeostasis of microglia. This seems to be a very important issue to address, for the
results of the study and for the field at large. 
-In F6, F7 and S4, the costaining is done with IBA1, and while these cells may be microglia they could also be infiltrating
monocytes. Costaining with TMEM119 or P2RY12 could be helpful here. Importantly, the quality of the immunostaining, or at
least the presentation of it, could use some improvement. Some larger, lower magnification images could help to get a better
sense of the immunostaining results, and the anatomical context of where these images were taken. 
-One minor issue is the need for some editing to address grammatical mistakes that are present throughout the manuscript. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors describe the regulation of microglia immune activation through phagocytosis via the MerTK signaling pathway.
Using a mouse model of pathological axonal degeneration, the authors demonstrate that MerTK-dependent phagocytosis
activates downstream phospholipase-C signaling, leading to the upregulation of PU.1 and IRF8, two key transcription
factors that orchestrate microglial functions. By leveraging chromatin immunoprecipitation datasets the authors further reveal
that PU.1 and IRF8 directly regulate TGFβ1 expression, and disruption of this regulatory site abolishes microglial TGFβ1
induction during neurodegeneration. Functionally, microglial TGFβ1 acts in an autocrine manner, with its deletion or the loss

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

of its receptors (TGFβR1 or TGFβR2) impairing microglial responses. Overall, this manuscript presents a compelling and
well-supported mechanistic framework linking microglial phagocytosis to transcriptional regulation, with significant
implications for neurodegenerative disease research. 
Although the study's demonstration of MerTK–TGFβ1 signaling in both the 5×FAD model and human AD patients is
valuable, microglial dysfunction, including altered TGFβ1 signaling, has already been implicated in AD. Previous studies
have shown that microglial phagocytosis of amyloid plaques is regulated by MerTK, and TGFβ1 has been linked to
microglial responses in AD pathology. Despite these points, the manuscript provides a novel mechanistic link between
MerTK-driven phagocytosis and PU.1/IRF8-dependent transcriptional regulation of TGFβ1. The identification of a specific
PU.1/IRF8 binding site necessary for TGFβ1 induction is a significant molecular insight. Moreover, demonstrating that
microglial TGFβ1 operates in an autocrine manner to sustain responses is an important refinement of our understanding of
microglial biology. 
Thus, while most aspects of this study builds on existing knowledge, the manuscript still makes a meaningful contribution by
detailing how MerTK signaling integrates phagocytosis with transcriptional regulation and feedback signaling in microglia.
The approach is simple but conceptual advancements justify its acceptance for publication. Nevertheless, the study lacks
rigor and clarity when it comes to its statistical methods and requires several revisions to strength the confidence of its
conclusions. 
Major Concerns: 
Sample numbers and their definition are not included in the figure legends. Instead on the statistical method the authors just
state “All the sample points (n) shown in the figures represent biological replicates.” The authors should expand their
definition of their sample numbers, to explicitly how they are represented. 
Are these averages from multiple sections across
different animals, or different wells of cultures derived from multiple animals? This is particularly concerning given the low
sample size (n=3-5 animals?) in their experiments, and the use of in vitro assays (e.g. Figure 2A, n=3 per group. Some of the
quantification provided in the study are represented as n=many cells. For example, Figure 3F and 6E-H. This approach
inflates the sample size and does not account for the number of biological replicates. This data should not be presented
without, in the least, a graph where averages from distinct animals are tested for statistical significance. 
Immunostaining and the associated quantification provided is rather simplistic. Images of microglia are poorly focused, and
IBA1 signal does not entirely show the cell morphology as it should. In the IHC for 5XFAD animals, it looks like microglia
have lost their tiling and are surrounding plaques. It is not possible to dicer this from the images because there are no
counter stains, for example for amyloid. It remains unclear if non plaque-corralling microglia are also activating similar
programs. The authors should improve their quantification and provide more nuance here. 
Cell cultures approaches to isolate microglia for in vitro assays are notoriously difficult, but images the authors provide for
Figure 2A and 2C make their results particularly difficult to interpret. The microglia seem to be extremely ameboid, and thus
likely to be very unhealthy or activated. The authors should provide additional staining, higher resolution images, and
ensure that their cell cultures are high quality. 
Minor Concerns: 
Page 4: “For instance, microglia are responsible for the phagocytic removal of protein aggregates such as Ab plagues,
which helps delay the onset and progression of Alzheimer's disease”. Please correct typo, plaques not plagues. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Huang et al. entitled “MerTK-triggered TGFb1 Autocrine Signal Designates Microglial Responses to
Neurodegeneration” presents insights into how phagocytosis not only serves a functional role for microglia during
neurodegeneration but also acts as a regulatory mechanism that shapes microglial responses. Using a mouse model of
pathological axonal degeneration, the authors identify the phagocytic receptor MerTK as a critical upstream mediator that
triggers a signaling cascade leading to the activation of transcription factors PU.1 and IRF8. These transcription factors, in
turn, directly regulate the expression of TGFβ1, establishing a feedback loop wherein TGFβ1 acts in an autocrine manner
via TGFβR1 and TGFβR2 to sustain microglial function in neurodegeneration. Notably, this mechanism could be of
relevance for both the 5×FAD Alzheimer's disease mouse model and human patient samples, since both demonstrate an
enrichment of selected central regulators identified here on the transcript and/or protein level. The work uncovers a central
molecular axis driving microglial responses to neurodegeneration and offers new perspectives on microglial regulation in
disease contexts. 
The overall structure of the manuscript is easy to follow, with the data presented in a comprehensible manner, and the
central idea expands nicely on the current state of knowledge. However, some major points should be addressed: 
Major points 
• Throughout the manuscript, please use a nuclear counterstain and provide higher resolution images. The localization of
proteins is not possible to evaluate from the provided images. Please use confocal imaging for high-resulution images.. 
• While the data presented for the role of TGFβ1, TGFβR1, TGFβR2, and pSMAD2 superficially look compelling, the
immunofluorescence stainings are a weak point of the manuscript. It seems not logical that TGFb or its receptors are mainly
localized in the cytoplasm or nucleus of microglia.

• Can the authors comment on the cell type that releases TGFb in the optical nerve besides microglia as Figure 3B shows
clear TGFb signal which is not localized to microglia. 
• The staining pattern of TGFb in the human AD cases is difficult to interpret. The antibody stains the complete membrane
and potentially also the cytoplasm as well as microglia processes, which is difficult to reconcile with the production and
secretion of TGF and binding of TGF to TGFbR on the surface. 
• In contrast to the argument made by the authors at the start of the manuscript that CD11b staining is the only possible
option to stain microglia, in between the author switch from CD11b staining to Iba1 staining. It would be advisable to stay
consistent in the staining throughout the manuscript. The argument itself is also not 100% convincing as CD11b is also
regulated under these conditions as visible in Figures 1-5. 
• Can the authors comment on the fact that Irf8 staining can be found outside of microglia (Figure 1B). 
• As the author rightly highlight, PU.1 is a lineage defining TF for microglia. Why is no PU.1 staining detectable in microglia
under homeostatic conditions? 
• Figure 2F, please co-stain for a microglia marker as well. 
• For the 5X FAD model and the stainings in human AD patients, please also stain plaques to allow for understanding how
plaques affect phenotype of cells and whether vicinity to plaques affects cells. 
• Can the authors please describe in detail how the two fantastic mouse models were made, what type of assays were used
to demonstrate specificity of the mutation/gene replacement and proper integration, etc. How faithful is the floxed TGFbR2
allele? Is TGFbR2 expression actually reduced in the conditional KO animals? Similarly, how about TGFbR1? 
• This generation of the PU.1/IRF8 biding motif deficient mice is very elegant way to show the importance of PU.1/IRF8
signaling/binding for TGFb induction. It would be nice to show cell type specificity by counterstaining microglia, as it remains
unclear which cell express TGF and whether TGF is actually reduced in microglia. 
• Is PU.1/IRF.8 binding indeed prevented by the induced mutation in the TGFb locus? Does PU.1 and IRF8 binding by ChIP
or chromatin accessibility change in comparison to WT animals? 
• What other cells express pSMAD2 as suggested by the stainings presented in the human AD cases. Please also show
overview pictures and quantifications. 
• Given that a recent report by Bedolla et al. described that TGFb1 deletion in microglia disrupted their homeostasis in the
brain of adult mice, leading to inflammatory-like phenotypes it would be important to assess how the microglia phenotype is
changed after TGFb deletion in this report as well. 
• Throughout the manuscript, please comment on numbers of animals and experimental repetitions. 
• For the gene expression data, please state number of samples assessed. It is reassuring that a selected number of genes
show no difference after Irf8 or TGFb knockout in microglia, it would be important to show the global differences after
deletion of both factors for microglia and not only zoom into a selected number of genes. 
• For the RNA-seq analysis presented in Figure 4 a very selective visualization of the data is used. Are genes of the direct
TGFb signaling pathway altered? How much change can be seen in comparison to cells from uninjured animals? 
• Where is the microglial proliferation quantified for the Cx3cr1CreER/+; Irf8fl/fl mice? (Page 10 “Figure 2B”) 
• Figure 1, in this model, and after in vitro phagocytosis, do the authors also observe pSMAD2 translocation to the nucleus? 
• At which timepoint were microglia analyzed after Axl deletion? Based on the number of microglia in the WT, this looks like
day 3. How does it look like at day 6? The effect in Mertk KO was also stronger at day 6. 
• The authors rely on their previous publication showing that peripheral myeloid cells could not be detected in the optic
nerve after traumatic injury in a parabiosis model, and therefore assume that the increased number of Cd11b+ cells detected
in the optic nerve after injury can be solely attributed to proliferation of the resident microglia. Please validate this
assumption (for example by labeling Ki67) - or phrase it differently. It is conceivable that, even if no peripheral myeloid cells
should be recruited, microglia from other parts of the optic nerve or even the retina migrate to the site of injury. 
• To show the effect of the inducible microglial-specific deletion of the respective gene, the authors always use the
respective Cx3cr1CreER/+ mouse as a control. While this is valid to exclude unwanted effects of leaky Cre expression, a
comparison with the actual Cx3cr1CreER/+ floxed animal after oral gavage with solvent instead of tamoxifen would have
been warranted to exclude any unwanted deletions/recombinations happening before the administration of tamoxifen,
potentially during mouse development. 
Minor points 
• On pages 9, 10 and 15, the figure numbers in the text do not match the actual figures. 
• Even in absence of MerTK/TGFb1/TGFbR, microglia still show a prominent response to the optic nerve injury as visible in
Figures 1+4+5, showing limitations in the models and/or independent pathways shaping the microglia response. Please re-
phrase discussion and summary accordingly. 
• Figure S2A+B: The 4 left panels lack a legend of the labeled targets.
