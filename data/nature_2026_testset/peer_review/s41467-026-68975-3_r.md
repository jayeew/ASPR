Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the submitted manuscript, Dr. Kishore Guda's group based on the familial clusters studies of the onset of Barrett's
esophagus, focus on a germline variants in the VSIG10L, inherited predisposition to Barrett's esophagus. Although this work
has certain merits on mice model by validating a key gene variant in BE pathogenesis, it is unfortunately pure descriptive
without novel mechanistic insights. Many of the analyses are at most speculations and many are well known facts in BE-
EAC studies. 
However, their analysis and validation is not comprehensive, and the proposal in VSIG10L as a key determinant in BE

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

pathogenesis is not innovate (2016, JAMA Oncol), either. I have some concerns and comments regarding the contents of
this study listed below. 
1.Strengthen Human Functional Validation and add human Cohort Support for VSIG10L Pathogenicity. 
While the authors have identified several rare VSIG10L variants in BE/EAC families and established mouse models carrying
key mutations, the current evidence primarily centers on genetic associations and animal phenotypes. Incorporating direct
functional validation in human cells or tissues—such as knock-in of the corresponding mutations—would help to more
definitively demonstrate the pathogenicity of these mutations. In addition, verifying these findings using a more human tissue
samples would further support the association between VSIG10L variants and disease risk. These additional studies would
enhance the causal inference and translational value of the work. 
2.Further Elucidation of the Gene-Environment Interaction Mechanisms and Downstream Molecular Pathways. 
The manuscript presents evidence that VSIG10L mutations, together with environmental factors such as bile acid exposure,
contribute to the development of Barrett’s-like metaplasia, and spatial transcriptomics have revealed perturbations in
epithelial homeostasis pathways. To further enrich the mechanistic understanding, the authors could consider deeper
analyses of their spatial transcriptomics and differential gene expression data to pinpoint key downstream effectors and
signaling pathways. Clarifying how VSIG10L deficiency influences epithelial repair, differentiation, and barrier integrity by
studying the specific downstream molecules or pathways—as well as detailing the interplay between genetic and
environmental factors at the molecular level—would provide a more comprehensive mechanistic framework. 
3. Enhancing the Statistical Generalizability and Population-Level Validation of Rare Pathogenic Variants. 
The study’s identification of pathogenic VSIG10L variants is largely based on familial screening, with most variants
appearing to be extremely rare or private to individual families. To further establish the broader relevance of these findings,
the authors might consider integrating analyses from population-based whole-genome or exome sequencing datasets
relevant to BE. Such efforts would clarify the population frequency, penetrance, and risk effect of these variants, thereby
increasing the statistical power and clinical significance of the study’s conclusions.

No subtitles in section of Results. Needs more data supports the working model (figure 6).

Reviewer #6 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study is a valuable extension of the authors’ prior work mapping the BE susceptibility variant S631G to VSIG10L. Their
efforts to characterize variability in VSIG10L expression and its potential contribution to BE susceptibility are commendable
and advance the early understanding of VSIG10L’s role in this disease. The study methodology are sound, and following the
first round of edits are well-described and comprehensive. 
Key strengths include the demonstration that VSIG10L RNA is selectively and consistently expressed in suprabasal
squamous cells; the finding that organoids harboring the VSIG10L-S631G variant exhibit disrupted squamous stratification
and maturation compared with controls; and mouse models showing reduced desmosomes in the suprabasal squamous
layer in S516G and knockout variants. These findings support ongoing work to define the pathogenic and phenotypic impact
of VSIG10L variants, while recognizing that further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms of BE pathogenesis and
causality. 
Please note that I served as a co-reviewer after Reviewer #4 became unavailable to evaluate the authors’ responses to their
earlier comments. The authors’ responses to Reviewer #4’s comments are satisfactory. I recommend that this manuscript be
accepted for publication.

We thank the editors and reviewers for their valuable time and volunteering to review  our 
manuscript, and for their overall enthusiasm  and endorsement of the study. Also, we very much 
appreciate the reviewers in recognizing the arduous efforts involved in  pursuing human genetic 
studies and generating animal models, and the high clinical-translational potential of such studies, 
especially in complex disorders like Barrett’s esophagus (BE).  
In brief, w e originally made the observation of inherited predisposition in BE/EAC, and 
have been accruing families across the U.S and Europe for over two decades . Using genetic 
association, high-throughput genomics, and subsequently developing orthologous animal 
models, we discovered VSIG10L to be a key determin ant in BE pathobiology. Our findings, in 
toto, underscore the many routes to BE genesis, in addition to identifying the genes and processes 
involved in BE predisposition. Also, the Vsig10l mutant mouse models we present here are the 
first of its kind in this disorder that is directly based on human disease and predisposition. We 
believe our study will stimulate new areas of investigations into VSIG family of proteins and their 
connection to esophageal SQ biology and BE pathogenesis. From a clinical -translational 
standpoint, o ur continued efforts in identifying such genetic determinants of BE/EAC should 
enable developing effective screening and surveillance strategies for patients, and in formulating 
improved prevention approaches for this complex disorder.  Below we have provided our 
responses to the reviewers’ thoughtful comments to the best of our ability.

3. For comparisons presented in Figure 3, the authors report a p -value estimated using a one -
tailed Fisher’s exact test. Should corrections or post-hoc tests for multiple group comparisons be 
applied to reduce the risk of false positives, particularly given the small number of animals in each 
group (n=7–12) and the number of groups (5)? 
 We contemplated this when analyzing the data . Basically, we enumerated the number of 
animals containing any changes at the squamo -columnar junction (even single glandular-
appearing lesions) occurring in each mouse exposed to DCA diet  (genotypes blinded to our 
pathologist). As mentioned and shown in the revised manuscript (updated Fig. 5), the lesions 
developing in the KI/KO mice were profoundly exacerbated , compared to the infrequent and 
minor glandular changes we saw in the WT, underscoring a fu ndamental difference. Given 
the pronounced phenotypic differences between WT vs. KI/KO, we strongly believe the 
likelihood of such qualitatively exacerbated lesions to occur by random chance in an otherwise 
normal/wild-type mouse (even exposed to  DCA) would be extremely rare. That being said, 
from a statistical perspective, given that we conducted 4 hypothesis tests, we can adjust 
significance at P = 0.0125 (0.05/4) using Bonferroni. We have now added this Bonferroni 
corrected P value for additional consideration in the Figure 4 legend (originally Figure 3)  of 
the revised manuscript.

Reviewer #6 (Remarks to the Author): 
This study is a valuable extension of the authors’ prior work mapping the BE susceptibility variant 
S631G to VSIG10L. Their efforts to characterize variability in VSIG10L expression and its 
potential contribution to BE susceptibility are commendable and advance the early understanding 
of VSIG10L’s role in this disease. The study methodology are sound, and following the first round 
of edits are well-described and comprehensive. 
 
Key strengths include the demonstration that VSIG10L RNA is selectively and consistently 
expressed in suprabasal squamous cells; the finding that organoids harboring the VSIG10L -
S631G variant exhibit disrupted squamous stratification and maturation compared with controls; 
and mouse models showing reduced desmosomes in the suprabasal squamous layer in S516G 
and knockout variants. These findings support ongoing work to define the pathogenic and 
phenotypic impact of VSIG10L variants, while recognizing that further studies are needed to clarify 
the mechanisms of BE pathogenesis and causality.

authors’ responses to their earlier comments. The authors’ responses to Reviewer #4’s comments 
are satisfactory. I recommend that this manuscript be accepted for publication.
