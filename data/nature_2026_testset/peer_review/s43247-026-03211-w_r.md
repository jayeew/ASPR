Response: We thank the reviewer for their positive comments about our submission and agree that 
revisions are needed to improve the manuscript. In detail, we agree that enrichment factors (EFs) of Cd 
vary between aerosol sampling locations and acknowledge that most of the EF(Cd) values reported by 
Shelley et al. (2025) are lower (EF < 20) between 7°N and 28°N  (Supplementary Table 4). However, 
we would like to emphasize that surface seawater integ rates Cd inputs over a much longer timescale 
than individual aerosol sampling events. While the atmospheric residence time of aerosols is on the 
order of 1–2 weeks, the residence time of Cd in surface seawater is 0.2–0.4 years (Supplementary Note 
4). Therefore, short-term or local variability in aerosol EF(Cd) is unlikely to be directly reflected in 
surface seawater signals, which represent the cumulative effects of aerosol deposition over longer 
periods. 
Across the study region, aerosol EF(Cd) values range widely from ~4 to 370, encompassing both natural 
background and strongly anthropogenic signatures. This variability, together with the residence time of

2 
Cd in surface waters, suggests that the observed seawater δ114Cd values are therefore best explained by 
the integrated influence of this broader EF(Cd) range, rather than by a one-to-one correspondence with 
locally sampled aerosols. Taken together, these  results highlight a partial decoupling between local 
aerosol EF (Cd) values and the Cd isotope composition observed in surface seawater. Long -range 
atmospheric transport of aerosols enriched in anthropogenic Cd and the relatively long residence time 
of Cd in surface waters provide a plausible explanation for the low δ 114Cd values observed at stations 
30, 36, and 40 in our study.  This is now clearly explained in the revised manuscript in the text 
highlighted in blue on Page 17.

importance of relating Cd mass fluxes to underlying water fluxes, especially given the large difference

Fatm + fupwell*[Cd]tcl = fdownwell*[Cd]ml + Fsbp

Comment 20: L395: is there data of δ114Cd for these different sources? Would it be possible to 
discriminate them? 
Response: As reviewed in Wang et al. (2019)  and Rehkämper et al. (2012) , current data suggest that 
natural Cd sources tend to have  heavier isotopic signatures (δ 114Cd ≈ 0–1‰), whereas anthropogenic 
sources — such as emissions from smelters and industrial processes — often exhibit lighter signatures 
(δ114Cd < 0‰). However, distinguishing between different types of anthropogenic sources (e.g., smelter 
vs. slag) based solely on δ114Cd is challenging, as their isotopic compositions can overlap. The limited 
data available for well-characterized sources reflect the volatility of Cd and the preferential release of 
isotopically light Cd to the atmosphere during anthropogenic activities. A dditional high -precision 
isotope measurements from defined sources are needed to possibly enable  the discrimination of 
different anthropogenic Cd inputs to seawater. No change was made to the text.
