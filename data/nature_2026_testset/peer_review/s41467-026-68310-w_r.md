Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript entitled “Diffractive Magic Cube Network with Super-high Capacity Enabled by Mechanical
Reconfiguration”, the authors proposed a diffractive magic cube network (DMCN). The DMCN is composed of three
cascaded layers and generates thousands of potential channels through permutation, translation, and rotation. Leveraging
the D2NN model, the authors have numerically and experimentally achieved 144-channel holograms, 108-channel single-
focus/multi-focus, and 60-channel OAM beam/comb generation. 
Given the burgeoning demand for optical information processing, the research topic is of importance to boosting the capacity
of optical systems. The proposed DMCN presents an effective paradigm for mechanically reconfigurable metasurfaces,
exhibiting its potential in the fields of optical storage, computing, and communication. The topic is in congruence with
contemporary research trends and is expected to draw the attention of researchers in relevant fields. Overall, this manuscript
can be considered for publication in Nature Communications after a comprehensive major revision to address the noted
issues that require attention. 
1. While the abstract and introduction of this manuscript provide in-depth descriptions of reconfigurable metasurfaces, the
analysis and experimental sections appear to lack the incorporation of any metasurface components. Considering that the
DMCN is put forward as an approach to improve the multiplexing capacity of mechanically reconfigurable metasurfaces, it is
essential to showcase the experimental results applied in metasurfaces for validation. Therefore, I think it is recommended to
supplement the metasurface-based experimental data to render the study more reasonable. 
2. In the experimental results of multi-channel holographic images (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movies), the quality of the
holographic images is not quite satisfactory to me, with many channels barely distinguishable. I suggest that the authors
could conduct a thorough and systematic analysis of the factors contributing to the degradation of image quality and explore
effective strategies for its improvement. 
3. The manuscript states that “the three-layer DMCN has 4179 potential channels, and a five-layer expansion could yield
around 36 million potential channels”. However, in practical scenarios where a satisfactory SNR is guaranteed, the quantity
of multiplexed channels is considerably lower than these theoretical values. A discussion regarding the maximum quantity of
multiplexed channels with a satisfactory SNR employing the proposed approach would be necessary. This would help
readers better understand the trade-offs between channel capacity and SNR, which is crucial for evaluating the feasibility
and efficacy of the DMCN in practical applications. 
4. The schematic of the DMCN system is of a transmissive type (Figure 1), while the experimental implementation utilizes a
reflective system (Figure 5). It would be beneficial to discuss whether the reflective system presents greater advantages over
the transmissive system. 
5. The manuscript states that the achieved system features a lateral alignment error within ± 1.25 μm and a longitudinal
alignment error within ± 0.1 mm. I think it is necessary to elucidate whether this degree of alignment accuracy is sufficient to
apply the proposed DMCN method to reconfigurable metasurfaces. Should this not be the case, the authors are advised to
provide an in-depth discussion of potential strategies for improving the alignment precision.

6. In the study, the optimization of DMCNs exhibiting diverse functions predominantly relies on the design of loss functions,
which necessitates extensive parameter adjustment. It would be beneficial to discuss the design of more universal or self-
adaptive loss functions, with the aim of reducing the burden of parameter tuning and enhancing the efficiency of the
optimization procedure. 
7. It would be insightful to discuss whether the proposed DMCN method possesses the latent potential to be implemented in
the design framework of Fresnel holography to further augment the quantity of multiplexed channels. 
8. The research utilizes a deep diffractive neural network as the network model. I think it is necessary to clarify whether the
deep diffractive neural network differs from the diffractive deep neural network (Ref. 20 and Ref. 22). If they are identical, it is
recommended to employ the same terminology as that utilized in previous studies. 
9. It is noted that some figures (Fig. S1-S3 and Fig. S6-S17) in SI contain multiple sub-figures, yet the corresponding
captions fail to provide individual concise introductions for the content of each respective sub-figure. 
10. It is suggested to explicitly indicate the specific combination cases for each figure or video, thus making their conveyed
information more clarified and readily understandable. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper the authors propose new techniques to increase the multiplexing capability of metaholograms, with the
additional feature of reconfigurability by means of mechanical degrees of freedom (permutations, rotations, translations). To
reduce the cross-talk between the channels they perform a diffractive neural network optimization where three layers are
considered. They provide both numerical experimental validation of their proposal, and they consider different kinds of
multiplexed information: information storage (letter images), focusing manipulation, and OAM multiplexing. The first two
situations deal with the intensity degree of freedom, whereas the third deals with complex amplitude, thus twice the number
of degrees of freedom, which as a result reduces the number of multiplexed channels. 
The paper is interesting and there is a very large amount of work and results. Partly this very large amount of data makes the
paper hard to follow, and it is necessary to re-read it a few times to finally set each step and each kind of simulation in the
broader context. My main criticism though is that, on my opinion, since the experiments have not been done with
metasurfaces, the paper lacks the necessary impact for the journal. I also think that the increase in the number of actually
realizable channels is not that large when compared with references given by the authors. Therefore, my recommendation is
reject. Then, there are other remarks I want to address: 
- to avoid confusion the context of the three kind of simulated results should be better introduced from the beginning: what
justifies enlarge the paper with three different kind of test objects (letters, focusing, OAM)? I can partly imagine, but it is not
that clear when reading the paper. 
- about the mechanical degrees of freedom, it is not clear to me how you will achieve permutations reconfigurability with
metasurfaces. Please, clarify this. 
- on my opinion, ‘super’ (in super-high) or ‘magic’ (in diffractive magic cube network) are not scientific descriptions. Probably
it sells more when reading the title, but I am not sure that this is the way to go with scientific publications. This is more on the
side of the editor to decide. 
- I find it misleading talking about thousands (or millions at the end of the paper) of potential multiplexing channels, when to
avoid cross-talk and have reasonable SNR, in the end about 100 are actually achievable. 
- When talking about the width of the model layer and output FOV, can you indicate the units? I imagine that they are pixel
number. 
- when you are talking about OAM combs, you mean linear combinations of 9 OAM modes equally weighted, is that correct? 
- the number of trainable parameters is the side length of the hologram multiplied by the number of layers? (i.e. 300x300x3) 
- in methods, after Eq.(1) and also before Eq. (3), the reference is [44] instead of [45]? 
- Fig. 3(d): letter to small to read

(Remarks to the Author) 
This article presents a mechanically reconfigurable diffractive optical network. By combining various mechanical operations
with deep learning-based joint optimization, it achieves an unprecedented optical channel capacity. Its performance is
validated across multiple tasks, including holography, focusing, and OAM generation, offering a new approach for next-
generation high-capacity optical information systems. However, after reading the article, the following questions need to be
addressed: 
1. The article supplements experiments with transmissive DOE elements. However, since the unit structures are much larger
than the wavelength, how is the zeroth-order spot handled, and does it affect image measurement? 
2. In the focusing experiment, the focal points are distributed at different positions. So, in the holography and vortex
experiments, are the images formed at the same position? Is there a fundamental crosstalk limit? 
3. The article mentions selecting 144 channels for holography, 108 for focusing, and 60 for OAM from 4179 potential
channels. Why were these specific numbers of channels chosen? Is there a risk of overlooking potentially better channel
combinations? 
4. The rotational operation is limited to four angles (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°). Were finer step angles (e.g., 45°) tested? Would this
yield more channels or merely increase redundancy? 
5. The manuscript needs to more explicitly compare its approach to other recent works for high-capacity multiplexing. The
authors should discuss how their method differs from existing approaches and demonstrate how it offers clear advantages in
terms of quantitate performance, such as isolation, cross-talk, and correlation coefficient matrix. In metasurface design, the
following articles may provide insights for this work: 10.1126/sciadv.abc0711, 10.1038/s41377-023-01140-3,
10.1038/s41467-024-50586-5, 10.1002/adma.202304161.

Authors Response 1: 
We thank Referee #1 for the suggestion regarding the inclusion of metasurface -based experiments.  Our 
DMCN model establishes a system transfer function for the D 2NN framework based on its forward propagation 
process. Guided by this transfer function, we systematically introduced three types of mechanical reconfiguration 
strategies into the D 2NN framework, enabling th e generation of a large number of distinct mechanical states. 
Combined with  tailored loss function design  and gradient descent optimization, this model facilitates low -
complexity and high-capacity light manipulation  systems across a variety of scenarios.  Recognizing the 
prominence of metasurfaces in free -space optical modulation research, our original manuscript adopted 
metasurface multiplexing and reconfiguration as the research background to motivate our work. However, 
considering that the DMCN model can be implemented by any device capable of pixel-wise phase modulation, we 
have expanded the background discussion in the revised manuscript to include two other widely used phase 
modulation devices—diffractive optical elements (DOEs) and liquid crystal devi ces. For practical reasons related 
to fabrication cost and convenience, we have conducted new experiments using a three -layer transmissive DOE -

based DMCN system across the three representative application scenarios. Compared with the previous SLM -
based experiments, these new DOE-based experiments demonstrate significantly improved performance. 
We further acknowledge the advantages of metasurfaces in terms of flexible control over wavelength and 
polarization, and their richer structural degrees of freedom co mpared to DOEs, which can potentially lead to 
further enhancements in system capacity. Therefore, in  Supplementary Note 3, we provide an additional analysis 
of DMCN implementations based on metasurfaces.  The main distinction between DOEs and metasurfaces in free-
space phase modulation is pixel size: DOEs typically feature pixels larger than ten wavelengths, while 
metasurfaces have subwavelength -scale pixels.  The lateral misalignment  tolerance of DMCN mode l is 
proportional to the modulation pixel size. To e nhance alignment tolerance in metasurface -based systems and 
ensure practical feasibility, a viable strategy is to use supercells, which group multiple subwavelength meta -units 
into larger effective pixels. This approach has also been adopted in cascaded me tasurface multiplexing systems  
reported in references [1] and [2]. For instance, consider a metasurface-based DMCN operating at a wavelength of 
532 nm, with unit cells measuring 400 nm and arranged in a 4000 × 4000 metaatom grid (Ref erence [3] has 
demonstrated metasurfaces with 5000 × 5000 pixels at visible wavelengths, making this configuration feasible for 
fabrication). By grouping the unit cells into 10 × 10 supercells, each modulation pixel effectively becomes 4 µm in 
size, forming a 400 × 400 pixel grid. The total longitudinal system length is set to 25 cm. Under this configuration, 
alignment can be achieved using the same differential -head translation stages described in the main text. The slot 
structure illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 27 can also be employed to assist in mechanical alignment.

2. In the experimental results of multi -channel holographic images (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movies), the

Authors Response 2: 
We sincerely thank Referee #1 for the constructive suggestion on improving the quality of the experimental 
images. Indeed, compared to numerical simulations, the image quality in our SLM -based experiments significantly 
degrades. Therefore, we have included  a detailed analysis in the supplementary materials of the original 
manuscript, identifying five primary sources of experimental error: (1) lateral alignment, (2) longitudinal 
alignment, (3) non -ideal planar wavefront of the incident beam, (4) tilted propa gation of the laser between the 
SLM and the reflective mirror, and (5) phase modulation errors of the SLM, along with the corresponding 
mitigation strategies. 
First, regarding alignment errors, we analyzed their impact on image quality in Response Figure 1. Here, ∆z₁, 
∆z₂, and ∆z₃ represent the longitudinal misalignment of layers 1, 2, and 3, respectively; ∆x₂, ∆y₂ and ∆x₃, ∆y₃ 
correspond to the lateral misalignments of layers 2 and 3. It can be observed that when the longitudinal 
misalignment is within ±0.5 mm and the lateral misalignment is within ±4.17 μm, the degradation in image quality 
is minor, and the characters remain clearly visible. During the experiments, we iteratively corrected the setup to

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

ensure the longitudinal alignment error was within ±0. 1 mm and the lateral alignment error within ±1.25 μm. 
Therefore, alignment errors were sufficiently suppressed and had a negligible impact on imaging performance.  
Second, for the tilted propagation of the laser, we also incorporated a tilted propagation model during the training 
of the network. Hence, this error source was effectively compensated and had minimal impact on the imaging 
quality. 
However, the errors caused by the non -ideal planar wavefront of the incident beam were not sufficiently 
addressed. Due to spatial constraints on the optical table, the laser and SLM were fixed at opposite corners, and the 
incident beam was directed onto the SLM through a complex multi -reflection optical path, whose effective 
propagation distance was difficult to measu re accurately. Moreover, as the three reflective regions on the SLM 
were located in close proximity, it was not feasible to expand the beam size further to approximate a planar 
wavefront. As a result, there might still be a mismatch between the Gaussian be am used during training and the 
actual incident Gaussian beam. In Response Figure 2 , we compared the performance under planar wave and 
Gaussian beam illumination. The network was trained with an ideal planar wave, and the input Gaussian beam had 
a diameter  of 2.25 mm and propagated over a distance of 3 000 mm. The image quality under Gaussian beam 
illumination decreased noticeably, with increased background noise in the gaps of the letters. This type of error can 
be alleviated by placing the laser and SLM cl oser together, such that the phase of incident wave becomes 
approximately uniform, and by accurately measuring the beam diameter using a camera. 
Finally, the most significant source of error stems from phase modulation inaccuracies of the SLM, which are 
primarily due to limitations of the device and are difficult to calibrate. In our SLM -based experiments, the laser 
was modulated three times by three separate regions of the SLM, each with a size of 300 × 300 pixels. Given that 
the total resolution of our SL M was 1024 × 1272 pixels, this meant nearly the entire modulation area was used. 
However, the SLM surface was not perfectly flat, introducing additional phase shifts caused by surface height 
variations among the regions. Although the manufacturer provided a phase compensation map measured under a 
20° incident angle, this did not match our experimental setup, which used a 3.5° incident angle. In practice, 
loading this compensation map degraded the image quality further. Additional evidence of the SLM's surfa ce 
unevenness was observed through the vertical pixel coordinates of the three reflection spots on the SLM: 650, 655, 
and 638, respectively. On a flat surface, the beam would either consistently shift upward or downward through 
multiple reflections. Howeve r, the observed behavior —first shifting down and then up —indicates added angular 
deviation caused by surface unevenness. In Response Figure 3 , we simulated the impact of this error on imaging 
quality by subtracting the corresponding subregions of the compensation phase map from the original phase layers 
of the model. The resulting image quality degraded significantly, with the letter 'D' becoming barely recognizable. 
The figure also shows that the central region of the SLM has lower phase deviation and  is relatively flat. Thus, this 
type of error has a negligible impact in typical single -reflection setups. To mitigate this error, we suggest using 
three separate SLMs and ensuring that only the central region of each device is used for modulation. 
In the revised version of our manuscript, we adopted a DOE -fabricated optical element for sample generation 
and expanded the laser beam to ensure collimated planar wavefront illumination. These changes effectively 
suppressed the aforementioned sources of error, resulting in a notable improvement in imaging quality.

Authors Response 3: 
We sincerely thank Referee #1 for the insightful suggestion regarding the trade -off between the practically 
achievable maximum number of channels and image quality. In the original manuscript, we demonstrated the 
scalability of our model through Fig. 6a , w hich analyzed the relationship between the number of channels and 
image PSNR under fixed layer width and FOV width. However, the original figure only presented numerical 
values. To more clearly illustrate the practically achievable maximum number of channe ls, we have plotted a new 
set of curves, as shown in Response Figure 4. Response Figure. 4a shows the relationship between PSNR and the 
number of channels under fixed layer width and FOV width. Although PSNR decreases as the number of channels 
increases, the letter images remain recognizable even when the number of channels increases to 576 —four times 
that of 144. At this point, the average PSNR is 16.5 dB, and the average PCC is 0.892. If this quality level is taken 
as the boundary for satisfactory performance, our model can support up to 576-channel holography. 
Response Figure. 4b illustrates the relationship between PSNR and layer width under fixed FOV width and 
channel number. When the layer width increases from 400 to 600, the ratio of layer width to FOV width increases 
from 2 to 3, resulting in a PSNR improvement from 23.27 dB to 25.05 dB. Moreover, our model was trained using 
a loss function that includes an imaging efficiency regularization term. During optimization, enhancing imaging 
efficiency and improving PSNR are often conflicting objectives (see detailed analysis in Supplementary Note 2). 
If the efficiency regularization term is removed and the model is optimized solely based on  MSE loss, the DMCN 
can achieve a mean PCC of 0.96 and  mean PSNR of 18.6 dB for 576 -channel letter holography. However, the 
average imaging efficiency in this case drops to only 0.06%. Response Figure 5  presents several channels from 
the optimized 576 -channel configuration, showing improved image quality under thi s condition. In summary, by 
further increasing the ratio of layer width to FOV width and lowering the system threshold efficiency parameter 
𝜂𝑇ℎ in the efficiency regularization term, the maximum achievable number of channels for DMCN can be further 
increased.  
Finally, we would like to emphasize that the statement in the discussion section of the original manuscript —
"the three -layer DMCN has 4,179 potential channels, and a five -layer expansion could yield around 36 million 
potential channels"—was primarily intended to illustrate the potential of DMCN for holographic encryption. Our 
model generates a vast number of optimizable channels by combining three types of mechanical operations. These 
channels indeed cannot be fully utilized to achieve high -quality i maging. However, from the encryption 
perspective, it is extremely difficult for a user without any prior knowledge to traverse  thousands of or millions of 
mechanical configurations to find around 3% optimized channles, especially considering the alignment precision 
required in optical setups. In contrast, a user who knows the valid channel parameters can efficiently retrieve the 
valid information using the practical slot discussed in Supplementary Note 3. Similar to conventional

the original object. Under this topic , a compressed sensing -based reconstruction framework [1] can provide new

images to reconstruct the object from fewer sampling points using compressed sensing algorithms.

ratio can effectively enhance model capacity. Inspired by the above framework, one potential approach is to first

Authors Response 2: 
We thank Referee #4 for the insightful question regarding the advantages of our DMCN model. Multi-channel 
multiplexing optimization methods primarily include two types: Gerchberg -Saxton (GS) algorithms and gradient 
descent algorithms. The GS algorithm is an iterative phase retrieval method known for its rapid convergence. It 
alternately applies Fourier transforms between two planes to recover the phase distributions. GS algorithms is 
usually used in early orthogonal multiplexing channels, where the modulation coefficients of each orthogonal 
channel can be independently controlled. Multiplexing can be achieved by independently optimizing the 
modulation coefficients for each channel. References [1] and [ 2] modified the GS algorithm by introducing 
interdependent loops for multiple channels and successfully demonstrated joint optimization of 2 and 3 channels. 
However, when the number of jointly optimized channels increases (e.g., to 10 channels), t he frequent interaction 
required among different loops significantly increases the algorithmic complexity and degrades the optimization 
performance. Thus, GS becomes impractical for large-scale joint channel optimization. 
In contrast, gradient descent algo rithms construct loss functions with modulation coefficients as learnable 
parameters and iteratively update them using gradients. Although these algorithms generally require longer 
iteration times than GS, they offer a simple and unified framework for join t optimization across multiple channels 
and can be applied to any differentiable loss function. References  [3]-[5] adopted gradient descent to jointly 
optimize mechanically reconfigurable channels of cascaded metasurfaces. However, these studies were limited to a 
small number of reconfigurable channels. This is due to the extremely short interlayer distances, which restrict the 
interlayer diffraction connectivity. For instance, in reference [3], the interlayer distance is 100𝜇m, with each layer 
having 62 pixels ( 8𝜇m pixel size) and operating at 800 nm wavelength, yielding a connection degree of 0.0101. In 
reference [4], the interlayer distance is also 100𝜇 m, with 80 pixels ( 5𝜇 m pixel size) and a 740 nm wavelength, 
resulting in a connection degree of 0.0186.  Reference [ 5] assumes an idealized scenario with zero interlayer 
spacing, thereby eliminating any effective diffraction coupling between layers.  These extremely short distances 
mean that interlayer diffraction is neglig ible, and the two modulation layers effectively act as a single layer (by 
simply summing their phases). This not only improves inter-channel correlation in mechanical reconfiguration, but 
also significantly limits the system’s degrees of freedom  (e.g., swa pping layer positions does not result in new 
channels). 
Reference [6] increased the interlayer spacing to ensure adequate diffraction connectivity and also adopted 
gradient descent to optimize reconfigurable channels generated by two rotational circular layers. This strategy 
allowed a moderate increase in the number of mechanical reconfiguration channels. However, it still has several

limitations: 1. Single-type mechanical reconfiguration (rotation) leads to increased correlation among channels as 
the number grows, making the system more suitable for generating continuous, similar holograms.  2. The use of

We appreciate referee #4's comments regarding efficiency and image quality. In the manuscript, the image 
quality is assessed by comparing the target holograms with the model-generated holograms using PSNR and PCC 
as evaluation metrics.  Our DMCN model is constructed based on the D 2NN framework, where the phase 
coefficients are optimized using a gradient descent algorithm. However, in D 2NN-based models, the output optical 
field quality (i.e., the difference between the output field and the target field) and the system transmission 
efficiency typically present a trade-off. From the perspective of model structure, the D2NN framework is composed 
of alternating phase m odulation layers and diffraction propagation layers. The phase modulation layer performs 
point-wise multiplication in the spatial domain, which is equivalent to convolution in the frequency domain 
(spatial Fourier transform). Since the phase modulation coe fficients are learnable parameters determined by the 
target optical field, the spectrum of the output field will contain the target information and be broadened through 
convolution. The diffraction propagation layer, acting as a spatial filter, performs hi gh-frequency filtering in the 
spectral domain. This process can be  physically illustrated by the fact that the high -frequency components of the 
modulated output field—corresponding to the large tilt angle plane wave components —cannot be captured by the 
next modulation layer and instead dissipate into the surrounding environment.  Therefore, the D 2NN framework 
essentially consists of a repetitive interplay between spectral broadening induced by convolution and high -
frequency filtering. Since the phase modulation layer is a passive layer, it can ideally ensure that the input and 
output field energies are equal. However, the filtering effect of the diffraction propagation layer introduces energy 
loss. To ensure sufficient optical field quality in the optimized D 2NN framework, the learned phase modulation 
parameters need to ensure adequat e filtering of the output field by the diffraction propagation layers to suppress 
high-frequency noise components, thus reducing system efficiency.

regularization term to ensure that the system transmission efficiency does not drop too low. In Response Figure 2,

1. The article supplements experiments with transmissive DOE elements. However, since the unit structures are 
much larger than the wavelength, how is the zeroth-order spot handled, and does it affect image measurement? 
We thank Reviewer #4 for the question r egarding zeroth-order diffraction. In our experiments, the DMCN 
model did not produce any zeroth -order diffraction, and the measured optical fields closely match the simulated 
ones. This is because, in all three application scenarios, the DMCN directly manipulates the 2D field distribution 
on the output plane (z = 60 cm, real space). During experiments, the target field  was directly captured by a camera 
with pixel size of 3.5 μm * 3.5 μm, without requiring additional lens components. Consequently, the experimental 
measurements are fully consistent with the simulations, and zeroth-order spot does not affect the results. 
Zeroth-order spot typically occurs in far-field holography, where the wavefront modulator controls the spatial 
Fourier spectrum of the holographic pattern  and a lens is used for Fourier -transform-based displaying. 
Unmodulated stray light can converge through the lens and produce a high -intensity zeroth-order spot at the image 
center. The DMCN can also be extended to Fourier -space field manipulation by applying a Fourier transform to 
the output of Layer 3 (L3 in Response Figure . 1) during forward propagation, followed by the same gradient -
descent optimization. In this case, the inter -layer spacing degrees of freedom remain unchanged, wi th d1 and d2 
still tunable. If the target field is a hologram, reconfigurable far -field holography can be realized; if the target is a 
focal spot, reconfigurable beam deflection can be achieved. 
To avoid zeroth-order spot in far-field holography, the target hologram can be positioned in a high -frequency 
region of Fourier space, away from the central zero frequency. In this configuration, unmodulated light still 
converges at the center, while the target hologram is deflected away, eliminating the impact of z eroth-order 
diffraction.

2. In the focusing experiment, the focal points are distributed at different positions. So, in the holography and 
vortex experiments, are the images formed at the same position? Is there a fundamental crosstalk limit?  
We thank Reviewer #4 for the question regarding the spatial positions of the holograms and vortex fields. Our 
144-channel hologram and 60 -channel vortex fields are both set on the same p lane (z = 60 cm) within the same 
FOV region. The DMCN model employs the phase coefficients of multi -layer DOEs as trainable parameters, with 
sufficiently large inter-layer spacings to ensure diffraction connectivity. This allows a planar wavefront to undergo 
effective diffraction –modulation transformations within the DMCN, facilitating the joint optimization of high -
capacity channels. Moreover, the combination of three types of mechanical reconfiguration operations m inimizes 
correlations between the system transfer functions of different channels. As a result, the well-trained DMCN  
model can generate high-capacity, high-quality target fields at the same spatial positions without requiring spatial 
multiplexing to avoid crosstalk. 
For multi-channel holograms, we did not analyze inter-channel crosstalk using mutual correlation coefficients 
because the target holographic patterns, such as letter images or the CIFAR -10 grayscale images in 
Supplementary Fig. 33, inherently contain certain mutual correlations. Instead, we evaluate imaging quality using 
PSNR and PCC for each channel, and the high -quality imaging results demonstrate that crosstalk between 
channels is negligible. 
Response Figure. 2 presents the corr elation-coefficient matrix of the 144 -channel holograms. Both the 
simulated and experimental results exhibit low inter -channel crosstalk, with average off -diagonal values of 0.21 
and 0.23, respectively. The simulated and experimental results of reconfigurable vortex beam generation shown in 
Fig. 4 of the manuscript also show that the channels are free from inter -layer crosstalk. The generated 51 -channel 
OAM fields of different orders exhibit near-zero mutual correlation, demonstrating good orthogonality. The 
experimentally measured interference patterns also display clear differentiation, accurately corresponding to the 
topological charges of each channel. Besides, although the multi -channel focal spots are located at different 
positions, the focusing function is realized by optimizing the field over the entire FOV such that energy is 
concentrated at the target points while approaching zero elsewhere. Thus, crosstalk can also be analyzed from the 
focusing energy distributions of each channel. Both the 2D and 1D field distributions in Fig. 3 indicate that each 
channel concentrates energy only at its designated target position, with no leakage to other positions, further 
confirming that no crosstalk occurs.  
In summary, the DMCN model does not require spatial multiplexing to suppress inter-channel crosstalk. This 
allows the camera to capture the target fields at fixed positions, avoiding the substantial pixel inefficiency or the 
need for camera repositioning that would otherwise arise in spatial multiplexing.

3. The article mentions selecting 144 channels for holography, 108 for focusing, and 60 for OAM from 4179 
potential channels. Why were these specific numbers of channels chosen? Is there a risk of overlooking potentially 
better channel combinations? 
We thank Reviewer #4 for the insightful question regarding the selection of channel  numbers and 
combinations. When the number of trainable parameters and the FOV size  in the DMCN model  are fixed , the 
achievable number of reconfigurable channels is constrained by the trade-off with two factors —the reconstructed 
field quality and the transmission efficiency —both of which decrease as the number of channels increases (see the 
Discussion part and Supplementary Note 2  for details). Our final choice of 144, 108, and 60 channels reflects a 
balanced compromise among these competing factors. 
On one hand, the transmission efficiency must not be too low . Otherwise, the measured field quality would 
significantly degrade in ex periments due to the low-SNR detection of the camera. Following Ref. 1, where a 55 -
channel holography system operated at an average efficiency of 10%, we set a target efficiency of 3% –4.5% for 
our hundred-channel models. (For OAM generation, the optimization of both amplitude and phase of the complex 
field can be considered equivalent to generating 60×2 channels.) On the other hand, we also require that all output 
fields maintain sufficiently high quality to clearly demonstrate that the DMCN effectively suppresses crosstalk and 
significantly enhances system capacity. Therefore, the number of channels for holography and OAM generation 
was determined under the constraint of achieving PCC ≈ 1. For focusing, we ensured accurate single-spot focusing 
and uniform power distribution of dual-spot focusing. Based on these considerations, we arrived at the 144, 108, 
and 60 channel settings. If the efficiency or field -quality requirement is relaxed, the achievable channel counts can 
be increased further. 
To rule out the possibility of better -performing channel combinations among the 4179 potential candidates, 
we conducted analyses from two complementary perspectives, as detailed in Supplementary Note 4  and 
Supplementary Note 6 . First, by evaluating the PSNR of multiplexed channels under each type of mechanical 
reconfiguration, we derived the priority rules for channel selection. For interlayer spacing, channels with larger 
interlayer spacings are more amenable to optimization. For rotation, rotation of the second layer should be 
prioritized for generating multiplexed channels. For permutation, full permutations of all three layers should be 
preferred for channel construction. Based on these criteria, we selected the channel comb inations shown in Fig. 2 
of the manuscript for optimization.  In Supplementary Note 6 , we further examined additional channel 
combinations that generated 144 -channel holography (three extra combinations), 108 -channel focusing (two extra 
combinations), and 6 0-channel OAM beams (two extra combinations). These combinations involve different 
mixes of the three mechanical reconfiguration operations, all using three phase -modulation layers with interlayer 
distances ranging from (13, 13, 34) to (21, 21, 18), and ro tation angles arranged according to the rule that the 
second layer rotates first. The simulation results show that the PCC, PSNR, and efficiency of these models are very 
similar to those in the main manuscript, with only slightly lower performance.

4. The rotational operation is limited to four angles (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°). Were finer step angles (e.g., 45°) tested? 
Would this yield more channels or merely increase redundancy? 
We thank Reviewer #4 for the suggestion regarding increasing the rotational degree of freedom. We did not 
consider finer angular steps beyond (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) because such degrees of freedom are redundant. They 
would not only increase the computational burden during model optimization but also reduce the practicality and 
scalability of the hardware implementation. 
Using the 45° step angle as an example, we analyze this redundancy from the following three perspectives: 
(1) Model optimization: Response Fig. 3 shows two adjacent modulation layers with a 45° relative angle. The 
modulation layers may be designed as squares or as regular octagons formed by the intersection of two squares . 
The field inside the square or octagonal region serves as the secondary source for diffraction to the next layer. 
Under a square sub -grid discretization, a 45° rotation leads to misaligned sampling grids between adjacent layers. 
After diffraction , the propagated fie ld must be interpolated using nearest -neighbor sampling before phase 
modulation at the next layer . It will significantly increase the computational cost when optimizing numerous 
channels of a multi -layer DMCN, and also raise the discrepancy between the numerical model and its practical 
implementation. 
(2) Hardware implementation: The 45° step angle would require an additional rotation stage, which not only 
introduces angular adjustment errors but also complicates the system. This makes it incompatible with our slot -
based mechanical structure (see Supplementary Fig. 26 ) thereby hindering the implementation of the three types 
of mechanical operations. 
(3) Hardware scalability: Under linearly polarized plane -wave illumination, a 45° rotation requires the 
modulation units to be isotropic. Otherwise, the polarization conversion at each unit must be described using Jones 
matrices, further increasing the computational complexity of the forward propagation.  In contrast, The  90° step 
setting can accommodate for any polarization and any type of modulation units. 
Finally, the 90° step angle, combined with the other two types of mechanical reconfiguration, already 
provides a sufficiently large set of potential channels for DMCN optimization. Although finer angular increments 
could in principle introduce more potential channels, those channels would exhibit higher mutual correlation than 
those created by 90° increments. Consequently, they would not provide channels superior to those generated with a 
90° step angle and would not effectively increase the number of achievable channels.

We appreciate Reviewer#4’s constructive suggestion s on comparing our work with prior studies and the 
insightful references provided.  In the Introduction of the manuscript, we categorize existing high -capacity 
approaches into optical multiplexing strategies that rely on the intrinsic properties of light, a nd reconfigurable 
strategies that generate multiple optical fields through system -level reconstructions. Among recent high -capacity 
multiplexing strategies, metasurfaces are widely used for wavelength and polarization multiplexing because of 
their unique a dvantages in spectral/polarization control and their rich design degrees of freedom  (DOF)24,27,46–50. 
These works typically start from the physical expressions describing the wavelength and polarization responses of 
meta-atoms, from which the potential DOF can be identified. A meta -atom library is then constructed through 
FDTD sweeping. Finally, high-capacity meta-devices are designed either through physics -driven models—such as 
dispersion-dependent Jones matrices, quadratic phase profiles, or vortex wavefr onts—or through optimization 
algorithms including evolutionary methods and gradient -based methods. The abundant DOF of the meta -atoms 
enable a single metasurface layer to accommodate a large number of designable and optimizable parameters, 
effectively concentrating multi -dimensional control into one layer. However, as the multiplexing dimensionality 
increases, the impact of modeling errors caused by inter-cell coupling among meta-atoms with different geometric 
parameters becomes more significant  because the output optical fields of different channels become increasingly 
sensitive to such errors. It ultimately degrades the experimentally generated field quality. 
In contrast, our work takes a system -level mechanical-reconstruction perspective. We construct a large 
number of non -orthogonal reconfigurable channels by coarsely reconfiguring the relative positions of multiple 
diffractive layers, without relying on the multidimensional physical properties of lig ht itself. Therefore, our 
strategy only requires the diffractive neural network (D 2NN) model to describe the forward propagation of single -
wavelength, single -polarization light through multilayer phase masks. By optimizing the phase profiles with 
gradient descent according to specific task -dependent loss functions, a high -capacity model with diverse
