Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript addresses an important and emerging area of research, linking eating window patterns to cardiovascular
health outcomes in a large pediatric cohort, with integration of lipidomic and proteomic analyses. The work is novel in
extending chrononutrition concepts to childhood cardiovascular risk, and the combination of clinical phenotyping with multi-
omics approaches represents a significant strength. However, while the study is methodologically sound in many aspects,
several major concerns related to statistical rigor, causal interpretation, and the robustness of the omics findings require
revision before the manuscript could be considered suitable for publication. 
Comments 
1. The nested case-control analysis for the omics component, while thoughtfully designed, is based on a relatively small
sample size (n=51). Although the power calculation is acknowledged, the limited sample increases the risk of false positives
and model overfitting, particularly in multi-omics studies. Furthermore, the findings were not validated in an independent
external cohort, which significantly limits the generalizability and robustness of the omics-derived biomarkers. 
2. Although the manuscript applies mixed-effects models and cubic spline analyses, the Methods section lacks information
regarding model diagnostics. It is unclear whether residual analyses, multicollinearity assessments, or model fit evaluations
(e.g., using AIC or BIC) were performed. Furthermore, given the large number of correlated cardiovascular outcomes
analyzed, it would be appropriate to apply multiple testing correction (e.g., FDR adjustment) across these models to control
for false positives. 
3. Clarification is needed regarding the categorization of eating window patterns. The distinction between "Extended EW"
and "Late EW" groups, although described, may still be confusing to readers. A schematic figure or flowchart illustrating the
classification criteria would substantially enhance clarity. 
4. The molecular mechanisms proposed to explain the observed associations should be discussed more cautiously, given
the absence of experimental validation. 
5. Although the study demonstrates strong associations between eating window timing and cardiovascular parameters, the
observational design precludes causal inference. Throughout the abstract, results, and discussion sections, the authors
should avoid causal language and instead frame the findings as associations, emphasizing the hypothesis-generating
nature of the work. 
6. While the study’s focus on a Southeast Chinese pediatric cohort is a valuable strength, claims regarding generalizability
to broader populations should be moderated. Ethnic, cultural, and lifestyle differences can substantially influence dietary
behaviors, circadian biology, and cardiovascular risk profiles. The authors should discuss these limitations more explicitly
and avoid overgeneralization.

REVIEWER COMMENTS 
Reviewer #1: This manuscript addresses an important and emerging area of research, 
linking eating timing patterns to cardiovascular health outcomes in a large pediatric cohort, 
with integration of lipidomic and proteomic analyses. The work is novel in extending 
chrononutrition concepts to childhood cardiovascular risk, and the combination of clinical 
phenotyping with multi -omics approaches represents a significant strength. However, while the 
study is methodologicall y sound in many aspects, several major concerns related to statistical 
rigor, causal interpretation, and the robustness of the omics findings require revision before the 
manuscript could be considered suitable for publication. 
Response: We sincerely thank reviewer for recognizing the novelty and significance of our 
chrononutrition research in pediatric populations, while also providing critical feedback that has 
substantially strengthened our manuscript. We have enhanced our statistical methodology by 
implementing comprehensive model diagnostics including residual analysis, multicollinearity 
assessment, and model fit evaluation. We applied FDR correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg 
method across all cardiovascular outcomes and omics analyses, with all reported p-values now 
clearly indicated as FDR-adjusted. We have also systematically revised the entire manuscript to 
eliminate causal language and appropriately frame our findings as associations rather than causal 
relationships. We acknowledge the limitation of our nested case-control sample size (n=51) and 
have implemented multiple internal validation strategies. We have substantially moderated our 
conclusions from the omics analyses, explicitly framing these findings as exploratory and 
requiring replication in larger, independent cohorts before clinical applications can be considered.  
Comments 
1. The nested case-control analysis for the omics component, while thoughtfully designed, is 
based on a relatively small sample size (n=51). Although the power calculation is 
acknowledged, the limited sample increases the risk of false positives and model overfitting, 
particularly in multi-omics studies. Furthermore, the findings were not validated in an 
independent external cohort, which significantly limits the generalizability and robustness of 
the omics-derived biomarkers. 
Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for raising this fundamental methodological concern, 
and we acknowledge small sample size as a significant limitation that affects the interpretability of 
our molecular findings. We acknowledge that sample size is a critical consideration in multi-omics 
studies, and we have carefully evaluated our approach within the context of existing literature in 
this emerging field. A systematic review of chrononutrition and time-restricted eating multi-omics 
studies reveals that our sample size (n=51) is comparable to or larger than many published studies 
in this specific domain. For instance, Dawson et al. (2024) examined early time-restricted eating 
effects on cardiometabolic health in only 16 healthy adults using controlled feeding conditions[1]; 
Pang et al. (2024) investigated time-restricted feeding effects on metabolic dysfunction -associated 
steatotic liver disease in 19 participants[2]; and Chen et al. (2024) examined time-restricted eating 
effects on immune system and intestinal microbiome in 49 participants[3]. None of these multi-
omics studies included pediatric populations, highlighting the unique contribution and innovation 
of our research in examining eating timing patterns and their molecular consequences in children

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

and adolescents. Our sample size was determined by practical constraints including participant 
burden, ethical considerations for additional blood sampling in children, and funding limitations 
for comprehensive multi-omics analyses. To compensate for the lack of external validation 
cohorts, we implemented multiple rigorous internal validation approaches: (1) Consensus machine 
learning approach using four different algorithms (Random Forest, SVM, XGBoost, Elastic Net), 
considering only metabolites selected by all methods as high-confidence markers; ( 2) Cross-
validation using WGCNA and differential expression analysis for proteomic finding; (3) Stringent 
multiple testing corrections  using FDR adjustment across all omics analyses. We have 
substantially moderated our conclusions from the omics analyses throughout the manuscript. The 
abstract now states our findings "suggest associations" rather than establishing definitive 
mechanisms. We have added prominent discussion emphasizing that "these molecular findings 
represent exploratory associations requiring validation through larger, independent cohorts and 
experimental studies before clinical applications can be considered." The limitations section now 
explicitly acknowledges that "the absence of external validation cohorts represents a significant 
limitation restricting the generalizability of our molecular biomarker findings." 
2. Although the manuscript applies mixed-effects models and cubic spline analyses, the 
Methods section lacks information regarding model diagnostics. It is unclear whether residual 
analyses, multicollinearity assessments, or model fit evaluations (e.g., using AIC or BIC) were 
performed. Furthermore, given the large number of correlated cardiovascular outcomes 
analyzed, it would be appropriate to apply multiple testing correction (e.g., FDR adjustment) 
across these models to control for false positives. 
Response: We completely agree that comprehensive model diagnostics are essential for ensuring 
analytical validity. We have substantially enhanced our statistical methodology section to address 
these critical concerns. All mixed-effects models now undergo comprehensive diagnostic 
evaluation including: (1) Residual analysis to assess normality and homoscedasticity assumptions; 
(2) Variance inflation factor (VIF) assessment to evaluate multicollinearity (all VIF values <3); 
(3) Model fit evaluation using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information 
Criterion (BIC), and likelihood ratio tests. We have created a new Supplementary Table 6 that 
presents complete model fit statistics.  
Regarding multiple testing correction, we have applied FDR correction using the Benjamini-
Hochberg method across all cardiovascular outcomes in our main analysis. All reported P-values 
in tables and figures of results section are FDR-adjusted values, which we now clearly indicate 
with "P.adj" notation throughout the manuscript. This approach provides appropriate control for 
multiple comparisons while maintaining reasonable statistical power (Supplementary Table 1).

3. Clarification is needed regarding the categorization of eating window patterns. The 
distinction between "Extended EW" and "Late EW" groups, although described, may still be 
confusing to readers. A schematic figure or flowchart illustrating the classification criteria 
would substantially enhance clarity. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for pointing out this important clarity issue, and we 
agree that clear categorization criteria are essential for readers to understand and 
potentially replicate our methodology. In the Methods section, we now provide 
detailed definitions with specific examples: "Extended Eating Window (Extended 
EW): eating duration >12.5 hours, regardless of last meal timing (e.g., first meal at 
07:00, last meal at 20:30, duration = 13.5 hours); Late Eating Window (Late EW): 
eating duration ≤12.5 hours with last meal after 20:00 (e.g., first meal at 08:00, last 
meal at 20:30, duration = 12.5 hours); Early Eating Window (Early EW): eating 
duration ≤12.5 hours with last meal no later than 20:00 (e.g., first meal at 07:00, last 
meal at 19:30, duration = 12.5 hours)." 
We have also enhanced our graphical abstract to include a clear flowchart illustrating 
these three distinct eating patterns with visual examples, making the classification 
immediately comprehensible to readers. Additionally, we have added detailed 
justification for our 12.5-hour threshold selection using restricted cubic spline 
analysis with model selection criteria (AIC and BIC), which identified this as the 
primary inflection point in the dose-response relationship.
