Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I congratulate the authors on this clear and elegant manuscript. They describe changes in a targeted panel of 149 proteins
measured using Olink technology, embedded within the comprehensive study design of the HOLBAEK cohort. With this,
they capture systemic and pathophysiological plasma proteome alterations induced by obesity and non-pharmacological
obesity intervention, as well as covariate-driven proteomic changes related to sex, age, and puberty. 
I have only minor suggestions, primarily concerning the display of data, which I hope the authors will consider as friendly
recommendations to further enrich their figures and enhance interpretability. 
Figure 2: 
While the individual volcano plots effectively highlight top-correlating proteins and covariate effect sizes, they do not show
inter-covariate relationships. For example, some proteins (e.g., LEP and GH) appear as significantly associated with both
sex and overweight/obesity, but not in the same direction. To better understand these relationships, it would be helpful to
include scatter plots of β-values across covariates or a heatmap displaying β-values (as color codes) for all proteins across
all covariates, similar in style to the extended data figures. Additional clustering (e.g., hierarchical clustering) could reveal
groups of proteins with shared covariate associations. I believe some of these visualizations, or similar alternatives, could be
accommodated in the main figure and would aid readers in grasping the dataset more intuitively. 
Moreover, the correlation with UK Biobank data is well described and available in a supplementary table. However, for
readers who may not wish to prepare these plots themselves, it would be helpful to include a β-value correlation scatter plot
within the manuscript. 
Figure 3: 
The authors state that there is a more pronounced difference between age groups 1 and 3 in the normal weight vs.
overweight/obesity group. However, this is not immediately apparent from the PLS-DA plots. Would it be possible to quantify
or visualize this separation in a different way to support the statement more clearly? 
Figure 4: 
The predictive model is described as using elastic net regularization, but the methods later mention LASSO regression for
feature selection and reduction to the final three-protein panel. It would be helpful to clarify this point. To my understanding,
feature selection does not itself produce classification performance. It is also unclear what classifier was used after feature
selection—was it logistic regression, elastic net, or another method? Clarifying this pipeline would improve transparency.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study presents a significant investigation into the relationship between pediatric obesity and cardiometabolic risk,
utilizing advanced proteomic techniques to identify potential biomarkers. However, some shortcomings should be
considered. While the study claims to identify biomarkers associated with various cardiometabolic phenotypes, it lacks
specificity regarding the selection criteria for the 149 plasma proteins analyzed. A more detailed explanation of the rationale
behind the choice of these specific proteins would enhance the reader's understanding of their relevance to the study's
objectives. 
The abstract does not provide information on the validation of these biomarkers in independent cohorts, which is crucial for
establishing their robustness and generalizability. 
The use of machine learning to identify a three-protein panel (CDCP1, FGF21, and HAOX1) is a noteworthy approach;
however, the abstract does not elaborate on the machine learning methodology employed. Details regarding the training and
testing datasets, feature selection processes, and potential overfitting issues are essential for assessing the reliability of the
model. 
Furthermore, while the improvement in ROC-AUC from 0.77 to 0.83 is statistically significant, the clinical relevance of this
increase should be discussed. A modest improvement in predictive accuracy may not necessarily translate to meaningful
clinical outcomes. 
The abstract mentions a non-pharmacological obesity intervention and its association with reduced circulating inflammatory
cytokines, yet it fails to provide context regarding the intervention's design and duration. Without this information, it is
challenging to evaluate the intervention's effectiveness and its potential impact on the observed changes in inflammatory
markers and cardiometabolic risk profiles. 
Moreover, the abstract does not address potential confounding factors that may influence the observed associations, such as
genetic predispositions, socioeconomic status, dietary habits, and physical activity levels. These factors are critical in
pediatric populations and could significantly affect both obesity and cardiometabolic outcomes. 
While the findings suggest that circulating proteomic signatures mediate obesity-related cardiometabolic risk, the abstract
lacks any information of the underlying biological mechanisms that may explain these associations. A more comprehensive
exploration of how these proteins interact within metabolic pathways would provide valuable insights into the
pathophysiology of pediatric obesity and its comorbidities. 
The paper presents a compelling overview of the relationship between pediatric obesity and its implications for
cardiometabolic health, emphasizing the role of systemic inflammation and disrupted metabolic pathways. The authors
effectively highlight the significance of their research in the context of existing literature, noting the limitations of prior studies
in pediatric populations. This acknowledgment sets a solid foundation for the relevance of their findings. 
The use of advanced proteomics technology, particularly mass-spectrometry (MS) and proximity extension assay (PEA)
methods, is well-articulated. The authors successfully convey the innovative aspects of their study, particularly in quantifying
a substantial number of plasma proteins in a large cohort of children and adolescents. This methodological rigor enhances
the credibility of their findings and underscores the potential for identifying novel biomarkers of cardiometabolic dysfunction. 
The comparison of their results with those from adult populations, such as the BAMSE cohort and the UK Biobank, is a
strong point in the text. It not only contextualizes the findings within a broader framework but also suggests that the
mechanisms underlying obesity-related cardiometabolic risk may be conserved across different age groups. The
identification of age-specific associations, particularly concerning proteins related to bone metabolism, adds a valuable
dimension to the discussion, indicating that the impact of obesity may vary significantly during different developmental
stages. 
However, while the text provides a thorough overview of the findings and their implications, it could benefit from a more
detailed discussion of the potential biological mechanisms linking obesity to changes in circulating protein markers.
Additionally, the authors might consider addressing the clinical implications of their findings more explicitly, particularly how
these biomarkers could be utilized in early intervention strategies for pediatric obesity. 
Overall, the text is well-structured and presents a significant contribution to the field of pediatric obesity research. The
integration of proteomic data with clinical outcomes offers a promising avenue for future research, and the authors are
encouraged to continue exploring the intricate relationships between obesity, inflammation, and metabolic health in children
and adolescents. 
The text provides a detailed examination of the protein signatures associated with cardiometabolic risk patterns, presenting
a nuanced understanding of the relationships between specific proteins and various metabolic traits. The identification of
proteins such as HAOX1, ACE2, and CDCP1 as strong positive predictors of plasma ALT levels is particularly noteworthy,
as these proteins have been previously linked to adult metabolic dysfunction, specifically MASLD. This connection
underscores the relevance of the findings in both pediatric and adult populations, suggesting potential pathways for further
investigation. 
The discussion of lipid-related traits, particularly the association of PRSS8 with triglyceride levels, is well-supported by
references to genome-wide association studies. This integration of genetic data enhances the credibility of the findings and
provides a solid foundation for understanding the role of PRSS8 in lipid metabolism. The divergent pattern observed for

follistatin (FS) is intriguing, as it highlights the complexity of cardiometabolic interactions. The inverse association of FS with
glycemic traits, contrasted with its positive correlation with other risk factors, suggests that FS may play a multifaceted role in
metabolic regulation, a notion supported by previous Mendelian randomization analyses. 
The exploration of inflammation-related phenotypes is another strong aspect of the text. The significant associations
between high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and white blood cell (WBC) counts with LOX1, a protein linked to
ischemic stroke, provide valuable insights into the inflammatory processes underlying cardiometabolic risk. The authors
effectively connect their findings to prior research, including their own work on LOX1 in pediatric obesity, reinforcing the
relevance of inflammation in this context. 
Overall, the text presents a comprehensive analysis of protein signatures related to cardiometabolic risk, effectively
integrating findings from various studies to build a cohesive narrative. The authors are encouraged to further explore the
mechanistic pathways underlying these associations and consider the clinical implications of their findings in the context of
pediatric obesity and cardiometabolic health. 
The text provides a critical reflection on the limitations and strengths of the study, offering a balanced perspective on the
findings related to proteomic profiling in pediatric obesity. The acknowledgment of the cohort's predominantly European
ancestry is an important consideration, as it highlights the need for caution in generalizing the results to more diverse
populations. This limitation is significant in the context of obesity research, where genetic and environmental factors can
vary widely across different ethnic groups. 
The authors rightly point out that the targeted proteomics approach may have restricted the analysis to proteins captured by
the Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) panels, potentially overlooking other relevant biomarkers. This limitation underscores
the importance of employing comprehensive proteomic strategies that can capture a broader spectrum of proteins, which
may be critical for understanding the complex biological pathways involved in obesity and its comorbidities. 
The omission of genetic predispositions and lifestyle factors, such as diet and physical activity, is another notable limitation.
These factors are known to significantly influence circulating protein levels and metabolic health, and their absence in the
analysis may lead to an incomplete understanding of the observed associations. The authors' caution regarding the
relatively small number of hepatic steatosis cases is warranted, as this could impact the robustness of the predictive model's
performance and the generalizability of the findings. 
The lack of a control group in the longitudinal analysis is a critical point that limits the ability to draw causal inferences about
the impact of the obesity management intervention. This limitation is particularly relevant in intervention studies, where
establishing causality is essential for understanding the effectiveness of treatment strategies. 
The study limitations are well addressed , the authors effectively highlight the strengths of their study, particularly the
inclusion of deeply phenotyped cohorts of children and adolescents from both clinical and population-based settings. The
integration of cross-sectional and intervention data, along with comparisons to an adult cohort, enhances the study's overall
robustness and relevance. 
In conclusion, the paper emphasizes the potential of proteomic profiling to identify novel circulating biomarkers associated
with pediatric obesity and its cardiometabolic comorbidities. The identification of a three-protein panel for hepatic steatosis
as a promising non-invasive diagnostic tool is a significant contribution to the field, suggesting ways for earlier detection and
intervention.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
1. The paper has insufficient discussion of causality in the association between 
protein markers and cardiometabolic risks. The authors could explore potential 
causal relationships using methods such as longitudinal analyses with longer 
follow-up periods. This would help clarify whether changes in protein markers 
directly contribute to the development of cardiometabolic diseases or are merely 
secondary manifestations of obesity and its related complications. 
 
Response:  
We agree that understanding whether protein markers contribute to cardiometabolic risk 
is important. Our study includes a longitudinal component; however, it is limited by 
having only two measurement points and a relatively short follow-up (median 1.1 years, 
IQR 1.0–1.2), which restricts our ability to determine whether changes in protein levels 
precede subsequent changes in risk. Establishing causality would require additional 
types of studies beyond the scope of this work, such as advanced Mendelian 
randomization analyses or experimental approaches. We have revised the discussion to 
clarify this limitation. 
 
Revised text (Discussion, page 22, lines 505-510):  
“Furthermore, the longitudinal analysis, with only two time points and a relatively short 
follow-up (median 1.1 years), limits our ability to establish temporality, whether changes 
in protein levels due to the obesity management treatment, precede changes in 
cardiometabolic risk. Determining causal relationships would require follow-up studies, 
using methods such as Mendelian randomization.” 
 
2. Individual variability, liking baseline protein marker levels, genetic background, 
lifestyle habits, and intervention compliance, in protein marker responses to 
obesity interventions should be further explored. 
 
Response:  
Inter-individual variability in proteomic responses to obesity interventions may indeed 
reflect genetic background, baseline protein levels, lifestyle behaviors, and adherence to 
treatment. However, investigating genetic contributions is beyond the scope of this paper 
and is the focus of our separate work on the genetic architecture of circulating proteins 
(https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.03.18.25324169; PMID: 39972214), where we show that 
genetics explains approximately 11% of protein variance, more than age, sex, and 
obesity combined. Recent evidence also indicates that lifestyle factors account for a

relatively small proportion of protein variation compared to non-modifiable factors such 
as genetics, age, and sex (PMID: 39327534). Unfortunately, we lack detailed adherence 
metrics beyond response status (BMI SDS reduction), limiting further exploration of this 
aspect. 
 
Revised text (Discussion, page 21, lines 497-503):  
“We did not account for genetic predispositions45, lifestyle factors such as diet and 
physical activity46, or treatment adherence, all of which may influence changes in 
circulating protein levels in response to obesity interventions. Recent evidence suggests 
that lifestyle factors explain only a small fraction of protein variation compared to non-
modifiable factors, including genetics (PMID: 39327534). However, understanding these 
sources of individual variability could help identify subgroups more likely to benefit and 
guide personalized treatment strategies.” 
3. Insufficient integration of multi-omics data to elucidate the molecular mechanisms 
of pediatric obesity. 
Response:  
While the present study focuses on proteomics, we agree that multi-omics integration is 
critical for understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying pediatric obesity. This 
work is beyond the scope of the current manuscript, but is the focus of ongoing 
complementary studies. For example, we recently reported genetic influences on the 
circulating proteome using Olink and mass spectrometry-based platforms 
(https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.03.18.25324169; PMID: 39972214) and investigated 
proteomic–lipidomic interactions in the same population (PMID: 39304782). 
Notably, Huang et al. (PMID: 39304782) demonstrated strong correlations between 
ceramides and Olink proteins, including the three proteins predictive of steatotic liver 
disease in the present study (CDCP1, FGF21, and HAOX1). These observations 
suggest shared molecular pathways linking lipid metabolism, protein biomarkers, and 
obesity-related cardiometabolic risk. 
Revised text (Discussion, page 21, lines 481–490): 
"Previous work from our group identified a circulating lipid panel predictive of SLD with 
similar performance to the present protein-based model, suggesting that combining 
proteomics and lipidomics could further improve risk prediction. Importantly, ceramides, 
key lipids associated with cardiometabolic risk, correlated with the three proteins in our 
predictive panel, highlighting potential interconnected proteomic–lipidomic pathways in 
metabolic regulation. Future research integrating proteomics, lipidomics, and genomics 
may help disentangle causal relationships and reveal underlying mechanisms of obesity-
related cardiometabolic risk.”
