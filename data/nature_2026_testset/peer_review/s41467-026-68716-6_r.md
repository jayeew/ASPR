Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
First, I want to congratulate the authors for designing and completing a clinical trial in this rare sarcoma type. This
manuscript reflects a scientifically sound design. The study is well designed and clearly written, and the data are presented
with appropriate subgroup stratification. 
• What are the noteworthy results? 
This manuscript presents the results of a phase 1b/2 trial assessing olutasidenib, a selective mIDH1 inhibitor, in patients
with advanced or metastatic IDH1-mutant chondrosarcomas (Cohort 3a, single-arm). The authors detail data on disease
control rates, progression-free survival, overall survival, safety, and pharmacodynamic effects. The study aims to address a
significant unmet need in sarcoma treatment and offers prospective clinical evidence in a rare, molecularly defined patient
subset. 
Notable findings include SD in 52% of response-evaluable patients and a median OS of 16.0 months, with 75% of patients
alive at 6 months. One patient maintained SD for over four years via a compassionate use program, as clearly stated in the
discussion. Olutasidenib had an acceptable safety profile. Gastrointestinal adverse events, fatigue, and elevated liver

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

enzymes were the most frequently reported treatment-related effects, which is consistent with the data presented in Table 2
and the Safety section. 
• Will the work be of significance to the field and related fields? How does it compare to the established literature? If the work
is not original, please provide relevant references. 
These results are significant in the field. Currently, there are no FDA-approved medications for patients with advanced or
metastatic chondrosarcoma, and therapeutic options are limited. The results are in line with those reported for ivosidenib in
chondrosarcoma (Tap et al., JCO 2020), but this study expands on that work by providing overall survival data,
pharmacodynamic data on 2-HG, and including patients with dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma. 
• Does the work support the conclusions and claims, or is additional evidence needed? 
Yes, the data support the conclusions in the manuscript. The authors appropriately interpret the disease control observed in
the context of the known limitations of RECIST in this setting. 
• Are there any flaws in the data analysis, interpretation and conclusions? Do these prohibit publication or require revision? 
I did not detect significant flaws in the data analysis. 
However, I did note one minor issue. In the cCS subgroup (n=16), the manuscript states that 10 of 16 had SD (63%) and 6 of
16 had PD (38%). These add up to 101%, which appears to be a rounding issue. This is a minor point and does not affect
interpretation. 
• Is the methodology sound? Does the work meet the expected standards in your field? 
The methodology is appropriate. Eligibility criteria, PK/PD analysis, and RECIST-based endpoints are clearly presented.
The use of a Simon’s 2-stage design is justified in the context of a rare cancer. 
• Is there enough detail provided in the methods for the work to be reproduced? 
Yes, the methods section includes sufficient information for replication. Eligibility, dosing, safety monitoring, and response
assessments are well described. 
In conclusion, this is a timely and well-executed study that adds valuable information to the limited literature on systemic
therapy for chondrosarcoma. While the small sample size precludes statistical significance, It would be interesting to explore
any association between specific IDH1 mutation subtypes and response, as this could offer insight into patient selection.
Similarly, more details on the relationship between 2-HG reduction and clinical outcomes would add value. 
Lastly, the study raises an important point about the limitations of RECIST in chondrosarcoma. Given the prolonged SD
observed in some patients, it would be helpful to know whether clinical or functional improvement (e.g., pain, mobility) was
observed. Quality of life data, if collected, would be highly relevant and could strengthen the overall impact of the
manuscript.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This is an interesting compound, mostly because it has entered into phase III. 
Major: However, I am less impressed by the results as this is a single arm study and the mPFS is short and 
no responses. I would only be impressed when the pretreatment progression was known and this study 
then showed that it had a positive growth modulation index. It is known that conventional CS may grow 
slowly: so is it really a drug effect? Especially in the absence of responses. 
RESPONSE: Thank you for the thoughtful and important critique. In reference to your question regarding 
pretreatment progression, we have added this statement to the Results section: “Patients had clinical or 
radiological disease progression prior to trial entry.” We agree that interpretation of single-arm studies in 
indolent tumors like conventional chondrosarcoma (cCS) requires caution, particularly in the absence of 
objective responses. However, we respectfully submit that this study demonstrates meaningful evidence 
of drug activity when evaluated within the accepted context of this rare disease. First, while cCS can be 
slow-growing, the majority of patients enrolled in this study had radiographically progressive disease 
prior to trial entry, and nearly half had received two or more prior lines of systemic therapy—indicating a 
more aggressive clinical course. Notably, 52% of response-evaluable patients achieved disease 
stabilization, with a subset maintaining stable disease for over 12 months, and one patient benefiting for 
more than 4 years on olutasidenib, including extended compassionate use. This durability goes beyond 
what would be expected from the natural history of advanced cCS and suggests a true drug effect, even in 
the absence of radiographic tumor shrinkage. Second, while the median PFS of 2.0 months in the overall 
population may appear short, this was influenced by a subset of dedifferentiated patients who progressed 
rapidly. Among patients with conventional histology who achieved stable disease, the median PFS was 
4.9 months, and median OS was 19 months, which is consistent with the 5.6-month PFS reported in the 
ivosidenib study in JCO, a trial of nearly identical size and design. Patients in this study were more 
heavily pretreated prior to enrollment than those in the ivosidenib trial (median 2 prior regimens in the 
olutasidenib study versus median 1 prior regimen in the ivosidenib study). Importantly, ivosidenib was 
not criticized for its lack of objective responses, yet it was deemed publication-worthy and is included in 
the NCCN guidelines. We believe the available data—combined with the pharmacodynamic evidence of 
2-HG suppression and long-term benefit in several patients—support the conclusion that olutasidenib 
exhibits biological and clinical activity in IDH1-mutant cCS. 
Suggestions 
1. Please add CONSORT figure and waterfall plot (up to editor for online or not). 
RESPONSE: A patient disposition figure and waterfall plot have been added to the Supplementary 
Figures. 
2. Line 76: 3.5 months is not durable, please change. 
RESPONSE: The language in the sentence has been changed to, “Olutasidenib was well tolerated and 
conferred disease control in cCS.” 
3. “…and olutasidenib may represent a treatment option” - delete and change to: Based on this study 
olutasidenib is currently investigated in a phase III study against placebo.

RESPONSE: Olutasidenib is not currently being investigated in any phase 3 trials. It has only been 
evaluated in phase 1 and phase 2 studies. In response to your comment, this statement has been removed 
from the Abstract. In the Conclusions, the statement has been changed to say: “On the basis of this study, 
olutasidenib may represent a treatment option for mIDH1 chondrosarcoma; however, placebo-controlled 
clinical studies of olutasidenib would be required to confirm its clinical utility.” 
4. Introduction: Radiotherapy is used in symptomatic locally advanced and metastatic disease in 
clinical practice (e.g. ESMO guidelines: RT can be considered for unresectable disease (primary 
or recurrent), after incomplete surgery and for symptom palliation [IV , B]. High-dose RT is

RESPONSE: Additional information on radiotherapy has been added as suggested (page 4, Introduction): 
“Radiotherapy (RT) may be considered for primary or recurrent unresectable disease following 
incomplete surgery and for palliative care. High-dose RT is currently recommended for patients with skull 
base chondrosarcomas. Although RT has been used to treat symptomatic locally advanced and metastatic 
disease in clinical practice, chondrosarcomas are often resistant to RT.” 
5. Line 130: unknown disease status 19% is quite high for a prospective study: please explain or 
resolve. 
RESPONSE: As specified in the protocol, cohort 3 included patients with relapsed/refractory locally 
advanced or metastatic chondrosarcoma. We are confident that all enrolled patients met these criteria; 
however, we do not have the information regarding which category these “unknown” patients fit, as we 
were limited to the data that were collected and summarized. In the context of other studies evaluating 
IDH1 inhibitors, another published trial reported unknown disease status for 5 (23.8%) patients (Tap WD, 
et al. J Clin Oncol. 2020;38(15):1693-1701).  
6. Line 219: Despite the lack of response as defined by RECIST (CR or PR) observed in the 219 
study, durable progression-free intervals were reported in several patients- This can be due to 
including non-progressing patients. As long as this is not contradicted I do not agree with the 
conclusion that RECIST is not useful.  
RESPONSE: Without knowing the expected progression rate in this population, it is possible that the lack 
of progression observed could potentially be due to the characteristics of the enrolled patients, rather than 
reflecting a treatment effect. Our intention is to point out that, in CS patients, prolonged stable disease 
(without progression) may well be a favorable response, even when not evaluated as “response” by 
RECIST, suggesting those criteria may not be as useful in this setting versus that in other solid tumors. 
We do not mean to imply that RECIST is not useful, but only that there may be limitations to its accuracy 
in CS–RECIST criteria, which rely on measurable tumor size changes and are not always appropriate for 
chondrosarcoma because they may not accurately reflect treatment response (especially with therapies 
that primarily have cytostatic or metabolic effects). Chondrosarcomas, particularly those treated with 
newer targeted therapies, can exhibit changes in viability, metabolic activity, and necrosis that RECIST 
doesn't capture, potentially leading to inaccurate assessments of tumor response—a similar phenomenon 
observed in the Tap et al. study. For clarity, we have deleted the statement “This study highlights the 
limitations of using RECIST to assess tumor response in CS patients”. We have noted instead that “The 
higher PFS rates observed in patients with non-dedifferentiated histology in both the ivosidenib study and 
this study suggest that IDH1 inhibitors may be more effective in cCS; however, although some patients

had a long progression-free interval while on study, without a comparator arm, the prolonged response 
cannot conclusively be attributed to olutasidenib treatment.”

from Cycle 1 Day 1 to Cycle 3 Day 1, with an increase in the proportion of patients reporting no 
problems. These findings have been added to the text and the manuscript Supplementary materials.
