Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents a novel hydrogel material—cell-programmed adaptive contraction (CPAC) hydrogel—that can respond
to alkaline phosphatase (ALP) signals secreted by cells, leading to contraction of the embedded microgels within the
hydrogel and resulting in local stiffness enhancement, ultimately forming a heterogeneous hydrogel structure. While the
CPAC hydrogel demonstrates innovative bio-responsiveness and potential for bone repair, there are several shortcomings
and potential gaps in the study. Therefore, we do not recommend the article for publication in Nat. Commun. at this stage. 
1.The design of the CPAC hydrogel relies solely on ALP as the key enzymatic marker for regulation. However, it remains
unclear whether other enzymes within the phosphatase family could also influence this process, potentially leading to non-
specific reactions. Additionally, the dynamics of ALP release during MSC osteogenic differentiation are not well defined. 
2.The CPAC hydrogel mimics the mechanical heterogeneity of natural ECM through the contraction of microgels and local
stiffness enhancement. However, whether the hydrogel can achieve precise and controllable gradient variations in local
stiffness remains a concern. In the complex in vivo biological environment, the hydrogel may be influenced by external
factors such as temperature and pH, leading to unpredictable fluctuations in its mechanical properties. This could affect the
cells' response to the material, particularly if the local stiffness of the hydrogel becomes uneven, potentially causing
misalignment in stem cell differentiation. 
3.In this study, the CPAC hydrogel responds to ALP and alters its structure within a short period of time, but its stability in
vivo or in long-term experiments has not been sufficiently validated. 
4.Although the hydrogel promotes MSC osteogenic differentiation, the effects on cell proliferation and survival have not been
adequately explored. The contraction of the microgels may lead to changes in the intercellular space, further affecting cell
expansion and proliferation. If the hydrogel does not provide sufficient support or space during differentiation, it may limit the
functional repair of the cells. 
5.The authors indicate that the expression levels of miR-124, miR-214, and miR-26a are elevated in the CPAC hydrogel
group, and this is significantly correlated with a decrease in the expression of EZH2 and its downstream target H3K27me3.
However, the lack of validation through anti-miR treatment raises concerns about the reliability of this correlation. 
6.In Figure 5g, the immunofluorescence images for the control group are not shown. 
7.In Figures 3b, 3c, and 5c, g, a significant proportion of suspected overexposed areas can be observed. This raises
concerns about the accuracy of the semi-quantitative analysis based on fluorescence intensity. 
8.The regulatory effect of H3K27me3 on osteogenic-related genes has not been validated through relevant experiments. 
9.In the in vivo experiments, only data from 8 weeks after hydrogel implantation are provided. This duration does not
adequately reflect the effects of local network stiffening in promoting MSC osteogenic differentiation.

10.The size changes of CPAC hydrogel in a 1 U/mL ALP solution have been studied; however, the ALP concentration
during in vivo or bone injury healing is unknown, and it remains unclear whether it would be sufficient to trigger the
hydrogel's adaptive remodeling. Additionally, the hydrogel's contraction under in vivo conditions has not been characterized.
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a study focused on the development of a hydrogel system that mimics the dynamically evolving and
heterogeneous extracellular matrix (ECM) environment during tissue development. Specifically, they engineer a cell-
programmed adaptive contraction (CPAC) hydrogel containing ALP-responsive microgels that contract in response to
alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity. This induces overall hydrogel network contraction and local matrix stiffening, thereby
promoting osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs). The authors provide and test a hypothesis for the
underlying mechanotransduction mechanism including upregulation of microRNA and demonstrate the translational
potential of their system in a rat critical-size bone defect model, where they observed enhanced bone formation. 
The study presents a dynamic hydrogel platform that responds to cell-released enzymes and modulates stem cell fate
through dynamically changing the mechanical signals. The concept is interesting, and the authors have sought to perform
both mechanistic investigation and in vivo validation. While the results are promising, additional experiments are required to
support their main claims such as ‘‘ALP-induced gel contraction’’, ‘‘local stiffening’’ and ‘‘mechanical heterogeneity’’.
Moreover, several aspects of the study require clarification, statistical information or further detail to strengthen its overall
scientific rigor, reproducibility and impact. 
Major comments: 
• Novelty and significance: It would be helpful if the authors can explain more why this work is novel and their findings are
significant for a broad impact. 
• Study design: While this study probed an interesting concept, their main claims seem to have several gaps, across
materials, in vitro- and in vivo models. 
o From the materials aspect, the reliability relies on a thorough characterization of the mechanical properties and
heterogeneity of their biomaterials. 
o The range of mechanical stiffness such as magnitude, anisotropy seems unclear. 
o From the cell biology perspective, whether the readouts are solely due to ALP, and cell-secreted ALP, or not, may require
more rigorous investigation by setting up controls and perturbation experiments. It is known that cells also secrete other
factors that block ALP acitivity. 
o From the in vivo aspect, the complexity further increases in terms of proteins and cells in involved. The authors are
recommended to add control experiments to demonstrate the enhanced bone formation is not by the gels along, nor other
matrix factors, but exactly by their claimed mechanism. 
• Abstract clarity: The abstract does not clearly convey how the hydrogel system works. Key elements, such as the main
components of the hydrogel as well as the control groups used in the study, are not mentioned but are critical for interpreting
the results. 
• Introduction: 
o The introduction would benefit from a comparison to naturally derived hydrogels such as collagen. How does the
contractile behaviour of the presented system compare, and what advantages does it offer over existing ECM-mimetic
materials? 
o The exact composition of the hydrogel remains unclear in the introduction and Figure 1. Including a schematic that clearly
explains the composition and assembly of the hydrogel from microgels and other components for all experimental hydrogel
groups would greatly improve clarity. Also pay attention to complete & matching legends for the schematics. 
• Figure 2g: It would be helpful to include confocal images of the hydrogels prior to ALP treatment to demonstrate the
changes induced by contraction. 
• Figure 2h: Please indicate which statistical test was used and annotate significance levels directly on the graph. 
• You claim that “CPAC hydrogel can better facilitate stellate spreading”. Please explain where this can be seen or provide
more morphological data to support it. 
• Figures 3b/c: The CPAC hydrogels appear to have a higher cell density compared to NC controls. Is this a localized effect
due to contraction, or does the material promote proliferation? Higher local cell density itself may influence osteogenic
differentiation. 
Related to this, can you comment on the macroscopic effect of ALP-mediated contraction on the entire hydrogel, does the
hydrogel size change? 
• Can you specify how the “high-modulus N,N-dimethylacrylamide (DMAA) microgels” compare to the previous control
microgels (Phos-HEA) in terms of mechanical properties? 
• The claim “NCLS hydrogels showed elevated osteogenic marker expression relative to NC hydrogels” is not evident from
the data shown, please justify or explain more. 
• Figure 4: Please provide rationale for the selection of mechanotransduction markers (pFAK, pMyosin IIa, Paxillin). 
• Figure 4: Why are the microgels not visible in the confocal images like in Figures 3 & 5? The figure caption makes it a bit
unclear, if cells were seeded on top of hydrogels rather than embedded. 
• Figure 4b: Collagen I appears to be predominantly intracellular. Is there an explanation for this observation? 
• The hydrogel is described as responsive to ALP secreted by MSCs, yet the immunofluorescence images show primarily
intracellular ALP. Please clarify how this observation aligns with the proposed mechanism of hydrogel contraction, which
relies on extracellular ALP activity. 
• A 3-day culture period is relatively short for observing osteogenic differentiation. Have longer culture periods been
considered?

Author Response: 
We thank the reviewer for raising these important concerns regarding the precise control 
over local stiffness gradients and the influence of external factors like temperature and pH 
in the complex in vivo environment. Below, we address both of these aspects based on 
additional experiments: 
 
1) Precise and controllable gradient variations in local stiffness: 
We agree that  achieving precise and controllable gradients in local stiffness is a key 
consideration for biomimetic materials. In our current design, the mechanical heterogeneity 
is dynamically evolved through a cell -driven feedback mechanism, rather than being pre-
programmed with static, absolute precision . The contractio n of microgels and local 
stiffness enhancement are primarily triggered by ALP secreted by MSCs themselves during 
early osteogenic differentiation , leading to self -regulated structural heterogeneity . This 
design more accurately mimics the dynamic and reciprocal cell-matrix interactions found 
in natural ECM, where the matrix evolves in response to cellular activity.  To characterize 
this dynamic process and assess its controllability, we performed the following analyses:

i) Microscopic mechanical mapping: Nanoindentation mapping (Figure S16a) indicates 
that, while a degree of heterogeneity is present, as intended to mimic natural ECM, the 
stiffness variation occurs relatively uniformly without large, unpredictable fluctuations that 
would lead to detrimental misalignment in stem cell differentiation. This suggests that the 
cell-instructed heterogeneity may be more physiologically relevant than a perfectly 
engineered but static gradient. 
 
ii) Macroscopic rheological analysis: Bulk storage modulus ( G’) of the hydrogel was 
monitored over time during osteogenic differentiation. The data ( Figure S16b) shows a 
consistent increase in overall stiffness, confirming that the CPAC hydrogel undergoes 
predictable and controlled temporal stiffening in response to cellular activity.

regions closer to the microgel surface, and this relationship closely matches our 
experimental data. This confirms that the CPAC hydrogel can prec isely control stiffness 
gradients locally while maintaining uniform bulk mechanical properties.

13. Figure 3 & 5: WesternBlott: please explain why proteins of interests and reference

Author Response: 
We thank the reviewer for raising these important concerns . The Western blot (WB) 
experiment was performed on the same gel . However, after electrophoresis, the gel was 
carefully cut into different sections corresponding to the molecular weight ranges of the 
target proteins and the reference protein (β-actin or GAPDH) for separate antibody 
incubation and exposure. This approach was adopted because different proteins often 
require distinct incubation conditions and exposure times for optimal signal detection. 
Running and exposing them separately avoids overexposure or underexposure of certain 
bands and prevents antigen loss that c an occur during repeated stripping and reprobing. 
Therefore, the separate presentation of the blots more accurately reflects the true protein 
expression levels under each condition.
