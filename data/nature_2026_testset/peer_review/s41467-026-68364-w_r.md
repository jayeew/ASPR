Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Feng et al, describes the design of a new self-amplifying RNA candidate, aimed at facilitating protein
replacement therapy. To achieve this, they engineered a Vpg saRNA vector from human Norovirus isolates, as the Vpg will
help facilitate high-fidelity replication. The Norovirus isolate replicon was initially characterised in vitro, leading to further
sequence modifications and later, adjustments to the GOI orientation. The cap-independent translation of the Vpg saRNA
was confirmed in mTOR knockout cells, prior to testing the vector in a CAC model. Further engineering of the Vpg was then
applied to increase fidelity of RdRp replication, which was confirmed using the GSDMDENG model. Other adjustments to
the vector, including mutations within the RdRp, UTR and arrangement of the construct were later applied. Overall, the
manuscript shows an interesting approach to the rational design of a Vpg saRNA vector specifically for gene therapy/protein
replacement approaches. 
While the topic is interesting and relevant, the layout of the manuscript is confusing and often important concepts and
achievements (particularly with regards to the engineering of the vector) get lost in background literature and proof of
concept studies. It is also unclear why proof of concept studies was performed after each iteration, and not at the end of the
design process. Lack of appropriate controls and incomplete methods add to the confusion.

Response: Thank you for your thorough and insightful review. Regarding the seemingly paradoxical phenomenon of
alphavirus replicons exhibiting high replication activity but low protein expression, we believe this is closely related to the
dynamic host response to translation suppression mechanisms, such as eIF2α phosphorylation. 
Translation of alphavirus genomic RNA initially relies on a 5′ cap structure (methylated by the viral non-structural protein
nsP1), following a typical cap-dependent mechanism. Upon activation of host antiviral sensors—such as PKR—eIF2α
becomes phosphorylated, leading to global suppression of cap-dependent translation. Consequently, the expression of viral
proteins is significantly reduced, as shown in Fig. 3L. This observation is consistent with previous reports (e.g., Ventoso et
al., J Virol, 2006). 
Despite this translational blockade, alphaviruses can sustain high levels of RNA replication (Fig. 3K), likely due to several
factors: (1) in the early phase of infection, before translation is shut down, sufficient quantities of viral replicase complexes
(e.g., nsP1–nsP4) are synthesized, allowing replication to proceed even under translational inhibition (Ventoso et al., 2006);
and (2) replication often occurs within membrane-associated compartments (e.g., endosomal or vesicular structures), which
may provide partial shielding from host antiviral factors such as PKR (Fros et al., PLoS Pathog, 2012). 
Thus, this “high replication–low translation” balance may represent an immune evasion strategy employed by alphaviruses
—minimizing overt protein production to reduce immunogenicity, while maintaining sufficient genome replication to ensure
progeny virion production. 
However, this feature poses a significant limitation when repurposing alphavirus replicons as vectors for mRNA-based
therapeutics. Excessive replication can trigger strong host antiviral responses, while inefficient protein translation
compromises therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, addressing this imbalance is critical for optimizing such replicon systems for
clinical applications.
