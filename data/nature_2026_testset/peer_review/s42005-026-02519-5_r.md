Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The article “Granular Ta-Te nanowire superconductivity exceeding the Pauli limit” by L. Zhao et al reports the observation of
an upper critical field significantly exceeding the Pauli-limit upper critical field in pressurized 1D Ta-Te granular nanowires.
While the reported upper critical field value (~16 T) is indeed remarkable and appears to exceed the Pauli limit by a factor of
two, I find the basis for this conclusion not fully convincing for the following reasons: 
1) The fitting of the temperature dependence of the upper critical field using a multiband model is not clearly justified,
especially given the strong disorder and random grain orientation observed in the system. Moreover, the magnetic field data
only extend up to 9 T, making the extrapolation to ~16 T uncertain. In such a disordered, granular 1D system, the use of a
multiband model requires stronger justification. The random grain orientation, combined with possible filamentary
superconductivity, further complicates the interpretation of fundamental properties. A granular network may support
percolative paths under pressure, leading to significantly different R–T behaviors in Run 1, Run 2, and Run 3. In the normal
state, Run 1 exhibits metallic behavior, whereas Run 2 and Run 3 show insulating behavior, and their resistance values also
differ significantly (assuming similar cross-sectional areas of the wires). 
2) The use of a 95% resistance drop as the criterion of Tc is too high, particularly given the broad superconducting
transitions observed. A more conservative or alternative definition (e.g., 10% or 50% drops) could provide a better basis for
comparing against the Pauli limit. If the 95% criterion is reasonable, the authors should present experimental evidence for
superconductor-insulator transition (SIT) behavior, particularly in light of the insulating behavior observed in Run 2 and Run
3. This would help validate the robustness of the defined Tc and provide deeper insight into the nature of the transition in this
granular, quasi-1D system. 
The authors need to further elaborate on the manuscript by taking the above points into consideration. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, L. Zhao et al. report on the growth and physical characterization of granular Ta-Te nanowires (possibly
Ta3Te4). They performed SEM, EDS, TEM, IR, XRD, and transport measurements under applied pressure. At ambient
pressure, they obtained an insulating behavior of their nanowires, in which they claim that a possible explanation is an
Anderson localization of electrons confined within the 1D structure. Their main claim is that the nanowires become
superconductors at high pressures (~ 20 GPa), with an HC2z beyond the Pauli limit, in which the 1D structure would play a
big role in interpreting their results. Finally, they compare the upper critical field of their nanowires with other reports from the
literature, claiming that they set a new record among all reported superconducting nanowires. The manuscript is clear and
concise, and most of the figures are clear. The synthesis and experimental challenges in studying these novel nanowires
(and likely a novel compound) are interesting and should be published in some form in some journal, but at the present form,
I do not think that the manuscript is suitable for Communications Physics. A few major points are put into question regarding
the central claims of the paper, which undermine the manuscript and should be taken into account. 
1) The term quasi-1D superconductivity can be applied to two situations: either the system by itself behaves as a quasi-1D
compound (either due to its crystal or electronic structure), or we confine two dimensions, which results in nanowires. For the
former, we have examples such as ACr3As3 (A = K, Rb, Cs), Bechgaard salts, some chalcogenides, Nb3Te4, and many
more. In most of them, it has been observed HC2z beyond the Pauli limit, which in most cases can be explained by SOC
effects – as far as I know, the only example where an FFLO phase has been more accepted is in the metallic organics. In the
1D dimensionality confinement case, there is a clear criterion that is well accepted: the nanowires normally are thinner (or at
least are comparable) to their superconducting coherence length [Nano Lett. 8, 136-141 (2008), PRB 71, 104521-104527
(2005), 34]. They estimate a coherence length of ~ 5 nm, while they have 100 nm nanowires. Normally, to prove that the
superconductivity is 1D, HC2 as a function of angle or comparing the nanowires results with the bulk counterpart are crucial.
Neither is done here. Another complication is that their crystal structure is 1D by itself, which means that anisotropy (and
effects on HC2z) is already expected without even considering confinement from being a nanowire. This is especially true
when also considering the strong SOC expected of this family. Even if we suppose it is a quasi-1D superconductor, the
comparison only with nanowires does not make much sense (and the authors did not put a few nanowire systems that have
been reported in the literature for the comparison), considering that we do not know which effects are coming from
confinement and which ones are from the crystal structure. One example of this intertwining is RbCr3As3, where both single
crystals and nanowires show clear anisotropy and enhancement of HC2z.

2) The claim that it is beyond the Pauli limit is also questionable. The data at 9 T hardly shows any kink that could result in a
superconducting transition, yet they predict an HC2(0) exceptionally high. I understand that the claims of violating the Pauli
limit are based on their fitting using a multi-band model, but the experimental data does not show this enhancement of
HC2(0). It is a speculation, not actual data points, showing this enhancement, and this is particularly troubling with claims
such as a new record among all reported superconducting nanowires. For me, it is hard to claim such an enhancement of
HC2z when the data at 9 T, by itself, does not show any suppression of the resistance. Also, their definition of TC changes
constantly throughout the manuscript. While in the first part of the manuscript, they use a criterion that is not the most applied
in the community (just the kink), for extracting HC2z they use the criterion “the resistance drops by 90% under the magnetic
field”. This second criterion is not applied, where they actually used the resistance dropped 10% (or recovered 90%). 
This is a complicated question about which criterion to adopt. Normally, s adopt some percentage of the resistance drop
(50% is the most common). In this case, it is even more troubling due to its broader transition. The broadness of the transition
also seems to change, at least as a function of pressure, which makes it even harder. It is problematic if you think that the
authors adopted a multi-band model, probably due to the apparent linearity of HC2 as a function of T (which I am not even
sure makes sense for this compound. I would expect that normal s-wave BCS fitting should be fine). This is essential to
analyze the SC phase properly, using the WHH formula, extracting the Maki parameter, and so on. I would also reinforce that
the analysis of the SC properties is lacking a bit and should be improved. 
3) Their structural characterization is not complete. As a novel compound, they should make the refinement of the XRD data.
What is the diameter distribution of the nanowires? It seems from Fig 1a that they have differences in size. Additionally, what
is the Ta-Te ratio extracted from EDS? 
The TEM data is more difficult to understand. From that, does it look like the wires have grains of 10 nm on top of an
amorphous mesh? However, the XRD does not show a huge background characteristic for measuring amorphous material,
which would point out the wire being formed all along by those grains. For me, it is a bit confusing (perhaps due to the
quality of the image), but I could not grasp the quality of their wires. 
4) For me, their normal state of resistance is also puzzling. They observe a clear insulating behavior at ambient pressure,
where it was expected to be a metal here (Nb3Te4 is a metal, for example). Although in Run 1, the superconductivity comes
out of a metallic resistance, for Run 2 and Run 3, they clearly observe an insulating behavior before the SC transition. They
attribute that to an Anderson localization due to the confinement of the electrons, but then why would applying pressure
reduce the confinement? Why would the normal state in Run 2 and Run 3 differ from Run 1? I wonder if a simpler
explanation is not at play here. For example, it is common to create an oxide layer around the nanowires when exposed to
air, which must be removed at the contact points before the measurements. Did the authors take care of their contact points?
What were the contact resistances? I can understand a scenario where applying pressure reduces contact resistance due to
a more conductive oxide surface layer. This point should be investigated better, and I would recommend re-doing the
measurements in case the problem comes from contact resistance. 
Also, a few minor points should be highlighted: 
1) The authors claim that “In a conventional superconductor […] the upper limit HC2z is given by the Pauli limit.” As far as I
know, most BCS superconductors are orbital-limited, not Pauli-limited. The reduction of dimensionality normally weakens
screening currents, pushing the orbital limit beyond the Pauli limit. I think that this phrasing needs clarification and at least a
citation. 
2) Their mean-field model assumes that Rashba SOC comes naturally due to the inversion symmetry breaking along the z
direction, but this mean-field model does not assume any specific characteristics of the Ta-Te nanowire. In other words, it is
valid for any material, but not all SC nanowires greatly enhance Hc2z. I do not see how this explains the results reported. 
3) They have a typo in the SI. Inverion should be inversion. 
In summary, although the data reported by L. Zhao et al. has value and deserves to be published, their claims are
moderately (or even weakly) supported by their experimental observations. This manuscript would probably be better
appreciated in a more specialized journal due to its limitations and nuances.
