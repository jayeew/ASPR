Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript introduces solvent-free microlithographic flow using an amorphous selenium (Se) mediator and PDMS-
assisted lift-off. The idea is highly relevant for solvent-sensitive materials and is supported by substantial characterization.
However, several key aspects—residue verification at scale, metrology choices, dose/feature-size dependence, perovskite
device-level validation, and substrate-dependent variations—require further evidence or clarification before acceptance. 
Major comment 
1.The main theme of this research is a lithography method for chemically sensitive materials. While the authors have
quantitatively demonstrated good performance for MoS
₂
, the data for black phosphorus (BP) appears to be limited to a
qualitative comparison. It seems that to truly validate the method's utility, obtaining quantitative performance data for a
genuinely chemically unstable material would be more compelling. We would be interested to hear the authors' perspective
on this matter. 
2.The authors claim a clean surface after the Se lift-off process, supported by AFM and Raman analysis. However, two
points require clarification. First, according to Supplementary Data 33, a final vacuum annealing step is performed after the
lift-off. The rationale for this step is unclear if the lift-off process itself is sufficient to produce a clean surface. Second, the use
of Raman spectroscopy to confirm the absence of residual Se is questionable. It is known that the laser excitation used in
Raman can induce the sublimation of Se. Therefore, this measurement may not be a reliable indicator of residual Se on the
as-processed surface requires further verification. 
3.The manuscript would be strengthened by an analysis of residual Se particles over a larger area. This is essential to
evaluate the uniformity and scalability of the proposed method. As a suggestion, the authors could perform a surface-
sensitive analysis, such as XPS, on a test substrate where the entire Se film has been sublimated by the laser. This would
provide more robust and quantitative evidence regarding the presence or absence of any non-volatile residues across a
wider region. 
4.I would expect a significant amount of residual Se to remain in the finer patterns, as they likely receive an insufficient laser
dose for complete sublimation. Could the authors provide direct evidence, such as high-magnification SEM images, to prove
that this is not the case? 
5.A potential issue may arise from the differing adhesion coefficients of Se on the substrate versus on the target material
(e.g., a perovskite flake). This could lead to non-uniformity in the deposited Se thickness, which in turn could negatively
impact the resolution and fidelity of the laser patterning process. This could become a potential issue when fabricating fine
and complex patterns on such heterogeneous surfaces. What is the authors' perspective on this matter? Furthermore, could
they provide direct experimental evidence, such as AFM line scans across flake edges, to quantify the Se thickness
uniformity?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an all-dry microfabrication strategy using inorganic selenium molecules as a lithographic mediator
for processing solvent-sensitive electronic materials. The selenium film is patterned with a localized photothermal
sublimation sequentially under light irradiation. Large-scale patterns and devices have been fabricated with this method.
The strategy developed in this manuscript is potentially interesting, but several critical issues need clarification and revision
to support the claims: 
1. Several all-dry microfabrication strategies for solvent-sensitive materials (e.g., perovskites) have already been developed,
including shadow mask techniques (Nature Protocols, 20, 1915–1929, 2025; Advanced Materials Technologies, 4,
1900519, 2019) and mechanical transfer methods (Nat. Nanotechnol. 17, 1206–1213, 2022). The manuscript does not

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

clearly articulate how the selenium-mediated approach outperforms these alternatives—for example, in resolution,
scalability, material compatibility, or device performance. A direct comparative analysis is necessary to highlight its unique
value. 
2. The reported device performance such as on/off ratios for MoS
₂
 FETs (average ~10⁷, best ~10⁹; Fig. 4f) is less compelling
when compared to recent works using device fabrication processes based on organic polymeric resists, which demonstrates
average on/off ratios of ~10⁸ for devices on SiO
₂
 or high-κ oxide substrates (Nature Electronics 8, 571–577, 2025; Nature
Electronics 6, 146–153, 2023; Nature Electronics 8, 135–146, 2025). This challenges the value of adopting the method
described in the manuscript. 
3. The manuscript attributes selenium’s photothermal sublimation to weak intermolecular van der Waals forces but lacks
critical details on how laser parameters (wavelength, power density, scanning speed) influence patterning precision.
Specifically: How do different laser wavelengths affect trench resolution? Does varying laser power lead to residual
selenium on the substrate, and if so, how is this quantified? Without such data, the reproducibility and controllability of the
patterning process remain uncertain. 
4. Information on the stability of selenium films during storage or device fabrication is absent. For instance: Does prolonged
exposure to air (e.g., oxygen, humidity) induce oxidation or degradation of the selenium layer? How does such exposure
affect the film’s adhesion to the substrate or its ability to be patterned via laser sublimation? 
These factors are critical for assessing the practical utility of the method in large-scale manufacturing. 
5. The claim that “patterns obtained are sharp edges in the unexposed area and no small particle residues in the irradiated
region” is insufficiently supported. The AFM height scale in the provided images is too large (~300 nm) to resolve nanoscale
residues (a few nanometers or smaller), which could still impact device performance and uniformity. 
6. Numerous typos and inconsistencies in figure numbering undermine readability. For example, references to “Fig. 2c, 2d,
2e, 2f” appear to correspond to “Fig. 3c, 3d, 3e, 3f” in the manuscript. A thorough proofread is required to correct these issues
and ensure clarity.

Comment 1. The paper describes laser-based removal of selenium molecules from the material
surface. Could the authors comment on the resulting surface condition of the treated material
surface? In addition, this paper demonstrates the feasibility of 532 nm and 633 nm laser sources.
Then, can ultraviolet laser source, which is often used in laser direct writing equipment, and even the
electron radiation in electron beam lithography be used here?
Response: We employed direct laser irradiation to remove the selenium molecules from the material
surface. To systematically evaluate the surface condition after processing, we examined the surface
morphology of five different types of sensitive electronic materials (Fig. 2). Optical microscopy
images revealed that the laser-treated materials remained structurally intact and exhibited a clean
surface. For more detailed surface characterization, we performed additional atomic force
microscopy (AFM) experiments to compare the surface topography of mechanically exfoliated MoS₂
flakes before selenium coating and after laser removal (Figure R1). The AFM results clearly
demonstrate that both the flake surface and the surrounding substrate remain highly clean, with no
significant change in roughness in the scanned regions. These findings collectively indicate that the
laser treatment and selenium removal process effectively preserve the original surface state of the
sensitive electronic materials.

Comment 2. The authors' one-step mechanical stripping technique enables wafer-scale patterning of
solvent-sensitive materials without chemical reagents (Figure 3), offering distinct advantages. Given
the broad applicability of metal arrays in electronics, could the authors discuss the feasibility and
potential adaptations required to extend this physical method to metallic systems?
Response: We thank you for recognizing the advantages of our one-step mechanical stripping
technique for solvent-sensitive materials and for raising this valuable question regarding its
applicability to metallic systems.
We are pleased to confirm that the proposed dry patterning approach is indeed fully applicable to
the fabrication of metal arrays. As illustrated in Figure R3, the process for metal patterning involves:
(1) laser etching of the pre-deposited selenium layer to define the desired patterns, (2) deposition of
the metal (e.g., Au) by physical vapor deposition, and (3) one-step mechanical stripping to remove
the excess selenium together with the overlying metal film, leaving behind well-defined electrode
patterns. By simply adjusting the laser-written patterns in the selenium layer, this method allows
flexible fabrication of electrode structures with varying geometries and numbers.
Furthermore, this process is readily scalable, enabling the production of large-area, uniform metal
arrays without the use of solvents or wet chemicals. The method preserves the advantages of being a
dry, reagent-free process, thus maintaining compatibility with solvent-sensitive materials while
extending its utility to versatile metal patterning for a broad range of electronic applications.
Figure R3. Metal Au electrode fabricated by the inorganic Se microlithography. Optical images of fabricating
two metal electrodes (a) and four metal electrodes (b). The process includes the laser etching on Se film, Au atom
deposition, and mechanical peeling removal for the metal electrode.

Phosphorus sulfide MgPS3 was synthesized by chemical vapor transport. Use Mg pellets, red
phosphorus, and sulfur powder as raw materials. A total of 2 g of the raw materials was weighed
according to the stoichiometric ratio. The mixed powder was loaded into a quartz tube, which was
then evacuated to a vacuum of 10-2 Pa and sealed. The sealed tube was placed in a dual-zone muffle
furnace and subjected to the following program: heating to 650 °C (source zone) and 500 °C
(deposition zone) over 20 hours, maintaining for 100 hours, cooling to 250 °C, and finally cooling
naturally to room temperature. Colorless transparent sheet-like crystals were obtained from the
quartz tube.
Black phosphorus crystals were purchased from Nanjing MKNANO Tech. Co., Ltd. Black
phosphorus flakes were obtained by mechanically exfoliating the black phosphorus crystals.
We subjected the obtained crystalline material to XRD tests, as shown in Figure R4. The
measured XRD peaks are well matched with the simulated patterns and have a small full width at
half-maximum (FWHM). This proves that the obtained sample is highly crystalline single-crystal
materials. The above information has been added to the Methods and Supplementary Fig. 17.

Comment 4. The study presents mechanical stripping and physical vapor transport as methods for
selenium removal. Given the well-known solubility of selenium in carbon disulfide (CS₂), could the
use of CS₂ solution as an alternative solvent-based removal method be explored for patterning
applications?
Response: We thank you for the insightful suggestion regarding the use of carbon disulfide (CS2) as
a solvent-based approach for selenium removal. To evaluate this possibility, we explored CS2 as an
alternative method for selectively dissolving excess selenium in patterning applications. In the
solvent-based removal process (Figure R5a), the selenium layer gradually dissolves in CS2, leading
to the automatic detachment of the overlying target film from the substrate. After complete removal
of the selenium layer, the pre-designed patterns remain intact on the substrate.
As shown in Figure R5b, the initially transparent CS2 solution turns yellow following selenium
dissolution, with fragmented metal film suspended in the solvent. With the dissolution of the
selenium interlayer, the upper metal film cleanly separates from the substrate, leaving well-defined
electrode patterns behind. By this method, we successfully fabricated metal patterns, such as Au
electrode and Cu circuit pattern (Figures R5c, R5d).
These results collectively demonstrate that the CS2-based dissolution method can successfully be
applied for patterned fabrication. We believe that CS2-based removal offers a universal and
alternative method to pattern delicate electronic materials.
Figure R5. CS₂ solvent removal method. a, Schematic of CS₂ solvent removal process, including object metal
deposition, selenium molecular dissolution, and metal pattern. b, Photo image of CS2 solution before and after
removing Se. c, Optical image of the Au electrode. d, Optical image of metal Cu circuit pattern.

Response: Sharing the same expectations as yours, we have previously conducted preliminary
attempts to push the patterning resolution toward the nanoscale. The achievable resolution is indeed
influenced by several key factors, including laser wavelength, spot size, power density, and the
material properties of the selenium layer. In our current changing the power density using 532 nm

our current laboratory hardware.
We firmly believe that achieving nanoscale patterning is fundamentally feasible with optimized
parameters, including shorter-wavelength lasers with adequate power density, reduced spot size, and
precise process control. Pushing the resolution toward the nanoscale represents a key and active
direction of our ongoing and future research efforts.

We believe that the successful demonstration of a functional MoS2 FET array, in combination with
the initial BP device, strongly supports the versatility and broad applicability of our lithographic
approach in atomically sensitive material processing.

Clarification on the vacuum annealing and lift-off step
We further clarify the rationale for the fabrication process of large-area transistor arrays (Previous
Supplementary Fig. 33). To clearly explain the process flow, we added the schematic Figure R11.
The initial lift-off step (Step 5) is performed to remove the patterned organic mask layer along with
the excess deposited metal. It is crucial to emphasize that the selenium layer itself remains entirely
intact on the substrate after this step. The subsequent vacuum annealing step (Step 6) is then
explicitly employed to remove this remaining selenium layer by leveraging its relatively low
sublimation temperature (as detailed in the Methods). This final thermal desorption cleanly exposes
the underlying active material channels and contact regions, completing the device fabrication.
Figure R11. Schematic diagram of transistor arrays fabrication by Se-assisted microlithography.
Re-evaluation of Raman spectroscopy for Se residue detection
The 633 nm laser we used is indeed suitable for characterizing bulk Se films or powder. As for the
selenium residues on the substrate surface, especially selenium nanoparticles, the Raman laser may
cause the selenium molecules already sublimated while detecting them. Upon careful consideration
of the comment that the Raman laser excitation itself may induce sublimation of trace Se, we agree
that this makes Raman spectroscopy an unreliable method for verifying the Se residue on the
as-processed surface. In light of this consideration, we have removed the Raman data about Se
residue previously presented in Supplementary Fig. 29 from the manuscript. More surface-specific
techniques (AFM or XPS) would be provided for better detection of residual Se.
