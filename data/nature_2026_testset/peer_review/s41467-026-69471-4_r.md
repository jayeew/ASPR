Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This well-written, high-quality manuscript makes a unique contribution to high-throughput proteomics. It presents an
impressive large-scale quantitative proteomics profiling of perturbed mouse tissues from different organs, utilizing Evosep
(>240 SPD) and nDIA with Atral. The authors optimized several parameters of the Astral instruments, resulting in noteworthy
data. 
Major issues: 
1, The claimed technical innovation of this study is unclear. The so-called "ultra-fast proteomics (UFP)" essentially combines
existing technologies (Evosep and Astral) from manufacturers. While parameter optimization is appreciated, it doesn't
represent a major conceptual novelty. Multiple approaches exist for achieving UFP, and labeling all of them as such without
significant innovation could lead to numerous similar abbreviations. It is recommended that the authors de-emphasize the
methodology (perhaps avoiding the term UFP) and instead focus on the capabilities enabled by the combination of Evosep
and nDIA with Astral. 
2, 
How were batch effects controlled during the processing of 507 samples? Include quality control metrics (e.g., CVs for
technical replicates). 
3, Longitudinal analysis across multiple time points (0, 1, 3, 5, 8 days) lacks clarity. Were mixed-effects models or repeated-
measures ANOVA used to account for temporal dependencies? 
4, The criteria for differential protein expression (fold change >2/<0.5, p <0.05) may inflate false positives in large datasets.
Adjustment of p values to correct for multiple-testing errors is essential. 
5, The link between ASNase-induced proteomic changes (e.g., ferroptosis in spleen) and clinical toxicity (e.g., VTE) is
correlative. Are functional assays (e.g., ROS measurement, clotting assays) or in vivo toxicity models provided to validate
these hypotheses? 
6, The manuscript emphasizes ASNase toxicity mechanisms but does not propose actionable therapeutic strategies beyond
monitoring ASN levels. How do the findings directly inform clinical practice or drug development? 
Minor issues: 
1, The rationale for selecting 13 tissues (e.g., why include adipose depots but exclude pancreas?) is unclear. 
2, How does the 1-Th isolation window in nDIA affect peptide identification compared to wider windows? Were spectral
libraries required, and how were they generated? 
3, Tissue-specific protein changes (e.g., spleen vs. liver) might reflect baseline inter-tissue variability. Were expression
levels normalized to tissue-specific controls? 
4, The metabolomics data show GSH depletion in the spleen (Fig. 7e), but how does this quantitatively correlate with
ferroptosis markers (e.g., lipid peroxidation assays)? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Lorenzi and coworkers introduces an ultra-fast mass spectrometry proteomics method (UFP-MS) that
enhances proteome analysis by enabling the identification of over 240 samples per day and profiling over 11,000 proteins
across various tissues. The UFP-MS method combines narrow-window data-independent acquisition (nDIA) with short-
gradient micro-flow chromatography. The study uses the Orbitrap Astral MS with enhanced acquisition rates of up to 200 Hz

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

and optimized settings such as 1 Th isolation windows and tailored injection times to maximize protein identifications, which
enables the identification of >6,000 proteins in a 1-minute gradient and ~7,500 proteins within a 2-minute gradient. The MS
acquisition strategy demonstrates robust quantitative performance across mixed-species samples, with accurate protein
quantitation and linear responses to varying sample inputs. The authors also introduce a high-throughput sample
preparation workflow that integrates adaptive focused acoustic (AFA)-assisted proteolysis, tissue homogenization, and
Evotip desalting. This allows the processing of up to 96 tissue samples in just 5 hours, significantly improving throughput
compared to conventional methods. This method is applied to investigate the effects of L-asparaginase (ASNase) in-vivo in
mouse tissues, revealing distinct tissue-specific mechanisms of drug efficacy and toxicity. Key findings indicate that ASNase
treatment leads to broad dysregulation in metabolic pathways, particularly those related to cholesterol metabolism and
coagulation, which may explain observed side effects like hypertriglyceridemia and venous thromboembolism. The study
highlights the importance of understanding the organ-specific responses to ASNase, particularly noting that the spleen
exhibits unique vulnerabilities and regulatory changes in amino acid metabolism, potentially influencing therapeutic
strategies for treating cancers such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). 
Main issues: 
1) The authors claim that they can identify >6000 proteins in 1-min, but with a maximum throughput of 240 samples analyzed
per day, the actual run-to-run time is at best 6 minutes for the so-called ‘1-min gradient’. So in reality, the UPF-MS method is
at best able to identify 6200 proteins in 6 minutes or about a thousand proteins per minute of actual LC-MS time. This needs
to be specified in the text. 
2) The study identifies dysregulated pathways (e.g., ferroptosis, cholesterol metabolism, and complement cascades), but
functional validation is lacking. The authors should use pharmacological inhibitors or activators to modulate these pathways
in vitro or in vivo. For example, they could test ferroptosis inhibitors (e.g., ferrostatin-1) in spleen tissue to confirm its role in
ASNase-induced toxicity. 
3) The study integrates proteomics and metabolomics but does not fully explore correlations between the two datasets. The
authors should perform pathway enrichment analysis combining the proteomics and metabolomics data to identify co-
regulated pathways and use network analysis to map interactions between proteins and metabolites affected by ASNase
treatment. 
4) The high-throughput workflow is efficient but may introduce biases compared to the standard workflow. The authors
should compare protein recovery and identification rates between their high-throughput workflow and state-of-the-art sample
preparation workflows for tissue proteomics across different tissue types. 
5) Post-translational modifications (PTMs) such as phosphorylation or acetylation likely play a role in the observed proteome
changes but were not analyzed. Given that the tissue digests are already prepared, the authors should consider performing
phosphoproteomics or acetylomics to identify PTMs associated with key proteins and pathways affected by the ASNase
treatment. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, Xiong et al. conducted a proteome study to investigate the impact of asparaginase (ASNase), a drug used in
the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), on the proteome composition of multiple organs in mice. Using a rapid
and sensitive mass spectrometry method, they identified thousands of proteins in each organ, several of which exhibited
different responses to wild-type and mutated ASNase. The data suggest dysregulation of proteins involved in coagulation
and cholesterol metabolism, as well as other pathways regulated in a tissue-specific manner. 
This study demonstrates the ability of the Orbitrap Astral to rapidly and comprehensively profile the proteome of multiple
samples. This technique was applied to analyse the effects of ASNase across various mouse organs. First, the paper fine-
tunes the experimental and MS parameters, inspired by previous work (Figs. 1–2). Then, it delves into the interpretation of
the multi-organ proteome dataset (Figs. 3–7). Overall, this work clearly showcases the size and scope of the data that can be
generated using current proteome technologies; however, its biological and mechanistic implications remain unclear. I have
two main concerns about the study: 1) the methodological optimisation focuses exclusively on maximising the number of
identified proteins while largely ignoring potential negative consequences for protein quantification, and 2) the paper
extensively describes up- and down-regulated proteins and processes that suggest several mechanistic hypotheses;
however, none of these are tested or related to clinical data. Therefore, while this study can serve as a valuable resource for
the field, it is not sufficiently groundbreaking in terms of either methods or mechanistic insights to warrant publication in this
journal. 
Specific concerns: 
1. The authors develop their workflow geared towards maximizing protein identifications (Fig 1). However, they overlook
how the short (2 min) LC gradient may compromise protein quantification. They claim that observed fold changes reflect
mixing ratios (Fig 1j), however these data are far from impressive, and the claim is only true for the most abundant proteins,
with a strong skew in the lower half of the abundance range (containing ~90% of the data, judging from Fig 1j). The authors
should scrutinize quantitative accuracy and precision in greater detail, at the very least indicating the number of data points
for each peptide used for peptide quantification (ideally reaching at least 5), and plotting number of data points against
deviation from expected ratio. 
2. The authors claim that they have developed a new workflow for rapid sample preparation (Fig 2a), however similar
strategies have been used by several others before using AFA ultrasonication, EvoTips and protein clean up in various
combinations (e.g. PMID 38777088, 32129943, 31885202). Moreover, cleanup in those studies was done on robotic
platforms providing another level of automation and throughput compared to the manual pipetting used here. The authors

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

must present their work in the context of this previous work, not only in the suppl discussion but also in the main text. 
3. A rationale should be provided for the use of severely immuno-deficient NSG mice, and against the use of immuno-
competent mice as a model for the use of ASNase in (clinical) practice. In particular, the immune system may contribute to
the absence of clinical benefit in a ASNase-mutant setting, which is completely missed in the current model. 
4. The authors observe a striking effect for ASNS dynamics in the spleen (Fig 4g), and they argue that this may be explained
because of the spleen´s role in regulating the immune system, in particular white blood cells (line 210). This contrasts with
the used mouse model which does not contain T, B and NK cells, and therefore the observed effect may be specific for this
particular model, and whose relevance will need to be confirmed in an immune-competent setting. 
5. Line 216 and following sections: the authors list many proteins indicating adaptive response to ASNase-wt treatment,
however this leaves a fuzzy picture whether these effects are common or unique to the various organs. In addition, the
authors do not mention any ASNase-mut-induced effects, thereby bypassing one of the main motivations of the study to
explain differences in efficacy of wt and mut forms of the treatment (Fig 3a). 
6. Line 309 and further: rather long listing of proteins falling in KEGG categories in previous figures. All in all, this is a long
and mixed set of that associate with wt vs mut ASNase, but causality to efficacy/toxicity remains to be shown. 
7. Many of the figures are very thin in information content or indicate (mostly straightforward) concepts that do not contribute
to assessing performance of methods or interpretation of data, e.g. Fig 1ab, Fig 2ghij, Fig 3bcd, 4a, 6fgh. These can be
easily moved to suppl figs.

proteomics. It presents an impressive large-scale quantitative proteomics profiling of perturbed 
mouse tissues from different organs, utilizing Evosep (>240 SPD) and nDIA with Atral. The

We appreciate the helpful suggestion and agree that the main contribution of our study lies in the 
careful integration and optimization of existing, cutting-edge technologies into a high-throughput, 
robust, and scalable workflow suitable for systems-level biological applications. As suggested, we 
have removed the "UFP" acronym throughout the manuscript ( lines 27, 36, and 73) and shifted 
the focus toward the practical capabilities and application. 
2, How were batch effects controlled during the processing of 507 samples? Include quality 
control metrics (e.g., CVs for technical replicates). 
We thank the reviewer for this important question. To minimize batch effects, all 507 samples 
were processed in a single, coordinated workflow using consistent protocols for tissue lysis, 
digestion, and peptide cleanup. LC-MS analysis was performed in a continuous sequence, 
ensuring analytical consistency across the entire batch. 
As suggested, we calculated coefficient of variation (CV) values for the three technical replicates 
in each sample group. As shown in Figure R1, median CVs were below 30% across all tissues 
and below 20% in most tissues—including SQWAT, GAT, lung, spleen, stomach, large intestine, 
heart, muscle, and bone marrow—demonstrating strong reproducibility and minimal technical 
variation. Detailed CV data are provided in the revised Supplementary Table 10.

3, Longitudinal analysis across multiple time points (0, 1, 3, 5, 8 days) lacks clarity. Were mixed-
effects models or repeated-measures ANOVA used to account for temporal dependencies? 
We thank the reviewer for prompting clarification of the analysis. Since individual mice were not 
sampled across multiple time points, we have revised the text to indicate the cross-sectional 
nature of the design, removing “longitudinal.” Given this design, statistical methods that account 
for repeated measures or temporal dependencies (e.g., mixed-effects models or repeated-
measures ANOVA) were not applicable. We have clarified this in the revised Methods (lines 188–
194). 
4, The criteria for differential protein expression (fold change >2/<0.5, p <0.05) may inflate false 
positives in large datasets. Adjustment of p values to correct for multiple-testing errors is essential.  
We thank the reviewer for this valuable comment. As suggested, we have calculated Benjamini-
Hochberg adjusted p-values (FDR) for all proteins and included them in the revised 
Supplementary Table 10 to improve statistical transparency. 
However, due to the limited number of biological replicates (n = 3 per group), FDR correction 
resulted in very few (or no) proteins meeting the adjusted significance threshold (FDR < 0.05). 
This limitation is well-recognized in proteomics studies with small sample sizes, where multiple 
testing correction methods—such as BH-FDR—can be overly conservative and substantially 
increase false negative rates (Pascovici et al., Proteomics, 2016).

Therefore, in line with common practice in the field (e.g., Tyanova et al., Nature Protocols, 2016; 
Schork et al., Springer Protocols, 2021; Levin et al., Proteomics, 2011 ), we retained the 
original criteria of fold change >2 or <0.5 and unadjusted p-value <0.05 for the main differential 
expression analysis and visualizations. Nonetheless, adjusted p-values are provided in 
Supplementary Table 10  for transparency and for readers who wish to apply more stringent 
thresholds. 
We believe this approach provides an appropriate balance between statistical rigor and biological 
interpretability, and we appreciate the reviewer’s suggestion, which has strengthened the clarity 
and robustness of our analysis. 
5, The link between ASNase-induced proteomic changes (e.g., ferroptosis in spleen) and clinical 
toxicity (e.g., VTE) is correlative. Are functional assays (e.g., ROS measurement, clotting assays) 
or in vivo toxicity models provided to validate these hypotheses? 
We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment and agree that mass spectrometry (MS) 
proteomic data alone are merely correlative. As suggested by the reviewer, to test the hypothesis 
that ASNase induces ferroptosis in the spleen, we performed new in vivo experiments. 
Three groups of mice (n = 3 per group) were treated with (1) vehicle, (2) 20,000 IU/kg ASNase, 
or (3) 20,000 IU/kg ASNase plus 10 mg/kg liproxstatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor. Spleen tissue 
samples were collected on day 8. We measured malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a widely used 
marker of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, using the Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (Abcam, 
Cat. No. ab118970). In support of the hypothesis, ASNase markedly increased MDA levels 
compared to vehicle, and liproxstatin-1 co-treatment decreased MDA ( Figure R2 and 
Supplementary Figure 23). These results confirm that ASNase can induce lipid peroxidation and 
ferroptosis in the spleen.

We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment and question. While our study primarily focuses

In the original manuscript, we had outlined several translational opportunities, including clinical 
evaluation of INBRX-101 in combination with ASNase to mitigate VTE side effects (lines 442-448)

side effects in patients receiving ASNase. 
2) Biomarker-guided monitoring: MDA and related lipid peroxidation markers could serve as early

Minor issues: 
1, The rationale for selecting 13 tissues (e.g., why include adipose depots but exclude pancreas?) 
is unclear. 
We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful comment. The tissues analyzed in this study were 
collected as part of a coordinated effort across several complementary projects. In designing the 
collection strategy, we prioritized tissues most relevant to ASNase metabolism and toxicity, while 
also ensuring high sample quality and availability. This allowed us to include key organs such as 
liver, spleen, kidney, bone marrow, and metabolically important adipose depots. Due to prior 
sample allocation and availability constraints, pancreas samples were not included in this analysis. 
2, How does the 1-Th isolation window in nDIA affect peptide identification compared to wider 
windows? Were spectral libraries required, and how were they generated? 
We appreciate the reviewer for this thoughtful question. The raw MS datasets in this study were 
processed using DIA-NN in spectral library-free mode, as employed in several recent proteomics

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

studies (Guzman et al., Nat. Biotechnol. 2024; Fu et al., Angew. Chem. 2024; Petrosius et 
al., Mol. Cell. Proteomics 2025 ). Specifically, DIA-NN generated a predicted spectral library in 
silico from the mouse protein sequence database, without requiring experimental DDA libraries 
(Demichev et al., Nat. Methods 2019). 
Regarding the isolation window, we evaluated windows ranging from 0.8-Th to 2-Th on peptide 
and protein identifications ( Figure 1f). While the 2-Th window identified the greatest number of 
peptides, the 1-Th window yielded slightly greater protein identifications. Specifically, across 
replicates, the 1-Th window identified an average of 6,797 protein groups and 45,638 peptides, 
whereas the 2-Th window identified 6,658 protein groups and 46,545 peptides. Pairwise overlap 
analysis showed that 75.1% of peptides were shared between the two methods, with 11% 
uniquely identified by 1-Th and 13.9% uniquely by 2-Th. Further analysis shows that unique 
peptides identified by 1-Th and 2-Th method exhibited similar distribution of length (Figure R4). 
We chose the 1-Th isolation window in this study to maximize protein-level identification. However, 
we acknowledge that optimal window size depends on study goals: for peptide-level analyses 
such as post-translational modifications or immunopeptidomics, or for low-input samples, a 2-Th 
or wider window may be advantageous. 
We greatly appreciate the reviewer’s insightful feedback, which has helped us strengthen the 
methodological rigor and clarity of our manuscript. Relevant descriptions were added in the 
revised manuscript (lines 380-386).

3, Tissue-specific protein changes (e.g., spleen vs. liver) might reflect baseline inter-tissue

We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful comment. In our analyses, each tissue type included its 
own vehicle-treated control group (Figure 3b and Supplementary Table 10). All reported protein 
expression changes were derived from pairwise comparisons between ASNase-treated and 
vehicle-treated samples within the same tissue. This within-tissue comparison design minimizes 
the impact of baseline inter-tissue variability and ensures that the observed changes primarily

4, The metabolomics data show GSH depletion in the spleen (Fig. 7e), but how does this 
quantitatively correlate with ferroptosis markers (e.g., lipid peroxidation assays)? 
We thank the reviewer for this insightful question. The metabolomic data shows glutathione (GSH) 
depletion in the spleen following ASNase treatment ( Figure 7e ), which is a key biochemical 
hallmark of ferroptosis. To evaluate how GSH depletion correlates with functional ferroptosis 
markers, we have now measured malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using the Lipid Peroxidation 
(MDA) Assay Kit (Abcam, Cat. No. ab118970) in the same experimental conditions (Figure R2). 
Consistent with the observed GSH depletion, MDA levels were markedly elevated in ASNase-
treated mice compared to vehicle controls, indicating increased lipid peroxidation. Co-treatment 
with liproxstatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, restored MDA levels toward baseline. These results 
demonstrate a quantitative and functional correlation between GSH depletion and ferroptosis-
specific lipid peroxidation in the spleen, supporting the mechanistic link between ASNase-induced 
metabolic changes and ferroptotic cell death.
