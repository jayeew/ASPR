Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Through simulations and experiments, the authors study Quincke colloids, which behave as self-oscillating units (active
oscillators). In this study, different collective states are observed, including temporal synchronization and directional
alignment, termed "synchronematic order". The authors distinguish between fluid-like clusters, where this order is short-
range, and crystalline clusters, where the system is characterized by global synchronization and circular alignment. 
This paper reveals interesting collective phenomena arising in Quincke colloids. The manuscript is well-written, even if a
careful revision of all the formulas (mainly in the methods) is needed -- several typos in the equations are present. This
paper is potentially suitable for publication in Nature Communications only if the authors address my questions and clarify
the mechanisms generating their experimental results. 
- I believe that this paper would benefit from a restyling: the authors should introduce sections, distinguishing between
introduction, results (perhaps with subsections), and discussion. 
- Could the authors clarify what they mean when they write "these spatiotemporal structures arise from different initial
conditions ..." or "By varying the initial particle distribution ..."? This point should be explained with more details in the main
text and methods. Do these different phases emerge when different packing fractions are considered? The authors should
clarify the experimental limitations: are the authors able to control the density in their system? What do the authors mean by
the sentence "when initialized within local regions of high density ..."? 
- The fluid-cluster snapshots (Fig.1 e and Fig.2 a) do not show a cluster with a fluid structure but (apparently) a
homogeneous fluid. I suggest that the authors show both an image where the cluster is evident and, additionally, the already
present zoom, which focuses on the internal structure (Fig. 2 a). 
- The phase-angle spatial order (exponential decay) resembles the short-range order observed in the velocity field of self-
propelled systems (without alignment interactions): 
* L Caprini, U Marini Bettolo Marconi, A Puglisi. Phys. Rev. Lett. 124 (7), 078001, 2020 "Spontaneous velocity alignment in
motility-induced phase separation" 
Could the authors comment on these previous results and provide an intuitive argument to explain the origin of the observed
phenomenon? 
- Concerning the synchronematic crystalline phase, is the phenomenon observed a result of finite-size effects? In other
words, does this effect disappear for larger cluster sizes? Alternatively, one can wonder if, in the absence of cluster
boundaries -- say, by considering a homogeneous crystal (in simulations) with periodic boundary conditions -- the same
synchronization effect appears. I expect that this will not be the case: this point should be clarified. Somehow, this state is
reminiscent of the self-reverting vorticity observed in clusters consisting of chiral active particles: 
* L Caprini, B Liebchen, H Löwen. Commun. Phys. 7 (1), 153, 2024 "Self-reverting vortices in chiral active matter" 
The authors may comment on this analogy in the final discussion. 
Minor points: 
- Along with the paper, the authors mention "close-packed oscillating crystals". Actually, I believe that it is more correct that
the authors refer to this state as "oscillating clusters with a crystalline structure".

(Remarks to the Author) 
The present manuscript is a combined experimental/theoretical study concerning the collective dynamics of oscillatory
Quincke rollers. Specifically, the authors study spherical particles that, under an applied electric field, exhibit a back-and-
forth rolling motion characterized by frequency, phase, amplitude, and axis n. They find that a semi-dilute monolayer of these
particles can form liquid-like phases with short-ranged synchronization and clustering (Fig. 1e, Fig. 2b). A more dense
monolayer forms rocking crystallites with circular alignment of the particles’ rocking directions (Fig. 3b). Numerical modeling
reveals that the behavior arises from the interplay of hydrodynamic interactions and dipole-dipole interactions, with an
additional role for electroosmotic attraction for the crystallites. 
Excellent quantitative agreement (Fig. 3d-e) is obtained
between the numerics and experiment with seemingly limited fitting. 
The study seems thorough and scientifically sound (although I have some questions, as detailed below). Turning to the
question of impact/significance: while the actual rocking motion seems, to honest, underwhelming in videos, what I judge the
authors to have achieved in this manuscript is linking temporal (synchronization) and spatial (clustering, alignment) forms of
ordering. I think this will open new directions in out-of-equilibrium colloidal self-organization. However, to make this case
more effectively, the authors need to situate their work with respect to previous work on “swarmalators” and chiral active
matter: the manuscript is missing, e.g., citations of D. Levis and J. Lintuvuori. How does the present work go beyond
previous work that links flocking and synchronization in circle swimmers? (In addition, I suggest citing the work on AC

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript investigates synchronization phenomena in colloidal Quincke rollers subjected to a DC electric field. By
varying confinement geometry, particle density, and field strength, the authors report the emergence of collective oscillatory
states and identify distinct synchronization regims. The topic is interesting, as it links active matter physics with non–linear
dynamics and synchronization theory. 
As experimental platform the authors use Quincke rollers, a well–established active colloid system, and the reported
oscillatory states are convincingly documented. The authors employ systematic variation of control parameters and
characterize the resulting collective dynamics through frequency distributions, order paramters, and phase diagrams. The
work demonstrates the potential of Quincke rollers as a model system for collective oscillator dynamics, which will be of
interest to researchers accross soft matter and statistical physics. The manuscript is clearly written and the figures are

informative, enhancing accessibility beyond the immediate field. At the same time, several points require clarification and
improvment before a final decision can be made. 
1. Although the discussion mentions Kuramoto-type models, the connection remains largely qualitative. A systematic
embedding—such as a reduced phase-oscillator model with an explicit coupling function—would significantly enhance the
interpretation and help place the results within the broader synchronization literature. 
2. Is synchronization bidirectionally coupled to the particle interactions? Or in other words: does the particle denisty depend
on the degree of synchronization? In particular in view of generalized swarmalator models, this would be an important point. 
3. The novelty of the present findings should be sharpened by explicitly stating how these oscillatory states differ from known
roller collectives such as flocks, vortices, or oscillations near electrodes. 
4. The phase diagrams provide a useful overview but remain descriptive. Some physical rationale for the observed transition
boundaries would add depth—for instance, scaling arguments based on hydrodynamic or electrostatic interaction strengths,
or density-dependent coupling ranges. 
5. Hydrodynamic couplings are invoked as a possible mechanism, yet they are not quantified. Without controlled pair
experiments, simulations, or order-of-magnitude estimates, their role relative to electrostatic or steric effects remains
speculative. Even an approximate analysis would strengthen the mechanistic discussion. 
In summary, the manuscript reports interesting and carefully executed experiments, but remains primarily descriptive in its
current version. The manuscript would largely benefit from a stronger theoretical embedding into existing frameworks and
quantification of relevant coupling mechanisms.

“Reduced-Order Swarmalator Model for Quincke Oscillators”, Figure 5, and Supplementary Notes

more direct comparisons to other simplified active matter models, such as that of Caprini et al.

Reply: We have addressed these questions using the new reduced-order model that governs
the dynamics of oscillator phases and orientations. Simulations of finite-size crystals show that
phase-locked states with circular alignment are stable only below a critical size (Figure 5c,d).
The competition between reciprocal phase interactions (which favor synchronization) and non-
reciprocal phase interactions (which accelerate oscillations) produces radial phase gradients within
the crystals: the phase of oscillators near the center or edge lag behind those in intermediate
regions. As the cluster size increases, these gradients grow and eventually destabilize the global
phase-locked state.
Simulations on a square lattice with periodic boundary conditions confirm that the circular
synchronization observed in finite clusters does not persist in homogeneous systems (see Supple-
mentary Note 7). In these periodic lattices, global phase and orientational order are disrupted by
the spontaneous formation of topological defects in both the orientation and phase fields, resem-
bling the defect-mediated disorder observed in the classical XY model. In one-dimensional chains,
the same coupling mechanisms give rise to traveling metachronal waves, reminiscent of those seen
in models of hydrodynamically coupled cilia.
We have added a comment to the conclusions highlighting connections to similar types of or-
der observed in active matter systems, including that reported by Caprini et al. (new reference
#53). Unlike that work, which considers polar, chiral active particles interacting through isotropic
potentials, our Quincke oscillators are apolar and achiral, possess an internal phase variable, and
interact via anisotropic, non-reciprocal hydrodynamic couplings. These differences in symmetry
and interaction type lead to qualitatively distinct forms of collective behavior.
