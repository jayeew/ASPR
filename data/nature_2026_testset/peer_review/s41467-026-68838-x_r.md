Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Unlike many natural phenomena, evolution involves a tension between chance and necessity. Natural selection necessarily
drives increases in fitness, but the variation it works upon is generated by stochastic mutation, and any variants can be lost
to random genetic drift. At the same time, the environment exerting selection is highly changeable. Stephen Jay Gould
famously argued that the involvement of chance makes evolution fundamentally contingent. Gould was wobbly on exactly
what he meant, but philosophers have come to define contingency in an evolutionary context as meaning that evolutionary
outcomes depend on exact historical sequences of events that could have occurred differently, and that different histories
can lead to different outcomes. Gould’s work has spurred many studies aiming to test the contingency of evolution, with
many being based in the use of experimental evolution with microbes and other fast-growing organisms. The backbone of
these studies have been efforts to instantiate his thought experiment of “replaying life’s tape”, in which he suggested that,
were one to restart evolution in the past and then have it proceed again to the present day, each replay would yield a
different result owing to chance differences in history. These studies have made use of experiments in which initially
identical replicate populations are allowed to evolve under the same conditions, and then the divergence or convergence of
their evolution is examined at different levels 
This manuscript details a large-scale study of the evolution of transcriptional changes in experimental populations of a
variety of organisms during adaptation to 22 different environmental conditions in the course of many published experiments.
It thus examines remarkably broad diversity of organisms and conditions for convergent evolution at the level of gene
expression. Under these conditions, divergence between replicates with and among experiments and organisms would be
consistent with gene expression evolution being contingent on the evolutionary histories underlying them. By contrast,
repeated, convergent evolution would suggest more deterministic evolution driven by natural selection regardless of
historical differences. The authors found a remarkable level of evolutionary repetition in gene expression, both between and
among the evolving populations examined that suggests a previously unexpected degree of determinism in phenotypic
evolution at that level. They propose that the findings may reflect the many-to-on nature of the genotype-phenotype-fitness
map, such that the mutational targets are large enough that phenotypic evolution can be highly repeatable, even when
genotypic evolution is not. They conclude that these findings indicate that the scope for contingency in evolution may be
more limited than thought. 
Overall, this is a remarkable and impressive piece of work. The scope of the research is astonishing, honestly. The paper is
also clear and well-written. The methods, data collection, and analyses make sense to me, and I have no real suggestions
there. I really only have some relatively minor, pedantic quibbles that I think the authors should take into consideration in
interpreting their results, and which suggest that their conclusions should be toned down a bit to be less categorical. I want
to stress, though, that my issues are genuinely minor, and go to the complexity of the topic. Please do not take them as major
criticisms of the work itself, which, again, is spectacular, but simply the take of someone who has spent far too much time
thinking about historical contingency in evolution than is likely healthy. 
I’ll start on a pedantic point about the description of Gould’s thought experiment in the manuscript. Gould was not especially
clear on contingency, even when describing his thought experiment. He actually presents and conflates two alternatives.
The first is, as the authors say, that different outcomes would results from replays from the exact same initial conditions. But
he then suggests that different outcomes would stem from differences in the initial conditions. Which he meant is still
arguable. The existence of some difference in the history of the replicates, whenever that difference comes in, and no matter

its source, is the important thing. This interpretation is consistent with the current understanding of contingency as
elaborated by philosophers of science, John Beatty and Eric Desjardins, that it is a property of historical causal systems in
which the past influences what can happen (path dependence) and different histories or event paths are possible, with at
least some of those different histories leading to different outcomes. (See: https://doi.org/10.1163/187226311X599916 and
https://doi.org/10.1007/s10539-011-9256-4) Contingency is not, as it is put in the introduction, simply that a mutation can
influence future evolution. 
Chance is often improperly conflated with contingency, but there are not the same. Chance is necessary for contingency, but
it is not contingency itself. Chance enables contingency by injecting unpredictability at nodal points in event paths, thus
allowing evolution to go down one path versus another depending on which of various possible events by chance occur at
the nodes. (Chance brings in a form of probabilistic causal dependence, which, along with path dependence, is necessary
for contingency, but contingency is an effect of how those two things interact, and is not synonymous with either one alone.)
Inputs of chance include those that are intrinsic to the core evolutionary processes, those coming from the stochasticity of
mutation and genetic drift, for instance. However, they also include chance coming from outside processes, including
coincidental, sometimes stochastic environmental shifts and the evolution of other populations making up the biotic
environment. Lab evolution experiments generally insulate study populations from such environmental sources of chance. It
is important to take that insulation into account when interpreting results because environmental sources of chance are likely
the most important in evolution. Where lab experiments are most important, typically, is in assessing the scope for
contingency in evolution owing to the intrinsic sources of chance within the core evolutionary processes. Hence, the
following part of discussion is a bit of a problem: 
“The relatively high evolutionary repeatability of gene expression discovered in the present work contrasts the prior
observations from comparative studies, suggesting that the seemingly low evolutionary repeatability of gene expression in
natural evolution is likely du to the variation in genotype and/or environment across evolutionary lineages (i.e., a lack of
opportunity for repeated evolution) rather than a genuinely low repeatability. In other words, the relative importance of
chance in evolution may have been overestimated.” 
This statement is problematic because it basically says that chance (here seemingly standing in for contingency) has an
overstated importance in evolution because evolution is likely highly repeatable if you exclude major sources of chance. It is
interesting that one of Gould’s points was that evolution’s causal structure might be such that there are regularities that
create repetition and predictability, but random environmental shifts and things like asteroid impacts disrupt them, making it
all unpredictable again. This subtlety needs to be taken into consideration. I think a good argument can be made that the
results do suggest that there is more predictability intrinsic to the core evolutionary processes than previously considered,
but that conclusion should carry the caveat that the results do not take into consideration environmental sources of chance.
Even so, one of the lessons from the Lenski experiment, especially the citrate work, is that, when only the core-most
evolutionary processes are operating, history may not have much of an effect on how evolution plays out most of the time,
because natural selection drives populations along evolutionary paths that generally lead to similar places. However,
sometimes history matters a great deal because evolution happens to take a path that leads to a very different and
consequential place/outcome, as occurred with the LTEE population in which aerobic growth on citrate evolved. 
Another facet of contingency that needs to be taken into consideration is the meaning of “outcome”. Evolution in nature does
not end. Outcomes are just intermediate points to later evolution. That facile observation is relevant because natural
selection may lead to the repeated evolution of similar phenotypes via different histories and based in different genotypes,
but those underlying differences might affect subsequent evolution in unpredictable ways. I suppose a way of thinking of this
is that evolutionary repetition could be transient, or that the apparent repetitions are not repetitions at all, but merely rhymes. I
think that this possibility might be particularly relevant when interpreting the results here, or at least considered as a caveat. 
The above dealing with fundamental concepts about contingency and how they might alter the implications of the findings
here is my principal issue. I am just laying them out there and merely asking that the authors consider being a bit more
precise in how they deal with contingency in the text, and then to reconsider just how categorical their presented
conclusions are. Beyond the above, I have a few small, minor issues, which I list below.

The reviewer asked how the large number of gene expression changes observed in some 
experimental evolution studies could be explained by mutations. In fact, a previous study 
addressed this question explicitly and showed that, by adding one mutation that was observed in 
E. coli experimental evolution to the ancestral strain, over a thousand genes changed their 
expressions
1. Hence, accumulated mutations in experimental evolution can cause expression 
changes of hundreds to thousands of genes.   
 
The reviewer wondered whether some of the gene expression changes observed in experimental 
evolution could be caused by phenotypic plasticity. We note that all studies profiled gene 
expressions in the new environment after some time of acclimation presumably till the plastic 
changes are induced, because all studies attempted to quantify gene expression evolution in the 
new environment. For instance, in the study aforementioned
1, the E. coli population was 
expression-profiled after growing for 1 day in the new environment, likely sufficient for a full 
induction of plastic gene expression changes. When a study contained several acclimation times, 
we always used the transcriptome after the longest acclimation to allow the fullest induction of 
plastic changes. While we cannot guarantee that the acclimation time used is sufficient in every 
study, we found high repeatability of gene expression evolution across all studies—including 
those that clearly had a sufficient time for acclimation such as the study mentioned earlier
1.
