Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper presents a timely analysis of the systemic benefits of using UHPFRC as a structural intervention strategy for
national bridge networks. Using the Swiss motorway system as a case study, the authors offer a well-structured, and
methodologically rigorous comparison between the UHPFRC method and conventional demolition-reconstruction
strategies, evaluated across both environmental (carbon) and economic (life-cycle cost) dimensions. 
The manuscript demonstrates that UHPFRC intervention is technically feasible for nearly 100% of Switzerland’s 3,903
motorway bridges. 
Projected carbon savings of up to 7.7 MtCO
₂
-eq and cost savings of 18.5 billion CHF over 80 years are robustly estimated
through life-cycle analyses. 
The results are supported by a unique dataset of 17 realized case studies and draw from an extensive reference base,
including Swiss national inspection and budgeting records. 
The contribution is scaling prior localized findings to the network level, which is crucial for infrastructure policy decisions.
The combination of technical feasibility mapping, life-cycle impact assessment, and network-scale management modeling
represents an advancement over existing literature, which has typically remained at the case or structural-element scale. 
This work is of direct interest to fields including structural engineering, sustainability science, infrastructure economics, and
civil policy. 
The methodological framework is sound and clearly documented. The use of scenarios (theoretical lifespan, degradation-
driven replacement, and constant budget) provides both upper and lower bounds for likely futures. 
The assumptions (e.g., discount rates, costs per m², GWP factors) are transparent and referenced to Swiss norms and
databases (e.g., KBOB, Ecoinvent). 
Supplementary tables (e.g., environmental impact factors, case study inventories) are detailed and sufficient to enable
replication or adaptation in other contexts. 
Areas for improvements: 
1. The case study is focused on Switzerland. In my opinion the broader relevance of the findings would be strengthened by a
short discussion on: 
o Variations in material supply chains and construction practices in other countries. 
o Applicability of UHPFRC in different climate zones (e.g., humid tropical, seismic zones), where deterioration profiles or
material behavior may differ. 
o Possible limitations in regions without access to industrial UHPFRC production or trained labor. 
2. The manuscript correctly mentions that UHPFRC interventions reduce construction time and enable continued bridge use.
If available, qualitative or semi-quantitative discussion of traffic disruption, user costs, or emissions from detours would make
the societal value proposition even clearer. 
3. The fixed annual budget of 100 MCHF and 4:1 cost ratio between demolition and UHPFRC intervention are plausible but
impactful assumptions. A brief sensitivity check (even qualitative) would help readers understand how robust the findings
are to budget fluctuations or cost inflation. 
4. Minor Editorial Suggestions 
o There are a few typos (e.g., “asphalte” in Section 4.2 → “asphalt”; consider rephrasing line 79 for readability). 
o Consider merging some dense introductory sentences (lines 7–13) to improve accessibility for interdisciplinary readers.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Key results 
This paper explores potential reductions for GWP and costs in bridge management by implementing ultra-high-performance
fiber-reinforced cementitious (UHPFRC) composite for the repair of bridges. This approach is compared to the conventional
practice in Switzerland at the end of the service life of bridges: demolition and construction of a new bridge. The authors
calculate GWP and costs for both alternatives both at asset and network levels, providing valuable insights for infrastructure
management, which is a sector where environmental considerations are often secondary. 
Validity 
The interpretation of the data and the conclusions drawn from it appear to be valid and robust for the most part. However,
some aspects should be clarified or strengthened to improve the robustness and transparency further: 
- Relevance of the construction of year of the bridge to determine the applicability of the UHPFRC method (lines 128-132):
The importance attributed to the construction year as a criterion for UHPFRC applicability is not fully convincing. Other
factors mentioned (e.g., number of spans, bridge type, etc.) seem more directly relevant. Please clarify why the construction
year is considered a meaningful criterion and how it contributes information not already captured by the other ratios. 
- Source of environmental data for new bridges (lines 182-183): The environmental data of the mentioned sources appear to
be from UK-based studies. Given potentially significant differences in materials, construction practices, and energy mixes (to
name a few) between the UK and Switzerland, it is unclear whether these values can be assumed in a Swiss study. Please
justify the choice of these sources, discuss any limitations (if any) in applying them to the Swiss context, and, if possible,
reflect these limitations in the interpretation of the results. 
- Correlation between cost and GWP (lines 272-273): The statement suggesting a direct correlation between the two
indicators is not fully supported by the presented results. The scenario with the highest GWP savings did not lead to the
highest cost savings. This statement should be either be revised or explained in greater detail to reflect the nuances in the
findings. 
Furthermore, some methodology-related points require attention (see “Data and methodology” below). 
Significance 
The conclusions drawn from the study have the potential to be significant for the field of bridge construction and
management, particularly due to the integration of sustainability-related considerations (GWP) into long-term infrastructure
planning. While the application of UHPFRC in bridge repair has been addressed in previous studies, this manuscript is, to
my knowledge, the first to explicitly incorporate a network-level perspective, which is a critical but often overlooked aspect in
environmental assessments of infrastructure. 
However, the overall significance of the work depends on clarifying certain methodological choices and addressing key
issues identified in the sections “Validity”, “Data and methodology”, and “Suggested improvements”. Resolving these points
would significantly strengthen the contribution of the study in both academia and practical applications. 
Data and methodology 
In general, the data used in the study appears to be robust. However, several points should be clarified to enhance
transparency and consistency: 
- The selection of environmental data sources for the new bridges should be briefly discussed and justified, especially given
potential regional differences (see comment in section “Validity”). 
- In the supplementary information, the authors refer to GWP values as “impact factors”. While this term occasionally appears
in literature, it is not technically correct in the context of LCA. The term “GWP” would be more appropriate. Additionally, in the
column “unit” of the same table, “GWP” is included as an unit, which is not correct. 
- The unit of GWP is written as kgCO2,eq. This notation is non-standard. Preferred and commonly accepted notations
include kgCO2eq or kg CO2 eq. 
- Line 186: Instead of “phases”, the proper terminology here would be "information module" or "stage". Please specify which
LCAs address these information modules - is it the LCAs conducted here or those from the reference? 
Regarding methodology, several inconsistencies in the application and terminology of the LCA and LCC methods need to
be addressed, either through correction or further clarification: 
- The term “life-cycle assessment” is not used correctly (especially in Section 4.2, lines 335-336). The writing in the
manuscript suggests that “life-cycle assessment” compares costs (LCC) and environmental impacts (LCA). This is not
correct. Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) refers to the method to quantify the environmental impacts of a product/project/etc. Life
cycle costs (LCC) is a method to quantify the costs of a product/project/etc over its life cycle. Please address this issue. 
- Lines 337-338: The statement regarding LCA standardization should be revised. LCA is standardized at a general level by
ISO 14040 and ISO 14044. While often used as reference for LCA of construction products, the EN 15804 is actually a norm
for Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs). None of these norms are related to LCC. 
- Lines 344-345: If modules A4-A5 are included in the assessment, it should be clearly specified that also the transport to the
construction site as well as the construction process are considered, not just production and transportation. 
- Lines 345-349: The system boundaries of the UHPFRC intervention are not clear. Is the repair (module B3) considered OR
a production, construction and EoL (A1-A5 and C1-C4)? I recommend aligning this with EN 15804, which provides clear
guidance on module definitions. 
- Line 357: This sentence is too general and can be misleading. LCA encompasses multiple impact categories. Since only
GWP is addressed in the study, the authors should state that this is sole environmental indicator analyzed. 
- Line 358: The term “life-cycle-impact inventories” is unclear. According to the ISO 14040 and ISO 14044, there are either
life cycle impacts or life cycle inventories. Please clarify the meaning. 
- Line 364: It would improve transparency to specify the type of fuel used for transport and the emission standard assumed
for the trucks (e.g., EURO class).

- Lines 365-366: The system boundaries and cost categories used in the LCC should be clearly mentioned in the text. It is
unclear, for instance, if labor costs were considered in the assessment. Also, were there any cost categories that were the
same for all explored variants or are they all different? Additionally, please specify the reference year used for the
conversion from CHF to USD to support reproducibility. 
- Lines 366-367: While confidentiality constraints are acknowledged, citing sources for the documented case studies
mentioned in the text would strengthen the transparency of the study. 
- Lines 369-370: The sentence about normalization is not accurate. According to ISO 14044, normalization refers to the
comparison of LCA results to a reference system (e.g., total EU environmental load per capita). This should be corrected or
rephrased. 
- Line 371: The term "linear life cycle analysis" is non-standard and remains unclear, even with the accompanying
explanation. Please clarify the intended meaning or use recognized terminology. 
Analytical approach 
The analysis of the results appears to be robust. However, its overall strength is limited by several inconsistencies and gaps
in the application and documentation of the LCA and LCC methods, as noted in the sections “Data and methodology” and
“Validity”. In particular, unclear system boundaries, imprecise terminology and the lack of detail on certain assumptions (e.g.,
environmental data sources and cost categories) limit the reproducibility and transparency of the analytical approach.
Addressing these issues would significantly enhance the credibility and rigor of the analysis. 
Suggested improvements 
The applicability analysis appears to be based on optimistic or best-case scenarios in certain instances (e.g., year of
construction and bridge type). To strengthen the robustness of the conclusions, the authors could include a sensitivity
analysis covering possible different applicability ratios and provide a more nuanced understanding of the method’s potential
at network level. 
Clarity and context 
The text is generally clear and well structured. While there are a few minor issues in wording (in addition to the terminology
issues mentioned in other sections), they do not significantly hinder comprehension. Nevertheless, they should be
addressed. These issues are mentioned below: 
- Some repetition could be found in the text. Please consider revising the text to reduce redundancy. 
- Please harmonize the used terminology. For instance, sometimes “UHPFRC-method” or “UHPFRC method” are used. 
- Line 35: Please be mindful of the term “sustainable” as it is very broad. Please specify which sustainability dimension is
being addressed. 
- Lines 45-46: The sentence is confusing and does not seem to fit the flow of the text. 
- Line 61: Please clarify what is meant by “structural design” in the provided context. Does it refer to the assessment of
structural design processes with LCA or the assessment of UHPFRC structures themselves? 
- Lines 75-76: Fig. 1 was not mentioned/referenced in the text 
- Line 78: While insightful, it is not clear whether Section 2.1 can be considered as part of the results chapter. It does not
appear to present new findings, but rather an overview of the application of the UHPFRC method. 
- Line 88: Please specify what a grade of 1 means. 
- Lines 122-149: The names of the ratios differ from those given in Section 4.3. Please harmonize the terminology and revise
notation to ensure consistency across sections. 
- Lines 140-141: Please revise the wording of the phrase “By examining in detail these bridges in detail…”. 
- Lines 153-154: Please explain what is meant with “misconceptions at the design stage”. 
The discussion section could benefit from a stronger connection to existing literature. Currently, it is not clear whether the
absence of such references is due to a lack of comparable studies or simply an omission. If no previous work exists, the
authors should explicitly say so, which would ultimately highlight even more the novelty of the study. Otherwise, it would
strengthen the manuscript to contextualize the findings by referencing and comparing them to relevant studies in the field. 
References 
The provided references appear to be appropriate. However, if similar studies have been previously conducted, these
should be included in the references (see section “Clarity and context”). 
Expertise 
I feel confident in my assessment of all parts of the manuscript.
