Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present their work on a phase engineering approach of van der waals crystals which allowed them to achieve
and demonstrate the tailoring of the ferroic behavior of CuInP2S(1-x)Se(x)6 from ferroelectricity to relaxor ferroelectricity to
superparaelectricity. The authors postulate and demonstrate that substitution of S atoms with Se will lead to a mixed phase
system of monoclinic and trigonal phases which can reveal new and interesting physical properties. They were able to show
a mixed phase diagram with support from TEM imaging to show the presence of possible polar nanoregions due to the
phase mixture, which they attribute as the main contributing factor to the observation of the relaxor phase. Although the
results seem to have some novelty, a careful evaluation of this manuscript raises several questions that must be first
answered before it can be considered for publication in a high impact factor journal such as Nature Communications. The
following lists my main concerns: 
1. Novelty of the results: The authors claim to have demonstrated relaxor ferroelectrics in low-dimensional materials for the
first time (line 20). There seems to be a convolution between the exact meaning of the terms van der waals crystals (which
are typically bulk crystal) and low-dimensional systems such as monolayers or few layer thick materials. This is important
because as Song et al (Phys. Rev. B 96, 235420, 2017) have shown, the ground state of monolayer CIPSe can be very
different from the bulk CIPSe crystals. In addition, the phase engineering of the multiple phases has been demonstrated
earlier in bulk crystals of CuInP2SxSe(1-x), and phase diagrams of the coexistence of mixed phases and mixed ferroic
behavior - relaxor as well as dipole glassy behavior has been demonstrated in the work by Macutkevic et al. (Phys. Rev. B
78, 064101, 2008). If the current results were demonstrated in a monolayer or few layer thick geometry, the results could
have been considered as novel. Can the authors demonstrate such relaxor behavior in a monolayer or few layers thick
systems and will the phase diagram remain the same in such systems? 
2. Technical questions: 
i. The phase diagram in Fig. 1c was said in the manuscript to be constructed from a combination of the temperature
dependent P-E loops and dielectric permittivity analysis in supplementary Fig. S2 and note S2. However, figure S2 contains
only the P-E loop information wrt temperature. This figure raises several questions. First of all, the x-axis of Fig. S2 is highly
confusing. Second, these P-E loops were taken up to 10C but in the phase-diagram of Fig. 1c, there are data points up to
Room temperature (25C). Can the authors show the RT data as well? if the loops are leaky, maybe a higher frequency might
be considered. Third, it is not immediately clear from the broadening of the loops with temp and corresponding increase in
remanent polarization for x=1.4% how they arrived at the conclusion that the loops resemble relaxor-like? Fourth, what is the
origin of the difference in the shape of the low temperature AFE loops for x=1.4% and x=2.5% with the x=2.5% loops
resembling more of the typical AFE-like loops? 
Did the authors mean to also include the information from supplementary note 5 and corresponding Fig. S8 to help explain
Fig. 1c? The phase diagram should be explained better - it is confusing currently as to how it was arrived at. 
ii. In the supplementary figure 8, the peaks of the real dielectric constant seem to shift towards a lower temperature with an
increase in the frequency, but the typical dielectric behavior is opposite - the peak shifts towards higher temperatures with
increasing frequency. The authors refer to reference 8 in the supplementary to explain this discrepancy. But, from as far as I
can tell Fig. 3 in Ref 8 has the opposite trend to what the authors show here. Can the authors explain this discrepancy?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Yang et al. is entitled “Phase engineering of relaxor ferroelectricity in van der Waals crystal” (id:
NCOMMS-25-70669-T), which realizes the relaxor ferroelectricity in low-dimensional materials by controlling phases. The
phase engineering is realized by partial substitution of S with Se in CuInP2(S1-xSex)6 and establishes competing
coexistence of monoclinic and trigonal phases. This approach reveals a sequential transformation of FE to RFE and SPE
phases at room temperature in the 2D materials. Through experimental techniques like XRD, SAED, TEM images, SHG
signal, and PFM, they tried to establish their goal. The data quality seems sufficient to support the main conclusions. They
use DFT to compare the energy barrier of phase transition and polarization of the two pristine monoclinic and trigonal
phases. 
Most of the manuscript is well written; however, it would benefit from a careful revision to eliminate repetitions, correct typos,
and fix mislabeled references. Before recommending publication in Nature Communications, the authors should consider
the following points. 
i) The authors claim that they have realized the relaxor ferroelectricity in 2D materials for the first time. However, there is a
report that previously indicated the realization of relaxor ferroelectricity in Se-doped 
2D PbTe by Gong et al. (Strain-induced
two-dimensional relaxor ferroelectrics in Se-doped PbTe; DOI: https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.109.054117). It is not clear
why the authors insist on this statement. Either the authors should be clearer with their statement or remove such a
statement. 
ii) As mentioned, the XRD diffraction peaks at 13.4° correspond to the (002) indicates the 2D crystal stacks along the c-axis.
According to the Authors, broadening the (002) diffraction 
peak suggests that atomic substitution induces grain size refinement, interlayer dislocations, and 
amorphous phases for Se content of x = 1.4%. However, when the concentration of Se further increased to x = 2.5% and x =
4.2%, the (002) peak again became sharp in nature. The reason for this anomaly is not discussed in the manuscript. Authors
are advised to discuss this to clear the anomaly. 
iii) Since these are the multilayers of CuInP2(S1-xSex)6. There is a high possibility of having different concentrations of Se
substitution in the different layers, leading to differences in the lattice parameter. Also, as the coupling between the layers is
expected to be weak, a moiré pattern should emerge; did the authors check for it with STM? If they have observed that, then
what is the role of that varying interlayer coupling for this phase transformation? 
iv) Using DFT, the Authors tried to support the easy transition along FE-RFE-SPE. However, the results of Fig. 4(c) and 4(d)
are for pristine CuInP2Se6 and CuInP2S6. Furthermore, this result is derived by connecting the P_down to the P_up
structure. The easy transition along FE-RFE-SPE, using these pristine calculations, does not make strong evidence for the
experimental support. The calculations using CuInP2(S1-xSex)6 structure will give more realistic evidence. 
v) The Authors mentioned that there is a decrease in switching voltages for domain reversal with increasing Se content (Fig.
4b). This claim was valid and consistent from x = 0% and x = 2.5%. However, for x = 4.2%, the switching voltage is higher
than x = 1.4% and 2.5% on the positive side. Please clear this anomalous observation.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors addressed an interesting work on phase engineering of relaxor ferroelectricity in van der
Waals crystals. Authors demonstrated a mixed-phase 2D CuInP2(S1-xSex)6 single crystals where tuning the ratio of
monoclinic and trigonal phases induced superlattice structures and heterogeneous local polar states, enabling transitions
from ferroelectric to relaxor, superparaelectric, or paraelectric behavior through a flattened free-energy profile. Structural,
compositional, electrical, and atomic scale measurements were performed using various characterization techniques.
Temperature-dependent dielectric and ferroelectric measurements confirmed relaxor behavior via hysteresis-free
polarization and frequency dispersion of permittivity, while PFM revealed domain size and coercive voltage evolution.
Integration into memristor devices further showed enhanced analog resistance states and low operating voltages,
highlighting potential for electronic applications. Although the manuscript was interesting. However, I regret that the
explanation and mechanism of the manuscript were not convincing to support many of their major claims. The mechanism of
the study remained confusing due to incomplete quantitative evidence and limited analysis. In its current form, the
manuscript cannot be considered for publication until all the raised concerns were properly addressed. 
Major comments 
1) A central concern was the issue of composition control and quantification. The reason for selecting a non-uniform
substitution trend of the Se ratio into the host lattice was unclear. Typically, to establish compositional trends and phase
boundaries, systematic increment doping strategies should be employed. The current approach raised questions about how
the authors controlled and verified the exact Se content, particularly at the extremely low substitution (around 1.4%) levels
reported. Without clear justification and compositional control, the scientific basis of the correlation between composition and
physical behavior was unclear. 
2) The authors synthesized a series of CuInP2(S1-xSex)6 crystals with the increasing Se content of 0%, 0.3%, 1.4%, 2.5%,
and 4.2%. Based on the formula, the Se should be homogenously substituted rather than the formation of a composite single
crystal, where a mixed region of monoclinic and trigonal would exist with shared boundaries, where two regions apply
interfacial strain. In composites, lattice mismatch at interfaces could generate strain. The authors should discuss why uniform
substitution was pursued over this alternative and whether the substitution was homogeneous or whether the authors
synthesized the composite single crystals. 
3) In Supplementary Table 1, the atomic percentage values of Cu, S, and Se were inconsistent, which challenges the
compositional analysis. EDS alone was insufficient for quantitative determination, especially at such low substitution levels.
The authors should validate their results with more precise techniques, such as the electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA)
technique [Phys. Rev. Materials 3, 084405 (2019)]. 
4) Phase identification was another weak point of the manuscript. The authors showed the coexistence of monoclinic and
trigonal phases, and the proposed superlattice formation, qualitatively. But there was no quantitative presentation, like
reporting of lattice parameters or phase fractions. 
5) Based on the STEM results, the authors attributed the formation of edge dislocations to the Se substitution, but the doping
levels were too low to justify such noticeable defect generation. Alternative explanations, such as strain gradients at grain
boundaries, temperatures, or intrinsic growth-related stresses, must be considered. 
6) Moreover, the authors correlated the relaxor behavior with the formation of the edge dislocations, which again raised
concern about the clear mechanism of the current work. A systematic investigation was required to establish whether these
dislocations were truly induced by substitution or were inherent to the growth process using quantitative defect density
analysis. 
7) The SHG results presented for the pure composition show dark regions that are not explained. Since SHG signals
typically appeared bright in non-centrosymmetric regions where inversion symmetry was broken [Adv. Mater. 35, 2210562
(2023)], the presence of dark regions in nominally ferroelectric domains was relatively confusing. The authors need to
discuss whether these dark regions arose from structural inhomogeneity, phase coexistence, or during the experimental
process. 
8) Peak splitting in current-electric field loops provides direct evidence of antiferroelectric switching. As the switching current
measurements are accessible to the authors. They should include these results to evidence the presence of antiferroelectric-
like behavior by showing the two switching peak characteristics. 
9) The P-E hysteresis loops exhibited noticeable imprint behavior, but the origin was not discussed. Given the presence of
edge dislocations, it was plausible that internal fields from defect dipoles or strain gradients contribute to double-like
hysteresis loops, where pinched hysteresis loops and linked double switching current behaviors were observed with the
formation of dislocations [ACS Appl. Electron. Mater. 6, 8328-8338 (2024)]. The authors should analyze and provide further
discussion on whether the imprint was correlated with dislocation/defect densities. 
10) In Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 1b), the authors claimed that the intensities of the Cu1+ vibration mode at 315 cm-1
weaken with higher Se content, indicating that the mixed S-Se chalcogen environment disrupted the hopping motion of the
Cu1+ cations. However, on a close look, it seems like the S-P-S and Cu+1 peaks were more widened with the increasing Se
content, with Cu+1 nearly comparable in intensity to the intensity of the peak at 346 cm-1. 
11) In Figure S2, the labeling of the horizontal axis (electric field vs. voltage) was unclear. 
12) For fair presentation of the results, the authors should show the full XRD results in wider range.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Tao Yang et al synthesized layered van der Waals CuInP2(S1-xSex)6 crystals with controllable coexistence of the
monoclinic and trigonal phases, which in turn leads to the appearance of relaxor-like ferroelectricity in the phase
coexistence region. This, indeed, can transform the ferrielectric long-range order of CuInP2(S1-xSex)6 into relaxor-type
ferroelectricity with superparaelectric features. As evidence of relaxor-type ferroelectric properties, the hysteresis-less
polarization switching and diffused phase transition were demonstrated. Also, HR-TEM confirms the principal role of edge
dislocations play in formation and stabilization of locally disordered polar structures. In overall, I support authors’ claim that a
phase engineering of layered van der Waals ferrielectrics, opening their great potential for advanced nanoelectronics and
related applications. 
I recommend the work for publication after a major revision. I regard that the formulation of the key findings made in this work
and corresponding terminology should be reconsidered and significantly softened due to the following argumentation 
1) To the best of my understanding and knowledge CuInP2(S1-xSex)6 are ferrielectrics (FI), not ferroelectrics (FE), which is
confirmed by extensive literature. See experimental and theoretical grounds in the works: 
Y. M. Vysochanskii, V. A. Stephanovich, A. A. Molnar, V. B. Cajipe, and X. Bourdon, Raman spectroscopy study of the
ferrielectric-paraelectric transition in layered CuInP2S6. Phys. Rev. B 58, 9119 (1998),
https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.58.9119 
N. Sivadas, P. Doak, and P. Ganesh. Anharmonic stabilization of ferrielectricity in CuInP2Se6. Phys. Rev. Res. 4, 013094
(2022), https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.4.013094 
A. N. Morozovska, E. A. Eliseev, A. Ghosh, M. E. Yelisieiev, Y. M. Vysochanskii, and S. V. Kalinin. Anomalous Polarization
Reversal in Strained Thin Films of CuInP2S6, Phys. Rev. B 108, 054107 (2023)
https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevB.108.054107 
The AFE-FI-FE transitions of hysteresis loops are very clearly seen from Supplementary Fig. 2, that in my opinion, can be
moved to the main text. Also, the figure shows a very significant contribution from the electric conductivity (losses) to the
hysteresis loops. 
2) The ferrielectric 3-well or 4-well free energy potential can be transformed into the relaxor-like relief, but the relative
dielectric permittivity (shown in Fig. 3e) is not very high (or “giant”) as it should be in true relaxors. The diffuse maximum of
permittivity does not exceed the values of 200-350, being much smaller than 104-106 (as in ferroelectric relaxors). While the
frequency dispersion of the permittivity is present in the frequency range 300 Hz – 3 MHz, the dispersion is much weaker
than in ferroelectric relaxors. As a matter of fact, the ferrielectric with a diffuse phase transition and relaxor-like properties is
obtained. 
3) I failed to understand why the author called the behavior shown in the bottom of Fig.3e as superparaelectric (SPE). Here
the relative dielectric permittivity is relatively low; it increases above 40-50 oC. The increase of dielectric permittivity above
40-50 oC can be related to the thermally-activated conductivity (Maxwell-Wagner type effects). This is clearly seen from
Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. 9, where the imaginary part of dielectric permittivity and tangent of dielectric
losses increases strongly above 40-50 oC. True relaxors and superparaelectrics (if any exist) should be dielectrics in the
actual range of these properties’ observation. Also, the polarization in saturation should be very high in a relaxor that
response to the external electric field as a super-dipole with a large dipole moment. However, the polarization shown in
Supplementary Fig. 2 for x=4.2 % is relatively low (below 2 µC/cm2), Thus, authors rather observe some SPE-like features,
but not the SPE state. 
4) The work is very well illustrated, but at the same time, the important detail shown in the figures are not commented neither
in the main text nor in the Supplement.

We sincerely appreciate your professional and insightful comments on the relaxor behavior 
in the few layers. Following your valuable suggestion, we have confirmed the relaxor

reveal progressively reduced ferroelectric domain sizes, indicating the formation of local polar 
nanoregions that are typical of relaxor ferroelectrics (Fig. R2). These results provide further 
evidence that the relaxor ferroelectricity could persists in few layers system.

We truly appreciate your careful reading and fully understand your concerns in Fig. 1c.

Regarding your fourth question, the crystal of 2.6% Se exhibits AFE-like loop with 
noticeable imprint behavior, which can be ascribed to the edge dislocation, as reported
