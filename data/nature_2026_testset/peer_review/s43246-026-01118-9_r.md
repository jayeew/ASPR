Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript by Byoung Ki Choi et al reported the direct spectroscopic evidence of plasmonic polarons established in
self-intercalated layered 1T-TiS2 by using angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), high-resolution electron
energy loss spectroscopy (HR-EELS) and first-principles calculations. In particular, the authors employed the alkali metal
deposition of the surface to control the dielectric screening effect, thereby revealing the plasmonic polaron state with the
plasmon energy ħΩ. Moreover, the authors observed plasmonic dynamics as a function of the temperature and the charge
carrier density. The good qualitative agreement between the ARPES spectra and the HR-EELS measurement may indicate
that the observed signatures originate from the conduction band electrons coupled to the plasmons of the self-intercalated
1T-TiS2. The results are clearly presented, and the conclusions are well supported. 
Overall, this manuscript meets the criteria for publication in Communications Materials. There are several points that I
recommend the authors consider. 
1. Ref. 12 applied the second derivative ARPES spectrum to identify higher order plasmon satellite peaks with higher
binding energy (2ħΩ). I suggest that the authors can also analyze the second derivative ARPES spectra at the different
charge carrier density to confirm the presence of higher order satellites. 
2. In Fig. 2, the authors carried out the Rb deposition on the cleaved sample surface. Would it be possible to attempt ARPES
measurements using other alkali metal, such as Na and K? Because these alkali metal atoms produce different dielectric
screening effects, they may show distinct behavior in terms of higher order satellites and the evolution of bosonic energy. 
3. As I understand it, Figs. 1c and 1d present ARPES spectra and EDCs of Ti self-intercalated 1T-TiS
₂
. However, in the
Methods part, the authors purchased pristine 1T-TiS
₂
 single crystals from HQ Graphene. Without additional treatment or
alkali metal deposition, would pristine 1T-TiS
₂
 display a polaronic state and conduction band below the Fermi level? This
raises potential confusion: Figs. 2b–e corresponds to Rb-doped 1T-TiS
₂
, whereas Fig. 1c appears to show pristine 1T-TiS
₂
without Rb deposition. I suggest that the authors describe it in detail. 
4. Ref. 23 indicates that extra atoms are randomly distributed between 1T-TiS
₂
 layers in the bulk, leading to a highly electron
doped semiconductor. If this is the case, the polaronic state may show the different behavior at different surface positions or
different samples. Can the authors show the band structures measured at different surface locations or from another sample
to address this possibility? 
5. In Fig. 2a, the authors present momentum-resolved HR-EELS data of self-intercalated 1T-TiS2 without Rb deposition. The
HR-EELS map reveals characteristic collective excitations along the Γ–M direction. The authors argue that the non-zero
plasmon loss energy at ~200 meV corresponds to a bulk plasmon mode, consistent with the bosonic state observed in the
ARPES spectra. However, while the HR-EELS bosonic mode emerges near the Γ point (not at the M point), the satellite
peak in ARPES is observed at the M point. Could there be an alternative origin of the ARPES satellite peak? 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents a comprehensive study of plasmonic polarons in self-intercalated 1T-TiS
₂
 using a combination of
ARPES, HR-EELS, and first-principles calculations. The work reveals tunable plasmonic satellite features and their
temperature dependence, offering valuable insights into electron–plasmon coupling in layered materials. The results are
original and significant, and the manuscript is well structured. I am inclined to recommend publication after the authors
address the following points to strengthen the rigor and completeness of the paper: 
1.Figure S11 shows normalized n and m^*as a function of temperature, but the main text does not sufficiently discuss how
m^* was extracted from the ARPES data. Since effective mass renormalization is a key signature of plasmonic polaron
formation, the authors should describe the method and fitting range used to obtain m^* from the experimental dispersion,
including associated uncertainties. 
2. The authors use a Voigt profile, i.e. a convolution of Gaussian and Lorentzian components, multiplied by the Fermi–Dirac
distribution to fit the EDCs. The rationale behind this choice must be clearly explained. Why is it necessary to employ both
Gaussian and Lorentzian components instead of a single line shape? The authors should clarify whether the Gaussian part
reflects the instrumental energy resolution and the Lorentzian part represents the intrinsic lifetime broadening of the
quasiparticles, or if other physical considerations motivated this choice. In addition, the additional peaks observed after Rb
deposition require a more detailed explanation. The authors should discuss whether these peaks are associated with defect
states, impurity scattering, or other intrinsic states, and whether their presence indicates sample-quality issues or intrinsic
physical effects.

3. The discussion attributes the reduction of plasmon energy at elevated temperatures to changes in the dielectric constant,
but the manuscript does not examine how the high-frequency dielectric constant behaves under different carrier densities.
Since ε_∞ 
plays a critical role in determining plasmon energy and screening, providing at least a qualitative dependence of
ε_∞ 
on carrier density from first-principles calculations would significantly strengthen the work and deepen the physical
interpretation of the results. 
4. The Supplementary Information states that the carrier density was extracted from the Fermi surface volume using
Luttinger’s theorem. However, the system exhibits k_z dispersion, whereas ARPES inherently probes only a two-
dimensional cross-section of momentum space. The authors should clarify how the out-of-plane dimension was treated
when converting the measured Fermi surface into a three-dimensional carrier density, specify the assumptions made
regarding the Fermi-surface geometry, and describe the calculation procedure in sufficient detail to ensure reproducibility. 
5. Reference 34 appears to contain some inconsistencies. The authors should carefully verify the bibliographic details and
ensure that the cited information is accurate and corresponds to the correct source

(1) Ref. 12 applied the second derivative ARPES spectrum to identify higher order plasmon satellite peaks with higher binding energy (2ℏΩ). I suggest that the authors can also analyze the second derivative ARPES spectra at the different charge carrier density to confirm the presence of higher order satellites.   (Reply) We appreciate Reviewer #1’s valuable suggestion. We have conducted the second derivative analysis on our ARPES data as a function of carrier density (from the Rb-doping experiment in Fig. 2) following Reviewer #1’s suggestion.  As shown in Fig. R1, while the first satellite (ℏΩ) is clearly visible and marked by a white dashed line, we could not resolve the second-order satellite (2ℏΩ) clearly above the noise level. This is consistent with our EDC profiles in Fig. S7 and the statement in the main text (page 8). The intensity of the first-order satellite is already weak compared to the quasiparticle bands, approximately 8 times less than the quasiparticle (QP) peak (see Fig. 1f), since the undeposited sample is already quite doped by Ti intercalation. Assuming a similar intensity reduction for the next-order satellite, the 2ℏΩ peak would be ~60-70 times weaker than the QP peak [Rev. Mod. Phys. 93, 025006 (2021)], placing it well below the detection limit of our experiment. Therefore, the absence of high-order satellites in our experimental data, which are often considered signatures of strong polaron coupling, does not contradict the polaronic picture. Instead, it suggests a weak coupling strength of our system.  We have added this new figure and a corresponding discussion to the Supplementary Information (Figure S10) to clarify this point.

Because these alkali metal atoms produce different dielectric screening effects, they may show distinct behavior in terms of higher order satellites and the evolution of bosonic energy.  (Reply) We appreciate Reviewer #1’s insightful comment. The primary motivation for our original Rb-deposition experiment was to systematically tune the charge carrier density (n) and to demonstrate the characteristic n-dependence of the satellite energy, which is the key hallmark of a plasmonic polaron. To clarify that the dielectric screening effect in our system is primarily governed by the change in the overall charge carrier density rather than by the specific species of the deposited alkali metals, as pointed by Reviewer #1, we have performed an additional experiment using Potassium (K) and Cesium (Cs) depositions on a newly cleaved sample, as shown in Fig. R2. Although K, Rb, and Cs may possess different intrinsic dielectric constants, they exhibit the same overall trend in our measurements: Both K and Cs depositions also lead to a clear increase in the satellite energy as the carrier density increases, regardless of the alkali-metal species (Fig. R2a).

Considering the plasmonic polaron observed in 1T-TiS2 originates from the coupling between conduction electrons and the bulk plasmon mode (Fig. 2), the dielectric screening introduced by alkali atoms localized near the surface or interface is expected to play only a minor role in modifying the plasmonic polaron behavior.   (3) As I understand it, Figs. 1c and 1d present ARPES spectra and EDCs of Ti self-intercalated 1T-TiS2. However, in the Methods part, the authors purchased pristine 1T-TiS2 single crystals from HQ graphene. Without additional treatment or alkali metal deposition, would pristine 1T-TiS2 display a polaronic state and conduction band below the Fermi level? This raises potential confusion: Figs. 2b-e corresponds to Rb-doped 1T-TiS2, whereas Fig. 1c appears to show pristine 1T-TiS2 without Rb deposition. I suggest that the authors describe it in detail.   (Reply) We thank Reviewer #1 for highlighting this important point of potential confusion and apologize for the lack of clarity. Reviewer #1 is correct: Figs. 1c and 1d present the as-purchased sample without any in-situ Rb deposition. The key fact here is that the as-purchased sample is inherently electron-doped due to self-intercalation of excess Ti atoms into the van der Waals gap. This property has been systematically investigated in previous studies [e.g., J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 10, 6996-7001 (2019)]. In addition, our manuscript provides direct evidence for this intrinsic Ti self-intercalation in 1T-TiS2: 1. Raman Spectroscopy (Fig. S1): The spectrum shows a distinct shoulder peak (Sh) on the high-energy side of the A1g mode, which is a well-known signature of intercalated Ti atoms [J. Raman Spectrosc. 50, 1114-1119 (2019); J. Phys. C: Solid State Phys. 14, 4067 (1981)]. 2. EDX Analysis (Fig. S2): Our EDX measurements confirm the sample is non-stoichiometric, with an average composition of Ti1.089S2. 3. ARPES (Fig. 1c): The presence of the electron pocket at the M-point, crossing the Fermi level, is a direct consequence of such self-doping. A perfectly stoichiometric TiS2 would be a semiconductor with its conduction band minimum above the Fermi level.
