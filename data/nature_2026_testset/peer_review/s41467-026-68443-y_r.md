Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript explores the relationship between lactate metabolism and diabetes-related erectile dysfuncton(DMED),
especially the role of lactate in the pathogenesis of DMED. The study found that blood glucose, serum lactate and penile
lactate levels were significantly increased in diabetic mice, and after lactate supplementation, the function of penile smooth
muscle cells (CCSMC) was significantly impaired, showing a shift from a contractile phenotype to a synthetic phenotype.
Further studies found that downregulation of Fbp1 (gluconeogenic enzyme) led to blockage of the gluconeogenic pathway
and accumulation of lactate in penile tissue, thereby affecting erectile function. The study also revealed the role of histone
methylation and fatty acylation (S-palmitoylation) in the regulation of Fbp1 function, especially Zdhhc13 promoted
palmitoylation of Fbp1 through its acyltransferase activity, thereby impairing cell function. Although inhibition of Fbp1
acylation or histone methylation failed to completely restore erectile function in DMED mice, the development of a Fbp1-
C282S mRNA-encapsulated lipid nanoparticle (LNP) treatment regimen successfully reduced lactate levels and significantly
improved erectile function in the DMED mouse model, showing new therapeutic prospects. Finally, the study pointed out that
there is still room for optimization of this treatment strategy, and the possibility of more drug combination therapy should be
explored in the future. It has a scientific value in this field. But there are still some issues and questions that need to be
addressed. Lacks of study in human erectile tissues (this is very important because, as far as I know, there is no study
showing the relationship between lactate and ED in human erectile tissue) and absence of data related to other important
cellular components of erectile tissue, such as endothelial cells, pericytes, and neuronal cells, are a major limitation of this
study. 
1. Was there a specific time-point of lactate accumulation in the corpus cavernosum during the progression of DMED? 
2. Lactate supplementation: How do the doses of lactate given (0.25 g/kg/d and 0.5 g/kg/d) relate to physiological levels?
Are these doses intended to mimic pathological conditions? Are there data on how lactate affects healthy tissues? How
should the dose be chosen? 
3. Clinical relevance of lactate measurements: While the study focuses on lactate accumulation in the corpus cavernosum,
how directly do these findings correlate to human DMED? Are plasma lactate levels in DMED patients also elevated to the
same extent as observed in the mice? 
4. Role of Zdhhc13-mediated palmitoylation: The role of Zdhhc13 in Fbp1 palmitoylation and its subsequent impact on
gluconeogenesis is intriguing. Could the manuscript expand on the mechanism by which palmitoylation at C282 disrupts
Fbp1 function? Is there a pathway implicated in the degradation or inactivation of Fbp1 upon palmitoylation? 
5. Therapeutic potential of Fbp1 rescue: Fbp1 C282S-loaded lipid nanoparticles (Fbp1 C282S-LNP) are proposed as a
potential therapeutic strategy. Can the manuscript include more detailed pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic data on the
efficacy of these nanoparticles in vivo, including potential off-target effects? 
6. Histological analysis: More specific histological analysis (e.g., immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence) could help
validate the effects of lactate on smooth muscle function and fibrosis. Were markers of fibrosis (e.g., collagen type I)
assessed in the cavernosal tissue? 
7. Comparative analysis of other gluconeogenic enzymes: Given the role of Fbp1 in gluconeogenesis, it might be beneficial
to compare the expression of other key gluconeogenic enzymes (such as G6PC, PCK1) in the corpus cavernosum of DMED
mice to ensure that the observed effects are Fbp1-specific and not part of a broader metabolic dysfunction. 
8. The introduction could benefit from a clearer explanation of the significance of Fbp1 in diabetic erectile

dysfuncton(DMED). Why is Fbp1 a key player in the pathophysiology of DMED? Why other targets, such as Hk2, Pklr, Pck1
were not select? These targets expression pattern looks similar with Fbp1. 
9. While Cbx3 is shown to interact with H3K9me3, what are its specific effects on Fbp1 function in terms of cellular
metabolism or other pathways? Could metabolic assays help elucidate its role further? 
10. Although Fbp1-C282S-LNP improves various functional parameters in DMED mice, the manuscript could benefit from a
more detailed discussion of how this strategy compares to traditional therapeutic interventions for DMED. Could this
approach be combined with other treatment modalities? 
11. Fbp1 knockout mice are used in several experiments; however, the role of Fbp1 in other tissues or systemic effects (e.g.,
liver or muscle) should be considered. Could the observed effects be specific to the corpus cavernosum or systemic in
nature? 
12. Lactate Accumulation and Erectile Dysfunction: The link between lactate accumulation and erectile dysfunction is
mentioned, but the underlying cellular mechanisms are not fully explained. Could the authors elaborate on how lactate
directly impairs CCSMC and erectile function at the molecular or cellular level? 
13. LNP-Based Therapy: The use of LNPs for delivering the Fbp1-C282S mRNA is promising, but the manuscript would
benefit from a deeper discussion of how LNPs are taken up by specific cell types, particularly in cavernosal tissue. What are

14. Limitations in Cell-Type Specificity: The study focuses on the effect of Fbp1 on CCSMCs, but the corpus cavernosum
contains various cell types. How might Fbp1-C282S affect other cells like endothelial cells, fibroblasts, pericytes, or
macrophages? Would the delivery system need to target these cells specifically? 
15. In Vivo Validation of mRNA Strategy: While the Fbp1-C282S mRNA strategy shows promising outcomes, did the authors
observe any long-term effects or changes in other metabolic pathways? How sustained are the improvements in erectile
function over time? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This investigation is concerned with the impact of diabetes on erectile dysfunction. The authors specifically investigate
lactate accumulation, the causes of this accumulation, and its impact on the function of the corpus cavernosum. They
propose that transcriptional and functional repression of Fbp1 is the cause of the lactate accumulation, and investigate
strategies to correct Fbp1 activity and erectile function. 
Major concerns – models and approaches 
1. The experimental model of diabetes is extreme. A blood glucose concentration of 30mM is exceptionally high and would
be fatal in humans. It is important to establish whether the findings are consistent in a model system that achieves a clinically
relevant blood glucose concentration. 
2. The in vitro models (e.g. Figure 1O) use extracellular lactate concentrations of 5mM and 10mM which massively exceed
the plasma lactate concentrations observed in the animal model (~3mM). Why? 
3. Glucose concentrations in standard culture media are often supra-physiological. Please explain which concentrations of
glucose were used for the in vitro experiments. 
4. The DMED mice are a highly selected group. Mice are first treated to induce hyperglycaemia, and then erectile function is
tested. Only those mice that show ED are investigated in the DMED group. What fraction of the hyperglycaemic group is
this? 
5. All diabetic mice receive injections of streptozotocin (daily for 5 days) and apomorphine. Were control mice injected with
vehicle? There are established impacts of stress (e.g. by daily injections) on plasma lactate that must be controlled for in
these experiments. 
6. The description of the acyl biotin exchange assay (line 615) is not adequate. What buffer was used for lysis? What
cleanup steps were done to eliminate ‘surplus reagents’ (e.g after biotinylation)? What was the final concentration of
hydroxylamine in the assay? Why was DTT absent from SDS PAGE buffer? 
Major concerns – figures 
1. Statistical analysis. Throughout – the legends should indicate the names of the statistical tests used. When reporting
values in the main text either state ‘significantly different / not different’ OR state values, measure of variation, or units (for
example – lines 107 currently simply says ‘0.47 VS. 0.52 VS. 0.76’, line 108 currently says 116.3 VS. 103.7 VS. 56.3 –
needs units and SD / SEM). In the methods section, please state whether one-tailed or two-tailed statistical tests were
performed. Where ANOVA results are reported, please provide the F values and degrees of freedom. Where t-test results are
reported, please provide the t-values and degrees of freedom. 
2. Fig 2A-2D. The lack of detail in the legend makes it very difficult to understand what this represents. What are N1, T1, etc?
Same problem in Fig 5K. 
3. Fig 3V appears to show that a ‘PROTAC’ has been developed that induces degradation of Cbx3. No experimental
evidence is presented that this molecule binds to Cbx3. The characterisation in Fig S3 is inadequate. What is the in vivo
bioavailability / half life of this reagent (Fig 4)? 
4. Palmitoylation assays should be presented with a palmitoylated housekeeping protein as a control for the assay (e.g.
throughout Fig 5). 
5. No quantification of palmitoylation assays is presented making it impossible to draw conclusions about whether any
changes in palmitoylation described are statistically significant (e.g. throughout Fig 5). 
6. The molecular model presented in Fig 5F is meaningless. No information is provided about how this was generated. It is
impossible to distinguish which protein is which, how they are interacting, etc. 
7. zDHHC13 is a polytopic membrane protein, which are notoriously difficult to purify in an intact, functional state. Only

zDHHC20 and zDHHC9 from this family have been purified enzymatically competent (PMIDs: 29326245, 38182928). It is
highly unlikely that GST-zDHHC13 (purified from what expression system?) retains catalytic activity during purification in
RIPA buffer (Fig 5R). 
8. Fig 6B – palmitoylation anchors proteins to membranes. Why does the amount of Fbp1 in the cytosolic fraction not change
when zDHHC13 is silenced? Fig 5 indicated reduced palmitoylation of Fbp1 in the absence of zDHHC13, which would
reduce its membrane association.

3. Fig 1G – what does the x axis in these traces represent?

11. Fig 5R – why is there beta actin in these samples? They are purified recombinant proteins.

13. Fig 7H – quantification of Fbp1 expression is missing. 
14. Figure S3C – please include a description of what the numbers on the right of the graph refer to. 
Other comments: 
1. In the abstract the authors state that the “Suv39h1-mediated H3K9 trimethylation and Ezh2-dependent H3K27
trimethylation synergistically suppressed Fbp1 transcription”. In the manuscript, evidence is provided for each of these
repressing transcription individually, however no evidence is presented to support “synergistic effects”. Does simultaneously
increasing (or decreasing) Suv39h1-mediated H3K9 trimethylation and Ezh2-dependent H3K27 trimethylation cause
additional modulation of Fbp1 transcription compared to increasing (or decreasing) only one of these? 
2. How realistic is Fbp1 or lactate accumulation as a therapeutic target? Is modulating processes involved in cellular
metabolism likely to be associated with undesired effects? Data presented from MOVAS cells (derived from mouse aortic
smooth muscle) indicates that the effects of Fbp1 modulation are very similar to those in the CCSMCs. Very similar effects in
both cell types would presumably mean that interventions targeting or involving Fbp1 would risk off target effects in vascular
smooth muscle cells, and that therapeutic drugs would have to be very selectively targeted to CCSMCs. 
3. At various points throughout the results section, reference is made to ‘increases’ or ‘decreases’ in various measures in
some groups compared to other groups. ‘Increases’ or ‘decreases’ implies that measurements have been made from the
same group before and after an intervention. When comparing values between different groups, please use ‘higher’ or
‘greater’ and ‘lower’ instead to describe differences. Please adjust this in lines 107, 100, 108, 113, 162, 163, 212, 217, 232
and any other instances. 
4. zDHHC13 is a Golgi-localised enzyme (PMIDs: 16647879, 19299482), yet Fbp1 appears to be present in the plasma
membrane and the nuclear membrane. How is it possible that Fbp1 interacts with and is a substrate for zDHHC13 when
they are expressed in different cellular compartments? 
5. Fbp1 palmitoylation has been reported before by others: https://swisspalm.org/proteins/Q9QXD6 . Other palmitoylation
sites have been described in this protein (C39, C93). How do you account for mutation of only C282 abolishing
palmitoylation? 
6. What was the dosing frequency of the LNP (line 728)? 
7. Were control and DMED mice age-matched? Please include the ages of mice used for these experiments.

(Remarks to the Author) 
In the present study the authors suggest that excessive lactate accumulation in the corpus cavernosum is a key pathological
driver of DMED, promoting structural degeneration, smooth muscle cell loss, and erectile dysfunction in diabetic mice.
Mechanistically, they show that transcriptional repression and functional impairment of Fbp1 (fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1,
Fbp1), a rate-limiting gluconeogenic enzyme, underlined corpus cavernosum lactate pathological accumulation. Epigenetic
profiling revealed that Suv39h1-mediated H3K9 trimethylation and Ezh2-dependent H3K27 trimethylation synergistically

gluconeogenesis related function, aggravating lactate accumulation. To rescue these defects, the authors engineered Fbp1
C282S-loaded lipid nanoparticles (Fbp1 C282S-LNP), which restored Fbp1 expression and abrogated pathological
palmitoylation in the corpus cavernosum, improving erectile function in DMED mice.

Reviewer #6 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Xiao et al identifies fructose 1,6 bisphosphatase 1 (FBP1) activity in corpus cavernosum smooth muscle
cells (CCSMCs) as a potentially important component to erectile function in mice and the overall health of CCSMCs. This is
by virtue of FBP1's role in the gluconeogenic pathway, which the authors claim is necessary to prevent lactate accumulation
that may otherwise impair CCSMC function. The implications are significant because this study provides a potentially novel
target for ED treatment that could in theory be used alone or in conjunction with traditional therapies such as
phosphodiesterase inhibitors. The authors report that FBP1 expression and function in CCSMCs is reduced in a model of
diabetes mellitus-induced erectile dysfuncton(DMED). This happens as a result of epigenetic and post-translational events,
leading ultimately to elevated lactate concentrations in the corpus cavernosum. The authors go to great lengths to identify
the mechanistic basis for changes in FBP1 expression (via histone methylation pathways) and functon(via palmitoylation),
using in vitro methods as well as their mouse model of DMED. The molecular biology experiments are fairly thorough by
design (although underpowered), and complimentary approaches are used. In particular, the use of lipid nanoparticles to
deliver palmitoylation-resistant FBP1 is a compelling approach to correct aberrant metabolic processes. 
While concerns raised by Reviewer 2 on palmitoylation were adequately addressed by authors, there are several major
issues, described below, which diminish the overall impact of the manuscript. In general, while many of the experiments
shed light on the regulatory mechanisms controlling the functional expression of FBP1 in various systems, extrapolation of
these findings to the author's central hypothesis is limited by several concerns. 
Major concerns: 
1 - The central hypothesis of the manuscript rests on the idea that dysregulation of FBP1 in CCSMCs leads to lactate
accumulation via suppression of gluconeogenesis. However, the authors do not provide evidence, nor do they cite previous
work showing that CCSMCs actually carry out gluconeogenesis to a significant degree. Aside from transcript-level data and
a western blot showing that a few other gluconeogenic enzymes were not altered the corpus cavernosum of DMED mice
(Sup Fig 2), there is no supporting evidence that lactate accumulation in the corpus cavernosum happens as a result
impaired flux through the gluconeogenic pathway within CCSMCs. Relying on the quantification of enzyme abundance,
rather than measuring glucose or metabolite production directly, is not a rigorous way to test the extent to which glycolytic or
gluconeogenic pathways proceed in CCSMCs under the experimental conditions. Without a clear demonstration that
CCSMCs carry out gluconeogenesis and that inhibition of this pathway specifically leads to lactate accumulation, it is

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

impossible to interpret the study's findings in the context of the authors' proposed model. The authors must definitively show
that CCSMC-localized FB1 participates in "lactate clearance via gluconeogenesis," so to speak, otherwise the premise of
the study is not sound. 
The authors do mention that conversion of lactate into glucose via the Cori Cycle is a key part of lactate metabolism.
However, in this pathway, lactate generated by muscle under anaerobic conditions is converted to glucose in the liver. If a
similar pathway exists within smooth muscle cells, the authors should include a reference which describes it. Likewise,
many of the references to FBP1 function in lactate metabolism and/or diabetic models involve studies using liver cells or
liver tissue. It is not appropriate to draw parallels between the findings of this study and others that deal with these pathways
specifically in the liver. 
2 - Throughout the study, elevations in lactate concentration, either systemically or locally, are reported in association with
functional/structural/biochemical changes in the corpus cavernosum. However, these observations are correlative and there
is a lack of evidence suggesting the downstream effects are a direct result of lactate accumulation consequent to
suppressed gluconeogenesis/FBP1 activity within CCSMCs. In addition, there should be more discussion of how excessive
lactate might cause the phenotypic effects that are described. What is the underlying pathological mechanism by which
lactate accumulation causes dysfunction? 
3 - Do the authors suggest that lactate is produced in CCSMCs under aerobic conditions? Does oxygen availability in
corpus cavernosum have an impact on lactate generation? Is it possible that aerobic metabolism is somehow impaired in
CCSMCs in the DMED model, and that this leads to lactate accumulation independently of FBP1? If there are other
metabolic disturbances in CCSMCs in the DMED model, the mechanism(s) leading to lactate accumulation may or may not
be connected to the functional impairment of the corpus cavernosum. As mentioned above, it would be helpful to discuss a
clear mechanistic link between excessive lactate and impaired CCSMC function. 
4 - In the model of the heterozygous Fbp1-/+ mice, genetic disruption is global, making it difficult to parse out local effects in
the corpus cavernosum from effects elsewhere in the body (i.e., the liver). Is it possible that metabolic disruption from global
Fbp1-/+ produces the functional changes in CCSMCs as part of a broader systemic phenotype? Is it possible that CCSMC
dysfunction happens coincidentally with an elevation in circulating lactate, but not necessarily because of it? This does not
rule out the possibility that elevated circulating lactate in Fbp1-/+ mice has a detrimental effect on CCSMCs, but it is difficult
to justify the conclusion that CCSMC-localized FBP1 dysregulation is driving the observed effects. 
5 - Apomorphine, the drug used to induce erection in mice, is a centrally acting dopamine receptor agonist targeting
dopamine signaling pathways in the brain. It is thought to be most effective for psychogenic erectile dysfuncton(in humans)
and works via a mechanism separate from PDE inhibitors, which act locally. How do the authors rule out that experimental
interventions (DMED model, lactate injection, metabolic changes from Fbp1-/+, etc.) do not interfere with neural control of
erectile function? 
Minor concerns 
1 - The authors describe H-PROTAC as a CBX3-sepcific degrader. However, Supp Fig 5 does not demonstrate specificity.
The authors did not test whether degradation of other proteins, aside from CBX3, were altered by H-PROTAC treatment. 
2 - Experiments assessing the palmitoylation status of FBP1 should include both positive and negative controls (proteins
known to be palmitoylated and known to not be palmitoylated) to demonstrate that the assay was successful. In addition,
there should be quantitation for these experiments, and not just a representative immunoblot. 
3 - Typically, palmitoylation of proteins is thought to promote an interaction with lipid membranes. Are there other examples
of palmitoylation causing structural/functional changes in cytosolic enzymes? If so, this should be discussed. 
4 - In general, the figure legends and in-text figure descriptions require much more detailed information. Figure legends in
particular should have details of the experimental conditions, spelled out acronyms for the text in the figure panels,
description of the assays used for generation of each panel within the figure, description of the statistical tests used, etc. Also
many of the experiments have an n=3. A larger sample size would increase the rigor of the study. 
5 - Line 75-80: The previous observation that sildenafil reduced lactate accumulation in exercising rats does not necessarily
imply a connection between lactate metabolism and erectile function. It only suggests that PDE5 inhibition reduces lactate
accumulation caused be strenuous exercise.
