Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Kasalo et al. present here a quite theoretical study on the relation between the energy costs of producing AA to the loss of
the pathways needed to synthesise essential AA in Metazoa. The study is mainly theoretical, presenting several interesting
hypotheses based on statistics taken from different papers and a broad scale protein presence / absence analysis. I found
the paper interesting but I have several points I’d like to address: 
My main issue with the paper is the use of the permutation test to justify that AA pathways were lost because of high energy
costs. In Figure 1A it is already shown that the 9 EAA are the ones with a higher opportunity cost, therefore the permutation
analysis in the particular case just shows that the many other combinations of AA have a lower energy cost. No need to
perform the permutation analysis. When the same is applied to direct costs, then the conclusion is different, there’s a small
percentage of combinations of AA that have a higher direct cost than the one formed by the EAA, therefore other
combinations of AA could have become essential according to the direct cost measure. 
It may have been interesting to measure such permutations with different numbers of AA and then average the energy costs
by number of AA considered to keep it comparable. Would a combination of 10 AA or 12 AA have a higher direct energy
cost? Would that match to the more sparse AA losses observed in some Metazoa species? 
Also, is the permutation test really a proxy for detecting selection? The paper shows two different reasons as to why these
specific AA may have been outsourced (energy cost and pleiotropy), but that certainly was not enough for non-Metazoan
species to lose AA pathways given that many other species across the tree of life have kept theirs. Therefore, despite the
high cost of producing certain AA, there needs to be a much more complex evolutionary process that would create the
mechanisms present in the ancient Metazoan ancestor for such losses to not be deleterious and for them to offer fitness
advantages. Mechanisms that have little to do with energy cost given that other species are still able to synthesise the most
expensive AA despite their cost. 
Another point of interest is that different AA have a different usage depending on the species. A very energy expensive EAA
may not have a big impact on the average cost of all AA when its use in a given proteome is sparse. What would the energy
cost graphs / permutation tests look like if the number of times an AA appears in the proteome was considered? 
Are there estimates of the different usages of EAA per different animal species? Is there a large variation? How does that
compare to non-EAA? 
While opportunity cost does indeed follow the trend of being higher for EAA, the direct cost does not follow the same trend.
How do the authors explain this observation? 
Figure 1A should be split in two plots, one for opportunity costs and a second one for Direct cost with the AA sorted
accordingly, else the representation coupled with the colouring of the bars can mislead the readers. Figure 2 could then be

(Remarks to the Author) 
I have read with interest the manuscript by Kasalo and colleagues. The manuscript focuses on animal-specific essential
amino acids (AA), which due to gene losses early in their evolution made them reliant on obtaining these AA from their diet.
The study argues that this was driven by the high energy cost of producing these amino acids under the oxygen-rich, high-
respiration conditions typical of animal life; this outsourcing allowed greater protein evolution and points to energy
management as the driver of this change. 
I had reviewed a previous version of this manuscript, and I believe it has gone through significant changes since the last
time I read it. Many issues raised about how to calculate the energy costs, the incorporation of respiratory modes, and the
use of focal species have been dealt with. I’d like to congratulate the authors for their efforts. I still have some concerns I’d
like to share with the authors. 
The first one is that the manuscript states a few times that the analyses support that selective forces drove the AA
outsourcing. However, my understanding of their analyses is that by using permutation tests, the correlation between the AA
that are essential and their energy costs is statistically significant and it is not random. However, I can’t identify any clear
indication that this is caused by natural selection, the lack of randomness could be caused by something else; for example, a
very fantastical option is that these AA were floating around in excess in the environment and animals lost the capacity to
synthesize them, not because they were expensive but because they were overabundant. While I agree that this is the most
likely explanation and it should be mentioned as a possible cause in the Discussion section, I don’t think the analyses
(permutation tests) and results reveal anything about the driving force behind this AA outsourcing, just that it is not random.

complement similarity search with HMM profile search (HMMER) and/or clustering approaches (OrthoFinder).

2) Line 26 and later, could the authors elaborate why they think this is result of “stabilizing selection”? Wouldn’t expect the

Response 2: We thank the reviewer for this comment. In response to previous reviewers' requests, we developed a statistical approach to test whether selection on energy costs, rather than random processes, led to the outsourcing of the observed EAAs in animals. Initially referred to as a "permutation analysis," we now use the term combinatorial phenotype selection (CPS) test and provide its explicit definition for clarity (Lines 182-187, 573-608).  In short, we define the CPS test as a statistical method for evaluating whether a quantitative factor (e.g., synthesis costs) exerts directional selection on a complex phenotypic trait suitable for combinatorial analysis (e.g., the auxotrophic/prototrophic status of amino acids), using neutral evolution as the null hypothesis. The test estimates the probability (p-value) that the observed combinatorial state of the phenotypic trait arose by chance, based on an empirically derived probability mass function (PMF) obtained through permutations.  While the descriptive statistics (Fig. 1A) show that the 11 EAAs represent an energetically extreme combination, such descriptive statistics alone cannot assess the likelihood of this outcome occurring by chance. The CPS test addresses this by providing p-values that quantify the probability under neutrality. This is especially important when considering direct costs (Fig. 1B), where some possible combinations may appear more extreme, but the actually observed EAA set still yields low p-values (Fig. 4C, D)—supporting the role of energy-related selection. We have revised the Methods and the Results to more clearly explain the purpose of the CPS test and the interpretation of its results. We have also provided the code used to run the CPS test.  Comments 3: It may have been interesting to measure such permutations with different numbers of AA and then average the energy costs by number of AA considered to keep it comparable. Would a combination of 10 AA or 12 AA have a higher direct energy cost? Would that match to the more sparse AA losses observed in some Metazoa species?

Response 4: As explained above, the "permutation test"—now referred to as the CPS test—estimates the probability that the observed AA grouping arose by chance with respect to a given quantitative factor (e.g., energetic cost or pleiotropy). A low p-value suggests directional selection, but does not imply that the tested factor is the sole driver of the observed AA phenotype. Other factors may also contribute, potentially acting in concert. In fact, we demonstrate through a similar set of tests that energy costs and pleiotropy represent counteracting selective forces. We have now explicitly acknowledged the possibility of additional contributing factors in the revised manuscript (Lines 477-488).  We agree with the reviewer that a unique constellation of factors in early metazoans likely enabled the outsourcing of energetically expensive EAAs while minimizing deleterious effects and conferring fitness advantages. Our model proposes that environmental amino acid availability and respiration mode were key conditions that set the stage for this evolutionary shift. We have expanded the Discussion to more explicitly recognize the complex interplay between various factors that could have influenced the evolution of Metazoa, as well as the likely contribution of stochastic events in this process (Lines 477-488).  However, when selective pressures are comparable, similar EAA patterns are expected to emerge independently. Indeed, we recently demonstrated that aerobic predatory bacteria (Bdellovibrionota) evolved an EAA set strikingly similar to that of animals (https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26073024). This cross-domain convergence strongly supports the idea that amino acid synthesis costs represent an important selective pressure influencing EAA composition. Our work on predatory bacteria further suggests that high respiration rates together with obligate predation—ecological features common to both Bdellovibrionota and animals—create favorable conditions for outsourcing costly EAAs (Lines 443-447).

Instead of “permutation test,” we now use the term combinatorial phenotype selection (CPS) test and provide an explicit definition for clarity (Lines 182–187, 573–608). Briefly, the CPS test assesses whether a quantitative factor (e.g., biosynthesis cost) exerts directional selection on a combinatorial phenotypic trait (e.g., auxotrophic/prototrophic status of amino acids), under neutral evolution as the null hypothesis.
