Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their paper, Castellanos-Girouard et al. compare gene and protein interaction networks for the yeast Saccharomyces
cerevisiae (yeast). The authors find evidence of meaningful overlap between gene interaction and protein interaction
networks. After aligning the networks, the authors show how strong negative epistasis is likely being driven by disrupted
protein interaction networks. I agree with the authors' claim that studies to date have not demonstrated many convincing
relationships between gene and protein interaction networks. The reason for the progress in this current study is the
availability of realistic and high quality gene and protein interaction networks for yeast. While we have had useful gene
interaction network data for yeast, derived from screens of knockout/knockdown lines, until recently the protein interaction
networks were quite simplistic (typically measured by experimentally measuring whether two proteins would bind together).
The key study, Michaelis et al. 2023, extract protein complexes from yeast and identified which proteins were involved in the
interaction using a sophisticated mass-spectrometry based approach. As a result, for essentially the first time we have a
picture of which proteins actually interact inside of the cell (as opposed to those which functionally could bind in pairs).
While I am enthusiastic about the general approach of the paper, I found the methodological approach very hard to parse
(especially in terms of various assumptions made about input data sets and how they were processed). Additionally, I found
it quite difficult to map various hypotheses about drivers of the relationship between gene and protein interaction networks to
the various results presented in the paper. 
As a result, I think it's quite challenging to draw strong conclusions from the
current presentation. However, I am also concerned that the main result of the paper my derive somewhat trivially from how
the experimental data were collected. Namely that by defining gene interactions using knockout/knockdown data and
comparing that to protein interactions networks we should almost certainly see evidence for negative epistasis correlating
with protein interactions. I don't actually think this is a serious a critique as it may sounds because, as the authors point out,
even showing expected relationships between gene and protein interaction networks has been quite uncommon in past
studies. I expand on these points below and hope that the authors find my comments constructive. 
1. I had to go back-and-forth between the original gene interaction and protein interaction papers and the current work to
understand what exactly is meant by various data elements. Once I had these definitions written down, I then needed to map
them onto various kinds of genetic interactions (pos/neg epistasis, pleiotropy, independence). After doing all this, I felt like I
had a better handle on what was actually uncovered in this paper. I believe that going through this process presents way too
much work for the reader and also leaves open the very likely possibility that the reader will either make a mistake or diverge
in assumptions from what the authors' envisioned. I believe the authors need to carefully lay out, maybe in a table, the
specific definitions of the gene and protein interactions and how various kinds of relationships point back to different kinds of
genetic interaction, e.g., epistasis. 
2. I believe the authors need to try and answer two questions for the readers. 
2a. First, what does it mean that we cannot fully align gene and protein interaction networks? In theory, because every
protein was transcribed and translate from a gene we should be able to completely map the two. While there are violations of
the central dogma, I believe the vast amount of our challenge aligning these networks comes from experimental design and
measurement error. The authors point out a few of these challenges in the current study. However, I do not believe they
engage enough (especially in the discussion) with how the underlying experimental procedures used to generate the data
might bias our interpretation. For example, quantifying gene interaction using primarily knockout data is going to heavily bias
towards negative epistasis. The authors need to engage much more actively with how we should couch their results in the
context of the way the data were gathered. 
2b. Second, what do the results tell us about different kinds of gene interactions? The main conclusion of the study is that a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

strong source of epistasis comes from protein-protein interactions. From a trivial perspective, this almost must be true. The
only other places where epistasis could come from our be interactions mediated by RNA or with metabolites from the
environment. Certainly these two things happen, but how common are they? My sense is that the authors have nicely
demonstrated that we finally have gene and protein interaction networks that are biologically realistic enough that we can
start to study them. But, I'm curious whether there's actually a way to go further. For example, what would pleiotropy look like
using your approach? What about other kinds of interactions? 
3. The authors spend a bit of time introducing the yeast data (although as I mentioned I believe a lot more work is needed
here) but provide almost no information on the human data. In fact, in the paper it reads as though this is exclusively a yeast
study until human data appears. I think it's a strength to include the human data because the similar patterns found
potentially strengthen the conclusions, but I believe a lot more work is needed explaining how the data were collected and
how we should compare and contrast the human and yeast data from the perspective of how they were collected. One
obvious place to start would be the vastly different y-axis ranges in Fig 2. 
How should we interpret the values being 10x
higher in humans? I assume it's sampling bias in terms of which genes were included in the human study. 
4. It's not clear to me why we should expect the same functional shape for positive vs negative gene interactions. In the
original paper where the gene interaction data were collected, the authors spend quite a bit of time articulating how
biologically different positive and negative gene interactions likely are. 
5. A minor point, but I think using the terms positive and negative are misleading. If I understand correctly, positive means
that it was less bad than predicted based on individual knockout/downs, not that there was a positive effect on the overall
phenotype. While I agree this is still an important form of epistasis, it's not the same as positive vs. negative epistasis. I
understand that these terms are standard in the gene interaction literature, but again this is why I think we need a table or
something explaining the data and how they map onto various hypotheses about epistasis. 
6. One opportunity to potentially enrich the manuscript is to ask whether the patterns found differ by molecular pathway
and/or other features shared by the interacting proteins. One strong finding in multicellular organisms is that tissue type is a
major organizer of gene interactions measured through RNA expression. I would assume the same is true between
molecular pathways and/or broad classes of protein type.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Genetic interactions represent a ubiquitous evolutionary force that also play key roles in the molecular basis of disease.
Though various high throughput approaches have generated empirical maps of genetic interactions for a variety of
organisms, the molecular mechanisms that give rise to such interactions typically remain unclear. For these and other
reasons, it remains challenging to develop predictive genetic and/ or evolutionary models. General aspects of biochemical
regulation in conjunction with biophysical principles have proven useful to dissect the molecular basis of genetic
interactions. However, biophysical measurements are typically sparse and it can be quite challenging to predict biophysical
parameters. In this work, Castellanos-Girouard et al. devise a means to estimate the binding affinities of protein-protein
interactions based on proteomic mass spectrometry experiments, then show that these estimated binding affinities are
correlated with epistatic interaction strengths in yeast. Based on this relationship, they show that one network can predict
general trends in the other. Finally, they devise a means to identify trends in connectivity across genetic interaction and
protein-protein interaction networks. The manuscript is quite intuitive and easy to read and several their findings appear to
be strikingly robust. Furthermore, despite the nuances involved in this subject matter, the investigators do an excellent job of
distilling the findings down to their core principles. 
I believe this work constitutes an important landmark investigation that
will serve as inspiration for a range of future investigations on the mechanistic drivers of genetic interactions. The
methodologies employed, the analyses used, and the interpretation of the data are generally sound. I suggest the following
minor changes to try and improve the scope of the discussion and the readability of the manuscript: 
-The approach for estimating binding affinity appears to be elegant in its’ simplicity, but there are certain aspects of the
assumptions that are not entirely clear to me. Specifically, the approach to estimate copy number could be clarified. It is
unclear to me how this is estimated from mass spec data. I see that the authors cited a previous proteomic work in their
explanation. However, given the centrality of this calculation, I think the authors should explain on a more fundamental level
how their measurements are converted from an MS-based intensity measurement to a copy number (in the Methods section).
The volume approximation of cells is reasonable, but it may be worth mentioning that the effective volume is likely smaller
due to the occlusion of space from organelles such as the ER. I suspect this would simply add systematic error that would
not affect the general trends. 
-I suspect the presence of membrane-bound organelles in eukaryotes are unlikely to alter the authors’ general thesis.
However, differences in cellular localization could potentially add new layers to the evolutionary scenarios associated with
gene duplications the authors discuss. It may be worth considering whether paralogs that wind up in different cellular
compartments are subject to distinct stoichiometric selection pressures. I think the discussion could benefit from some
additional discussion on these matters, as compartmentalization may represent a key factor that differentiates how PPIs
shape the GI networks of eukaryotes versus prokaryotes. 
-Title might sound better as “Protein-Protein interactions are a Major Source of Epistasis in Genetic Interaction Networks”

Overall, I enjoyed reading this work and believe it will have a lasting impact in the fields of molecular genetics and protein
biophysics. I think it is highly suitable for the broad readership of Nature Communications.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary 
Castellanos-Girouard et al., explore the relationship between genetic and protein-protein interaction networks primarily in
yeast but also in human. 
Because the availability of human GI data was limited the trends tended to be weaker, most of my
comments (as well as the data in the paper) are primarily focused on analysis of yeast datasets: Yeast GI = Costanzo et al
2016, Yeast PPI = primarily Michaelis et al 2023 
Previous study (Hein et al) showed that Quantitative analysis of MS data enables measurement of: [1] Abundance
stoichiometry = ratio of abundance of interacting proteins [2] Interaction stoichiometry = total number of PPIs between a pair
of proteins (ie 1:1 = all of protein 1 is complexes with all of protein 2) 
Based on abundance and interaction stoichiometry measurements Castellanos-Girouard et al., developed a mathematical
model to estimate binding dissociation constants (Kd) for all tested protein-protein pairs. 
Indeed, this study provides the first
report of a proteome-wide estimate of in vivo protein-protein binding affinities 
Relationship between quantitative GIs and PPI biochemical metrics 
• The authors show an interesting relationship between genetic interaction score strength and stoichiometry metrics
(abundance and interaction). Specifically, pairs with stronger GI scores tend to involve protein pairs with 1:1 abundance and
interaction stoichiometries suggesting that genetic interaction more frequently connect proteins involved in abundant and
stable protein complexes. This trend was observed for both negative and positive interactions, but the trend observed for
negative interaction was much stronger 
• Comparison to estimated Kds revealed an impressively strong relationship between PPI affinity and GI strength.
Specifically, a strong negative correlation indicated that PPIs with high affinity (low Kd) tend to have negative GIs with strong
magnitude. A weaker but significant negative correlation was also observed between PPI Kd and magnitude of positive GIs. 
• Clustering analysis based on PPI Kds was able to recapitulate functional modules captured by the GI matrix indicating that
PPI Kds could be used to predict GIs and vice 
versa. 
General Comments 
This is a very creative and interesting manuscript. The observed relationship between quantitative GIs and PPI metrics is
important and should have a major impact on our ability to build predictive models. 
A strong overlap between negative GIs and PPIs was previously shown and this overlap was driven largely by essential
genes. ~50% of essential gene pairs whose products physically interact also show a negative GI (i.e. 10X enriched for
negative GIs)(Costanzo et al 2016). This result is supported by supplemental Data Figure 5 but is not mentioned in the
manuscript. Importantly, the relationship between Kd and negative GI strength remains significant (albeit weaker) when the
analysis is restricted to nonessential gene pairs, indicating that this indeed a general relationship between PPI Kd and
negative GIs rather than a property of essential genes alone. 
I would suggest that the authors should delve more into the
details of essential vs nonessential gene/protein interactions 
A weaker but significant negative correlation was also observed between Kd and positive GI strength. However, based on
Fig. 2C, the trend seems to involve only the subset of positive interactions with positive GIs scores below 0.07. Based on
replicate screen analysis, Costanzo et al., (2016), uses a GI score cutoff of 0.08 suggesting that gene pairs with positives GIs
below this threshold were less reproducible, involving less functionally related gene pairs, and thus deemed, lower
confidence. Why does the curve in Fig. 2C stop below a mean GI score of 0.07? Were gene pairs with stronger positive GIs
not considered here? While it is possible that comparison to PPI Kds may be able to identify functionally informative gene
pairs among the set weak positive interactions, the expectation is that a stronger trend should be observed among gene
pairs with stronger positive GIs 
Figure 3: Paralog analysis 
The authors tested if PPIs involving duplicated genes would lead to a lower Kd because the number of complexed protein
pairs should remain the same but the selection on Kd should be relaxed. As a result, they hypothesize that this should also
lead to weaker genetic interaction scores. Examine the following groups: 
[1] PPIs among non-duplicated genes 
[2] PPIs where one protein/pair was duplicated 
[3] PPIs where both proteins were duplicated 
[4] PPIs where a protein physically interacts with its duplicate

Reply: We now provide additional discussion on how experimental design and measurements 
could affect the alignment of protein and gene interaction networks. 
 
We agree with the reviewer that aligning the two networks is simple in principle: because every 
protein in our study originates from a gene, it should be straightforward to simply find overlaps 
between gene pairs (in GI networks) and protein pairs (in PPI networks). The main challenge lies 
in finding overlaps in the interactions: instances where two genes/proteins are connected in both 
the PPI and GI networks. It has been demonstrated that integrating the networks using alternative 
topological features (such as “modules”) increases t he coherence of the alignment  between 
interactions. Nonetheless, as mentioned by the reviewer,  imperfect experimental data remains an 
important challenge. We highlight a few sources: 
 
1) There is i ncomplete overlap between  the nodes in  GI and PPI networks. S ome essential 
genes do not have a temperature -sensitive mutant and therefore cannot be screened for in 
GI screens. Equally, some proteins do not appear in mass spectrometry screens for 
technical reasons (poor peptide ionization, severe misfolding because of the tag, etc.). 
Therefore, not all nodes in a GI network will be in the PPI network, and vice versa. This is 
somewhat mitigated by the way the data is merged, because we only keep the 
genes/proteins that appear in both networks. 
 
2) For technical reasons, not all PPIs reported in the original article were conducive to 
computing stoichiometries and Kd, meaning there are “missing” edges in our quantitative 
network. (We now include this point in our discussion; lines 517-519) 
 
3) Finally, the PPI dataset was generated from a mass spectrometry stud ies of cell lysates , 
which could affect many PPIs. 
 
 
Additionally, beyond experimental limitations,  a methodical challenge in aligning the two 
networks is properly defining the GI network. Because most datasets use growth/fitness as a 
readout to evaluate perturbations of gene pairs, there exists a very wide distribution of genetic 
interaction scores. Most interactions are very low (near 0), and some are strong (much lower/higher 
than 0). Conventionally, a threshold is applied to define a set of “True” GIs. Different thresholds

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

will yield different GI networks and, consequently, this will affect the extent to which GI networks 
overlap with PPI networks. 
 
We address this challenge of defining the GI network in a novel way, by relaxing all assumptions 
of where thresholds should be set to define a GI, and instead, we use the entire dataset. The main 
focus thus becomes only the concordances in the quantitative features of both networks. In this 
case, the quantitative  features are binding stoichiometries and affinities (K d) for protein -protein 
interaction networks, and GI scores for genetic interaction networks , with no predefined 
thresholds. 
 
Finally, with respect to biases in the GI network  stemming from using knockouts, conceptually 
“genetic interaction” broadly refers to functional connectivity between genes, which can be 
assayed by various genetic perturbations (KO, SNP/mutations, structural variants, etc.) and by 
various phenotypic readouts (growth, morphology, etc.). Here, we clarify that “genetic interaction” 
and GI network is operationally defined by a growth phenotype following pairwise knockout (or 
knockdown), as done in literature ( Costanzo et al., 2016 ). Of course, the GI network could be 
different for SNPs or other genetic perturbations, and also for different phenotypes. To clarify this 
nuance, we have added additional lines to the discussion.

Reply: Hierarchical clustering is a common methodology to identify modular  structures in 
biological networks. As part of the hierarchical clustering algorithm, a distance  metric must be 
chosen to then evaluate the “closeness/similarity” between pairs of elements (in this case genes). 
To identify clusters in  their GI network, Costanzo et al. ( Science, 2016) chose the Pearson 
Correlation Coefficient (more specifically, 1-PCC) as the distance metric to be applied on the GI 
profiles of the direct GI network for clustering.  
 
Therefore, in our case, the similarity network is essentially just an intermediate transformation of 
the direct GI network that, for our purposes, only serves the goal of identifying clusters (functional 
modules). Although it is common to use the profile similarity GI network for specific analyses, as 
was done in Costanzo et al. , for instance, this is not the case for our analysis. We are only using 
these sets of genes (clusters) to annotate the direct GI network, we are not integrating the edges or 
weights of the similarity network to that of the direct GI network. 
 
What we mean by “topological features” is stated in the main text and/or methods: PPI modules = 
stable multimeric protein complexes, GI modules = clusters in the GI network, connectors = nodes 
that form the shortest path between two modules. The statement we make throughout the article is 
only that these specific features overlap significantly in both PPI and GI networks  (Fig. 4, and 
related text), and that this overlap appears to be functionally coherent.
