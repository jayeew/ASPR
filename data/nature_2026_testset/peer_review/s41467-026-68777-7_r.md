Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this nice piece of work, the authors present the cryo-EM structure of the dimeric OS9-SEL1L-HRD1 complex, in which
HRD1 dimerizes within the membrane to facilitate substrate translocation, while the SEL1L and OS9 form a claw-like ER
luminal domain that mediates substrate recruitment. Importantly, a series of biochemical experiments using pathogenic
SEL1L mutants and disease-associated HRD1 variants support the functional necessity of this dimer formation in ERAD.
The structure also reveals two methionine-rich crevices in the TM domain of the HRD1 dimer, which likely serves as
conductive channels for membrane protein substrates, similar to those in the EMC. 
Overall, the authors appear to have conducted cryo-EM analysis of the dimeric ERAD core complex, along with biochemical
experiments based on the structural information, in a sound and rigorous manner. The present data provide new mechanistic
insights into the ERAD pathway mediated by the OS9-SEL1L-HRD1 complex, highlighting scientific significance of this
work. However, several important issues need to be addressed appropriately with some additional experiments to make this
paper more convincing and compelling, as described below. 
Major comments: 
1) The most important issue regarding the ERAD pathway mediated by the dimeric OS9-SEL1L-HRD1 complex is how
diverse ERAD substrates are dynamically and efficiently transferred to the retrotranslocation channel formed by the HRD1
dimer through the OS9-SEL1L luminal domain. This reviewer is particularly interested in the dynamic and, if present,
cooperative movements of the luminal and transmembrane domains within the ERAD core complex during the substrate
transfer. In this context, the authors should discuss in greater detail the relative arrangements and/or spatial proximity
between the OS9-SEL1L luminal domain and the conductive channel of the HRD1 dimer. What is the distance between the
end of the putative substrate binding region of the OS9-SEL1L luminal domain and the entrance of the conductive channel of
the Hrd1 dimer? How dynamic is the luminal domain relative to the transmembrane domain of HRD1? What are the size and
chemical features of the putative substrate binding region of the OS9-SEL1L luminal domain? What is the diameter of the
conductive channels formed by the HRD1 dimer? The model shown in Fig. 8 and the text should be revised by incorporating
these critical points. 
2) According to the data processing workflow shown in Extended Data Fig. 3, the authors merged Class 1 (far left) and Class
2 (second from the left) during the 5th heterogenous refinement. However, these two classes appear to have distinct relative
arrangements or orientations of the SEL1L-OS9 luminal domains. Specifically, Class 2 appears to adopt a more open
luminal domain arrangement than Class 1. Given that, the subsequent data processing of local refinement with a C2
symmetry should be performed separately for Class 1 and Class 2, rather than after merging them. This issue is closely
related to the above comment 1. 
3) In the cryo-EM map of the dimeric OS9-SEL1L-HRD1 complex, the local resolution does not appear to be very high for the
TM domain. The authors should examine whether the density map can further be improved by performing a focused
refinement using a mask around the TM domain of the complex as well. 
4) This reviewer has a serious concern regarding the experimental procedure used for the native disulfide crosslinking
experiments. The authors resuspended cell pellets in non-reducing SDS loading buffer that lacks thiol-blocking reagents.
Because the cytosol is a highly reducing environment due to the abundance of reductants such as glutathione (GSH), cell

lysis often results in cleavage of disulfide bonds formed in the ER or other oxidative compartments. To prevent such artificial
reduction, the lysis buffer should contain cysteine-blocking reagents such as NEM. The authors are therefore advised to
repeat this assay using a buffer supplemented with a cysteine-blocking reagent. 
5) In Fig 5, the effects of the T93A/F mutations on HRD1 dimerization are assessed solely based on disulfide bond formation
of the R96C mutant. These data appear to be insufficient. Additional biochemical analysis, such as native-PAGE or size-
exclusion chromatography, are required to strengthen the conclusion. 
Minor comments: 
6) Line 119; Insert a phrase “in a nearly equal amount” to the end of this sentence. 
7) The positions of the asterisks indicating the retrotranslocation channels formed by the HRD1 dimer do not appear to be
identical between Fig. 7a and Extended data Fig. 9a. This should be corrected. 
8) Lines 244 & 245; “Fig 7a” should read “Fig. 7b”. “Extended Data Fig. 9a” should read “Extended Data Fig. 9b”. In this
connection, one of the protomers in Fig. 7b should be labeled as Hrd3. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a cryo EM structure of a human OS9–SEL1L–HRD1 complex, proposes that the HRD1 dimer
forms the retrotranslocation channel for ERAD L substrates, and supports this model with mutational and substrate
degradation data using proAVP(G57S). 
This is a very valuable dataset and the structural work will be of high interest to the ERAD community. However, the current
mechanistic conclusions are too far
‑
reaching, especially in light of the well
‑
established model for Hrd1 function in ERAD
‑
L
in yeast, in which Hrd1 operates monomerically with the Derlin Der1 to form the retrotranslocation channel. I support
publication in Nature Communications after revisions. The authors should either: 
(1) clearly qualify their model by stating that they have not excluded – and have not even functionally tested – Derlin
involvement for their substrate; or 
(2) directly test Derlin dependence for proAVP(G57S) in their system, and ideally probe additional ERAD
‑
L substrates to
assess whether their findings are general. 
Addressing this point, and engaging fully with the existing body of literature in this mature field, is essential before claiming a
mechanistic divergence from the currently accepted paradigm. 
Reasoning and major points: 
1. “Most conserved pathway” – but divergent proposed mechanism 
The authors emphasize in the Abstract and Introduction that the SEL1L–HRD1 branch is “the most conserved” branch of
ERAD. This is correct: the Hrd1 pathway is the only known branch that handles ER
‑
luminal substrates (ERAD
‑
L) in yeast,
animals, and plants. Given such evolutionary conservation, conservation of mechanism would be expected for the core
channel function. 
In yeast, ERAD
‑
L is most likely mediated by monomeric Hrd1 in complex with Der1, with which it forms complementary
“half
‑
channels” in the membrane. This mechanism is supported by cryo
‑
EM structures, functional assays, and even
in
‑
membrane substrate photocrosslinking (most importantly: Wu et al. 2020, Science, PMID: 32327568; Pisa and Rapoport
2022, JBC, PMID: 35970394). The mechanism proposed here — a symmetric HRD1 dimer alone forming the pore with no
Derlins — differs fundamentally from this well
‑
supported yeast model. The manuscript does not confront this conflict
sufficiently or provide a clear rationale for such a dramatic mechanistic divergence in an otherwise highly conserved
pathway. If the differences between their structure and the yeast Hrd1–Der1 channel reflect a true evolutionary divergence,
they should explain why and discuss possible functional drivers. Alternatively, the dimer may represent one of multiple
co
‑
existing states (as in Guo et
 
al., Nat Commun 2025 PMID: 405938782025), rather than the active retrotranslocon. 
2. Derlin involvement not functionally addressed 
Also in mammals, multiple studies (for example: Oda et
 
al. 2006, JCB, PMID: 16449189; Huang et al. 2013 JBC, PMID:
23867461) have shown that Derlin 2/3 can be required for luminal glycoprotein degradation. (Already Lilley and Ploegh
(2005, PNAS, PMID: 16186509) showed that Derlin-2 forms a complex with HRD1/SEL1L complexes). 
The authors purify a stable OS9–SEL1L–HRD1 "core" without Derlins and infer Derlins are dispensable. But: no Derlin
knockdown/knockout tests are presented for proAVP(G57S). Furthermore, absence from a detergent purified prep does not
imply
 
in vivo absence. I find it likely that Derlin association is very sensitive to solubilization conditions and probably lost in
most purifications. Purification in Extended Data Fig. 2b in DMNG shows additional bands; judging from the molecular
weight, these could be HERP1 and DERL2 - please comment. 
The recent Guo et
 
al. (Nat Commun, 2025, PMID: 40593878) structures seem to contradict the authors’ interpretation: they
reconstituted Derlin containing HRD1–SEL1L–XTP3B complexes and showed that Derlin presence breaks the HRD1 dimer
and induces major SEL1L rearrangements, even when Derlin helices are unresolved. This supports Derlins as functional
components in at least some mammalian ERAD L assemblies. These observations need to be discussed in detail in the
current manuscript, with explicit consideration of how the Guo et
 
al. findings impact the interpretation of the OS9–SEL1L–
HRD1 dimer as the active ERAD
‑
L translocon. 
3. Substrate choice and pathway assignment

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

The sole test substrate is proAVP(G57S). This is an established disease linked ERAD substrate, but the manuscript treats it
implicitly as a “pure” ER luminal (ERAD L) substrate without noting caveats. Crucially, it does not cite or discuss the detailed
biochemical study by Friberg, Spiess & Rutishauser (JBC
 
2004, PMID: 14996841). That work showed: (1) Most WT and
mutant pre pro vasopressin enters the ER lumen, is cleaved by signal peptidase, and is N glycosylated confirming bona fide
luminal targeting for the majority of molecules. (2) A significant fraction, however, never enters the ER due to inherent
inefficiency of the native vasopressin signal peptide. These unglycosylated, uncleaved “pre provasopressin” molecules
(Form
 
2) remain cytosolic and are also degraded by the proteasome. (3) Misfolded ER imported molecules are
retrotranslocated and deglycosylated into the cytosol (Form
 
3), consistent with canonical ERAD L processing. These
features are present for both WT and mutant precursors, including in neuroendocrine like cells, so G57S has dual
localisation: part ER luminal, part cytosolic. The current manuscript’s degradation and ubiquitination readouts therefore
monitor a mixed population, and the fraction of ER imported substrate is not established. This matters when making
mechanistic claims about ERAD L. My recommendation is to cite Friberg et
 
al., quantify or at least discuss the cytosolic
mistargeted pool in the presented system, and clarify whether functional readouts here reflect specifically ER imported
substrate. 
4. HRD1 dimerization: interpretation and scope 
The TM3 mutagenesis data show T93A and T93F disrupt crosslinkable dimer formation and impair proAVP(G57S)
degradation without destabilising SEL1L/OS9 binding. However, (1) only one substrate is tested, and — see Friberg et
 
al. —
it is not exclusively ER luminal. (2) The claim that HRD1 forms a “stable, stoichiometric” dimer is too strong; crosslinking
captures ~40% dimer. It is unclear if this limit is technical or if the rest of HRD1 is simply not engaged in homodimers. (3)
TM3 mutations could disrupt not only HRD1-HRD1 pairing but also possible HRD1-Derlin contacts, if Derlins contribute to
the functional pore. Other subtle allosteric effects on translocation cannot be excluded. From the structural position of Ala91,
it is not immediately clear by what direct mechanism this substitution would prevent dimerization. This raises the possibility
of more subtle distortions that could destabilize dimers indirectly or affect other assemblies or interactions. 
5. Dimerization in comparison with the homologous yeast system 
There is no debate that Hrd1 dimerizes in yeast. Multiple studies have demonstrated this biochemically and/or structurally
(Horn et
 
al., Mol Cell
 
2010; Carvalho et
 
al., Cell
 
2010; Schoebel et
 
al., Nature
 
2017; Wu et
 
al., Science
 
2020). The point of
contention is not whether dimers exist, but whether they are the functional form for ERAD
‑
L. In active ERAD
‑
L in yeast, the
prevailing model — backed by Wu et
 
al., 2020 and earlier biochemical work — is that Hrd1 functions monomerically with
Der1, with each protein contributing complementary membrane “half
‑
channels” to form the pathway for substrate
retrotranslocation. Symmetric Hrd1–Hrd1 dimers are not thought to be required for luminal
‑
substrate channel activity.
Dimerization in vivo may nevertheless have other roles, for example in scaffolding, ERAD
‑
M substrates, or complex stability.
Pisa & Rapoport (2022, JBC, PMID: 35970394) is particularly relevant here and should be discussed explicitly. Using
cross
‑
linking in various genetic backgrounds, they concluded that the symmetric Hrd1 dimer is likely off
‑
pathway or
non
‑
functional for ERAD
‑
L, and that the active retrotranslocon is the monomeric Hrd1–Der1 complex. 
The current manuscript’s proposal of a symmetric HRD1 dimer as the mammalian ERAD
‑
L pore therefore directly
contradicts the functional interpretation from the yeast system. This is a mature field, and the authors – in my opinion –
cannot simply propose a radical mechanistic departure from established, well
‑
supported models without thoroughly
engaging with the existing scholarly debate. The authors should address this difference head
‑
on, explaining why a
symmetric dimer would be active in mammals when an analogous arrangement in yeast is viewed as off
‑
pathway, and
considering the plausible alternative that their observed dimer reflects one of several assembly states rather than the sole,
physiologically active channel. 
6. Statistical analysis of biochemical data 
Several key biochemical figures present quantitative data as averaged values from multiple independent experiments, but
without any indication of variability between replicates or formal statistical analysis. For example, in the crucial Fig.
 
5d,
averages from three independent experiments are indicated. Without measures of variance, it is not possible to assess the
reproducibility and robustness of the reported differences. 
Minor: 
7. When referring to yeast complexes, use yeast gene/protein names (Yos9, Hrd1, Hrd3, Der1). Use mammalian names
(OS
‑
9, HRD1, SEL1L, Derlin
‑
1/2/3) for human proteins. This avoids confusion when comparing yeast and mammalian
results. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled Structural basis and pathological implications of the dimeric OS9-SEL1L2-HRD1 ERAD Core
Complex presents the cryo-EM structure of a mammalian ERAD complex comprising OS9, SEL1L and HRD1. The authors
concentrate on an ERAD complex that excludes Derlins, EDEMs, and other ERAD components, based on their preliminary
mass spectrometry and Western blot experiments, which identify the three-component complex as the most stable. They
propose a model describing a structure formed by two identical monomers held together by the HRD1 dimer within the
membrane. The authors speculate that this structure may provide a binding site/conduit for the ERAD substrate. This work is
significant for the field of ER protein degradation, despite the existence of previous studies on similar complexes in yeast or
even mammals, because the structural features reported here are highly distinct. 
Although the data provide a valuable
contribution to our understanding of ERAD complex structures, the following points should be addressed prior to final
decision:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

1. To properly assess the ERAD complex analysis by Western blot, the experiments should be performed in triplicate and
the data shared with reviewers. All quantifications should come from independent experiments, not merely technical repeats,
and should include appropriate statistical analysis. 
2. The authors claim that FAM8A1 deletion has no effect on ERAD function. However, the assessment of ERAD function
based on the Western blot shown in (Extended Data Fig. 1e) is inconclusive. The quantification of Ire1, Cd147, and OS9
levels in KO cell lines should be based on multiple replicates with statistical significance. Moreover, comparing protein
levels across different cell lines is not ideal, as it lacks direct kinetic information. Pulse-chase or cycloheximide chase assay
would be more informative in assessing ERAD function. 
3. The data on the disruption of the ERAD complex in the presence of SEL1L mutants are not entirely clear. For example,
contrary to the authors' conclusion, the double mutant 155 G585D/S658P does not completely abolish interactions with OS9
(as seen in lane 5, Fig. 2f), as some interaction with OS9 is still detectable. 
4. To convincingly demonstrate ERAD function in Fig. 5d, the degradation of the ERAD substrate should be followed by
chase experiments in cells expressing wild-type or mutant forms. Direct comparison of steady-state protein levels of ERAD
substrates across different cell lines is prone to substantial errors. 
5. The authors should discuss how their structural findings compare to previously published data on ERAD complexes,
particularly those from mammalian and yeast systems. Several ERAD complex structures have been identified, and these
structures differ significantly from one another. A comparison with these existing models would help contextualize the current
findings and highlight both commonalities and differences.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have included in the revised version the original blots upon which their conclusions are based. However, a
major concern arises from examining the blots intended to demonstrate reproducibility. Specifically, in Figure 3d, the non-
reducing gel (anti-OS9) shown for experimental replicates 1 and 3 appears to be the exact same gel presented twice(and
HRD1 and HSP90 are shown only for 1 experiment) . The authors must explain how this duplication occurred. Beyond the

Additional points regarding the revised manuscript: 
The findings do not support the claim that this study reveals the “structural framework for mammalian SEL1L-HRD1 ERAD.”

At minimum, Derlin1—absent from this study—could dramatically alter the structural configuration. The results shown reflect
a state of the ERAD machinery lacking key components, not necessarily the native physiological assembly. It remains
unresolved whether the functional entity is a dimeric conduit or a monomer cooperating with a Derlin-based conduit, as
proposed by Wang et al. (Communications Biology, 8:1481, 2025). Therefore, the manuscript should be revised to clarify

Experimental interpretations: 
Although the revised data support the role of dimerization in efficient ERAD of model substrates, the cycloheximide (CHX)
experiments should be properly referred to as turnover assays. The correct interpretation is that the ERAD substrates exhibit
a shorter half-life in the presence of a dimeric complex, rather than simply “accumulating” in its absence. The current
explanation, which focuses on protein accumulation, is imprecise. For example, lines 212–217 describing proAVP G57S*
and POMC C28F* degradation need to be rewritten to reflect this mechanistic clarity. 
Terminology and reagent consistency: 
There is inconsistency in naming and describing materials such as Strep-Tactin (IBA, #6-6350). The manuscript and
Methods annex should explicitly acknowledge that these refer to the same reagent.
