Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents the first high-resolution (4.1 and 4.2 Å) structures of the AP-4 complex core, obtained following chemical
cross-linking of recombinantly produced purified protein complex. The authors observed two conformations in their data,
representing ‘open’/’undocked’ and ‘docked’/’closed’ states. They are able to find the same structures within the
uncrosslinked data, so this indicates that the crosslinking has trapped structures that are present in the absence of
crosslinker. 
The authors compare these structures to each other and to a subset of the published structures of other AP complexes. They
conclude that the closed conformation of AP-4 is destabilized in comparison to that of AP-1, and this provides an
explanation for why they see a mixture of two conformations for AP-4, but AP-1 adopts only its closed conformation in the
absence of cargo binding. 
The authors then use a combination of subunit-subunit fusion constructs and chemical crosslinking to obtain the structure of
AP-4 bound to ARF1. They obtained two conformations again, corresponding to the docked and undocked states, and both
of these ARF1-bound structures were lower resolution (~6-7 Å). These structures resemble the ARF1-bound structures of
AP-1 and AP-3, and are compatible with the known membrane-binding geometry of ARF1. 
The authors engineer FRET probes onto AP-4 and perform FRET. They are able to detect FRET signals that correspond to
the docked and undocked states as well as a third, intermediate state. 
Finally, the authors compare the extent of AP-4 recruitment to membranes by Arf1 and a cargo peptide (from ATG9A). They
found that Arf1 was sufficient to recruit AP-4 to liposomes but the cargo peptide was not. Combining ARF1 and the cargo
peptide enhanced the interaction. 
This study is interesting from the perspective that it presents the first structures of the AP-4 complex. While the analysis of
the minute structural details of which residues interact with each other in the different conformations is very extensive, the
more interesting analysis of how the different conformational states relates to what is already known from other AP
complexes is a bit shallow. In particular, the authors could have presented a more comprehensive comparison of the
structural biology of the four AP complexes to make it more clear which states are similar or different from the two states of
AP-4 presented here. 
Major points: 
1. The title “Structural basis for ARF1-mediated membrane recruitment…” implies the structures were determined on
nanodiscs or liposomes, but this is not the case. As the structure of the ARF1-AP-4 complex may be influenced by
membrane binding (and therefore would be different from the structures presented here), I think it is important to change the
title to make it a more accurate reflection of the findings. 
2. Lines 184-185 state that the undocked AP-4 conformation resembles a similar ‘open’ conformation of AP-3. The authors
should include a figure panel comparing the undocked AP-3 and AP-4 structures. The authors should clarify whether there
are similar open conformations of AP-1 or AP-2 that have been determined, and what was needed (i.e., cargo, PIPs, etc.) to

stabilize the open conformations of these other complexes. 
3. The authors compare the docked conformation of AP-4 to the closed AP-1 structure. However it is unclear whether any of
the known structures of AP-2 or AP-3 also have similar closed conformations. The authors should be more clear on this
point. 
4. The authors should provide a more comprehensive comparison of the existing AP complex structures to the new
structures presented here. 
Minor points: 
5. In the abstract the authors state: “AP-4 stands out as a non-clathrin-coated”. Yet AP-3 is also reported to function without
clathrin. 
6. There is a similar statement on line 84-86, but AP-4 is not so unique, as AP-3 is also reported to function without clathrin. 
7. Lines 67-70 imply that all major vesicle coats include AP complexes and clathrin, but this is not correct. Several major
coats do not include either AP complexes or clathrin (i.e. COPII, COPI, retromer, etc).

to bind to 1 or 2 Arf1 molecules based on the previously published data. The authors should more explicitly describe the
previous findings regarding two binding Arf1 sites on AP-4.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work elucidates the structure of the AP-4 complex and its interaction with ARF1. The authors determine the structures of
AP-4, both in its apo state and bound to ARF1, using cryo-EM. Through single-molecule FRET, they demonstrate that AP-4
is conformationally flexible and populates multiple structural states in solution. Interestingly, ARF1 binding does not appear
to constrain this flexibility. Pull-down assays and bead-based fluorescence recruitment experiments confirm a direct
interaction between AP-4 and ARF1, and reveal cooperative effects in the presence of additional ligands such as ATG9A.
These data collectively offer insight into the structural organization and biochemical interactions of AP-4, a relatively
understudied trafficking adaptor with potential roles in clathrin-independent pathways and trans-Golgi network function. 
While the manuscript is technically rigorous and employs a wide range of structural and biochemical tools, the overall
mechanistic advance appears modest. The conformational flexibility and ARF1 interactions described for AP-4 resemble
previously characterized features of AP-1 and AP-3, and do not reveal fundamentally new principles of adaptor complex
regulation. Additionally, the physiological relevance of the observed interactions remains somewhat unclear, particularly
given that key experiments were performed in solution or artificial recruitment contexts rather than in the presence of native
membrane environments. I believe this limits the extent to which the findings can significantly shift our understanding of
membrane trafficking mechanisms. Nonetheless, I appreciate the quality of the work and provide the following comments for
consideration: 
1. The primary motivation for studying AP-4 here its role in membrane trafficking, but the interactions between AP-4 and
ARF1 are studied entirely in solution environments. There was no work involving membrane-associated forms of the
complex. While I acknowledge that the experiment in Figure 5 partially approximates membrane presentation through
surface tethering of ARF1 to beads, this does not recapitulate the physical or biochemical environment of a lipid bilayer. The
system lacks key features of biological membranes, such as lateral fluidity, curvature, and membrane charge, all of which
can influence recruitment and binding specificity. As such, the manuscript does not demonstrate whether AP-4 is functionally
recruited to membrane-bound ARF1. Reconstituting this interaction on supported bilayers or liposomes would strengthen the
physiological relevance of the findings. 
2. While the authors provide structural insights into the conformational flexibility of AP-4 and its interactions with ARF1, the
functional consequences of this flexibility remain unclear and unexplored. For instance, how these different conformational
states impact cargo recognition, vesicle formation, or intracellular trafficking is not addressed. Without connecting structural
states to functional outputs, it is difficult to assess the biological significance of the observed conformational dynamics.
Incorporating assays that probe how different AP-4 conformations affect cargo sorting or trafficking efficiency would

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

substantially enhance the mechanistic impact of the work. Either additional experiments or provide enhanced discussion
that contextualizes the significance of these results in a broader biological context. I note that there is currently some
discussion already (e.g., AP-4 deficiency syndrome, parallels to AP-1 and AP-3), but the current discussion is quite brief and
doesn’t meaningfully connect the observed structural states of AP-4 to cellular function and trafficking outcomes. 
3. The authors mention and reference previous studies describing how there are two ARF1 binding sites on AP-4 one in the
ε subunit and another in μ4-CTD. However, the second ARF1 site on μ4-CTD is not resolved in any of the presented cryo-
EM structures. While the authors detect binding via pull-down and crosslinking, the absence of structural resolution leaves
uncertainty regarding the geometry, specificity, and functional role of this second interaction. Further work to stabilize or
visualize this interaction could clarify its contribution to AP-4 membrane association and function.

The authors have now fully addressed my earlier concerns. My primary issues were the lack of membrane-based validation
and the absence of data connecting AP-4 conformational states to functional outcomes in cells. In the revision, they added a

consider all of my concerns addressed.

Based on the cryo-EM structures of the AP-4 core complex, AP-4 exhibits at least two 
distinct conformations in solution, in which the C-terminal domain of 4 ( 4-CTD) adopts 
either a docked or an undocked state ( Fig. 1c-f ). Structural comparisons among the AP 
complexes (AP-1 to AP-4) suggest that the μ4-CTD-docked and -undocked structures of AP-4 
correspond to the closed and open conformations of AP-1 and AP-2, respectively

CTD are both docked into the central cavity of the bowl-shaped core, resembling the μ4-CTD-
docked conformation of AP-4 ( Supplementary Fig. 5a-c ). In contrast, in their open 
conformations, μ1-CTD and μ2-CTD become undocked from the central cavity and tend to 
associate with the periphery of the structural core ( Supplementary Fig. 5a-c), resulting in a

contrast, AP-4 can adopt both closed and open conformations in solution, suggesting its 
intrinsic conformational dynamics.

and instead predominantly exists in an open conformation featured with an empty central cavity

CTD may become transiently associated with the periphery of the structural core under certain

Summary  Wang and colleagues present structures of the mammalian AP-4 complex expressed and purified from baculovirus in the presence and absence of Arf1. The authors also perform some biochemical, fluorescence-based, and bead-based experiments to address Arf1 binding sites, conformational flexibility, and recruitment into a membrane mimic. This represents the first report of AP-4 expression and purification, but all structures of proteins without cross-linking are at very modest resolution (6.5-7 Å), so the authors can only observe secondary structure elements (at best). There are two major concerns about this manuscript. The first concern is inadequate reporting of single particle data processing and analysis combined with limited overall resolution. Many of the claims made in the paper lack data to support them. The second major concern is how authors failed to cite multiple important and foundational structural papers in the field, and to compare AP-4 structures adequately or at all to relevant AP family members (AP-2, COPI, AP-3). The presented data suggest AP-4 is conformationally dynamic, but AP-2 has been observed in multiple conformations (outlined below), and single particle cryo-EM has further demonstrated how AP-3 is conformationally flexible.  Major comments  1. Cryo-EM data analysis, reporting, and resolution. Supplementary figures 2 and 6 do not adequately report how single particle data were processed. We have almost no information about how 2D and 3D classifications were carried out. The recent paper reporting soluble AP-3 structures (Figure S2; Begley et al., PNAS 2025) represents a gold standard example of how single particle data should be reported.  The manuscript provides some structural and validation data supporting analysis of the AP-4 core (Figure 1C-F; Supplementary Fig. 1 and 2) and AP-4/ARF1 complexes (Figure 2B-E; Supplementary Fig. 6). The authors include global and local resolution estimates, angular distribution plots, FSC curves, and some biochemical validation to support claims. However, several technical aspects of the cryo-EM analysis and interpretation, particularly concerning resolution limitations, particle orientation bias, and side-chain level interpretations, warrant careful consideration and clarification. The AP-4/ARF1 complex was resolved to ~6.6–6.8 Å, and AP-4 core to ~4.1–4.2 Å (cross-linked) by gold-standard FSC (GSFSC). While cross-linked AP-4 core maps may permit some side-chain modeling in well-ordered regions, the limited resolution of AP-4/ARF1 maps does not permit confident side-chain placement, especially at protein-protein interfaces. Yet, the authors discuss interaction residues in molecular detail. Were these assignments assisted by rigid-body docking, homology models, or side-chain densities? Overall, it seems authors have over-interpreted atomic contacts at these resolutions.  Supplementary Figures 1C, 1G and 6C, 6F present angular distribution plots for each class. These plots suggest moderate to strong preferred orientations, particularly in the μ4-CTD-undocked classes. The authors should include directional FSC (3D FSC) plots to quantify any anisotropy in resolution. This will help readers interpret map reliability in different directions and assess whether preferred orientations might affect model accuracy.
