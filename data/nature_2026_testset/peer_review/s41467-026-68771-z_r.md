Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is an interesting report describing the development of a caged dihydrotetrazine that is liberated to react with
transcyclooctene by exposure to H2O2. The authors describe the synthesis and in vivo characterization of the boronate
caged dihydrotetrazine, noting good uncaging in ~6h at 100 uM H2O2. They develop an on demand bioorthogonal creation
of a protac in response to high H2O2 and evaluate the compound for BRD4 degradation. Finally, they describe an H2O2
triggered click-to-release pair of reagents for generating free doxycycline after bioorthogonal reaction. They evaluate the
click to release strategy in vitro and in a mouse tumor model. 
I enjoyed this report, but there were a few points that subdued my enthusiasm: 
First, although the combination of a bioorthogonal reaction and an H2O2 labile cage has, surprisingly, not been reported, the
chemistry for each is reported and the combination of cage and this bioorthogonal reaction has been reported. The H2O2
labile boronate cage has been around for over a decade, and the caged dihydrotetrazines have been reported to be used, in
the same way, with other well-known caging groups. The application of an existing cage to control an existing reaction is
highly novel if that reaction has not been controlled before in the manner described. The use of a known cage to control a
reaction in the same way as previously reported has somewhat less novelty. In this paper, the interesting applications help
boost the paper’s novelty, but I have some questions about one of them as discussed below. 
Second, and more importantly as I think the above novelty concern can be addressed with applications as the authors do in
this report: the authors note in their mouse tumor model study statements like “Furthermore, we demonstrate a ROS-
triggered tetrazine ligation enabled tumor-selective drug delivery in both living cells and mice” – but both the ros-triggering
and tumor selectivity are not convincingly supported. The authors show no experiments to support that the tumor has high
H2O2 concentrations and that these concentrations lead to release of the drug in proximity to the tumor site. Indeed, in their
in vitro studies, the authors note ~10% formation of reactive comp. 1a after 12 h. The authors allow many days of exposure to
the compounds (the mice are sacrificed on day 12). But, given the concentrations they use (Btz is 115.2 umol/kg, TCO-Dox
was 28.74 umol/Kg) one could expect after 12 h the release of ~11 umol/kg of Btz, which could produce ~11 umol/kg of dox,
and the positive control dox concentration was only 8.6 umol/kg. This is without exposure to H2O2 and would not be
localized at the tumor site. Accordingly, I don’t see how the authors can make the claim of ros-triggered ligation or tumor
selectivity. 
If this second concern is adequately addressed, I think the paper warrants publication in this journal. However, if the results
from the mouse tumor study arise from the H2O2 cage hydrolysis or lability in the absence of high H2O2 in mouse serum,
and if there is no convincing reason that the drug release is H2O2 triggered at the tumor site, I would expect this report to be

published in a less high profile journal.

Figure 1a: authors should note what the error bars represent. i.e., stat test used as well as number and type (biological vs.
technical) of replicate. 
Review citations 1 and 2 are great for bioorthogonal chemistry in general. Inclusion of a review covering on demand and/or
cell selective bioorthogonal reactions would be especially useful for readers in this paper.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the effort by the authors to support the hypothesis that the compound 1+8 conditions in the Dox mouse xenograft
tumor experiments produce tumor-associated release and targeting of Dox. However, I remain skeptical that the effects seen
in these experiments result from tumor associated H2O2 triggered dox release. 
Figure 4: The fact that there is high H2O2 in tumor is not evidence that tumor-derived H2O2 triggers localized Dox release.
Subcutaneous tumor site injected compound 1 could produce localized dox through hydrolysis, given the high
concentrations used. 
Fig. 5b does appear to show that, under the conditions tested, there is more Dox in non-tumor tissues in the free dox
injections vs. the 1+8 injections. However, a direct comparison between these two conditions in this assay is difficult. The
concentrations of Dox released in 1+8 are not known, there may be 2-fold less dox released in 1+8, which could account for
the tissue distribution differences seen. 
Fig. 5c: The ratio between dox level in tumor and liver/heart/kidney does appear to support the notion that there is localized
delivery to the tumor site, but interpretation is again confounded by the different and unknown concentrations of Dox being
delivered/released, and the tumor-localized injection of compound 1, which may be converted to active 1a by H2O2 or
hydrolysis. 
It would be more convincing to have control(s) without high H2O2 in this mouse line (e.g., intratumor injection of catalase, or
exposure to H2O2 scavengers like N-acetylcysteine with or without PD-1 blockers, with an experiment shown in fig 4
showing that the exposure decreases H2O2 in their hands). Decreased efficacy of the 1+8 treatment in these conditions
would help support more directly the authors proposed mechanism. Another useful control would be a compound 1 analog
with similar hydrolysis rates but lacking sensitivity to H2O2. Finally, it would also be more convincing if the compound 1
injection site were not at the tumor site – does the effect go away, or is the tumor/non-tumor tissue ratio altered if 1 is s.c.
injected on the opposite side? Any of these, or other controls addressing these issues, would go a long way towards
supporting the author’s proposed mechanism. 
Another option would be to rewrite this section with less weight on the tumor localized H2O2-triggered drug delivery
conclusion. Although this would decrease the novelty somewhat.
