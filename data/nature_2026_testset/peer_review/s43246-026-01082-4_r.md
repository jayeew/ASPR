Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This paper utilized ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations to investigate the underlying mechanism of a graphene-
based ion transistor. The authors studied a graphene channel system filled with K ions to explore the transistor's on/off
switching effect, signal amplification, and ultra-high ion diffusion rate. Some issues should be emphasized: 
1. The total simulation trajectory time is 2000 fs, which is relatively short for an ion diffusion process. Would a longer
simulation time be influencing the correlation coefficient or the molecular dynamics results? 
2. When studying the ion dehydration mechanism, the author points out that the resonance between the external ion's
oscillation and the channel ions' oscillation leads to rapid dehydration. Is this resonance effect universal? For ions of
different sizes (such as Na+ or Li+) or different types of solvent molecules, would the resonance frequency and dehydration
mechanism change? What are the implications of this for designing ion transistors with different ion selectivity? 
3. Given that graphene was synthesized long ago, what makes the application of graphene in this specific field a novel
contribution? In other words, what is the innovation of this work? 
Reviewer 2: See attached file. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents a computational investigation into graphene-based ionic transistors (GITs) using ab initio
molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations. It explores the quantum correlation of confined ions as a potential mechanism
underlying energy-efficient neuromorphic devices. 
1. The idea of quantum-correlated ion dynamics in graphene channels is original and could significantly advance ionic
neuromorphic systems. 
2. The AIMD simulations are well-parameterized (LDA, 500 eV cutoff, 0.2 fs timestep) and yield atomistic insights beyond
classical MD. 
3. The identification of π–π stacking vs cation–π competition and collective oscillation modes as drivers for transistor
ON/OFF states is convincing. 
4. The connection to ultralow-energy neuromorphic computation is conceptually strong 
Recommendations 
1. The manuscript lacks direct experimental validation or comparison with published data beyond ref. 12 (Science 2021).
Include additional quantitative comparison (e.g., energy barriers, transport coefficients) with experimental ionic transistors or
DFT studies. 
2. The quantum correlation concept is primarily inferred from ionic trajectories and charge redistribution. Provide a
quantitative descriptor (e.g., charge-density correlation function or time-dependent electron localization function) to
substantiate “quantum coupling. 
3. The figures are clear but need improved captions and discussion. 
4. Several sentences are redundant or grammatically awkward (e.g., “ultrahigh ion diffusivity of the transistor is enabled
through the rapid ion dehydration…”). A careful language edit for conciseness and technical precision. 
5. The manuscript would benefit from a deeper comparison with existing neuromorphic paradigms, e.g., memristive or
polyelectrolyte-based systems. Discuss how ionic transistors compare in terms of energy per synaptic event or scalability. 
6. The implications for real devices (integration, stability, fabrication) are underdeveloped. Add a short “Outlook” section or
paragraph in the Discussion connecting the atomic-scale insights to device-level engineering. 
7. The AIMD time scale (2 ps) is too short to fully capture ion transport statistics; the authors should justify or extrapolate this
limitation. 
8. A few citations (e.g., Ref. 12, 55–57) could be expanded with brief contextual remarks rather than just listings. 
9. Some figure legends repeat text from the Results — integrate interpretation directly into Results for better flow. 
Overall Decision (Major Revision) 
The paper offers strong mechanistic novelty but requires clearer validation, quantitative evidence for quantum effects, and
tighter presentation. If these revisions are addressed, the manuscript could make a meaningful contribution to
Communications Materials.

The resonance effect is ion-specific, not universal. Li⁺ exhibits oscillation peaks at 6-7 THz, 
showing severe mismatch with water libration frequency (3 -4 THz) and remains 
incompletely dehydrated at approximately 2 waters after 16,000 fs. Na⁺ shows oscillation 
peaks at 4-5 THz with partial mismatch, achieving complete dehydration in approximately 
8,000 fs, which is 8 times slower than K⁺. K⁺ exhibits oscillation peaks at 3.7 THz with 
perfect resonance match, completing dehydration in 1,000 fs. 
The oscillation frequencies qualitatively follow the simple harmonic oscillator model, 
where lighter ions with stronger hydration exhibit higher frequencies and consequently 
worse resonance matching with water molecules. Implications for transistor design: 
First, K⁺- selective devices can exploit resonant dehydration for ultrafast switching on 
picosecond timescales, while intrinsically rejecting Na⁺ (8 times slower) and Li⁺ 
(incomplete dehydration) through kinetic selectivity. Second, the design pri nciple can be 
generalized by matching channel properties (dimensions and surface chemistry) to the 
target ion's oscillation frequency for tunable selectivity. Third, frequency mismatch 
provides intrinsic selectivity without chemical functionalization, simp lifying device 
fabrication. Fourth, for non-aqueous solvents with different libration frequencies, the same 
framework applies with different ions showing optimal resonance. 
This ion-specificity is advantageous for rational design, providing a predictive framework 
for engineering transistors with desired selectivity.

Key innovations:
