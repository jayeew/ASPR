Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Wang et al. presents a method for generating vascularized cell spheroids through peptide enzymatic self-
assembly. The formed intercellular nanofibers modify the extracellular matrix and trigger mechanotransduction pathways,
resulting in 3D spheroids. 
Co-culturing with endothelial cells promotes angiogenesis with enhanced transport and
communication. The vascularized spheroids mimic human tissue physiology, showing improved functionality such as insulin
secretion and tumor gene expression similarity. This approach advances tissue engineering by replicating ECM architecture
and integrating mechanical cues. The findings offer a valuable tool for studying cellular interactions, drug responses, and
cancer therapy, paving the way for improved regenerative medicine and organoid development. This work may lead to the
production of larger size tissues, currently limited by the lack of vasculatures. Therefore, I support its acceptance after the
authors address the following issues. 
1. I wonder if CSP is susceptible to proteolytic hydrolysis? 
2. The authors are suggested to provide LC-MS results corresponding to the EM images to confirm the hydrolysis of CSP at
2h and 24h. 
3. The authors are suggested to discuss why increasing the concentrations of CSP beyond 50 μM deceases the size of
spheroids and 
why 200 μM fails to form spheroids. 
4. The authors used the analog CSP2 to demonstrate the necessity of enzymatic responsiveness for spheroid formation, yet
Figure S21 shows its applicability across cell lines with varying ALP expression levels. Do these findings contradict each
other? 
5. The formation of spheroids among some cell lines (Saos-2, HepG2) seems not intuitive. The authors are suggested to use
a unified parameter to characterize the spheroids. Images of untreated control cells should also be included for comparison. 
6. The authors should at least mention the use of CSP/NBD-CSP mixture for visualizing the peptides in vitro and specify the
ratio used and how the ratio was selected. 
7. It seems that data points from the 50 μM CSP-treated group in Figure 2d, Figure S22 and Figure 3e are the same. If these
experiments were conducted separately, a same-batch control group should be included instead of reusing data. 
8. What is the concentration of CSP used in Figures 3e and S26? 
9. I wonder if CSP could also trigger the formation of HUVEC spheroids? 
10. Despite the HUVEC is used, the size of the spheroids is still small. It would be useful for the authors to comment that. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Lu et al. developed a peptide self-assembly strategy to promote spheroid formation. Before acceptance, major
revisions are suggested to enhance the manuscript: 
Major points: 
1. A key suggestion is to systematically compare spheroids generated using the CSP method with those formed in a low-
adhesion plate, a widely used technique. The authors should perform assays such as imaging, live/dead staining,
immunostaining, and RNA sequencing. Additionally, testing a cell line that does not readily form spheroids in a low-
adhesion plate, such as MDA-MB-231 cells, would be beneficial. If the CSP method enables spheroid formation in 231 cells,
it would further validate its advantages over traditional approaches. A potential drawback of the CSP method is the difficulty
in controlling spheroid size and cell number. The authors could attempt forming spheroids with CSP in a low-adhesion plate

to ensure all cells aggregate into a single spheroid, allowing for better control over cell numbers. 
1.2 A related concern is the presence of gaps between cells in CSP-formed spheroids. In low-adhesion plates, spheroid
formation relies on tight adhesion molecules, such as tight junctions, particularly in epithelial-based spheroids. It remains
unclear whether CSP-formed spheroids exhibit similar tight junction formation or if nanofibers occupy space, leading to
looser aggregates. The authors should address this point through discussion if no additional data are available. 
2. It is unclear how U87MG cells express ecto-phosphatase. The authors need to specify the construct or plasmid used in
the methods section. Furthermore, the expression of ecto-phosphatase in other cell lines is not clear either. Considering
potential applications of CSP, it is impractical to overexpress phosphatase in every cell line or primary cells. The authors
should investigate whether adding alkaline phosphatase (ALP) to the culture medium induces similar levels of spheroid
formation and compare the effects of ALP supplementation versus using cell lines expressing ecto-phosphatase. 
3. Additional characterization of CSP-A is needed, ideally similar to the CSP characterization shown in Figure 2. The
authors should also assess the effects of CSP-A on endothelial cells alone (without tumor spheroids). In addition, the
authors should test CSP-A on CSP-tumor spheroids alone (without HUVECs). It is unclear whether CSP-A competes with
CSP, potentially loosening tumor spheroids. Furthermore, details on spheroid collection are needed—were they removed
mechanically or enzymatically (e.g., with trypsin)? Were all spheroids collected from each well or only a subset? Were single
cells adhering to the well surface also collected? Additionally, the authors should clarify whether CSP-A was added 24
hours after seeding into a new plate or it was added from time 0. The method used to form spheroids in Matrigel and agarose
groups is also unclear. More details should be provided, including whether all spheroids (or only CSP/CSP-A groups) were
CSP-treated and whether spheroids were embedded in Matrigel or grown on top. In Figure S27, better controls would
involve CSP-formed spheroids co-seeded with endothelial cells in CSP-A, Matrigel, or agarose, rather than introducing two
variables (CSP treatment and different matrix types). The authors should repeat the experiment with appropriate controls for
structural comparisons. 
Minor points: 
1. In the abstract, the mention of insulin secretion lacks context, as the surrounding discussion focuses on tumors. The
authors should clarify that this relates to an islet cell line model. 
2. In Figure 1, the full name of “SA” should be spelled out in the figure legend. If it stands for “self-assembly,” this should be
explicitly stated.

adjusted so that Figure S1 is referenced first rather than Figure S17. A similar adjustment should be made for Figures 2d
and 2e.

µM CSP does not induce aggregation. Additionally, they should specify the buffer used to dissolve CSP and whether it
affects cell viability at high concentrations (e.g., if DMSO is used). 
5. In Figure S20, the CSP group has a high Z-distance from the coverslip. It would be useful to examine Congo Red staining
in non-aggregated cells close to or on the coverslip. If the authors lack fixed samples for re-imaging, this reviewer doesn’t
request new experiments. The authors can simply describe previous observations. 
6. In multiple figures, the CSP group is presented before the control group. The authors may consider reordering figures so
that control groups appear first. 
7. The description of Figure 2g is unclear. The blue line should be explicitly defined in the legend. The authors state that
after 5 hours, cells aggregate around nanofibers, but it is difficult to visualize how cells (which are >10 µm in size) aggregate

8. Figure S21 should include images of control cells without CSP (time 0). Additionally, the authors should clarify whether

9. In Figure 2b, the black line indicating 6.3 nm in the CSP group appears shifted. The authors should correct this. 
10. In the legend of Figure 2g, there is an extra “t” before “CSP,” which should be removed.

images were taken at the top or bottom of spheroids. 
12. More experimental details on AFM are needed. The authors should clarify whether the cantilever was coated with any

13. Discussion is needed for Figure 3d. The authors should explore whether the observed effects result from reduced

14. More details are needed on YAP experiments, including CSP treatment duration before fixation. A discussion of YAP’s
role in spheroid formation would be valuable. Ideally, YAP staining could be performed on spheroids formed in low-
adhesion plates as a control (no CSP) for comparison. If this exceeds the authors’ capacity, a discussion alone would
suffice. 
15. Figures 3i and 3j look to be gene expression profiles rather than proteomic analyses. The authors should verify and
correct this in the text. 
16. In line 192, there is an extra space between “inducing angiogenesis” and “of cell spheroids.”

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study proposes a potentially interesting strategy for engineering spheroids with angiogenic capacity using peptide-
based self-assembly. However, several conceptual overextensions, insufficient validation of critical claims (e.g.,
vascularization, mechanical mimicry, transcriptomic relevance), and missing methodological details undermine the scientific
rigor of the current manuscript. This reviewer suggest that this paper is not suitable for Nat Comm and its readership due to
lack of innovation, scientific rigor, and in general not unto the high standards of this journal. 
Below are many major and minor points. 
1. Incomplete Validation of "Neo-Capillary" Formation 
The manuscript refers to the emergence of “neo-capillaries” upon co-culture of CSP-treated spheroids with HUVECs.
However, this claim is supported only by immunofluorescence staining for CD31 and visual evidence of endothelial cell
elongation and sprouting. No clear lumen structures, flow, or other characteristics are shown. Moreover, FITC-dextran
diffusion is marginal and appears superficial, without quantitative analysis of penetration depth or rate. Authors claim of
these structures as "neo-capillaries" is not rigorously supported by data and it is not convincingly demonstrated. Functional
vascularization must be validated through additional experiments (e.g., perfusable lumen imaging, microbead flow assays,
or vascular permeability analysis). 
2. Lack of Quantitative Mechanical Characterization of ECM Environment 
The claim that CSP-induced peptide nanofibers mimic ECM and mechanically modulate the microenvironment is primarily
supported by decreased adhesion force via AFM. However, this single-point measurement lacks comprehensive mechanical
profiling such as matrix stiffness, viscoelasticity, and elasticity – parameters critical for ECM function. The authors should
perform mechanical characterizations (e.g., rheometry or nanoindentation) to quantify matrix stiffness and validate how their
system compares to native ECM or widely used hydrogel models. Direct comparisons with Matrigel or collagen-based
systems are necessary to contextualize the mechanical relevance of CSP nanofibers. 
3. Absence of Biochemical Cues in Synthetic ECM 
Although the authors present data (Fig. S23) indicating the presence of ECM proteins such as laminin and fibronectin within
the spheroids, these components appear to be secreted by the cancer cells themselves rather than being part of the
synthetic peptide matrix. This indicates that the CSP nanofiber system lacks built-in biochemical cues typically present in
native ECM—such as integrin-binding motifs (e.g., RGD sequences) and enzymatically cleavable sites. The system instead
depends on endogenous ECM production by the embedded cells, which may vary across cell types and experimental

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

conditions. Such reliance raises concerns about the reproducibility, controllability, and consistency of the ECM environment
across different experiments. The authors should explicitly acknowledge that biochemical ECM functionality is not intrinsic to
the CSP system. They should discuss the variability of endogenous ECM secretion and its potential impact on cell behavior
and experimental reproducibility. Where possible, comparative studies should be added to assess how reliably ECM
components accumulate across cell types and under varying conditions. 
4. Characterization of 3D Culture Model 
The system is presented as a 3D in vitro model; however, it consists of homotypic spheroids without supporting ECM or
vascular scaffolds. This format lacks the spatial complexity and multicellular organization characteristic of advanced 3D
cultures (e.g., organoids, organ-on-chip models). Consequently, the functional claims (e.g., tumor modeling, vascular
transport) may be overstated. Authors should acknowledge that spheroids without ECM scaffolds do not fully recapitulate the
3D architecture of in vivo tissues. Comparison with advanced 3D culture systems (e.g., Matrigel-embedded spheroids,
microfluidic chips) is essential to justify the translational relevance of the model. 
5. Comparison with Conventional Models 
While the authors position their system as a superior alternative to Matrigel or other ECM-mimetic hydrogels, no direct
functional comparisons are presented. There is no demonstration that CSP-based spheroids exhibit improved
vascularization, cell viability, or functional performance relative to conventional ECM. Furthermore, vascularization of
U87MG is verified with other nature ECM in other references (e.g. S Lee, Microsystems and Nanoengineering, 2022).
Therefore, head-to-head comparisons with spheroids cultured in Matrigel or fibrin gels are needed, examining parameters
such as growth kinetics, marker expression, gene profiles, and functional readouts (e.g., insulin secretion, diffusion
capacity). 
6. Transcriptional Similarity to Clinical GBM 
The claim that vascularized spheroids closely resemble patient-derived GBM is based on differential gene expression
comparisons and GSEA, using data from a public dataset (GSE119834). However, no clustering analysis, batch correction,
or quantitative similarity metrics (e.g., correlation coefficients) are provided. The inherent differences between in vitro
spheroids and patient tumors (cellular heterogeneity, stroma, hypoxia) are not addressed. More rigorous comparative
transcriptomic analysis is needed. Authors should conduct principal component analysis (PCA) or UMAP clustering and
provide correlation coefficients between models and patient samples. Batch effect control and normalization steps should be
explicitly stated. Furthermore, comparisons must be made with in vitro controls (e.g., Matrigel spheroids) to justify the
relevance of their model. 
7. Interpretation of YAP Signaling Pathway 
The manuscript links actin remodeling induced by CSP nanofibers to YAP nuclear translocation but fails to demonstrate
intermediary steps within the Hippo pathway, such as LATS1/2 kinase activity or YAP phosphorylation status. The claim that
actin remodeling “activates” YAP is thus mechanistically incomplete. Authors should include upstream Hippo pathway
analyses, such as LATS1/2 phosphorylation levels and phospho-YAP/total YAP ratio, to provide mechanistic support for the
observed YAP localization. This would clarify whether the observed nuclear YAP translocation is indeed
mechanotransduction-driven or due to alternate pathways. 
8. Validation Approach for the In Vitro Model Using Vascularized MIN6 Spheroids 
Although a physiological in vitro spheroid model is expected to demonstrate increased insulin secretion due to glucose
delivery via the vasculature, the figure shows that the vascularized spheroid appears to uptake glucose independently of the
vasculature (Fig 6.e-f). It is recommended to modify the experimental design to ensure that glucose is specifically delivered
through the vasculature. 
Again, it is necessary to verify with images and other data that the vessels are perfusable and have
lumens. 
9. RNA-seq Sampling Methodology 
It is unclear whether RNA-seq was performed on individual spheroids or pooled samples. Given the typically low RNA yield
from single spheroids, pooling is likely but not specified. This lack of detail impairs reproducibility and undermines the
reliability of differential expression results. Authors must clarify the number of spheroids used per sample, pooling strategy (if
any), and replicate numbers (biological vs. technical). This is essential for evaluating data reproducibility and ensuring
adequate sample complexity. 
10. Misleading or Oversimplified Data Presentation 
Figure 1 contains a schematic that inaccurately reflects the experimental setup, particularly showing a petri dish layout while
the actual experiments were conducted in 96-well plates. Additionally, other figures such as FITC-dextran diffusion, YAP
localization, and endothelial sprouting rely largely on qualitative or semi-quantitative interpretations. These lack rigorous
statistical analysis, spatial quantification, or fluorescence intensity profiling to support the conclusions. For instance, FITC-
dextran fluorescence is interpreted as enhanced transport, yet no depth profile or diffusion quantification is provided. Authors
should provide quantification for all imaging data (fluorescence intensity profiles, spheroid size distribution,
nuclear/cytoplasmic YAP ratios). Schematics should accurately reflect experimental conditions (e.g., 96-well plates vs.
illustrations). 
11. Omission of Abbreviation Explanations 
The manuscript shows inconsistency in the explanation of abbreviations. In some instances, the same abbreviation is
unnecessarily repeated (e.g., ECM is defined more than once), while in other cases, key abbreviations such as CSP, NBD,
or CT are introduced without any definition or explanation. This lack of uniformity can lead to confusion among readers,

particularly those who may not be familiar with the specific terms, ultimately detracting from the clarity and professionalism of
the document.

Addition in main text: 
 
The decrease in spheroid size at higher CSP concentrations (>50 µM) and the failure to 
form spheroids at 200 µM are governed by the balance between the generation rate of the 
hydrolyzed product and cell aggregation dynamics. While the enzymatic hydrolysis rate 
per se may remain constant (as enzyme concentration is fixed  of the same density of the 
cells), increasing the CSP concentration elevates the amount of hydrolyzed product due 
to greater substrate availability. The hydrolyzed product forms at a moderate rate at 50 
µM CSP, allowing cells to aggregate into larger spheroids before becoming entrapped in 
the assembled matrix. At 100 µM CSP, the higher precursor concentration increases the 
cumulative hydrolyzed product over time, leading to earlier and more ext ensive matrix 
formation. This restricts cell -cell contact at a smaller spheroid size.  At 200 µM CSP, the 
rapid accumulation of hydrolyzed product overwhelms the system, entrapping 
individual cells before they can establish stable contacts. This prevents sp heroid 
formation entirely, as the matrix solidifies around dispersed cells.  Therefore, the total 
hydrolyzed product generated  determines whether cells form large spheroids, small 
spheroids, or remain isolated. The threshold concentrations reflect a kinetic  competition 
between cell aggregation and matrix entrapment.

form spheroids at 200 µM are governed by the balance between the generation rate of the 
hydrolyzed product and cell aggregation dynamics. While the enzymatic hydrolysis rate 
per se may remain constant (as enzyme concentration is fixed  of the same density of the 
cells), increasing the CSP concentration elevates the amount of hydrolyzed product due 
to greater substrate availability. The hydrolyzed product forms at a moderate rate  at 50

the assembled matrix. At 100 µM CSP, the higher precursor concentration increases the 
cumulative hydrolyzed product over time, leading to earlier and more extensive matrix

formation. This restricts cell -cell contact at a smaller spheroid size.  At 200 µM CSP, the

hydrolyzed product generated determines whether cells form large spheroids, small 
spheroids, or remain isolated. The threshold concentrations reflect a kinetic competition 
between cell aggregation and matrix entrapment.
