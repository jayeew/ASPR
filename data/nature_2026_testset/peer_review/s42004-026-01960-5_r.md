Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript reports the results of molecular dynamics and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics simulations on the
interactions of the 
QCy(MeBT)3 fluorescent probe with B-DNA compared to Quadruplex DNA. The claim is that
QCy(MeBT)3 has some conformations which bind preferentially to Quadruplex DNA while other conformations bind
preferentially to B-DNA. These different conformations of QCy(MeBT)3 also have different fluorescence and absorbance
properties that give rise to the apparent shifts in spectra upon binding to their targets. The manuscript is largely based on
experimental evidence reported in Anal. Chem. 2022, 94 (10), 4269–4276. No further experimental evidence to test the
proposed model from the simulations is presented. No statistical significance is reported and the few error bars that are
given are not defined. While there are potentially interesting findings, the current form of the manuscript is not sufficiently
persuasive. It would be helpful if predictions from the model were subsequently tested experimentally rather than simply
developing a model to explain previous experimental observations of a different group. In addition, the following issues
should be addressed. 
1. The claim "we present a multiscale computational protocol" is questionable, since only molecular simulations are
performed. 
2. There is no section describing the statistical methods used except a few for ligand force field parameterization. 
3. The calculated fluorescence emission spectra of Figure 5 do not appear to be a tight match to the previously published
experimental fluorescence emission spectra; although they do indicate an overall shift. 
4. How do the authors propose to lock a specific conformation tailored to recognize a single DNA structure? 
5. The software used such as for the molecular dynamics and quantum mechanical simulations are not described in the
methods. Also, the parameters for such simulations are not given such as vs implicit solvent vs explicit solvent, etc.

Response to Referee #1: 
 
The manuscript reports the results of molecular dynamics and quantum mechanics/molecular 
mechanics simulations on the interactions of the QCy(MeBT)3 fluorescent probe with B-DNA 
compared to Quadruplex DNA. The claim is that QCy(MeBT)3 has some conformations which bind 
preferentially to Quadruplex DNA while other conformations bind preferentially to B-DNA. These 
different conformations of QCy(MeBT)3 also have different fluorescence and absorbance properties 
that give rise to the apparent shifts in spectra upon binding to their targets. The manuscript is 
largely based on experimental evidence reported in Anal. Chem. 2022, 94 (10), 4269–4276. No 
further experimental evidence to test the proposed model from the simulations is presented. No 
statistical significance is reported and the few error bars that are given are not defined. While there 
are potentially interesting findings, the current form of the manuscript is not sufficiently persuasive. 
It would be helpful if predictions from the model were subsequently tested experimentally rather 
than simply developing a model to explain previous experimental observations of a different group.  
We thank the Reviewer for recognizing the potential interest in our findings . Our study employs a 
multistep computational approach to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying B -DNA/G4 
recognition by QCy(MeBT)₃, as well as the subsequent electronic absorption and fluorescence 
properties. Notably, QCy(MeBT)₃ represents the first experimentally reported switch -on fluorescent 
probe capable of distinguishing between two distinct DNA conformations  – double-stranded B-DNA 
and G4  – through emission of different fluorescence colors. We believe it is of fundamental 
importance to understand the physicochemical basis of QCy(MeBT)₃ behavior, which cannot be 
convincingly addressed without the detailed computational simulations we provide. Establishing 
these mechanistic insights lays the groundwork for rationally proposi ng modifications to 
QCy(MeBT)₃, thereby enabling future experimental validation of our computational predictions. In the 
revised main text, we also briefly outline a plausible experimental strategy (see our response to Issue 
4 raised by the Reviewer). 
Concerning statistical significance and error bars, we have improved the revised main text and 
Supplementary Information as follows: 
- In Figure 2B, we have clarified the meaning of the error bars related to the interaction energy 
in the caption and, more specifically, in the revised Methods section. 
- In the same figure, the histogram showing the highest relative population is derived from 
Boltzmann distribution analysis. To clarify this point, we have added the term “Boltzmann” 
to the caption. Section 1.6 of the Supplementary Information describes in d etail how the 
relative population of each conformation –binding mode pair constituting the DNA complex 
was determined through Boltzmann distribution analysis. As shown in Eq. S8, the relative 
populations depend on the temperature and the energy differences between conformers. 
Since the temperature was kept constant throughout all simulations (298.15 K), the only 
source of uncertainty arises from the en ergy differences between conformers.  In this 
context, reporting error bars is meaningful primarily when comparing conformers with 
similar relative populations, where uncertainties could potentially lead to an inversion of 
their ranking. However, the aim of  this analysis is not to establish a precise quantitative 
ordering of all conformations, but rather to identify the dominant binding modes of 
QCy(MeBT)₃ with B-DNA and G4. From this perspective, the histograms clearly indicate that 
ABD and DAB are the most relevant conformations for G4, while ADB and CDB dominate for

B-DNA. Therefore, we consider the inclusion of error bars unnecessary for the purpose of 
this population analysis. We have briefly explained this point in the revised Methods section. 
- For Figure 3B, the associated errors are now reported in the Supplementary Information 
(Data S1 and S2), including average (AV) and standard deviation (STDDEV) values in kcal/mol 
for the electrostatic (EELEC), van der Waals (EVDW), and total (TOTAL) interaction energies. 
Only physically meaningful errors are presented, namely those corresponding to 
interactions between QCy(MeBT)₃ (column “R1”) and selected nucleotides (column “R2”) 
that exhibit the most relevant interaction energies (i.e., the minima in the plots of Figure 3B). 
The column labeled “filename” identifies the initial (T) and actual (F) conformations of 
QCy(MeBT)₃, allowing the grouping of data according to the actual conformation. 
- In the Supplementary Information, we provide d in its original version a detailed quantitative 
assessment of the uncertainties associated with each bond (Table S3) and angle (Table S4) 
force constant defining the QCy(MeBT)₃ force field for classical molecular dynamics. The 
captions explicitly state that “the reported standard deviation corresponds to the highest 
deviation among the individual parameters, while the accompanying %diff values quantify 
the percentage difference between the highest and lowest parameters. ” Furthermore, 
Figures S2 and S3 present a graphical validation of the calculated bond and angle force fields 
through the coefficient of variation, defined as CV (%) = (standard deviation / mean) × 100. It 
is worth emphasizing that such a comprehensive statistical characterization of force  field 
parameters is rarely performed or reported in the literature, underscoring the robustness and 
transparency of the present parametrization.

4. How do the authors propose to lock a specific conformation tailored to recognize a single DNA 
structure? 
The most straightforward strategy to conformationally lock QCy(MeBT)₃ consists in introducing two 
bridges that generate intramolecular links between the arms of the chromophore, such that one arm 
is connected to the other two. This synthetic approach has already been employed to restrict the 
conformational freedom of various chromophores and to modulate their photophysical and 
photochemical properties. Notable examples include its application to s tilbene, azobenzene, and 
pyrene, giving rise to the well -known stilbenophanes (J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2019, 10, 5, 1063 –1067; 
Eur. J. Org. Chem. 1998, 149 –154; Tetrahedron Lett. 1981, 22, 2313–2316.), azobenzenephanes  
(Chem. Commun. 2021,  57, 10079 –10082; Ch emPhotoChem 2023, 7, 10, e202300126; Sens. 
Actuators B Chem. 1995, 29, 148–153; Liebigs Ann. 1996, 1213 –1216.), and pyrenophanes  (J. Org. 
Chem. 2012, 77, 1, 57 –67; J. Org. Chem. 2019, 84, 9, 5271 –5276; Angew. Chemie Int. Ed. 1996, 35, 
12, 1320–1321.). 
Concerning the nature of the bridges, alkyl chains, polyether macrocycles, and diazamacrocycles of 
different lengths have been used, among others  (Chem. Rev. 2015, 115, 16, 8736 –8834.). In the 
specific case of QCy(MeBT)₃, a diazacyclophane could be envisioned to connect two arms through 
the nitrogen atoms of the benzothiazolium units, which are already methylated, provided that both 
nitrogen atoms are oriented toward the two arms to be linked. Varying the length of the bridging chain 
could allow discriminat ion between the two double -bond conformations (A  and B or C and D, see 
Figure 1), either bringing the N-methyl groups closer together or moving them farther apart. 
For conformations in which the N-methyl groups of the benzothiazolium units are not located on the 
same side, alternative bridging strategies may be considered, such as linking an N-methyl group to a 
position on the benzene ring of the other benzothiazolium unit or connecting the benzene rings of two 
different benzothiazolium units.

Response to Referee #2: 
The manuscript by Gramolini et. al. presents a multiscale computational protocol aimed at 
rationalizing the dual -color fluorescence and dual -target binding of QCy(MeBT)₃, a recently 
developed probe capable of recognizing both B -DNA and G -quadruplex DNA (G4 ). The work is 
technically ambitious and provides a detailed attempt to explain how this tri -branched fluorophore 
differentially engages with canonical duplex DNA and G -quadruplex structures. The study is 
scientifically interesting, and the overall framewo rk is promising. However, several methodological 
assumptions require stronger justification, the force -field development needs more rigorous 
validation, and several mechanistic interpretations should be either supported with additional 
evidence or moderated. Addressing the following issues is necessary before the manuscript can be 
reconsidered for publication in Communications Chemistry. 
1.The manuscript outlines a multi-step procedure for building ground- and excited-state force fields 
(S₀/S₁) for QCy(MeBT)₃ (SI §1.4). However, no benchmark comparison against established small -
molecule force fields (such as GAFF2 or CGenFF) is provided. I n addition, the excited -state force-
field parametrization is left unvalidated, which is a crucial omission for a system where electronic 
structure changes strongly influence geometry and dynamics. 
We provide a comparison between the S₀ and S₁ force fields generated using our procedure and those 
obtained with the GAFF2 force field. The main differences can be summarized as follows: 
- The S₀ and S₁ GAFF2 atom types are identical  (see picture below) , whereas our procedure 
allows for the discrimination of distinct atom types (see Figure S1). 
 
- The S₀ and S₁ GAFF2 parameters are also identical (the corresponding frcmod file is provided 
for review purposes only ); as a result, the GAFF2 approach is unable to capture the 
geometric differences required to properly describe electronic absorption and emission 
processes. 
- Referring to the nomenclature shown in Figure 1, GAFF2 reverses the bond length alternation 
of branch 2 in the S₀ state, while in branches 1 and 3 the π-conjugation is misrepresented, as 
evidenced by an exaggerated bond length alternation (1.45/1.35/1.45 Å vs 1.44/1.37/1.42 Å). 
- Consequently, the S₀ GAFF2 dihedral barriers are significantly underestimated compared to 
those obtained with our procedure  (and as expected by a fully conjugated chromophore ),

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

particularly for the torsions around the C –C bonds connecting the central ring to each 
branch, where the barriers are reduced by approximately 50%. 
- In the S₁ state, the GAFF2 procedure fails to capture the differentiation among the three 
branches observed with our approach (see Figure 4B). 
- Additionally, in the S₁ state, GAFF2 does not reproduce the expected shortening of the C=O 
bond, which is correctly described by our methodology. 
We have included these findings in the revised Supplementary Information (SI, §1.4.7) and have 
added a corresponding reference to this comparison in the main text (Methods section).  
 
2.Classical MD simulations on an excited state are inherently delicate, particularly for extended π-
systems whose S₁ potential energy surfaces are often non -harmonic. No assessment of state 
ordering stability across MD snapshots is reported, making it difficult to judge whether the S₁ 
trajectory remains physically meaningful. 
We address comments 2 and 3 together, as they are closely related (see below). 
3.The authors implicitly assume that S₁ corresponds to a single, well -defined local minimum. It is 
unclear how the possibility of S₁/S₂ mixing or state reordering is excluded. Similarly, the potential 
impact of charge averaging or conformation-dependent charge redistribution on the S₁ force field is 
not discussed. 
We agree with the Reviewer that an assessment of the nature of the S₁ electronic state after the S₁ 
MD simulations is necessary to verify that the trajectory remains physically meaningful and to clarify 
the possible occurrence of S₁/S₂ mixing or state reor dering. Moreover, such effects may indeed 
depend on the specific probe conformer considered. 
In the revised Supplementary Information, we provide, for each of the most relevant probe 
conformers (seven bound to G4 and five bound to B -DNA; see Figure 2B), the absorption spectra 
including vertical excitations to the lowest excited states computed aft er the S₀ MD simulations, as 
well as an analysis of the nature of the S₁ state based on 100 snapshots extracted from the S₁ MD 
trajectories. 
The results show that, for all conformers, the absorption spectra are well described by considering 
transitions from the ground state (S₀) to the five lowest excited states (S₁–S₅). In all cases, the S₁ state 
corresponds predominantly to a HOMO→LUMO (H→L) transition, while S₂ is mainly characterized by 
a HOMO→LUMO+1 (H→L+1) transition. This electronic character is preserved in the snapshots 
extracted from the S₁ MD simulations used to compute the emission spectra, thereby allowing a 
reliable assessment of possible S₁/S₂ mixing. More specifically, for each frame analyzed, the S₁ state 
remains largely dominated by the H→L transition, with only minor contributions from the H→L+1 
transition observed in a limited number of cases. Importantly, no state reordering is observed for any 
of the conformers. 
We can therefore conclude that the S₁ trajectories remain physically meaningful and do not exhibit 
any dependence on the specific probe conformation. In light of the importance of this aspect, we 
have included the corresponding results in Figure S14 of the Supplementary Information and added 
a dedicated paragraph in the main text to introduce and briefly discuss the validation of the emission 
spectra calculations: 
“Concerning the electronic nature of the S₁ state responsible for fluorescence, we analyzed in detail 
the emission properties of all the main conformers identified (Figure 2B). We find that the S₁ state is

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

predominantly characterized by a HOMO→LUMO transition, similarly to what observed for 
absorption, with only minor contributions from the HOMO→LUMO+1 transition (corresponding to S₂ 
in absorption). This indicates the absence of significant S₁/S₂ mixing prio r to fluorescence emission 
(Figure S14).” 
 
4.Docking is performed on rigid conformers, yet QCy(MeBT)₃ exhibits substantial conformational 
flexibility (32 conformers identified), and MD simulations reveal rapid interconversion. Rigid -body 
docking may therefore misclassify binding modes due to artifi cial conformational locking. This 
limitation should be acknowledged, and ideally a more flexible docking or ensemble docking 
approach should be used. 
We thank the Reviewer for bringing this issue to our attention. We would like to clarify that the number 
of conformations (32) was defined a priori by considering all possible isomers generated by rotation 
around the single bonds of QCy(MeBT )₃. Each of these 32 conformations was subsequently 
optimized at the QM level on the ground state and in implicit solvent. The optimized structures were 
then individually subjected to rigid docking with G4 and B -DNA, in order to preserve each specific 
conformation during the docking process and to generate well -defined initial complexes 
(QCy(MeBT)₃/G4 and QCy(MeBT)₃/B-DNA) for the subsequent MD simulations, as detailed in Scheme 
1 and in the Methods section. 
This strategy ensures that no possible conformer is excluded at the start of the MD simulations. As 
correctly noted by the Reviewer, the MD simulations indeed show rapid interconversion among 
conformers—made possible by the generated QCy(MeBT)₃ force field . However, by employing rigid 
docking, we ensured that all possible conformations were initially represented. In contrast, the use 
of flexible docking would likely bias the sampling, as the starting conformation might not be 
preserved during the docking procedure. 
To reinforce this point, we have added the following sentence in the Molecular Docking subsection of 
the Methods section: 
“This procedure guarantees the preservation of each specific QCy(MeBT)3 conformation during the 
docking process.” 
 
5.The manuscript relies heavily on MMPBSA binding free energies (reported values around −20 to −22 
kcal mol⁻¹) to support claims of affinity and selectivity (Figure 2B). MMPBSA is known to perform 
poorly for highly flexible and multiply charged ligands. Entropic contributions appear to be neglected, 
and no replica calculations or uncertainty estimates are provided. Error bars, uncertainty analysis on 
per-residue decomposition, and cross -validation with MMGBSA or alchemical methods would 
substantially strengthen the conclusions. 
We agree with the Reviewer that the MMPBSA approach employed in this work does not explicitly 
include entropic contributions. We have adopted this approximation for the following reasons. First, 
the inclusion of entropy estimates within the MMPBSA framewor k (typically via normal -mode or 
quasi-harmonic analyses ) is known to be computationally demanding and often affected by poor 
convergence for biomolecular systems (Expert Opin. Drug Discov., 2015, 10, 449 –461). Moreover, 
such approaches have been shown to m iss important contributions, including conformational 
entropy and solvent rearrangement effects (Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys., 2018, 20, 14450–14460).

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

On the other hand, entropic contributions are expected to be relatively small and comparable across 
the different systems considered here, since the G4 and B -DNA scaffolds are only minimally 
perturbed upon ligand binding, and the differences among the QCy( MeBT)₃ conformers arise 
primarily from intramolecular rotations. 
Regarding uncertainty estimation, we have now clarified the meaning of the error bars in Figure 2B 
and explicitly reported standard deviation values for the relevant interactions with selected 
nucleotides (Data S1 and S2). These values provide an estimate of the variability at the per-nucleotide 
level, as the interaction energies shown in Figure 3B are obtained from a linear decomposition of the 
total interaction energy. To further improve clarity, we have added a dedicated subsection entitled 
“Statistical Significance and Error Analysis” to the Methods section, with additional details provided 
in the Supplementary Information. 
Finally, although cross-validation of MMPBSA results against alchemical free energy methods would 
indeed represent a more rigorous benchmark, such approaches are computationally prohibitive for 
the present system, which involves 32 conformers for two disti nct DNA sequences and unbound in 
water solvent (Chem. Rev., 2019, 119, 9478–9508). Moreover, MMGBSA was not considered, as it has 
been shown to underperform relative to MMPBSA in predicting binding free energies due to less 
accurate generalized Born solvation models compared to Poisson-Boltzmann (J. Chem. Inf. Model., 
2011, 51, 69–82). 
Summarizing, we selected the MMPBSA method to estimate binding free energies as it represents an 
effective compromise between accuracy and computational cost, while allowing the analysis of a 
large number of MD trajectories. To clarify this point, we have added the following sentence (with 
appropriate citations) to the Molecular Dynamics and Binding Energy Estimation subsection of the 
Methods section:  
“Considering the large set of MD simulations performed, the use of the MMPBSA method to estimate 
binding Gibbs free energies represents a compromise between accuracy and computational cost, in 
comparison with the less accurate MMGBSA approach and the more rigorous but computationally 
prohibitive alchemical free energy methods. ”
