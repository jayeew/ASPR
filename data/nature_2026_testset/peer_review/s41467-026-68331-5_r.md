Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Zhong P et al. describes the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) three-dimensional (3D) structures of the
human vasopressin V1a receptor (V1aR) in complex with three different types of antagonists, and in its apo state. These are
the first high-resolution structures of V1aR ever described. Interestingly, all structures presented in this manuscript are those
of homodimeric V1aR particles. In addition, the authors show that the apo state structure of V1aR displays a flat extracellular
loop 2 (ECL2), a significant difference in conformation with that of the antagonist-bound receptor states (in which this
extracellular loop exhibits a typical antiparallel beta-sheet). 
This work is a nice combination of recombinant receptor expression and purification, nanobody selection and production,
cryo-EM structure acquisition and analysis, site-directed mutagenesis and cell-based pharmacological assays. This study
provides a comprehensive understanding of V1aR assembly and opens the way to structure-guided development of new
antagonists for treating central nervous system disorders. It also gives information about antagonist receptor selectivity. 
The methods developed are state-of-the-art and the results presented in the article are really interesting for G protein-
coupled receptor (GPCR) research community. However, I have one major concern and also some other criticisms before
the manuscript would be accepted for publication in Nature Communications. 
1/ major comment: 
My main criticism is focused on the ligand-free state of the V1aR. The author claim that the structures reveal a distinct
conformation of ECL2 in the apo state compared to the antagonist-bound states. I agree with this nice idea but I think the
present results do not support the conclusions. 
The authors indicate that in the apo state, the ECL2 adopts a flat configuration whereas in the ligand-bound states this loop
is in an open (up) conformation with a typical antiparallel beta-sheet conserved in most class A GPCRs. Moreover, the
canonical disulfide bond between ECL2 and transmembrane domain 3 (TM3) is absent in the apo state while it is present in
the antagonist-bound conformations. I think this disulfide bond is not seen because the ECL2 could not be constructed
totally. Why the conserved disulfide bond would not be formed in the apo state? If I remember well, this post-translational
modification should be done in the endoplasmic reticulum, before targeting the receptor to the plasma membrane and before
interactions with ligands. Again, the antiparallel beta-sheet is not seen since part of the loop is missing in the density map,
and amino-acid residues 191-201 are not present in the corresponding model. ECL2 is probably more flexible in the ligand-
free state, thus I can understand it is more difficult to get a high-resolution density map in this region. 
However, would it be possible to collect more movies (and substantially increase the number of particles) with this apo state
sample in order to be able to solve completely the ECL2 and to conclude accurately? Indeed, authors collected only 4860
movies for the apo-state, and were able to collect 12117 or 6163 for the atosiban-bound state and the balovaptan-bound
state, respectively. 
If a new cryo-EM session is acquired and if the complete density of the ECL2 is seen, I would be happy with the authors
conclusion. It would be quite interesting to discuss the role of the ECL2 in a “flat” conformation and its transfer into an “up”
conformation in the presence of ligands. Could this loop close the solvent-exposed orthosteric binding pocket to help in
maintaining a basal receptor state? Could this loop mimic a neutral ligand (no activation)? Since this apo state is the first
one solved in the vasopressin/oxytocin receptor family as compared to active and inactive conformations of vasopressin V2
receptor (V2R) and oxytocin receptor (OTR) already available, the conclusions about the difference in ECL2 conformations

between ligand-free and antagonist-bound states are crucial. 
2/ minor criticisms: 
a) If we look at the density map of the apo state, there is a density on the intracellular side at the dimer interface between
TM1 and TM7 which has not been assigned. This density is present in the two copies of the V1aR, symmetrically. Would this
density correspond to a phospholipid? To a cholesterol molecule? Could this density correspond to part of helix 8 which is
actually not seen? 
b) Again, in the apo state structure, if we overlap the density and the corresponding three-dimensional model at the level of
the extracellular loop 3 (ECL3), it is quite difficult to fit the residues in the density. I think some residues of the ECL3 should
be removed. 
c) All the structures solved in this study are those of dimeric V1aR. Indeed, size-exclusion chromatography (supplementary
figure 1) and SDS-PAGE of the cryo-EM samples show purified dimeric species, in agreement with the homodimers seen in
live cells. However, monomeric species are also seen in the samples that were pooled for subsequent cryo-EM grid
preparations. I understand that image processing was started with an initial set of particles from which the software was
trained to define those particles as templates containing only dimers, before picking the complete set of particles across all
micrographs. Do the authors also see monomer particles in their cryo-EM preparations and micrographs? Would it be
possible to process these particles like dimer particles, keeping in mind the size of these particles is perhaps too small for a
single particle cryo-EM approach? 
d) In absence of the nanobody 1 (Nb1), the resolution of the maps was very limited, whereas in the presence of Nb1, the
overall resolution of the four maps is high (between 2.6 and 3 angstroms). Thus, the presence of Nb1 certainly stabilizes the
complexes. Although Nb1 facilitated particle classification and alignment, its density was not resolved in the final maps. The
authors suggest that Nb1 binds to a relatively flexible part of V1aR. Based on the stability of the complexes, is this
conclusion not paradoxical? 
e) To my opinion, the comparison of the different antagonist-bound V1aR structures is very interesting and is not sufficiently
discussed. In supplementary figure 6, the structure of V1aR bound by atosiban, balovaptan and SRX246 were
superimposed, but only the receptor is shown. Once the three structures are aligned based on the receptor structure, would it
be possible to overlap the three ligands? I think it would be very useful to zoom on this ligand overlap and to present this
illustration as a main figure. Comparing the docking poses of the three ligands in the orthosteric binding pocket in a single
panel would be highly informative and would strengthen the discussion part on receptor antagonism and subtype selectivity.
Since the orthosteric binding pocket is well conserved in the vasopressin/oxytocin receptor family, the binding sites of
atosiban, balovaptan and SRX246 could be compared to that of retosiban in OTR, and those of tolvaptan and
mambaquaretin in V2R. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper presents the first high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the human V1aR in its apo state and in complex with three
clinically relevant antagonists, atosiban, balovaptan, and SRX246. A major finding is the dimeric assembly of V1aR, which
contrasts with previously resolved monomeric structures of related receptors and most of other class A GPCRs, providing
crucial insights validated by cell-based assays. The study also elucidates significant conformational changes in ECL2,
which transitions from a "flat" conformation in the apo state to an "up" conformation upon antagonist binding, creating a
ligand-accommodating cavity. Furthermore, the structures and associated functional assays detail the distinct binding modes
of the antagonists and identify key molecular determinants governing V1aR antagonism and subtype selectivity. This
comprehensive mechanistic framework for V1aR regulation and assembly offers invaluable insights, paving the way for
structure-guided design of novel antagonists. 
However, while the structural data are of high quality and the reported V1aR dimer interface is intriguing, several major
concerns regarding receptor function and pharmacology studies significantly limit the mechanistic impact and physiological
relevance of the findings. 
1. The only assay used to characterize the engineered V1aR as V1aRcryo is the 3H-AVP radioligand binding assay. This
does not assess whether the receptor is functionally competent. It is critical to perform signaling assays such as Ca2+
mobilization or G protein coupling to demonstrate that the engineered receptor retains signaling capacity. Without such data,
it is unclear whether the receptor represents a physiologically relevant signaling-competent conformation. 
2. For the radioligand binding assay, the saturation binding curve for 3H-AVP binding to V1aRcryo (Supplementary Fig. 2a)
does not reach a plateau and appears nearly linear across the concentration range tested. This calls into question the
reliability of the reported Kd values and suggests that AVP binding is severely compromised (not slightly compromised).
Accurate Kd determination under equilibrium conditions is essential for subsequent pharmacological analyses. It is
commended to include more data points in the 3H-AVP saturation binding assay. 
3. The competition binding assays for the antagonists are also highly problematic. The authors show that all three
antagonists exhibit similar IC50s (higher IC50 for balovaptan) for V1aRcryo compared to wtV1aR, and state that “Notably,
the binding of antagonists atosiban, balovaptan, and SRX246 to V1aRcryo, which are the focus of the current study, were
almost unaltered” (line 109). However, according to basic pharmacological principles, if AVP binding is compromised (i.e.,
increased Kd), and the IC50 values for antagonists are unchanged, it implies that the Kd values for the antagonists must also
be compromised to the same extent. The authors should calculate and report the Kd values of all three antagonists using the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

Cheng-Prusoff equation. 
4. This issue also applies to the mutagenesis studies. In the mutagenesis studies, comparisons of IC50 values across V1aR
mutants are presented without confirming whether AVP binding is preserved in each mutant. This is a critical oversight, as
changes in AVP affinity would influence competition binding results. The proper approach is to first determine AVP Kd for
each mutant and then calculate the corresponding antagonist Kd values to allow meaningful interpretation. 
5. The observed dimer interface is structurally interesting, but its physiological significance remains uncertain. Class A
GPCR dimerization is still a controversial topic. It has been well established that class A GPCRs do form clusters or loose
oligomers under physiological conditions. However, whether they form stable dimers and how the oligomerization of class A
GPCRs affect their signaling is still elusive. The authors should consider whether the observed dimerization may result from
cryo-EM sample preparation artifacts such as the nanobody or the receptor modifications. In this regard, functional
validation, such as ligand binding or signaling assays for dimer interface mutants like K52A and A59Y, is essential to
demonstrate the relevance of dimerization to receptor function. 
Moreover, it is important to report whether monomeric particles or highly oligomerized particles were also observed in the
cryo-EM analysis. Please reference the recent study reporting higher-order oligomeric structures of a class A GPCR
(doi.org/10.1038/s41594-024-01397-1). This would help clarify whether V1aR forms obligatory dimers, as seen in class C
GPCRs, or if dimerization represents just one of several possible oligomeric states. 
6. Previous studies have reported dimeric or oligomeric structures of other class A GPCRs, including the apelin receptor
(Nat Struct Mol Biol 2022; Nat Commun 2024) and CXCR4 (Nat Commun 2023; Cell Rep 2025). Comparing the V1aR dimer
interface with these receptors would help contextualize the findings and clarify whether this dimerization mode is unique or
shared. 
Other minor issues 
7. It would be highly informative to discuss ligand selectivity in more details, as it is an important issue for developing
antagonists for the AVP and OT receptor family. Atosiban is commonly used as a OXTR antagonist. 
8. Since structures of V2R-AVP and OXTR-OT are available, it would be informative to model the binding of AVP and OT to
V1aR and discuss its preference for the vasopressin and oxytocin peptides.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Zhong et al. present the structures of human V1aR in its apo state and in complexes with different
antagonists. Overall, the manuscript is well-written. I am not a structural biologist and so am only commenting on the single
molecule photobleaching experiment in Figure 2 b-f which I believe needs additional clarification and replicates to
demonstrate statistical robustness. 
Briefly - the authors perform single molecule photobleaching experiments in fixed cells that are transiently expressing GFP-
tagged versions of the WT and K52A/A59Y versions of their protein. They conclude that the WT protein shows
predominantly two-step photobleaching whereas the mutant protein shows single step photobleaching. Below are
comments and suggestions for improvement: 
Major Comments: 
1) The authors describe a need to maintain “very-low” expression levels to minimize overexpression artifacts, yet it does not
appear that they actually quantify the expression levels in cells. How do the fluorescently tagged protein levels compare to
endogeneous levels in WT cells? 
2) It would be helpful if the authors could confirm that the GFP-tagged constructs are still functionally active. E.G. do cells
expressing the GFP-tagged constructs respond similarly to agonism/antagonism compared to the non-fluorescently tagged
proteins? 
3) The authors show a representative image in Figure 2c, but do not state what the cell in the image is expressing.
Furthermore, they indicate that only spots within the yellow circles were chosen for further analysis, but they do not state how
these spots were selected or why they were chosen instead of the many other puncta-like objects in their image. I’m also
concerned that the representative image shows many large aggregates of protein in addition to single diffraction-limited
spots. What are these large aggregate structures? Are they an oligomerization artifact due to the tagged protein? 
4) There are no error bars on Figure 2 e or f, nor do the authors provide information about the sample size (number of spots,
number of cells, nor number biological replicates). Are these plots from just a single ROI in a single cell each? 
5) At least in my experience, it is very unusual to have single molecule photobleaching defined by only monomers or dimers
and not have any higher-order oligomerization states (even if only at a small percentage). Were these curves discarded from
the analysis?

images to 8642 images now), which increases the final particles from 319,985 to 504,370. The 
overall resolution of the cryo-EM map has been improved from 2.9 Å to 2.8 Å. While the

separated, with bulky residues such as F207 and W111 positioned between them (Fig. 3b). This 
arrangement provides strong structural evidence that the two cysteines remain unpaired in the 
apo state.

the right ligand, while minimizing nonspecific interactions, particularly because the ligand-
