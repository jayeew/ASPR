Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a novel and significant approach for assembling open-framework cubic superlattices from
spherical nanoparticles without relying on directional "valence-like" interactions. The authors demonstrate the assembly of
various cubic structures including rock salt, CsCl, zinc-blende, diamond, and simple cubic phases by grafting gold
nanoparticles with oppositely charged, end-functionalized PEG polymers and controlling electrostatic interactions through
pH modulation. This work represents a significant advance in nanoparticle self-assembly with important implications for
materials science and photonics. The core findings are sound and the experimental approach is well-designed. This
manuscript may be accepted after minor modification. 
Specific comments: 
1. The Methods section on pages 11-12 must replace HCl concentration estimates with direct pH measurements using
microelectrodes, include detailed PEG grafting density quantification via NMR or XPS, and provide reproducibility data
across multiple sample preparations. 
2. The manuscript requires addition of TEM/SEM images in Figure 2 and Figure 3 for direct structural visualization and
selected area electron diffraction patterns to confirm SAXS assignments. 
3. Quantitative comparison between predicted phase boundaries in Figure 4b and experimental observations in Figure 4a is
necessary, with explicit explanation of discrepancies. The "particle fractionalization" mechanism in Figure 3c requires
detailed explanation. 
4. A comprehensive table comparing this method with DNA-origami, patchy particles, and other assembly approaches must
include assembly time, yield, structural quality, and cost metrics. The manuscript needs specific discussion of scalability
limitations, long-term stability testing over weeks to months, and thermal compatibility with device fabrication processes to
establish practical viability beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors report a strategy to create superlattices using valence-free nanoparticles (NPs). They thoroughly studied the
diverse lattices formed by charged polymer-grafted NPs and demonstrated the effect of experimental parameters (e.g., the
ratio of hydrodynamic sizes between two NPs) on the assembly structures. Specifically, they achieved a wide range of
lattices, such as rock salt, CsCl, zinc-blende, diamond, and the exceptionally rare simple cubic phase, with tunable lattice
constants. The lattice formation was confirmed through comprehensive in situ SAXS measurements and performing MD
simulations. Although many previous works have demonstrated lattice formation in binary microparticle systems via
controlled electrostatic interparticle interactions, relatively few studies have achieved this at the NP level. Therefore, I
recommend the publication of this work in Nature Communications after revisions and addressing following
questions/comments to clarify the manuscript. 
1. The authors emphasized the importance of using “valence-free” NPs for assembly by stating that “The fabrication of
diamond-like superstructures has traditionally relied on patchy or anisotropic NPs, with directional interaction sites.” and
“Only report of a diamond superlattice, assembled without valence, exhibited broad X-ray diffraction peaks, signaling poor
structural quality.” However, it is unclear in the manuscript why using “valence-free” NPs is more important than utilizing
“valence-like” bonding. Could the authors provide a more detailed explanation of the rationale behind employing “valence-
free” NPs for assembly? 
2. The authors mentioned that “Although ZnS-like assemblies consistently exhibit 1:1 stoichiometry, they can arise from

diamond (Fig. S9) lattices. 
3. In the introduction, the authors mentioned that “Only report of a diamond superlattice, assembled without valence,
exhibited broad X-ray diffraction peaks, signaling poor structural quality.” In this manuscript, the authors emphasize their
SAXS signals are highly strong. Are the authors aiming to highlight that stronger SAXS signals can be obtained through an

further improve the clarity of the manuscript as below. 
1. The same symbol Q is used for two different meanings in the manuscript: (1) surface charge and (2) q-value in SAXS data.
This dual usage could be confusing, and I suggest distinguishing the notations more clearly. 
2. Both terminologies “LRO” and “SRO” are used in Fig. 1c, but their definitions appear only in the Methods section, which is

Response: We appreciate the reviewer's point. In our model, the nanoparticle charge 
is initially approximated as a point charge, but in reality, the charge is distributed across 
the polymer shell. At large interparticle separations, Gauss’s theorem ensures that 
treating the charge as either a point or a uniform surface distribution yields the same 
contribution to the free energy. However, at short separations, the uniform 
approximation neglects the fact that charges are discrete and can interact through 
explicit electrostatic correlations. To account for these effects, we introduce the 
parameters ΔqA and ΔqB (note: as per the reviewer's request, the notation ΔQ is 
changed to Δq), which represent fractional charges distributed over spherical angles on 
the nanoparticle surface. These terms capture local electrostatic interactions at the 
nearest-neighbor distance (rc), thereby incorporating correlation effects that are 
essential for understanding the dependence of the phase diagram on the size ratio γ. 
Action taken: We expanded the Fig. 1 caption to include the sentence: “∆qA and ∆qB are the 
amount of charge that needs to be considered explicitly to account for electrostatic correlations, 
see SI for a more detailed derivation.” In the main text, immediately after introducing the 
parameter α, we added: “where ∆qA and ∆qB denote the amount of surface charge specified 
within a defined solid angle, where the charge needs to be considered explicitly to account for

5. In Fig. 2, the authors mention the zinc-blende and rock salt structures transform into 
diamond and simple cubic lattices, respectively, when the pH is changed from 3 to 4. Is 
this transformation reversible? For example, do the diamond and simple cubic lattices 
transform back into zinc-blende and rock salt structures, respectively, by lowering the 
pH from 4 to 3? 
Response:  We thank the reviewer for this question. In principle, the transformation 
between zinc-blende/rock-salt (at pH ~3) and diamond/simple cubic (at pH ~4) is 
reversible, as it is driven by changes in protonation/deprotonation state of the terminal 
groups and thus by electrostatic interactions. However, in practice, achieving full 
reversibility is experimentally challenging. Lowering the pH is straightforward by the 
addition of HCl, but raising the pH afterward requires the addition of alkaline agents 
such as NaOH, which produces NaCl as a byproduct. The presence of NaCl disrupts 
the delicate ionic interactions responsible for lattice stability, preventing a clean reversal. 
Therefore, while the assemblies are highly responsive to pH changes and the mechanism 
is theoretically reversible, in our current system, the chemical constraints of adjusting pH 
(particularly the accumulation of salts) limit our ability to demonstrate reversible cycles. 
Developing salt-free strategies to tune pH would be a promising avenue for future work.

6. The current SAXS measurement is performed in situ, meaning that the assemblies 
are formed in the solution state. What changes in the lattice structures would the authors 
expect if the assemblies are fully dried? Would the lattices be expected to remain 
unchanged? Alternatively, could the drying process induce lattice deformation, leading 
to changes in the lattice constant or transformations in the lattice type? 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this important question. Most nanoparticle 
crystallization studies, including ours, are performed in either aqueous suspensions or 
organic solvents, and one of the long-standing challenges in the field is how to extract 
intact crystals from such suspensions. Drying is not a trivial process because it 
inherently alters the conditions that stabilize the assemblies, such as pH, ionic strength, 
and hydration. In our case, the assemblies rely on soluble polymers (PEG) in aqueous 
environments, similar to DNA-programmed assemblies, where drying can induce 
conformational changes of the polymer or DNA strands. 
As a result, drying may indeed alter lattice parameters and introduce defects. However, 
a complete transformation from one lattice to another is unlikely. Instead, the more 
probable outcomes are deformation of the lattice constants and the introduction of 
structural defects, which can disrupt long-range order. For this reason, in-solution SAXS 
provides the most reliable structural information for these assemblies in their native 
stabilized state.

conditions.
