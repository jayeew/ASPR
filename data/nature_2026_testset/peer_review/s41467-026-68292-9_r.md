Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary 
This paper introduces DeepSTD, a deep learning-based method for compressing medical images by decoupling them into
shape and texture components relative to a predefined template. Shape information, representing geometric deviations from
the template, is encoded using a Cycle Implicit Neural Representation (INR) approach. Texture information, captured after
aligning the image to the template, is compressed using a data-driven 3D Transformer-CNN Mixed encoder with Positional
Encoding (3DTCMPE). The method aims to combine the high fidelity of INRs with the speed of data-driven approaches.
Experiments on large-scale CT and MRI datasets, along with X-ray and other MRI datasets, demonstrate superior
performance compared to state-of-the-art methods in terms of compression quality and encoding speed. The paper also
highlights practical features like multi-GPU acceleration, handling limited training data via augmentation and distillation, and
flexible compression ratio control. 
Major Concerns 
1.Template Selection and Visualization: The template is central to the method, yet its selection process and characteristics
are insufficiently detailed and justified. 
- The "widest imaging range" criterion needs more rationale. For example, MRI sometimes high noise and suffer from the
uniformity problem. From your description, the image which has a severe artifact could be selected as the template. Its
robustness to images with significant artifacts (common in MRI) or anatomical outlier shapes is not discussed. How are
potential negative impacts on registration accuracy for the rest of the dataset mitigated? 
- Visualizations of the actual templates selected for the main datasets (CTSpine1K, Amos) should be provided in the main
text or supplementary materials. 
2.Clarity and Definitions: 
- The acronym "3DTCMPE" should be explicitly defined upon first use (presumably 3D Transformer-CNN Mixed encoder
with Positional Encoding). 
- The training process needs slight clarification. Confirm explicitly that shape alignment (Cycle INR optimization) is
performed for training samples before/during the 3DTCMPE training phase. 
3.Handling Limited Data and Distillation Details: 
- The knowledge distillation setup requires more detail for reproducibility. Explicitly state the teacher/student datasets used
for the experiment in Fig 4e (presumably full Amos vs. limited Amos). 
- The value of the loss weighting factor α (Eq. 10) used in the distillation experiments should be provided. 
4. Scope and Limitations (OOD/Functional Data): 
- The paper should explicitly discuss the limitations regarding Out-of-Distribution (OOD) data. While applicability across
different retrained datasets is shown, the performance on unseen variations (pathologies, scanners, protocols) within a
dataset is not explored. 
- A critical limitation regarding applicability to functional imaging (fMRI, PET, etc.) should be discussed. The reliance on
anatomical registration for shape-texture decoupling makes the current approach unsuitable for modalities where intensity
lacks consistent anatomical correspondence. 
The paper presents a promising and high-performing approach for medical image compression. The core idea of shape-
texture decoupling leveraging both INR and data-driven methods is sound and yields impressive results. However, revisions
are needed to improve clarity, particularly regarding the crucial template selection process, provide necessary details for
reproducibility, and explicitly discuss the method's scope and limitations, especially concerning OOD and functional data.
Addressing these points will significantly strengthen the manuscript for publication. 
(Remarks on code availability) 
The code above is not fully prepared for the release. They just provide the 3DTCMPE and cycle INR for texture encoding
and image deformation, receptively. Authors describe that they will release the code after accept. The full process of
DeepSTD should be added in the repository. Moreover, the benchmark results described in the paper will be helpful if they
are included in the README.md.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present DeepSTD, a novel approach to medical image compression that decouples shape and texture
components for separate encoding. This method aims to overcome limitations of existing compression techniques by
combining the advantages of Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) and data-driven methods. The manuscript
demonstrates impressive performance across various metrics (compression ratio, encoding quality, and speed) on CT and
MRI datasets, achieving up to two orders of magnitude faster encoding than competing methods while maintaining superior
quality. While the concept is innovative and the results are compelling, several areas require clarification and improvement

## Major comments: 
1)The authors mention that this approach was inspired by research on medical templates (lines 44-56), but they don't go into
much detail about the theoretical basics. How exactly does the suggested decoupling connect to the well-established
principles of shape-texture separation in image processing? And what ensures that this method actually achieves the best
possible separation between shape and texture components? 
2) The authors mention (lines 490-495) that the widest imaging range sample is selected as the template, but do not
sufficiently investigate how template selection impacts performance. How sensitive is the compression performance to the
choice of template? Would an "average" template constructed from multiple samples yield better results than a single
exemplar? 
3) The authors present DeepSTD as a hybrid approach that integrates both autoencoder-based compression and INR for
shape encoding. While lines 56-63 briefly suggest this combination aims to leverage the strengths of both paradigms, the
manuscript lacks an explanation of why this particular fusion is optimal. A more thorough analysis of how the shape-texture
decoupling specifically enables this synergy would strengthen the contribution. 
4) The manuscript shows significantly better compression performance on CT data (a compression ratio of 256× with an
MAE of 6.8 HU and SSIM of 0.993) compared to MRI data (128× compression ratio with an SSIM of 0.993). However, the
authors don't offer a deep dive into why this big difference exists. This gap raises some important questions about the
method's basic assumptions and limitations. I suggest the authors look into whether differences in how images are acquired,
signal-to-noise ratios, or natural tissue contrasts impact how well the shape and texture decoupling works. Also, the
manuscript should spell out more clearly the specific conditions and limitations that define where DeepSTD works best. 
5) While the authors present shape and texture encoding as separate components, there is limited discussion of potential
interdependencies between these aspects. How do errors or limitations in shape encoding propagate to affect texture
encoding performance? Conversely, how sensitive is the overall system to variations in texture encoding quality when
shape registration remains constant?

3. What theoretical or empirical background supports the decoupling of shape and texture in 
medical imaging data for compression purposes, these are several lossy and lossless 
compression algorithms so which compression algorithms provide the optimum results? 
The rationale for decoupling shape and texture in medical image compression builds on 
the long-established template-based paradigm in medical imaging, where anatomical variability 
is represented by geometric deformations (shape) and aligned intensity patterns (texture). Shape 
can be compactly expressed as a continuous deformation field, making it naturally suited for 
implicit neural representations (INRs). After template registration, texture redundancy is 
significantly reduced, enabling data -driven encoders to efficiently achieve high compression 
fidelity. This conceptual basis is supported by prior research on medical templates and 
deformable registration. 
Our experimental results further confirm this rationale. The proposed Cycle INR achieves 
superior registration accuracy and efficiency over B -spline and other variants, while the 
3DTCMPE encoder consistently outperforms JPEG2000, HEVC, DVC, and TCM for texture 
encoding. When benchmarked against both lossy and lossless algorithms (JPEG, JPEG2000, 
HEVC, VVC, BRIEF, DVC, TCM), our DeepSTD framework demonstrated leading 
performance, achieving up to 256× compression on CT (MAE = 6.8 HU, SSIM = 0.993, DSC 
= 90.6) and 128× on MRI (SSIM = 0.993, DSC = 87.8) with speed improvements of up to 165× 
over BRIEF and 46× over VVC. 
These findings establish shape –texture decoupling as both a principallysound and 
experimentally validated strategy. They confirm DeepSTD as the algorithm that pr ovides the 
best balance among fidelity, compression ratio, and speed across various medical imaging 
modalities.

5. How the overfitting issues commonly seen in data-driven compressors are overcome by the

We appreciate the reviewer for highlighting the significant concern about overfitting in 
data-driven compressors. DeepSTD addresses this challenge through several strategies. First, it

ratios. Overall, these findings demonstrate that DeepSTD effectively overcomes the overfitting 
issues commonly associated with purely data-driven compressors.

9. What specific role do spatial or anatomical priors play in the success of the shape -texture 
decoupling mechanism in DeepSTD? 
We thank the reviewer for this insightful question. The success of DeepSTD ’s shape -
texture decoupling critically relies on incorporating spatial and anatomical priors. Specifically: 
1) Template-based anatomical prior. As described in our manuscript, DeepSTD introduces 
a reference template that reflects the “average” or “typical” anatomical structure of a given 
modality and body part. This template serves as a spatial prior, guiding the decomposition 
of each sample into shape (geometrical deviation from the template) and texture (intensity 
patterns after alignment). Without this anatomical prior, the separation between global 
structure and local details would be ill -posed, and texture encoding would still be 
confounded by structural variability. 
2) Regularization of shape encoding.  By constraining shape encoding to capture only 
elastic deformations relative to the template, DeepSTD leverages the spatial continuity and 
smoothness of anatomical structures. This ensures that the Implicit Neural Representation 
(INR) only models meaningful large -scale variations, improving both registration 
accuracy and compression efficiency. 
3) Facilitation of texture encoding.  Once spatial variability is normalized by anatomical 
alignment, texture features become semantically aligned across individuals. This spatial 
prior enables the 3DTCMPE encoder to efficiently exploit redundancy and improves 
generalization, as evidenced in our ablation (Extended Data Figure 8b in the manuscript), 
where texture encoding quality significantly deteriorates without prior shape alignment. 
To clarify this role, we have revised the text in the Introduction as follows: 
Benefiting from the shape-texture decoupling, supported by a template -based anatomical 
prior, one can adopt proper encoding strategies separately for two components. The template 
provides a spatial reference ensuring that shape encoding captures smooth anatomical 
deformations while texture encoding operates on anatomically aligned data, thus enhancing 
fidelity and compression efficiency.

This paper introduces DeepSTD, a deep learning -based method for compressing medical 
images by decoupling them into shape and texture components relative to a predefined template. 
Shape information, representing geometric deviations from the template, is encoded using a 
Cycle Implicit Neural Representation (INR) approach. Texture information, captur ed after 
aligning the image to the template, is compressed using a data -driven 3D Transformer-CNN
