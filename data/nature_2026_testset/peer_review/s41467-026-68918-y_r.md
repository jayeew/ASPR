Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript entitled “Spatial-chromatic encoding cosmetic contact lenses for enhanced natural eye tracking,” the
authors present an eye-tracking system that is integrated into cosmetic contact lenses that leverages spatial-chromatic
patterns for enhanced performance. The main contributions of this work include: (1) the design of eye movement feature
enhanced (EMFE) contact lenses; (2) the demonstration of highly accurate eye movement recognition under complex
lighting conditions and off-axis camera placements; and (3) the validation of <1° tracking accuracy and calibration-free gaze
estimation in both model eyes and human subjects across indoor and outdoor environments. This study offers a promising
and practical approach to achieve accurate eye-tracking technology. However, from my point of view, there are several
critical issues that need further supplementation and clarification in the current version of the manuscript. Therefore, I
suggest the publication of this manuscript in Nature Communications after addressing the major issues listed below. 
Firstly, there are fundamental errors in some explanations and experimental setup. 
1. On page 9, the authors state that common cameras, due to their low frame rate, result in motion blur during saccades and
lead to loss of critical information about rapid eye movement trajectories. However, commodity HMD eye trackers frequently
operate at ≥ 60–120 Hz (e.g., Pico Neo3 Pro: 60/90 fps; HTC Vive Pro Eye: 120 Hz), while the head-mounted eye tracker
used in this study records at only 30 fps. Therefore, to substantiate claims about “rapid eye movement trajectories,” the
authors should acquire data using head-mounted eye tracker with cameras at ≥ 60–120 fps. 
2. At the current sampling frequency, a typical saccade of ~60 ms would be sampled at only ~2 time points, which is
insufficient to reconstruct a rapid trajectory or estimate reliable velocity profiles. Therefore, the authors should increase the
sampling frequency (e.g., ≥5–10 samples per saccade) for reliable recordings. Otherwise, the authors should drop these
claims, and any conclusions based on 30 fps restricted to regimes where it is adequate. 
Secondly, the manuscript provides limited evaluation and insufficient explanation of the key characteristics of the EMFE
contact lenses. 
1. The authors claim that specific patterns with vivid colors enable accurate and robust eye tracking. However, the
manuscript lacks an explanation of how key design parameters—such as the number, size, shape, and color of the green
circles or red dots—were optimized. To support this claim, the authors are encouraged to describe the design rationale and
provide experimental evidence comparing different pattern configurations. For example, it would be helpful to show whether
increasing the number of dots enhances tracking accuracy, or to include comparative results for different colors, shapes, and
arrangements. 
2. Since the contact lens remains in continuous contact with tears, it would be advisable to assess the potential leakage of
cosmetic dye from the lens under tear-mimicking conditions. In particular, the authors are recommended to investigate
whether prolonged exposure to artificial tears or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at physiological temperature leads to dye
leakage or color changes. 
Thirdly, additional experiments and descriptions are required to enhance the completeness of the manuscript. 
1. The authors report that the system achieves an accuracy of <1° based on tracking experiments. However, this value
reflects tracking error from relatively coarse target spacing rather than the system’s actual resolving capacity. Therefore, the
authors are recommended to rigorously evaluate the system’s angular resolution by testing point arrays of 1° or sub-degree
spacing to more precisely quantify the minimum resolvable angular separation. 
2. The authors demonstrated that the system recognizes targets under various environmental conditions, such as outdoors,
indoors, and near windows, suggesting robust recognition performance. However, these results are largely qualitative. A
more quantitative evaluation of accuracy would better substantiate the reliability of the system. For example, analyzing how
accuracy varies with light intensity or light color would provide more rigorous validation. The authors are therefore

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

encouraged to include such additional analyses to enhance the reliability of the system. 
3. Although the authors claim to have addressed common challenges of vision-based eye tracking under individual
variation, these aspects are not sufficiently demonstrated in the current manuscript. In particular, the robustness of the
system should be validated under conditions that reflect inter-individual differences such as pupil size, eyelid coverage, and
blinking. The authors are encouraged to provide experimental data from multiple subjects with diverse ocular characteristics
to substantiate the claimed advantages of the proposed method. 
Lastly, the following statements require appropriate references to support the claims. 
1. “Additionally, due to the human eye's attention mechanism, the brain primarily analyzes visual information within a 1-2°
field of view corresponding to the fovea.” 
2. “Similarly, an eye movement angle error of less than 1° is sufficient to accurately determine the gaze point and analyze the
regions of visual attention.”

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript, “Spatial-Chromatic Encoding Cosmetic Contact Lenses for Enhanced Natural Eye Tracking” introduces an
innovative and practical approach to enhance eye tracking in natural scenes by designing spatially and chromatically
encoded cosmetic contact lenses (EMFE lenses). It cleverly utilizes widely available and highly acceptable cosmetic contact
products, avoiding the challenges associated with integrating complex electronic components into contact lenses. The
experimental validation is rigorous and comprehensive, demonstrating high accuracy (<1°) and excellent recognition rates
(93%) across a range of tests, from model eye verification to human trials. Furthermore, demonstrations in diverse
application scenarios underscore the method's significant advantages in enhancing the robustness of eye movement feature
recognition, tracking accuracy, and overall system applicability. However, several specific aspects require further scrutiny,
as detailed below. 
1. The manuscript establishes a crucial foundation by demonstrating the biosafety of the EMFE lens materials through
cytotoxicity tests. However, for a device intended for prolonged ocular surface wear, biocompatibility is a multifaceted
characteristic. Currently, the study lacks the characterization of two key physicochemical properties that are indispensable
for evaluating any contact lens material: surface wettability and oxygen permeability. These properties are directly correlated
with wearing comfort and ocular health and should be comprehensively assessed. 
2. The manuscript currently assumes the stability of the encoding pattern during wear. However, contact lenses are subject
to constant movement on the ocular surface during blinking and are exposed to the complex tear film environment. A critical
concern is whether the pattern could experience wear, deformation, or blurring due to mechanical friction against the eyelid,
tear permeation, or protein deposition. Such degradation could significantly impact the pattern's optical performance and the
stability of eye tracking recognition. 
3. While this study introduces a novel eye-tracking method leveraging encoded-pattern cosmetic lenses and reports key
performance metrics, it lacks a systematic comparison against state-of-the-art academic solutions and commercially
available devices. To more effectively articulate the unique contributions and overarching value of this research, it is
recommended to supplement a comparative analysis. This comparison should specifically address critical system
parameters such as accuracy, robustness, and sampling rate, positioning the proposed method within the existing
landscape of eye-tracking technologies. 
4. The natural blinking reflex, occurring 10-20 times per minute with durations of approximately 100-400 milliseconds each,
leads to the complete or partial occlusion of the cornea by the eyelid. This transient obscuration of the EMFE lens pattern
presents a significant challenge. The manuscript should clarify how the proposed algorithm effectively handles the sudden
absence of tracking features during blinks. Specifically, it is crucial to address whether this phenomenon can result in
tracking signal interruption or lead to anomalous output generation. 
5. The scope of eye movement tested during human trials should be detailed, specifically the maximum tested eye
movement angle. Large-angle eye movements can potentially induce contact lens displacement, leading to measurement
errors. Moreover, the dynamic interaction between the eyelid and the lens during blinking, involving rotational and
translational forces, can cause instantaneous, albeit minor, displacements, even with good lens-cornea adhesion. It is
critical to investigate the impact of such displacements on absolute pattern position, and on the geometry-based 3D pose
estimation. Furthermore, the time required for the algorithm to regain stable tracking after a blink needs to be quantified. 
6. The manuscript validates the system's accuracy under an ideal fixed configuration. In practical scenarios, factors such as
variations in head-mounted device tightness, positioning, and frame pitch and roll angles during donning and doffing can
alter the relative pose between the camera and the eyeball. This variability can significantly affect the accuracy of the
calibrated mapping function or geometric model. The manuscript should address whether these real-world deployment
challenges could lead to degraded accuracy in practical applications. 
7. The manuscript utilizes 12 uniformly distributed green circles as the core static tracking features. However, the rationale
for selecting this specific number of feature points is not sufficiently elaborated. It is unclear whether a different number of
feature points could achieve comparable or superior performance. The authors should clarify whether this choice was
empirically determined or derived through systematic optimization. Providing the relevant design rationale or experimental
validation for the feature point selection would significantly strengthen the manuscript. 
8. In this article, the authors spend considerable space introducing bioelectronics eye tracking technology. We suggest that
the authors add some important research in the field of bioelectronics (see the following references: DOI: 10.1038/s41467-
023-39315-6; 10.1038/s41528-025-00458-9; 10.1126/sciadv.abl8379).

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper describes a new method for implementing camera-based eye trackers using contact lenses. The system consists
of two parts. First a contact lens with a blue peripheral ring dotted with green and red dots spaced at regular intervals. The
second part is the spectacle frame equipped with RGB cameras. 
The working principle consists in taking images of the lens, use the high colour contrast of the large green dots on the blue
ring to compute the centroid of the detected green dots (called circle in the article, I do not think it is the correct word), thereby
yielding the gaze direction. Likewise, the red dots (much smaller than the green ones) generate streaks than are used to
extract eye movements. 
The proposed new eye tracker implementation is interesting since it is using “classic” contact lens, the contact lens remains
passive (no power), does not require high quality camera and is not prone to parasite light interference. 
Overall, I find the paper well written with very interesting results in the field of eye tracking. Still, there are some issues that
needs to be addressed. I’m listing some below. 
The state-of-the art analysis is missing a recently published method which is similar to the one presented in the sense that it
uses passive contact lens with specific features that can be detected using a camera to determine the gaze (F I. M. Fradkin,
et al., "Contact lens with moiré labels for precise eye tracking," Proc. SPIE 13414, Optical Architectures for Displays and
Sensing in Augmented, Virtual, and Mixed Reality (AR, VR, MR) VI, 134141W (21 March 2025);
https://doi.org/10.1117/12.3058057). 
I don’t think that embedding optolectronic devices on lens compromise transparency and raises biosafety as claimed in the
introduction if proper precautions are taken as describes in the references. I agree that it is easier to meet these
requirements using the proposed methods. 
One thing that should be better explained. Based on the description, if the camera is significantly off centred with the eye,
e.g. positioned below the eye as in Fig. 2., the camera sees more green dots at the bottom of the eye, even though the
person looks straight ahead. So computing the centroid, would indicated the person is looking down rather than straight
ahead. That does not seem correct. 
I do not understand why it would not possible to use the green discs to obtain trajectories? Why the need to use tiny
(compared to the green dots) red dots? 
You have not explained how the various algorithms are implemented. What hardware/software platform is used? Is it
embedded in the spectacle frame shown in Fig .1? You show outdoor experimentations, are computation done in real or
differed time? 
It would also be interesting to better described your experiments, how many lens were tested, how many persons involved,
etc were done to measure accuracy. 
Also, in Fig2 would be nice to have a short description of the experimental set up used to produce the results. You give a
recognition rate but you don’t say how many tests were done, on how many persons. 
Fig 2 and Fig 2 caption refer to CCL, better use EMFE as defined in the main text. 
The human eye can have a wide rotation eye movement, but usually above 16° horizontally, one prefers turning the head.
So steps of 10° seems a bit too large to assess accuracy, shouldn’t you have reduce the study between -16°and +16° using
smaller steps? 
The abstract claims that no calibration is required. Yet, in section Eye tracking Characterization a 9-point calibration is used.
This needs to be clarified, calibration or not calibration?

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have proposed an interesting approach to enhance eye-tracking robustness by integrating spatial-chromatic
encoding patterns into cosmetic contact lenses. The use of pad printing and the emphasis on spatial-chromatic features to
overcome environmental lighting challenges are noted as practical engineering efforts. However, after careful review of the
revised manuscript and the authors' responses, I remain concerned about several fundamental limitations that undermine
the scholarly impact and practical superiority of this work in its current form. 
1. The system’s sampling rate is limited to 30 fps, which is insufficient for capturing rapid ocular dynamics such as saccades.
While the authors have refined their claims to focus on "continuous gaze positioning," this low temporal resolution
significantly narrows the application scope. For a device requiring the physical wearing of functional contact lenses, the
performance trade-off compared to non-invasive, high-speed (60–120 Hz) video-based trackers is not sufficiently justified. 
2. The initial appeal of this work relied heavily on its "calibration-free" potential. However, the authors’ acknowledgment of
the necessity of a calibration procedure to account for the Kappa angle diminishes the comparative advantage of the
proposed method. Once calibration is required, the benefit of using artificial chromatic patterns over established iris/pupil-
based algorithms becomes marginal, especially considering the invasive nature of contact lenses. 
3. The optimization of the spatial patterns (e.g., the number and placement of circles) appears to be based on empirical trials

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

rather than a rigorous theoretical or information-theoretic framework. Furthermore, although short-term biocompatibility was
addressed, the potential for peripheral visual interference or long-term wearer discomfort from these specific chromatic
patterns remains a critical concern for a wearable device. 
In conclusion, while the manuscript presents an interesting engineering integration, the lack of high-speed tracking
capability and the retreat from the calibration-free claim suggest that the work does not yet represent a significant
breakthrough in the field of eye-tracking or wearable bioelectronics. Therefore, I recommend a rejection of the manuscript.
However, the final decision remains entirely at the discretion of the Editor.
