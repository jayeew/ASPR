Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Li et al represents a follow up work of a previous paper recently published in Nat Immunol by the same
team, reporting on the tumor-supporting action of ILC1s in AML. 
In the current work, the authors focus on human ILCs, while the previous work was centered, but not exclusively, on
assessing the role of murine ILCs in the interaction with LCSs and in leukemogenesis. While new data are provided, in
particular on the in vitro generation of CD161-ILC1 with anti-tumoral functions, several observations have been previously
reported by others or by the same group, and no in vivo validation of the antitumor potential of this novel cell-based product
has been shown. 
First part of the results reporting on the relative ILC1 expansion in AML, with concomitant impaired Type 1 cytokine secretion
has already been published by others (E.g., Trabanelli et al, Haematologica, 2015). 
Although the authors claim that the mechanisms of action of human ILC1 on LSCs were not reported in the previous work,
the pro-apoptotic role of human ILC1 on human LSCs or HSC was already shown by the same group in Ref 8 published in
2022 (Fig 1g-k, n; ext Fig 2e-f; Fig 6b). 
Fig2A-F. Although difficult due to low cell numbers, at least for some patients, the authors should perform the same
experiments with ILC1s isolated from AML patients, +/- rhIFNg, +/- rhTNFa depending on the experimental setting (i.e.,
apoptosis vs M2 differentiation vs CFU assay). 
Fig2G-J. It would be very informative to have a comparison of the anti-tumor effect of conventional NK cells and ILC1s in
vivo. This would support the validity of the claim that producing ILC1s for in vivo adoptive cell transfer is superior to the
easier expansion of cNK cell products. 
Extended Figure 1A and 1H-J. Which markers define CD161- ILC1s, and allow to claim that they are ILC1s? The reviewer
has this obvious question since the authors use T-bet, Eomes and IFNg to describe these cells (known markers to be
expressed by cNKs). Eomes should not be expressed by helper ILC1s. Do these cells express CD200R1, CD16, CD56,
KIR, NKG2D, NKG2A, Granzymes, Perforin…before/after expansion? 
If the in vitro expanded CD161- ILC1s from CB should be used for cellular-based therapies, the authors need to show tumor
protective data in vivo in AML bearing animals, as done for the primary human ILC1s. 
How do the authors define the “ILC1 signature”, and are confident to be specific for ILC1, a cell type that has no specific
marker expression? Is the definition based on the use of IFNG, TNFA, TRAIL, CD49A, NKP46 and RORA only, as reported
in the methods? 
Side-by-side experiments using LSC and HSC should be performed to determine the effect of human ILC1s and CD161-
ILC1s generated by the CB (Ext Fig 1L) on the healthy stem cell counterpart.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zhenlong LI and Rui Ma et al. showed type I innate lymphoid cells (ILC1s) suppressed leukemic stem cells (LSCs) by
production of IFNγ and by blocking differentiation of LSCs toward M2 macrophage-like, leukemia-supporting leukemic blasts
through TNF. They further identified human ILC1 subsets as Lin-CD127+CD161-CRTH2-CD117- and tested the possibility
of clinical usage of expanded human ILC1s from human umbilical cord blood. 
Authors showed interesting findings about the interaction of LSCs and recently identified immune cell subsets through in
vitro and in vivo experiments. I have several suggestions as below. 
Major comments> 
1. Z Li et al. used CD34+CD38- cell fractions as human LSCs for co-culture with human ILC1s. CD34+CD38- cell fractions
have been widely used for LSC studies using primary AML samples. However, it is also revealed that many true LSCs which
are engrafted in immunocompromised mice do not always reside in CD34+CD38- fractions but in CD34+CD38+ or even in
CD34- fractions [Stanley N K Ng et al. Nature 2016, 17 gene stemness score in AML]. Have you done experiments using 5
different patients’ samples with AML and every sample was engrafted into mice OR using a divided samples from the same
patient for 5 mice groups? 
Since AML is a remarkably heterogenous disease entity, it is very difficult to generalize the cross-talk between LSCs and
immune cells using small number of patients’ samples. It would be also good to see the relationships between LSCs and
immune cells in view of different mutational subtypes of AML OR only in a specific molecular subtype of AML. (ex. FLT3-
mutated AML) 
At least, detailed description about baseline characteristics, molecular and cytogenetic profile of AML patients used in this
experiment is needed. 
2. CD34, CD33, CD133, CD7 and CD13 can be AML markers with aberrant expressions, but inappropriate as LSC markers.
Have they seen relationships between ILC signature and AML-LSC signatures previously proposed (ex. MPP-like, LMPP-
like, and GMP-like LSCs [Goardon N et al. Coexitence of LMPP-and GMP-like..... 
Cancer Cell 2011] or LSC-17 gene
signature [Stanley N K Ng et al. Nature 2016] for the analyses using public databases? 
Minor comments> 
1. In figure 1B, cell distributions in dot plots are very different between healthy donors and patient with AML. What’s the
cause of this difference? Please check single staining control, compensation plot, and positive/negative control in each
sample. 
2. Presentation of absolute cell numbers of ILCs and IFN+ ILCs might enhance understanding of readers. 
3. Authors insisted that IFNγ production of ILC1s have an impact on LSCs. Did they measure the IFNγ level by ELISA? 
Main
AML cell niche is bone marrow rather than peripheral blood in many cases. How was the percentage and ILC subsets in
AML samples from patients’ BM? 
Is information from TCGA LAML database the results from PB samples or BM samples? 
4. Please provide representative images of G- and GM- colonies that authors counted.

(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript the author investigated the functional role of human ILC1s in targeting/controlling AML stem cells and

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the additional work performed by the authors to substantiate their findings. However, in my opinion, the study
and the presented data remain problematic in 3 aspects: novelty of the findings, accuracy of ILC1 definition, and potential
advantage of the use of ILC1 in translational settings in AML patients. 
Specific comments: 
1) The authors outline the characterization of novel findings as compared to previous work by them and by others. The
novelty mainly resides in the capacity of human ILC1s to alter M2 polarization of AML cells in vitro, an observation that was
not shown previously and that is interesting. However, the in vivo relevance of this observation has not been tested and the
TNF blockade in vitro only has partial effect, suggesting that other factors might have even stronger effects. 
Throughout the manuscript, the definition of human ILC1s (as also pointed out by Reviewer 4) remains problematic, to
adequately distinguish ILC1s from NKs and T cells. 
The new scRNAseq performed after EasySep™ Human Pan-ILC Enrichment Kit purification gives very different results as
compared to a very similar scRNAseq recently published by Jaeger et al, Nat Immunol, 2024. In that work, bona fide human
ILC1s are not identified in the peripheral blood, but only in tissues. The authors don’t refer to this work, while they should
verify the identity of their « ILC1s » with respect to both NK cells and T cells. 
As mentioned previously (also by Reviewer 4), the signature used to define ILC1s for survival analyses includes NKp46,
which is not an ILC1 marker, since it is expressed by a large proportion of NK cells. This analysis does not allow correlation
of ILC1 with survival, since it includes NK cells. On top of this, the results are not statistically significant (p=0.1, p=0.6).
Hence, conclusions on the impact of increased ILC1 (high ILC1) on survival are not supported by the data.

paper recently published in Nat Immunol by the same team, reporting on the tumor -supporting action 
of ILC1s in AML.

exclusively, on assessing the role of murine ILCs in the interaction with LCSs and in leukemogenesis. 
While new data are provided, in particular on the in v itro generation of CD161 -ILC1 with anti-tumoral

Response: Thank you for the reviewer's comments. In  the human part of  our previously published 
work, our focus was solely on the examination of human ILC1 -induced LSC cell death in vitro. In the 
previous publication, w e did not delve into whether human ILC1s could influence human LSC 
differentiation, nor did we investigate whether human ILC1s possess an anti-AML function in vivo.  
In the current manuscript, we present novel findings  that reveal the mechanism by which the in vitro 
observations occur, and we combine this mechanistic work with our first in vivo studies in the human 
system. We reveal that the production of IFNγ by ILC1s contributes to the reduction of CD34 −CD38+ 
cells that were differentiated from LSCs , while TNF α production by ILC1s suppresses CD34+CD38+ 
cells that were derived from LSCs differentiation into CD34−CD38+ cells. Interestingly, the decrease in 
CD34−CD38− cell numbers induced by ILC1s was not suppressed by either IFNγ  or TNFα, indicating 
that additional factors or pathways, independent of these cytokines, may play a role in regulating 
CD34−CD38− cell numbers in the context of ILC1 -LSC interactions (Fig. 2, in the revised manuscript, 
recapitulated below for the reviewer’s convenience ). Additionally, we demonstrate that human ILC1  
production of TNFα impedes LSC differentiation into M2 macrophage-like leukemia-supporting cells, 
which have been shown to support leukemia growth (Fig. 3a -3h, in the revised manuscript, 
recapitulated below for the reviewer’s convenience ). Finally, we demonstrated that these combined 
effects play a crucial role in limiting human leukemogenesis in vivo (Fig. 4, in the revised manuscript, 
recapitulated below for the reviewer’s convenience).
