REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript attempts to reinterpret isotopic heterogeneities in soil water using the concept of hydrodynamic
fractionation. While the study presents compelling experimental evidence of non-evaporative isotope fractionation under
controlled infiltration scenarios, it unfortunately leans heavily on the Two Water Worlds (TWW) hypothesis as a central
framework for interpretation. This is problematic for several reasons, primarily due to persistent methodological shortcomings
in the original formulation of TWW and the growing consensus in the isotope ecohydrology community that its foundational
assumptions are flawed. 
The original TWW hypothesis was built upon isotopic separations observed between xylem and bulk soil water, with a key
methodological feature being the use of cryogenic vacuum extraction (CVE) for isolating water from soils and plant tissues.
A large and growing body of recent literature has clearly demonstrated that CVE introduces significant isotopic biases,
particularly through the co-extraction of mobile water and immobile water linked to organic matter and mineral surfaces. In
addition to the co-extraction issue, temperature-induced artifacts also introduced enigmatic depletions, turning CVE a no
reliable technique for ecohydrological studies. 
Recent research using more selective extraction methods, especially centrifugation (see references in the PDF document
attached), has consistently failed to replicate these dual isotope domains, especially in tropical and subtropical
environments where the TWW framework has been least supported. For example, Sánchez-Murillo et al. (2023,
Ecohydrology) show that soil and plant water in Costa Rica’s wet tropics plot consistently along the LMWL, further rejecting
claims of stable isotope divergence due to water pool separation. 
Thus, by invoking TWW without adequately addressing the extensive and growing body of contradictory evidence and
analytical reassessment, the manuscript builds upon a scientifically debated foundation. 
While the authors present a novel and mechanistically grounded argument for hydrodynamic fractionation as a plausible
contributor to isotopic shifts during infiltration, they do not sufficiently disentangle these new findings from the historical
baggage of the TWW framework. The manuscript repeatedly references ecohydrological separation as if it remains an
empirically supported phenomenon, without acknowledging that its original documentation has been largely invalidated by

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

improved extraction techniques and revised analytical protocols. 
Moreover, the authors state that their findings "provide a mechanistic validation for the two-water-worlds hypothesis," yet
they do not sample xylem water or in-situ immobile water pools, making such a conclusion speculative at best. Without direct
comparison to biologically relevant water fractions (e.g., root-uptake zones), this interpretation lacks evidentiary support. 
By continuing to cite early TWW literature without sufficient context or mention of critical responses, the manuscript risks
misinforming readers about the current consensus in isotope ecohydrology. Several prominent review articles and
methodological intercomparisons have called for moving beyond TWW as a guiding paradigm. 
The authors have presented valuable mechanistic insights into infiltration-driven isotopic shifts in the absence of
evaporation. However, to meet the rigor and clarity expected of a Nature Communications publication, the conceptual
framework must be decoupled from the Two Water Worlds hypothesis. 
Required Major Revisions: 
Focus instead on the novel contribution of hydrodynamic fractionation and its implications for isotopic transport in soils. 
Explicitly address the analytical bias in cryogenic vacuum extraction and cite recent centrifugation-based studies that
undermine the TWW hypothesis. 
Reframe interpretations around direct observations, not inferred water pools that were not sampled or analyzed (e.g., xylem
or immobile water domains). 
Clarify the chemical and physical basis of the proposed hydrodynamic fractionation mechanisms, especially how
isotopologues transport is mediated by pore structure, matric potential, and flow regimes. 
This work has the potential to make a significant contribution to ecohydrology, but only if it clearly breaks from outdated
paradigms and articulates its findings within the evolving understanding of isotopic transport in soils.

I am reviewing the initial submission to Nature Communications earth and environment of a manuscript with title “Isotope

water (tightly bound waters for plant uptake) in soils. 
I see several limitations to the present study and its setup: 
1- in small soil columns the occurrence of preferential flow is far greater than in nature. Water may bypass the soil and flow
downwards along the column walls to leachate. The authors should discuss the very representativeness of their results. On
a side note, it would be interesting to test if the obtained relationships are soil texture dependent (the authors mention
different soil textures in their methods but do not make use of it…). 
2- Preferential flow is soil water content-dependent. However, neither the initial soil water and isotope conditions were
recorded and confronted to the drainage δ-Q curves. 
3- Preferential flow – therefore the observation of isotopic separation between drainage water and soil bound water – is also
a function of the amount of rain and the duration of its event. Considering only the flow rate density of drainage is
oversimplifying. 
4- I can only suggest to the authors to compare their drainage water isotope data with simulations from a soil water transport
model that includes (diffusive & advective) isotope transport in both liquid and vapor phases to see if, given the current
knowledge on water and isotope transport in a bare soil, it is possible to match the observations. It is not enough to state
otherwise. 
5- The concept of “hydrodynamic isotope fractionation” is not defined in the text or in an equation but is fundamental to the
study. 
Generally, an appropriate experimental design should include the monitoring of soil water and isotope status in addition to
precipitation and drainage amounts and isotopic compositions to enable the solving of water and isotope balances.

Abstract 3 
Quantifying the mixing of infiltrating rainwater with pre -existing pore water remains a central 4 
challenge in ecohydrology. Traditional stable water isotope tracers often assume this process is 5 
solely controlled by evaporative fractionation, yet the underly ing interference mechanisms have 6 
long been unclear. Through controlled  soil column experiment s, we demonstrate hydrodynamic 7 
isotope fractionation independent of evaporation in unsaturated soils. Using intact soil columns 8 
under natural rainfall events with high-frequency leachate sampling (15-minute to 3-hour intervals), 9 
we reveal a statistically significant negative correlation (Spearman ’s r = -0.41 to -0.47, p < 0.001) 10 
between seepage flux and its stable water isotope composition ( δ2H and δ18O). This indic ates 11 
preferential downward flushing of lighter isotopologues, while heavier ones remained in the soil 12 
matrix. Fractionation exhibited a threshold behavior: it was most pronounced at low water fluxes 13 
and diminished at higher fluxes. Our findin gs provide a m echanistic explanation for the often -14 
observed isotopic separation between mobile water and bound water in soils, even in the absence of 15 
evaporation. Incorporating flux -dependent fractionation mechanisms into isotope -enabled 16 
hydrological models significantl y improves predictions of water partitioning in terrestrial 17 
ecosystems, offering critical insights for hydrological tracing and ecological modeling under climate 18 
change. 19 
Introduction 20 
Understanding how rainwater infiltrates and mixes with pre -existing soil water is a long -standing 21 
challenge in hydrology. Differences in stable isotope composition between soil water pools have 22 
traditionally been attributed mainly to evaporative fractionation at the soil surface 1 or to sampling 23 
artifacts during water extraction 2. However, widespread observations of “two water worlds” – for 24 
example, mobile water feeding streams and groundwater that is isotopically lighter than water taken 25 
up by plants – indicate a possible separation between tightly bound soil water and freely dr aining 26 
water3,4. The mechanistic basis for this ecohydrological separation has remained elusive. Global 27 
isotope surveys often rely on metrics like deuterium -excess (d-excess) or line -conditioned excess 28 
(lc-excess) to infer such separation, implicitly assuming evaporation is the primary driver of isotope 29 
differences5. Hydrodynamic isotope fractionation has emerged as a novel mechanism that challenges 30 
this assumption. This mechanism posits that physical transport processes and soil–water interactions 31 
can pref erentially partition water isotopologues during infiltration, independent of evaporation. 32 
Recent experimental evidence supports this idea, suggesting that conventional tracer interpretations 33 
may be misleading if hydrodynamic effects are at work 6. Here we p resent new insights into 34 
hydrodynamic isotope fractionation, drawing on theory and controlled experiments, and discuss the 35 
implications for soil water mixing, preferential flow dynamics, and climate -driven hydrological 36 
disconnection. 37

Results 39 
Flow Flux-Dependent Isotopic Shifts 40 
Our experiments reveal that stable water isotope shifts depend strongly on flow flux. In our intact 41 
soil columns (Fig. S1), seepage flow flux ( Q) was negatively correlated with the δ18O of outflow 42 
(Spearman’s r ≈ –0.41, p < 0.001) an d δ²H (r ≈ –0.47, p < 0.001), while d -excess showed a mild 43 
positive correlation ( r ≈ 0.14, p < 0.01) (Fig. 1 left) . These results indicate that rapid infiltration 44 
preferentially transports isotopically lighter water, leaving behind residual pore water enriched in 45 
heavier isotopes. Consequently, the percolating drainage water became more depleted in heavy 46 
isotopes and more data points plotted in the upper left region relative to the Local Meteoric Water 47 
Line (LMWL) (Fig. 2b), a pattern consistent with fi eld observations of soil and seepage waters 7. 48 
This discovery fundamentally challenges the long -standing paradigm that isotope fractionation in 49 
the vadose zone is negligible without evaporation 8. It demonstrates that non -equilibrium 50 
hydrodynamic fractionation can occur under purely liquid -phase flow, providing new mechanistic 51 
insight into the two-water-worlds hypothesis. 52 
Threshold Effects and Segmented Flow 53 
Hydrodynamic fractionation in our experiments exhibited a clear threshold behavior under 54 
different flow regimes. When we segmented the data by flow fluxes (Fig.S3), we found that at 55 
low seepage flux (Q < 0.2 mm·min-1·cm-2), the correlation between Q and isotopic 56 
composition was pronounced (Spearman’s r ≈ –0.33 for δ18O, –0.38 for δ2H; p < 0.001), 57 
whereas at higher fluxes (Q ≥ 0.2) the relationship weakened and became statistically 58 
insignificant (Fig. 1 left panel). We also observed that the largest and fastest isotope shifts 59 
occurred predominantly under low-flow conditions, further confirming that hydrodynamic 60 
fractionation is most active at lower fluxes. This threshold effect helps explain why many 61 
stream and groundwater isotope values cluster along the LMWL despite fractionation 62 
occurring during low-flow events – in natural settings, groundwater recharge is often 63 
dominated by heavy rainfall events9 that produce high-flux infiltration with minimal 64 
fractionation. In essence, under modest rain events the slower, matrix-dominated flow allows 65 
significant isotopic partitioning, whereas intense storms generate fast preferential flows that 66 
dilute or bypass these fractionation effects. 67 
Discussion 68 
Our data challenge the traditional view that soil water isotopic values plotting lower right the LMWL 69 
are determined exclusively by evaporative fractionation 3,5,7,8. Instead, we found that hydrodynamic 70 
processes in the unsaturated zone can significantly affect isotope ratios (Figures S4 to S5). As water 71 
percolates through heterogeneous soil matrix, differences in pore structure and flow velocity result 72

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 28 -->

in the preferential transport of lighter isotopologues along faster channels—a behavior analogous to 73 
a reverse Rayleigh distillation process 10. This leads to mobile (drainage) water that is isotopically 74 
depleted and a residual water fraction that remains enriched  in heavy isotopes . Although our 75 
experiments did not directly sample the immobile soil water in situ, such partitioning patterns have 76 
been observed in other controlled experiments (though not fully explained at the time)6,11. Our results 77 
confirm that substantial kinetic fractionation occurs during infiltration, independent of evaporation. 78 
The threshold effects observed in our study are particularly important  for understanding 79 
ecohydrological separation . Un der low -flux conditions, pronounced differences in pore  water 80 
velocities maximize hydrodynamic dispersion and isotope fractionation. Once flux exceeds a critical 81 
threshold, flow becomes more uniformly distributed and  the fractionation effect is greatly 82 
diminished (Fig. 2). This mechanism helps explain why plant -accessible water often appears 83 
isotopically heavier than concurrently percolating water feeding streams and aquifers 3,12. Indeed, 84 
our findings align with the conceptual framework proposed by Sprenger  & Allen (2020) that roots 85 
can access water from tighter pore domains while new rainfall travels quickly through macropores13. 86 
Moreover, rainfall intensity experiments have shown that only exceptiona lly high rain intensity 87 
produce a persistent two-domain (dual isotopic) behavior under typical storms, exchange between 88 
rapid flow paths and the matrix prevents long -term isotopic divergence 14. Together, these insights 89 
provide a mechanistic validation for the two -water-worlds hypothesis by highlighting the role of 90 
preferential flow paths, pore connectivity, and rate-limited exchange in generating distinct isotopic 91 
pools. 92 
Our findings have significant implications for isotope -based hydrological modeling. Many current 93 
models assume that infiltrating water is well -mixed in the subsurface and attribute any isotopic 94 
deviations from meteoric norms solely to evaporation effects or mixing of older water ages 5,15. 95 
Neglecting the flow -dependent fractionation mechanism identified here may lead to 96 
misinterpretation of isotope  data and an overestimation of evaporative influences on subsurface 97 
water budgets16. In practical terms, accurate interpretation of unsaturated-zone mixing is crucial for 98 
water resource assessments – failing to account for hydrodynamic fractionation could result in 99 
erroneous estimates of groundwater recharge and misinformed water management decisions. We 100 
advocate for future work to quantify these fractionation effects under a range of controlled 101 
conditions and to incorporate the observed processes into the next generation of isotope -enabled 102 
hydrological models. Particularly noteworthy is that the latest research 17 indicates that compound 103 
climate stressors (warming  + elevated CO 2 + drought) trigger an irreversible "soil -water 104 
disconnection" by altering soil structure and hydrological pathways, fundamentally challenging the 105 
core assumption of "uniform mixing" in traditional models. This further underscores the necessity 106 
of integrating hydrodynamic fractionation and its threshold effects into hydrological model s under 107 
future climate change scenarios. 108 
Furthermore, the concept of an “seepage line” (Fig. 2) analogous to the soil evaporation line, offers 109

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 29 -->

a more nuanced framework for interpreting isotopic evolution during rainfall events. Each rainfall 110 
event causes lighter isotopes to move downward, leading to a lasting isotopic layering that supports 111 
ecohydrological separation. This integrated view, which considers evaporation, root uptake, and 112 
hydrodynamic fractionation, can reconcile discrepancies observed in diverse field settings18,19. Such 113 
an approach is essential for advancing our understanding of water transport in the Critical Zone and 114 
for developing accurate isotope-enabled Earth system models20. 115 
In summary, our results highlight a previously under-recognized process – hydrodynamic 116 
isotope fractionation – that plays a key role in unsaturated zone water partitioning. This 117 
process provides a missing link to explain the stable isotope separation observed between 118 
mobile and bound soil water pools, complementing (and in some cases outweighing) 119 
traditional evaporation-based explanations. By demonstrating how flow rate and pore 120 
connectivity govern isotopic outcomes during infiltration, we underscore the need to re-121 
examine how isotopic tracers are used in ecohydrology. Incorporating flow-dependent 122 
fractionation effects into predictive models will improve estimates of plant water availability 123 
and groundwater recharge, especially under scenarios of more intense rainfall and prolonged 124 
drought. Our discovery-driven approach sheds new light on the complex interplay between 125 
soil structure, hydrology, and climate in shaping the isotopic architecture of subsurface 126 
waters. 127

Experimental Setup and Field Deployment 133 
Undisturbed soil columns (30 cm diameter, 60 cm height) representing three common soil textures 134 
(clay loam, silt loam, and sandy loam) were collected. A total of nine columns (three replicates per 135 
soil type) were installed on a rooftop in Chengdu (30.67°N, 104.07°E; ~500 m elevation) to monitor 136 
natural rainfa ll infiltration. Refer to Figure S1  (experimental setup schematic), Table S1 (soil 137 
hydrological characteristics), and Tables S2-S3 (isotopic composition and hydrological parameters 138 
of water sources during monitoring) in the supplementary materials. 139 
Monitoring and Sampling 140 
At the base of each column, calibrated tipping ‐bucket flowmeters (each tip delivering 15.6 mL, 141 
corresponding to 0.23 mm of water) continuously recorded percolation flux ( Q). An event logger 142 
captured tip events with 1‐second precision, enabling the construction of detailed Q–t curves during 143 
each rain event. Water samples were manually collected at 15 –30-minute intervals using 250 -mL 144

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 30 -->

polyethylene bottles, immediately stored at 4 °C, and filtered through 0.45 μm membranes before 145 
analysis. 146 
Isotope Analysis and Data Processing 147 
Stable isotope ratios ( δ2H and δ18O) were measured using a liquid water isotope analyzer and are 148 
reported in ‰ relative to V-SMOW (measurement uncertainties: ±0.3‰ for δ2H and ±0.08‰ for 149 
δ18O). Deuterium excess (d‐excess) was calculated as: 150 
d-excess = δ2H − 8 × δ18O 151 
Percolation flux (Q) was calculated using: 152 
Q = (N × c × α) / (A × Δt) 153 
where N is the number of tipping events during the sampling interval (Δt), c is the equivalent depth 154 
per tip (0.23 mm), α is a correction coefficient (ranging from 0.73 to 0.95), and A is the cross -155 
sectional area of the soil column (~707 cm²). Variation rates of isotopic composition were computed 156 
from the difference between successive sample values divided by the corresponding time interval. 157
