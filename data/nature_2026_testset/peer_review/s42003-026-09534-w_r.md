Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript the authors are extending their previous studies on the nature of housing temperatures and the physiology
of mice, and in the current work, are including rats. 
This is an important area of study because of the massive use of
especially mice in biomedical research and the potential impact of housing temperatures on experimental outcomes. 
There
are several concerns with the manuscript however mostly regarding broad conclusions that should be limited given the data
presented. 
1). The authors do not provide periods of time for acclimation at various temperature tested, using only 24 hour intervals
between step-wise increases in temperature. 
Ambient temperature is a fundamental regulator of physiology and the full
impact of temperature is not realized in such short intervals. 
This is a significant limitation on this study as mice and rats
should be evaluated over a range of time periods at each temperature which allows a more accurate assessment and
acclimation to temperatures. 
2). This study uses singly housed mice for experiments. 
This imposes additional stress that is minimized by group housing
and it is not clear why the authors only tested singly-housed mice. 
Their conclusions are likely altered based on absence of
huddling and social interactions afforded by group housing. 
Related to this, in the Methods, it is stated that upon arrival mice
are group-housed (although the number of mice per cage is not provided.) 
Therefore, using only a single mouse per cage
would create additional stressors, not only increasing thermal stress, but also social isolation. 
3). The authors mention Ref. 19 in their discussion of how comparing efficacy in mice at 22oC and at 30oC 
did not affect
translatability. 
However, as they indicate, this study was limited to weight management drugs and may not apply to major
other diseases modeled in mice. 
There is insufficient emphasis on this point. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Dear authors, 
Thank you very much for this thorough and carefully executed study. The manuscript provides a comprehensive and well-
controlled analysis of thermoregulation in mice and rats across a wide range of ambient temperatures. 
In my view, the findings in mice largely confirm previously published work, and the main novelty lies in the direct comparison
with rats. Therefore, I would suggest considering a slight adjustment of the title to better reflect this focus, rather than
implying a completely new discovery. Also, from my perspective, a range of 28–29 °C could be seen as a (narrow)
thermoneutral zone rather than a single point, but I understand and appreciate why you prefer to continue advocating your
TNP concept. Overall, this is a solid and well-presented piece of work that makes a good contribution to the field. 
Further comments: 
1. Individual variability in TNP: Based on my personal experience, individual mouse lines can exhibit significantly altered
thermoneutral zones or points. Factors such as group housing, coat condition, fur density, or pelage status may influence
these measurements and should be explicitly addressed in the discussion. In addition, some mice lines don´t breed at
higher Tas.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

2. Acclimatization effects: It is unclear whether the animals were measured during acclimatization phases. Could this have
affected the reported TNPs? The authors should clarify how such confounding factors were controlled or ruled out. 
3. Insulating effect of obesity (line 224/311): The discussion of the insulating effect of obesity is not particularly novel.
Previous studies (e.g., DOI: 10.1152/ajpendo.00093.2016) have explored this in depth. The authors should provide a more
comprehensive discussion and situate their findings within the existing literature. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript supports the authors previous findings that mice have a thermoneutral point (TNP) at which total energy
expenditure (TEE) is altered to maintain internal temperature homeostasis with changing ambient temperature rather than a
more broad thermoneutral zone (TNZ) as has been suggested by others. They also provide new evidence demonstrating
that rats, despite their reduced sensitivity to colder ambient temperatures, also have a TNP at which TEE changes to
maintain internal temperature. They suggest an ambient temperature at which mice and rats should be housed at to mimic
human physiology. These findings are relevant given the current use of mice and rats to understand physiological
mechanisms governing the effects of weight loss medications in humans that are strongly influenced by energy metabolism.
Generally, the manuscript reads well, however, there are few points of clarification that need to be included and discussed. 
Comments: 
1. The authors should report the age of mice when they entered the facility for experiments and how long they were on the
diets and why are there differences in the age of the mice and rats comparing chow and HFD fed animals? Perhaps a
schematic could be provided to demonstrate the flow of animals during the experiments. How long were mice and rats
single-housed to complete all experiments? Not sure because of line 126, “after the last (warmest) day of the Ta ramp”. 
2. In Figure 1, if there could be a way to include the days that the mice were held at each temperature on the graph then this
would help describe how experiments were conducted. 
3. How did the authors measure fat mass and was lean mass also included in these measurements. Including the lean mass
would be helpful to compare across treatments as no major changes in ambient temperatures were determined
corresponding to fat mass. 
4. Panels in Figure 1 T-W in figure 1 are missing y-axis titles, they appear to be body mass? And time should be included for
all figs with temperature on the x axis. Figure 2 J-L is not described in the text. 
5. Food intake in panel R in figure 1 shows a steady decline in chow rat food intake as temperature increases but a
maintenance of body mass until 30º Ta, this is not mentioned in results section 3.2 but should be discussed. The following
statement in lines 186-188 may not fully describe the response to increasing ambient temperature in rats. 
6. Lines 272-275 are unclear in the context of the arguments being presented. More explanation is required especially in
regards to citation 44 to support the claim that the data presented show that mice don’t have energy consuming processes in
the body to decrease body temperature. 
7. The statement in lines 319-320 “We show here that adiposity is also not a major contributor to insulation in rats meaning
that the above factors apply despite rats’ larger size.” appears to contradict the results from figure 2 discussed in lines 193-
200, particularly the statement “Mice, due to their smaller size and higher conductance, are better at dissipating extra heat
production…". It may be worthwhile to add additional information to make lines 319-320 more clear. 
8. Scholander analysis needs to be better described to understand and interpret the results and relationship of the data. 
9. Figure 1: HFD fed mice and rats have ~0.5C lower Tb than chow fed mice and rats, could this be explained or explored
better in the discussion to describe results of the animals. 
10. CL316243, why using the different doses for this in rats vs. mice. There is a different response and so could the dosing
explain this? Were different doses piloted before running these experiments or choosing these doses. 
11. An additional limitation could be added to the discussion section of the paper is about the differences of acute vs. chronic
acclimation periods. The current study looks at these mechanisms in an acute manner and other papers such as de Jong
paper 2019 Nat Met which describes how human BAT is phenocopied by mice housed at thermoneutrality is mechanisms of
more chronic effects of being at thermoneutrality. It would be important to make a clear distinction that TNP may be important
for some whole body metabolic measures acutely but may not determine the chronic exposures. 
12. There are several points in the introduction about pharmacotherapies etc. that are important to discuss but were not
revisited in the discussion. Ideas should be tied together between the introduction and the discussion so there is better flow
of the entire manuscript otherwise it seems disjointed.

Background for the reviewers: 79 
Single housing is required for accurately measuring food intake and energy expenditure per 80 
mouse. Group housing allows only group averages and introduces social dominance effects, 81 
requiring assumptions for conversion to per mouse numbers. Additionally, our experimental 82 
system does not allow measurement of physical activity of group housed mice.  83 
 84 
We agree with the reviewer that group housed mice will huddle at room temperature. How-85 
ever, huddling mice do not obey the classic assumptions/conclusions of the Scholander anal-86 
ysis, that below thermoneutrality TEE is linear and heat conductance is constant (see ref 59). 87 
Thus, single housing is required for using the informative (and standard) analysis approach 88 
pioneered by Scholander.  89
