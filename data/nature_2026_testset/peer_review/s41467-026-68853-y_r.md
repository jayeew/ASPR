Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Meijiao Men et al. present a manuscript entitled “Polarization-modulated Programmable Photovoltaic Performance of a
Designed Ferroelectric Heterojunction.” The work addresses an important issue in ferroelectric photovoltaics by
demonstrating programmable photovoltaic responses in Pt/CIPS/graphene heterojunctions and showcasing potential
applications in in-sensor computing. The experiments are carefully designed, and the results are of interest to the
optoelectronic and neuromorphic device community. Overall, this is a good piece of work. However, in order to ensure
clarity, reproducibility, and stronger positioning of the results, I suggest the authors address the following points before the
manuscript can be considered for publication. 
1. In Fig. 1a, supplementary Fig. 1, and in device fabrication methods, the dry transfer process of graphene and CIPS is
mentioned briefly. Could the authors provide more fabrication details of transfer yield, possible contamination, or interface
quality that may influence device reproducibility? 
2. In Supplementary Fig. 2, the PFM data confirm ferroelectricity of CIPS. Could the authors clarify whether both out-of-plane
and in-plane polarization switching were characterized, and how stable the domains are under repeated cycling? 
3. In Fig. 2b, the device exhibits a large Ilight/Idark ratio (~900). While impressive, the stability and reproducibility of this
value remain unclear. Over how many cycles and what duration was this stability verified? Such details are important to
assess the robustness of the photovoltaic response. 
4. The claim of this work is that Cu
⁺
 ion migration governs the anomalous switchable FE-PV effect, overturning the
traditional FE-PV. While the I–V characteristics and SS-PFM loops are suggestive, they do not provide direct proof of Cu
⁺
redistribution. Have the authors considered more direct probes of STEM-EDX/EELS mapping or operando XPS to
conclusively verify ionic migration? Without this, the mechanism remains somewhat speculative. 
5. The manuscript describes multiple reversals of Voc and Isc as a function of Vp (Fig. 4a–b). However, the reproducibility of
this behavior across different devices, cycles, or measurement runs is not shown. Given that ionic processes can be highly
device-to-device dependent; the authors should clarify whether this anomalous switching is robust or sample-specific. 
6. The proposed mechanism suggests that the initial ferroelectric state (P↓) dictates the asymmetric switching behavior
under positive versus negative bias. However, this assumption seems critical: how is the initial P↓ state determined
experimentally in the as-fabricated heterojunction? Could other factors, such as electrode work function mismatch or
interfacial defects, bias the system towards this orientation? Addressing this would help solidify the foundation of the model. 
7. The F-score is reported as 1 in Fig. 5f, whether the score was calculated over multiple test images or only one input. This
clarification is important for reproducibility. 
8. The device is claimed to perform edge detection and binary pattern classification for brain-like neural computing. Could
the authors benchmark their device performance of accuracy and speed against existing neuromorphic optoelectronic
systems to highlight the advantages and limitations of their FE-PV heterojunction approach? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors investigate how the ferroelectric photovoltaic effect couples to Cu
⁺
 migration dynamics,
yielding up to a 10x increase in photocurrent and polarization-dependent polarity switching. By using simple structure of

Pt/CIPS/Gr, they demonstrate in-sensor computing application, including edge detection and pattern classification. The
results are promising, but several fundamental questions remain before the full potential and limitations of this approach can
be assessed: 
1- Figure 2 analyzes the effects of illumination power and polarization separately, but their combined influence is not
addressed. Under an applied VP, does illumination further modulate the device current? Please provide comparative data
(e.g., I-V or I-t curves) under dark vs. illuminated conditions at fixed VP for both polarization states, and quantify any
interaction. 
2- Figure 2e shows negligible contrast between +P and −P at 1 V, whereas a clear polarization-dependent change emerges
above ~1.5 V. Yet Figure 4 centers on VP = ±1V. Please justify this choice (e.g., device reliability, leakage, or comparability
constraints). 
3- The introduction claims “ultrafast switching dynamics” in ferroelectrics, yet Figure 2 reports a 60 s poling time to set
polarization. Please reconcile this discrepancy. Clarify whether 60 s is a stabilization/poling dwell rather than the intrinsic FE
switching time, and quantify the minimum pulse width needed to achieve saturated (or ≥80%) Pr. Including switching-current
transient’s data would help separate true ferroelectric switching from slower Cu
⁺
 migration effects. 
4- In Figure 4, the authors propose a voltage-driven polarization mechanism in CIPS attributed to Cu
⁺
 migration, but the
evidence provided is largely inferential. Please substantiate this claim with direct, mechanism-specific measurements (e.g.,
in-situ biasing TEM to track Cu site occupancy or switching-spectroscopy PFM and PUND versus pulse width/temperature
to disentangle ionic migration from intrinsic ferroelectric switching). Absent such data, the mechanism should be presented
as a hypothesis rather than a confirmed explanation. 
5- Please clarify how intralayer vs interlayer Cu
⁺
 migration in CIPS maps onto the in-plane (Px, Py) and out-of-plane (Pz)
polarization components. Is there a direct correspondence (e.g., interlayer displacement → Pz ; intralayer displacement →
Px, Py)? A brief explanation, ideally with a schematic and expected measurement signatures (vertical vs. lateral PFM, SHG)
would improve completeness and help interpret the Figure 4 evidence. 
6- The devices use ~25 nm-thick CIPS, yet prior reports indicate a pronounced thickness dependence, often with larger
remanent polarization and improved domain stability in thicker flakes (10.1002/smll.201904529). Please justify the choice of
25 nm. 
7- It appears the Prewitt operator is applied directly to raw inputs without denoising or contrast normalization. These can
cause the risk of noise-induced spurious edges. If pre-processing was performed, please detail the methods and
parameters; if not, please justify its omission. In addition, specify any post-processing (e.g., non-maximum suppression,
thresholding method/parameters, kernel size) and assess the sensitivity of the results to these choices (an ablation would be
helpful). 
8- Minor editorial issues: 
• In the Introduction, the following sentence appears twice in succession; please remove the duplicate: 
“Moreover, the spontaneous polarization and ultrafast switching dynamics of ferroelectric materials can emulate synaptic
plasticity in biological vision systems, facilitating highly efficient and intelligent neuromorphic visual perception and
processing systems.” 
• Reference 18 does not appear to be related to ferroelectric materials; please verify its relevance or replace it with an
appropriate citation. 
• Line 101: “Pt/Gr/CIPS” should be corrected and the stack notation standardized (Pt/CIPS/Gr).

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports a ferroelectric–photovoltaic (FE-PV) device based on an asymmetric Pt/CuInP
₂
S
₆
/graphene
(Pt/CIPS/Gr) heterojunction whose photoresponse can be programmed via ferroelectric polarization and associated Cu
⁺
 ion
migration. The device shows polarity-dependent rectification, sign-reversible short-circuit photocurrent under poling, and
~10× enhancement in the magnitude of the short-circuit photocurrent with appropriate polarization. The physical picture is
supported by density-functional theory (DFT) band-alignment analysis and by temperature-dependent measurements near
the CIPS Curie temperature. The authors further connect the programmable photoresponsivity to in-sensor computing tasks
(edge detection and 3×3 pattern classification), outlining a path toward neuromorphic vision hardware. In my view, this paper
is a promising contribution that can become publication-ready after substantial revisions focused on quantitative
benchmarking, direct mechanism validation, and clearer presentation. I therefore recommend Minor Revision 
1. The manuscript attributes the sign-programmable photoresponse to Cu
⁺
-ion migration coupled with ferroelectric barrier
tuning, supported by SS-PFM and DFT. However, PFM/SS-PFM and DFT are not species-specific and therefore cannot
uniquely exclude look-alike mechanisms such as (i) pure ferroelectric barrier modulation without ions, (ii) trap-mediated
photogating or persistent interfacial charging at the graphene/CIPS interface, (iii) photothermoelectric or pyroelectric effects,
(iv) Schottky/Dember photovoltages, or (v) electrochemical/adsorbate-induced work-function shifts in graphene. To elevate
the claim from “consistent with” to “caused by” Cu
⁺
 motion, please add direct, chemistry-resolved evidence (e.g., bias-treated
ToF-SIMS or gentle-sputter XPS depth profiles; pre/post-poling STEM-EELS), quantify post-poling drift versus temperature
to extract an ionic activation energy, and include discriminating controls (ultrathin ion-blocking interlayers, symmetric-contact
devices, and thickness/environment/capping sweeps). 
2. To complete the neuromorphic-vision story end-to-end, please address: write (poling) kinetics—report the minimum pulse
width/amplitude and corresponding energy per write required to reach each non-volatile state, and include a 2D pulse-
duration × voltage state map; read speed and bandwidth—quantify short-circuit photocurrent rise/fall times under modulated
illumination and the −3 dB optical/electrical bandwidth at the stated read condition, with full measurement setup and load;
transients—show representative time-domain traces for write→read and light on/off to extract rise, fall, latency, and jitter, and

clarify whether reads are non-destructive; and noise/SNR—report noise spectral density, NEP, and specific detectivity at the
read condition to bound achievable frame rate and MAC accuracy. 
3. The paper has included fabrication and measurements of an asymmetrical Pt/CuInP
₂
S
₆
/graphene heterojunction and
reports FE-PV tests under 450 nm illumination with DFT/SS-PFM support, which establishes the basic operation and

1. In Fig. 1a, supplementary Fig. 1, and in device fabrication methods, the dry

Reply: We are very grateful to the reviewer for your attention to this important technical 
detail. We fully agree that the quality of the dry transfer process is crucial to the 
performance and reproducibility of the devices. Below is a detailed description of the 
specific measures and evaluation methods adopted to enhance the transfer success rate, 
mitigate contamination, and optimize interface quality. 
(1) Our detailed dry transfer is carried out as follows. First, graphene and CuInP₂S₆ 
(CIPS) thin layers are obtained through mechanical exfoliation by the “Scotch Tape” 
and attached to PDMS films, respectively. Then, under an optical microscope, we use 
a precise micro-manipulation system to precisely stack the target material thin layers 
on the predefined electrode positions—CIPS first, followed by graphene. When the 
sample on the PDMS makes full contact with the silicon substrate, heating to 80 °C and 
then cooling back to room temperature can facilitate the transfer of the two‑dimensional 
materials from the PDMS onto the substrate. 
To ensure the reproducibility of the process, we only select thin layers with smooth 
surfaces, neat edges, and uniform thickness under the optical microscope for device 
fabrication. Through statistics of approximately more than 100 instances of device 
fabrication experiments, the final transfer success rate (the proportion of complete 
heterojunction devices successfully fabricated and available for electrical testing) is 
more than 85%, providing a basis for the reproducibility of the research.

(2) To reduce the potential sources of contamination, the following attempts were 
made i) To reduce the contamination from substrate cleaning and residues, we 
alternately ultrasonically clean the SiO₂/Si substrate with high-purity anhydrous ethanol 
and acetone, and only proceed with the subsequent process after confirming that the 
surface is clean and flawless under an optical microscope. ii) For photolithography 
process, we strictly control the photoresist curing or pre-baking time at 3 minutes avoid 
over-baking, which could cause "charring" and the formation of residues that are 
difficult to remove. iii) During the lift-off process, we use fresh high-purity acetone for 
thorough soaking and gentle ultrasonication to ensure clear electrode patterns and 
complete removal of residual photoresist. 
(3) The core challenge for the heterojunction interface quality lies in the microbubbles 
formed by gas trapped during the transfer process. These bubbles can cause local 
suspension of the material, seriously affecting the interface coupling and electrical 
performance. To address this issue, we immediately examine the morphology of the 
entire heterojunction region using an optical microscope after each dry transfer step. 
We have established rigorous device selection criteria: only devices exhibiting no 
obvious bubbles, wrinkles, or other defects at the heterojunction interface—with the 2D 
material making uniform contact with the substrate—are selected for subsequent 
electrical measurement and performance characterization. This strict selection criterion 
ensures that the data reported in this paper are all from devices with excellent interface 
quality, thereby guaranteeing the reliability and consistency of the electrical results.
