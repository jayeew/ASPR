Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have made a big effort to address all of the detailed sets of reviewer comments raised. Many of these questions
were satisfactorily answered. However, one of the main claims of the paper is that hot carrier extraction is improving device
performance. After reading the authors’ response, and their revised paper, I still do not agree that this conclusion can be
reached with complete certainty. I find the authors to have jumped to the conclusion that this is happening and are simply
fitting every observation to this conclusion without due consideration for alternative possibilities. For example, in performing
the requested fluence-dependent measurements, they obtained the opposite result at higher fluence, but argued this to be
due to Auger effects when they present no proof of this. The authors try to argue that the difference in hot carrier cooling at
the low fluence with and without TPPC is significant, but then go on to say that they cannot exactly quantify exactly what
factors are playing a role on allowing improved hot carrier extraction. I agree that this is very complex, and if the authors
cannot pin down the mechanism, they should not be presenting their preferred conclusion with such certainty. The authors
have also not attempted to quantify the degree of error. For example, if the authors made 10 samples without TPPC and 10
samples with TPPC and measured the hot carrier cooling fairly using exactly the same conditions, do they see a statistically
significant shortening (i.e., p value < 0.01 if they were to run a t-test) of the hot carrier cooling time with TPPC? To me an
alternative explanation is that the TPPC is simply improving interface passivation, such that more cold carriers are extracted,
which is a very ordinary phenomenon. From the results shown, I don’t think it is possible to quantify how much of the
improvement in Jsc is from hot carriers vs. cold carriers that are not lost due to interface recombination. We should also note
that the hot carrier cooling measurements were made on film stacks (i.e., open-circuit conditions), whereas the Jsc change is
under short-circuit conditions. Hot carrier cooling dynamics could change significantly in going from open- to short-circuit.
Ideally, the authors would have done pump-push-probe spectroscopy measurements on devices in-operando under short-
circuit conditions or at the maximum power point. 
If this paper were to tone down the discussion around hot carrier cooling and remove it from the title, I think the paper would
still be suitable for Nature Communications because the TPPC is novel (as the authors explain) and the performance is
currently the record value for Sn perovskites. These materials are also well characterized.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have addressed several points raised in the previous round of review. However, beyond the ongoing concerns
regarding the overall novelty of the study, several specific issues remain: 
1. While the authors point out that their illumination source (3000 K LED) differs from that in the cited literature (DOI:
10.1039/C9TA11894B, 3328 K LED), the reported incident power density in this study (0.285 mW cm-2) is slightly lower
than that in the reference (0.298 mW cm-2). Given that the perovskite absorber (Eg = 1.63 eV) fully covers the emission
spectra of both light sources, the theoretical Jsc limit in this work should not exceed the 138.2 μA cm-2 reported for the 3328
K light source. 
In fact, when estimating the Jsc limit under indoor illumination, the total incident power is the dominant factor, and while
spectral shape matters to some extent, small shifts in color temperature do not significantly impact the theoretical Jsc for a
fixed bandgap. 
Therefore, I recommend that the authors calculate the theoretical Jsc limit using the measured emission spectrum of their
actual light source, to ensure the reported performance is not overestimated. 
Additionally, the certification was carried out using a fluorescent lamp, rather than an LED source. While this is acceptable in
the absence of standardized LED testing protocols, it prevents direct comparison of Jsc values (certification and lab
measurement). By the way, the Jsc in the certification is lower than the theoretical limit, which is reasonable. 
2. The explanation of the shadow effect on Jsc appears questionable because Jsc=Isc/mask size. The authors mentioned
that no mask was used in their measurements. This could raise concerns about light leakage or over-illumination of the
device area. For example, in the 1.21 cm2 device, Jsc obtained without a mask is higher than that obtained with a 1.0 cm2
mask. This can be caused by light leakage or imprecise illumination area control. Anyway, the authors are suggested to
calculate the Jsc limitation depending on the actual incident light intensity and define the accurate illuminated area by a
mask to ensure reproducible and credible data. 
3. Given the limited number of studies reporting MPP tracking under 1000 lux indoor conditions, the authors are encouraged
to include the raw MPP tracking data in the supplementary materials. 
4. The efficiencies under lower light intensities are wrongly calculated. For example, the light power intensity at 500 lux,
which is 50% of 1000 lux (0.285 mW cm-2), should be 0.1425 mW cm-2. 
PCE under 500 lux should be 71.4 uA cm-2 * 0.659 V * 0.5657/142.5 uW cm-2 = 18.42%, not 9.34%. 
The PCEs under 250, 500, and 750 lux should be corrected.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
There are still major issues with how this paper is written. Although the authors wrote in their rebuttal letter that they have
softened their conclusion of enhanced hot carrier extraction, there are no changes made to the abstract or conclusions,
which still misleadingly presents this conclusion, which as the authors admitted is still conjecture. If the authors are not
willing to write a balanced paper, then this should be rejected. Far better than present misleading conclusions and waste
future researchers time trying to reproduce these results, or, even worse, would try to justify similarly unproven conclusions
by citing this work. 
We are still not convinced by the explanation from the authors of Auger recombination accounting for their fluence-
dependent measurements. Under low fluence, the hot carrier lifetime is shorter for the TPPC treated film, which they attribute
to faster hot carrier extraction. But at higher fluence, the opposite trend is observed where the TPPC treated film show longer
hot carrier lifetime, and the overall hot carrier lifetime for both films is reduced. They explained this as an Auger effect. For
hot carrier cooling process, Auger induced lifetime reduction usually happens when the carrier population is over 10^19 cm-
3. Before entering into this regime, we would expect to see an increase in HC lifetime due to the hot phonon bottleneck effect
(carrier density around 10^16-10^19 cm-3), which is not reported here. Increasing the fluence from 1.05 uJ/cm2 to 2.63
uJ/cm2 seems insufficient to raise the carrier population by a few orders of magnitude. Hence, the assertion the authors still
make that Auger recombination controlling hot carrier kinetics at high fluence is not well founded, and it is insufficient to
merely cite a few references without further critical evaluation.

Beyond this, further details are needed for the reporting of indoor PV performance. 
The authors need to quote the thickness of the light mask used. Since the authors argue that the diffuse nature of their light
source is causing discrepancies in performance without and with the light mask, the mask thickness is an essential detail. 
For certification, was the fluorescent light source collimated or diffuse? Can the authors quote the angle of divergence of the
light source? 
In the abstract, the authors quote a PCE value and the illuminance. I would argue that the field should move away from
reporting IPV performance in such a poorly specified way. The irradiance is needed, as is the CCT of the light source, and
whether it is from a WLED or fluorescent light source. The power density produced by the device should also be quoted,
ideally that measured under maximum power point tracking. 
Similarly, in the introduction, the authors talk about progress in PCE, but there is no mention of how comparable these PCEs
are if all reports did their measurements under different conditions (different light sources, illuminances, etc). Further
qualification is needed in this discussion. Again, quoting power density would be helpful. 
The phase "...we have achieved a significant high PCE (22.49%) thus far." is not grammatically correct. What does
significant high mean? It would be better if the authors just plainly say that their measured PCE without a light mask is higher
than previous reports, and then add a short discussion of how significant this really is, i.e., how similar or different was their
light source to those of previous record tin perovskite indoor PV? Can the authors compare power density of their champion
devices vs. the previous champion tin perovskite indoor PV devices? The authors need to plainly say here the problems with
not using a light mask, and the errors introduced if they did under diffuse light. Add in the thickness of the light mask.
Referring to a sketch of the setup and illustrating how using the light mask leads to errors is also helpful. This will help future
researchers. 
In the abstract, the authors refer to adsorption energy, but it is not clear what this means? Binding energy of molecules to
perovskite surface? 
Fig. 4f - please add the caveat in the figure caption that all devices were measured under different light sources. We cannot
make a 1:1 comparison across all data points because of this, but this provides a general indication of progress 
Fig. 4e - restate the light source used. WLED? Irradiance? 
Fig. 2f should have error bars added. Caption should quote the power density used for these measurements. Presumably cw
laser illumination? What wavelength? 
Fig. 2e needs to have the fluence used, excitation wavelength and repetition rate. 
The author should fit the integrated diffraction patterns of their GIWAXS measurements. Bragg-Brentano XRD only shows
two peaks. Hardly useful for proving that they have achieved the material they thought they had. Integrating the GIWAXS
measurements should give them more peaks to fit. 
Fig. S13 - I am concerned that the authors are fitting the lower energy edge of their excitonic peak. Did they try Elliott model
fitting? Why did adding C60 give rise to a peak near their primary edge? 
The light spectrum of their indoor light simulator needs to be much more prominently features. When they quote their PCE,
they should refer to exactly where the plot of their light source spectrum is. 
Please add the following to the Methods: 
The authors need to specify the angle of their light source and device under test for all measurements, both within their lab
and in the certification laboratory. 
How did they calibrate the spectrometer used? I think there is a misunderstanding with the authors regarding the angular
response. Even if the spectroradiometer is exactly in the position the DUT is, this spectroradiometer can have a different
response to light coming in from different angles than the DUT, leading to errors in the measurement. It is OK to still use this,
since there is no proof so far that using reference cells is better, but the authors need to make it clear that this is a potential
source of error. Did the authors use a cosine corrector on their spectrometer? 
Specify the tests the authors did to measure the uniformity and temporal stability of their light source. This data should be
provided in the SI and quoted in the Methods. 
The authors should specify the source-substrate distance used.

influence on PCE under indoor illumination. Specifically, owing to the physical 
thickness of the mask blocking a portion of the incident light, the actual irradiance on 
the device is significantly reduced, resulting in a decrease in PCE. We also
