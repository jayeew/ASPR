Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The work by Shan et al. describes the development of a CAR T-cell therapy targeting CD64 in combination with the over-
expression of IL15. While the work appears technical sound and of interest to the field, there are a few conceptual and
technical details that should be addressed prior to publication. 
1. In the introduction (lines 38), the authors mention that the CD64 CARs showed limited expansion and efficacy in an AML
mouse model. Can that authors elaborate on why the CD64 CARs showed limited expansion, and specifically how they
think that the expressionless of IL15 may be able to address this problem. 
2. In general it is not clear from the current work how the expression of IL-15 is controlled by the cell under resting and
stimulated conditions. The authors demonstrate in Figure 2D that the IL15 CARs secrete IL-15 on stimulation with PMA-
ionomycin by ELISA. However, in a non stimulation condition, does the IL-15 CAR secrete any level of IL15? In addition, do
the IL-15 CARs express cytoplasmic IL15 in resting conditions? The authors could confirm both of these using a
combination of ELISA and intracellular staining of IL15. 
3. In a related point, it is unclear from the current experiments whether intrinsic expression of IL15 by the CAR, or signalling
induced by secreted IL15 has the greatest effect on the both the improved expansion and improved in vivo results. This is an
important consideration, since supplementing growth media with additional IL-15 is a commonly used approach to improve
in vitro culture of CARs. To address this point, the authors should add a further experimental condition to their expansion, in
vitro killing and in vivo work, whereby CD64 CARs are cultured with supplementary IL-15. Comparing the IL15 CD64 CAR
to a CD64 CAR + IL15 would be a powerful means of demonstrating the relative benefits of both these approaches. An
alternative approach to the above, would be blocking signalling of the secreted IL15 via blocking its receptor on the CAR. 
4. It is not clear from the Figure 2,3,4, 5 if the data presented are technical repeats, e.g. CARs generated from 1 human donor
and tested in multiple killing experiments, or if the CARs are generated from 3 different donors. Could the authors clarify this.
Additionally, in the Figure 6, for 3 mice in each each condition, how many human donors contributed to the CARs, and how
were these donors distributed amongst the mice? 
5. The authors do not describe what is meant by the PCDH group. Could the authors add a more complete description of this
control condition used throughout the paper.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This study evaluates IL-15–engineered CD64 CAR-T cells as a strategy to improve treatment of relapsed/refractory acute
myeloid leukemia (AML). Building on the authors’ prior work (Sun et al., J Immunotherapy, 2022), the manuscript proposes
that IL-15 overexpression enhances CAR-T cell expansion, persistence, and cytotoxicity in vitro and in vivo compared to
CD64 CAR-T cells alone. The original CD64 CAR construct was reengineered to include IL-15, a cytokine previously shown
to support CAR-T cell function. While the therapeutic potential of CD64 CAR-T cells was demonstrated in the earlier study,
the addition of IL-15 represents an incremental advance. As such, the conceptual novelty of the current work is limited. 
Overall, the manuscript appears to rely heavily on the reader's familiarity with the authors’ previous publication on CD64
CAR-T cells (Sun et al., J Immunotherapy, 2022) as it lacks sufficient methodological and experimental detail to stand alone.
Aspects of the experimental design, rationale, and interpretation are only briefly mentioned or implicitly referenced to their
previous paper, which limits the accessibility and clarity of the current work. Additionally, many figure panels are described
only superficially in the Results, often limited to a sentence stating what was done, without adequate explanation or
interpretation of the findings. A more thorough presentation and discussion of the data are necessary to improve the
manuscript’s scientific rigor and readability. 
Figure 1: 
In Figure 1, the authors compare CAR-T cells (should be indicated as CD64 CART specifically on the figures) in vivo to a
group labeled as "PCDH T cells," which appears to represent a control vector group. However, the identity and role of this
control are not defined anywhere in the Results, Methods, or figure legends. The manuscript does not mention that "PCDH
T" serves as a control group, nor does it explain what this group represents. It is unclear whether "PCDH" refers to an empty
vector control, a mock-transduced T cell group, or another type of construct. This lack of clarity significantly impairs the
reader’s ability to interpret the comparative data across the manuscript. The authors should clearly describe the nature of the
PCDH T cell group and justify its use as a control, especially in comparison to conventional options such as untransduced
healthy T cells. 
In Figure 1A, the experimental schematic indicates that the authors measured survival, bioluminescence imaging (BLI), and
“CAR-T cell proliferation” in both the CD64 CAR-T cell group and the PCDH T cell group. However, since the PCDH T cell
group is intended as a control and presumably lacks the CAR construct, the label “CAR-T cell proliferation” in this group is
misleading and should be corrected for accuracy. Similarly, in Figure 1B, the authors report “CAR cells/ml,” but this
measurement is anyway expected to be zero in the PCDH control group as there is no CAR. 
The overall experimental setup in Figure 1 appears to be highly similar to that described in Figure 5 of the authors’ previous
publication. The manuscript notes that the only modification was a reduction in the number of CAR-T cells administered,
from 3×10⁶ cells/mouse in the prior study to 1×10⁶ cells/mouse in the current experiment. Notably, in the previous study, the
authors reported complete leukemia clearance as early as day 7, with mice remaining leukemia-free until day 28. In contrast,
the current manuscript shows substantial leukemic burden from day 7 through day 20 (Figures 1C and 1D), and the survival
of CAR-T cell–treated mice is limited to approximately 25 days, compared to at least 35 days in the prior study. The rationale
for reducing the CAR-T cell dose is not explained. Given that the authors had previously established a therapeutically
effective dose, it is important to justify why a lower dose was used in this study. Furthermore, the schematic in Figure 1A and
the Results section should clearly specify the tumor cell line used for in vivo injection, including relevant properties such as
luciferase expression, which is essential for interpreting the BLI data. 
Number of donors and xenografts analyzed per group
needs to be also noted in the legends and Methods. 
Figure 2: 
The authors acknowledge prior studies demonstrating that IL-15 exposure enhances the anti-tumor activity and persistence
of CAR-T cells. Based on this, they engineered a CD64 CAR-T construct to include IL-15 expression. However, to better
isolate the effects of transgenic IL-15 expression, the authors should have included a comparison between CD64 CAR-T
cells cultured in the presence of exogenous IL-15 and the CD64-IL15 CAR-T cells. Was such a comparison performed? If
so, it should be included; if not, the omission should be addressed and justified. 
In Figure 2B, the schematic indicates that CAR-T cells were purified at Day 5. It is unclear whether CAR-T purification was
performed for all subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments. This methodological detail is critical and should be explicitly
described in the Methods section. 
Regarding the in vitro data presented in Figures 2D–F: 
The number of independent donors used for these experiments is not stated, nor is the number of biological replicates. This
information is necessary to assess the reproducibility and robustness of the data. Should authors assume this as three as
there are three circles in the plots. Is it three donors or three independent experiments using same donor cells? 
It is also
unclear whether a control group (e.g., PCDH T cells) was included in these experiments. If so, the control data should be
shown in the figures; if not, the rationale for excluding it should be explained. 
In Figure 2E, cytotoxicity is assessed across multiple effector:target (E:T) ratios using THP-1, U937, and MOLT-13 target cell
lines. However, the method used for assessing cytotoxicity is not described in the figure legend or in the Results section.
The type of assay (e.g., flow-based killing, LDH release, luciferase-based viability, live cell imaging etc.) must be specified. 
In Figure 2F, only U937 is used for cytokine secretion analysis. For completeness and consistency, cytokine profiles should

Figure 4: 
In the main text (lines 137–154), the authors refer to Figure 4 as “Figure 5,” which appears to be an error and should be
corrected throughout. The figure legend contains an additional error, referencing panel “4F” when the corresponding data
are clearly in panel “4D.” This too should be corrected for consistency and accuracy. 
Panel 4D presents flow cytometry data showing intracellular TNF-α levels in CD64 CAR-T versus CD64-IL15 CAR-T cells.
The levels appear comparable between the two groups. However, this finding seems inconsistent with the cytokine release
data shown in Figure 2F, where TNF-α secretion was reported to be significantly decreased in the CD64-IL15 CAR-T group.
Is the difference due to methodological sensitivity, timing of measurements, or differential regulation of secretion versus
expression? It is important for interpreting the functional effects of IL-15 engineering on CAR-T cell cytokine production. 
Figure 5: 
In regard to Panels 5A and 5B, the manuscript presents GSEA results with nominal p-values, but it is unclear whether these
values have been adjusted for gene set size and multiple hypothesis testing. Nominal p-values alone are insufficient for
evaluating gene set enrichment across multiple comparisons. The authors must report false discovery rates (FDRs), which
are critical for proper interpretation in GSEA, as they account for both gene set size and multiple testing. Without FDR
values, the reliability of the enrichment claims remains questionable. 
Additionally, the origin and curation method of the gene sets used in the GSEA are not clearly described. Did the authors
use publicly available gene sets (e.g., from MSigDB or another database), or were custom gene sets created for this
analysis? This information should be clearly stated in the Methods section. If publicly available gene sets were used,
specific names and versions should be indicated. 
The number of biological replicates used for RNA-seq in each group should also be clearly reported. Replicate number is
essential for evaluating the robustness of the gene expression analysis.

Point-by-point responses 
Reviewer #1: 
 
The work by Shan et al. describes the development of a CAR T -cell therapy targeting CD64 in 
combination with the over-expression of IL15. While the work appears technical sound and of interest 
to the field, there are  a few conceptual and technical details that should be addressed prior to 
publication. 
 
1. In the introduction (lines 38), the authors mention that the CD64 CARs showed limited expansion 
and efficacy in an AML mouse model. Can that authors elaborate on why  the CD64 CARs showed 
limited expansion, and specifically how they think that the expressionless of IL15 may be able to 
address this problem. 
Response: 
Thank you for your question. In a previous study, we observed that a rapid disease relapse of 
AML, which may be associated with insufficient expansion of CD64 CAR -T cells in vivo (Sun, X. et 
al. 2022 ). This expansion may be related to hostile AML tumor microenvironments. Studies have 
shown that CAR T cell-derived cytokines can boost AML survival, leading to CAR-T cell exhaustion 
(Bhagwat, A.S. et al. 2024). It is believed that inhibitory receptors and suppressor cells may limit the 
efficacy of CAR T cells in AML (Epperly, R. et al. 2020). 
IL-15 is a potent cytokine that has been shown to activate T cells and promote their differentiation 
into memory phenotypes. Specifically, IL-15 supports the long-term survival of CD8+ memory T cells 
and augments anti-tumor activity in vivo (Klebanoff, C.A. et al. 2004). In addition, overexpression of 
IL-15 has also been demonstrated to enhance the anti -tumor activity of CAR -T cells that targeting 
CD19, CLL-1 and GPC-3 (Sánchez-Moreno, I. et al. 2024; Ataca, Atilla, P. et al. 2020; Batra, S. A. 
et al. 2020). Notably, IL-15 has also shown potency in promoting CAR -T cell expansion in an AML 
model (Ataca, Atilla, P. et al. 2020). This phenomenon that may be attributed to the combined effects 
of IL-15 promoting expansion and sustained enhancement. Therefore, to enhance the expansion and 
persistence of CD64 CAR-T cells, we chose to overexpress IL-15 as a synergistic factor.  
We have clarified these issues lines 41-44, page 3 of the Abstract ; lines 77-87, page 5 of the 
Introduction section and lines 98-101, page 6 of Results section in the revised manuscript.

2. In general it is not clear from the current work how the expression of IL-15 is controlled by the cell 
under resting and stimulated conditions. The authors demonstrate in Figure 2D that the IL15 CARs 
secrete IL-15 on stimulation with PMA-ionomycin by ELISA. However, in a non stimulation condition, 
does the IL-15 CAR secrete any level of IL15? In addition, do the IL -15 CARs express cytoplasmic 
IL15 in resting conditions? The authors could confirm both of these using a combination of ELISA 
and intracellular staining of IL15. 
Response: 
Thanks for your suggestion. We have performed the experiment in which CAR-T cells were 
stimulated with PMA /ionomycin or not, followed by measuring the secretion of IL-15 secretion via 
ELISA and the expression of cytoplasmic IL-15 via intracellular cytokine staining (ICS). 
The results showed that IL-15 CAR-T cells secrete IL-15 when stimulated with PMA/ionomycin, 
but exhibit minimal or undetectable IL -15 secretion under unstimulated conditions  as measured by 
ELISA. Additionally, intracellular staining was performed to assess cytoplasmic IL-15 expression. 
Under resting conditions, no cytoplasmic IL -15 expression was observe d in IL -15 CAR -T cells. 
However, following stimulation with PMA/ionomycin and the subsequent inhibition of secretion with 
GolgiStop, cytoplasm IL-15 expression was slightly detected within the cytoplasm.  
In our study, IL-15 CAR-T cells only exhibited detectable levels of cytoplasmic IL-15 and soluble 
IL-15 after PMA/ionomycin stimulation. The results indicate that IL-15 production by IL-15 CAR-T 
cells is tightly regulated and significantly induced upon strong activation, which is consistent with
