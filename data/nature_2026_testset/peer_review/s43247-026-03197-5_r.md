REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents a refined, multi-episode SO
₂
 forcing reconstruction for the 1783 Laki eruption and uses 30-member
“Laki” vs. 30-member “no-Laki” ensembles in CESM1.2 (and parallel WACCM experiments) to diagnose the mechanism of
the anomalous Northern Eurasian winter warming (WW) in DJF 1783–84. The authors validate their CESM results against
EKF400v2 and the new WinTEDA data assimilation products, and explore seasonality sensitivity by superposing other high-
latitude eruptions and hypothetical eruptions in different months. They conclude that substantial stratospheric aerosol
loading into autumn/winter is critical for triggering the Holton–Tan vortex-strengthening → NAO+ pathway and midlatitude
warming, and argue for the fidelity of their updated forcing for future paleoclimate and geoengineering studies. 
The paper’s core innovations and ensemble-based mechanistic diagnosis are strong, but it currently overstates its
conclusions by relying on a single event and mean-only diagnostics. Incorporating the required analyses—ensemble
outcome counts, N
ₘᵢₙ
 estimation, amplitude-vs-variability figure—and explicitly addressing model vs. real-world variability
and the tropical/extratropical theory gap will transform this manuscript into a robust, generalizable contribution. 
These are my major concerns; 
Model vs. Real-World Variability Demonstrating a forced response in an idealized ensemble mean is not equivalent to
reproducing the real observed winter, which combines forced + internal variability. The manuscript must explicitly
acknowledge this distinction. Agreement between model mean and proxy/reanalysis fields “means nothing” unless internal
variability in the real world is quantified and shown to be insufficient to explain the signal. 
Tropical vs. Extratropical Theory 
The stratospheric-heating → NAO+ pathway is supposed to be a tropical-eruption theory (although it has recently been put

into question-i.e Polvani and Camargo (2020) ). Appealing to it for a high-latitude Laki eruption (or to extratropical eruptions)
requires justification. 
The authors need to clarify why and how the same mechanism applies to Laki, or else temper claims by distinguishing
Laki’s quasi-tropical injection altitude and westerly-jet pathways from purely extratropical cases. 
Context with Dalla Santa & Polvani (2022). Prior work has shown that a single 80 TgS (~160 Tg SO
₂
) tropical injection can
yield robust Eurasian warming in one model. Laki’s SO
₂
 budget is comparable, so the result is unsurprising. Perhaps
discuss Dalla Santa & Polvani (2022), framing the current study as confirmation in a multi-model, ensemble context rather
than a novel dynamical revelation. 
Ensemble Member Outcomes. Reporting only the ensemble mean obscures the distribution of outcomes. How many
members actually produce statistically significant Eurasian warming (e.g. exceeding ±2σ of the control spread)? How many
produce cooling? Add a table or bar chart listing, out of 30 Laki members, the count with significant warming, insignificant
change, and significant cooling over the Northern Eurasian domain. 
Statistical Power & N
ₘᵢₙ
 Estimation. Dalla Santa & Polvani (2022) estimate that 5–10 tropical eruptions are required to
robustly detect a WW signal at 80 TgS. Only one such event (Samalas) exists in the last millennium. Using the control
spread, compute N
ₘᵢₙ
 for Laki-size eruptions in your setup (akin to Table 1 in Dalla Santa & Polvani). Discuss whether the
historical record contains enough events to reach statistical significance in observations/proxies. 
Amplitude vs. Internal Variability Figure. Without comparing the forced signal amplitude to unforced variability, we can’t
assess whether the modeled warming is exceptional. Produce a figure (like Fig 5 in Tejedor et al. 2024, J. Clim.) showing
the distribution of Eurasian winter anomalies in 30 control members vs. the 30 Laki members. Highlight where the ensemble
mean and individual members sit relative to the 2σ control envelope. 
ENSO Sensitivity Experiments Needed. The real 1783–84 winter coincided with an extreme El Niño, which by itself tends to
drive a negative NAO and Eurasian cooling—precisely opposite to the observed warming. To rigorously attribute the WW
signal to volcanic stratospheric heating, the authors should run at least three targeted sensitivity experiments: 
-“El Niño
‐
only”: prescribe the reconstructed 1783–84 SST anomalies in a no
‐
volcano ensemble to quantify ENSO’s sole
impact on Eurasian winter. 
-“Laki
‐
only”: the existing volcanic
‐
forcing ensemble but with SSTs nudged toward climatology (suppressing ENSO
variability) to isolate the pure aerosol pathway. 
-Combined “Laki + El Niño”: prescribe both the volcanic forcing and the observed SST anomalies to see how the two
forcings interact. 
Comparing these runs will reveal whether the volcanic signal truly overwhelms the ENSO tendency—and by how much—
rather than merely emerging in the ensemble mean by averaging out internal variability.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
WW are observed and modeled following the tropical eruptions. This manuscript provides evidence that high-latitude
volcano (Laki) can trigger WW as well, as shown in the observational reconstructions. Using climate models, the authors
show that the mechanisms that lead to WW are similar between tropical and high-latitude eruptions. The polar jets strengths
due to enhanced meridional temperature gradient and leads to positive phase of NAO, i.e. WW over Northern Eurasia. I
found this study interesting, however the paper is not written clearly and lacks in-depth analysis on the difference in the
mechanisms between tropical and high-latitude eruptions. 
One significant concern is the validation of the aerosol distribution. From Figure 1, the similar aerosol column mass declines
rapidly by 3 orders of magnitude in a few months. This is related to the lifetime setting of 2 months following Stevenson et al.
What altitude the aerosols are injected in the model? I am rather surprised by the short lifetime of extra-tropical volcanoes.
This is quite important as your conclusions on the first winter & winter aerosol loadings are heavily dependent on the short

lifetime of Laki aerosols. For modern volcano, like Calbuco, the lifetime is on the order of half of year. Besides, the timescale
from eruption to peak aerosol formation via SO2 oxidation and microphysical processes (i.e. nucleation, coagulation and
condensation) is about half year for Pinatubo, 3-6 months for Calbuco. 
Another missing piece is the paper lacks comparisons on the mechanisms that lead to WW between tropical and
extratropical eruptions. 
Line 41-44: Such a WW phenomenon challenges the conventional view that large volcanic eruptions typically cool Earth’s
surface by injecting substantial sulfur into the stratosphere, where it forms sulfate aerosols that efficiently backscatter
shortwave radiation. This is wrong. Conventional view of volcanic eruption that cools the Earth is on the hemispheric or
global scale, not on regional scale. The regional variations in surface temperature (warming and cooling) following eruptions
are already well known. 
The manuscript mentioned aerosol absorption of sunlight which warms the stratosphere in multiple places. However, sulfate
aerosols mainly absorb long-wave radiation from below rather than short-wave radiation. 
Line 57-63: It’s difficult to follow the logics of this paragraph. Why no WW was observed after 13 out of 20 large tropical
eruptions? How does this affect your results? CMIP5 models’ response is weaker especially for the three large events, why
is that? how does that affect your results?

REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
I appreciate the thoughtful revision. However, despite the improvements made in the revision, I still find that some of the
main claims remain too strong given the evidence, and key points about the role of internal variability and the nature of the
signal are underdeveloped. My detailed comments below; 
I appreciate that you have now quantified internal variability using WinTEDA and provided the probability distribution in
Figure R1. However, this figure complicates rather than reinforces your interpretation. The post-Laki winter is indeed the
warmest of the 23 events, and that is good, but there is also at least one event with strong post-eruption cooling that exceeds
the 95th percentile on the cold tail. This means that post-volcanic winters are not consistently warm, as they populate both
tails of the temperature distribution. 
This is a crucial result and must be stated clearly in the manuscript. It implies that the forcing does not deterministically
produce warming but rather modulates the probability of certain circulation states. I strongly recommend that Figure R1 be
included either in the main text or as a Supplementary Figure, as it is central to the argument. The text should explicitly
acknowledge that post-volcanic winters can produce both very strong warming and very strong cooling and reframe the
conclusions probabilistically, emphasizing that your results show a tendency toward warming but do not establish a
deterministic forced outcome. 
Next, your revision adds some helpful text clarifying why the same mechanism might operate for a high-latitude eruption like
Laki. However, the discussion still reads as though the Equator-to-Pole pathway is universally valid. This remains
speculative for Laki, and the paper should be more careful not to overstate dynamical certainty. I suggest tempering claims
and making clear that while the mechanism is physically plausible, the unique seasonality and latitude of the Laki aerosol
cloud mean that the response pathway may differ from canonical tropical eruptions and requires further testing. 
I appreciate that you have now provided the amplitude vs. internal variability figure (Fig. S4) and a table of ensemble
outcome counts. This is a welcome addition and should also be emphasized more prominently in the main Results section,
as it provides key evidence for the probabilistic nature of the response. Rather than presenting the ensemble mean as the
"expected" outcome, I encourage you to highlight the spread explicitly — how many members warm significantly, how many
cool, and what fraction show no significant anomaly. 
Finally, the conclusions remain too categorical. As written, they read as if the Laki-induced warming is a robust and
deterministic result. Based on the new analyses (Fig. R1, Fig. S4), a more defensible conclusion would be that there is
evidence that wintertime stratospheric aerosols from a high-latitude eruption can increase the likelihood of a positive NAO
and Eurasian warming, but internal variability remains large enough that the realized outcome of any single eruption may be
warm, neutral, or even cold. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author):

The primary concern is whether the aerosol forcing used in this study accurately represents the aerosol lifetime. While

REVIEWERS' COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The revised manuscript represents a clear improvement, particularly through the inclusion of probabilistic analyses that
explicitly quantify internal variability and ensemble spread. Figures S4 and S5 convincingly show that the post-eruption
winter temperature response over Eurasia is not uniform and spans a wide range of outcomes. While the post-Laki winter
emerges as the warmest among the selected events, the same distributions also include cases of pronounced cooling that
reach or exceed the cold tail of internal variability. This is a central result of the study and should be fully reflected in the
narrative, as it demonstrates that post-volcanic winters populate both extremes of the temperature distribution rather than
clustering exclusively on the warm side. 
As currently written, however, the manuscript still places disproportionate emphasis on winter warming, which risks
overstating the robustness and determinism of the response. The results instead support a probabilistic interpretation in
which high-latitude eruptions can shift the likelihood of certain circulation states but do not preclude neutral or strongly cold
outcomes, even for eruptions of comparable magnitude. Explicitly acknowledging that northern high-latitude eruptions can
lead to significant cooling as well as warming would bring the conclusions into closer alignment with the evidence presented
and strengthen the overall credibility and balance of the study.
