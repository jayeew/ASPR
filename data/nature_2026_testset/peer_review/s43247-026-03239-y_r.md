General Comments: 
In this study, the authors utilized satellite-derived vegetation indices and solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence (SIF)
datasets to investigate the discrepancy between leaf senescence and photosynthesis cessation across northern ecosystems
over the past 20 years. I have two major concerns regarding this study. 
First, while leaf senescence has been widely studied as a marker for the end of the growing season, the novelty of
continuing research in this area is unclear. What new insights can be gained from this study? The authors do not seem to
have clarified this point sufficiently. 
Second, as stated in the introduction, the relationship between photosynthesis cessation and environmental factors is
underexplored and challenging to study. The satellite-derived SIF technology provides a promising tool to address this
issue, which represents a key novelty of this research. However, the study solely relies on satellite remote sensing data
analysis without experimental validation, which makes the reliability and robustness of the conclusions difficult to assess.
For instance, the authors suggest that autumn temperature plays an important role in influencing leaf senescence and
photosynthesis cessation. This could be easily verified through simple experiments, but unfortunately, no experimental work
was conducted. 
Moreover, since the relationship between photosynthesis cessation and environmental factors is currently a critical scientific
challenge, the study could have focused exclusively on this question. Why was it necessary to combine this with the widely
studied leaf senescence timing to explore the discrepancy between the two? 
Specific Comments: 
Title: The title is unclear. What specific climate and spring phenology factors lead to the increased discrepancy? 
Abstract: The abstract merely presents and lists the results without providing mechanistic explanations. 
Line 18-19: “The significant increase in ΔEOSNDVI,GOSIF over the past two decades”—what are its physiological and
ecological implications? 
Line 25-26: What unique insights do the study’s findings offer for advancing scientific understanding in this field or for forest
management? 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Review by Michael Meier (https://orcid.org/0000-0003-1846-6071) 
Summary 
The study compares the end of the growing season (EOS) as approximated by changes in greenness (NDVI) and in
photosynthetic activity (GOSIF). Moreover, EOS dates were related to the start of the growing season (SOS; again based on
NDVI and GOSIF), the peak in NDVI and GOSIF, spring and autumnal temperature, solar radiation, precipitation, and CO2
concentration. While EOSNDVI occurred after EOSGOSIF, the difference between these two dates (probably calculated as
EOSNDVI minus EOSGOSIF) increased between 2001 and 2021. This increase likely stemmed from earlier SOS and
warmer autumn temperature. While both earlier SOSNDVI and earlier SOSGOSIF advanced EOSGOSIF more than
EOSNDVI, warmer autumn temperatures delayed EOSGOSIF less than EOSNDVI. The assessed paths from SOS and
spring temperature to EOS showed that (1) earlier SOS advanced EOS through the peak in NDVI and in GOSIF, and (2)
warmer spring temperatures advanced EOS though elevated GPP and earlier peaks in NDVI and in GOSIF. While these
effects of SOS and spring temperatures were assessed independently (i.e., through separate structural equation models), I
encourage the authors to include a combined assessment through extended structural equation models. These models
should further include autumn temperature, which was also also identified as a relevant factor. 
In summary, the study is based on the ideas behind Piao et al. (2008) and Zohner et al. (2023), and highlights that
greenness and photosynthesis react differently to bioclimatic conditions. One possible consequence of this is that projected
carbon uptake may be distorted if based on greenness proxies, which certainly deserves the attention of future studies. In
addition, these different reactions of greenness and photosynthesis raise questions, such as how greenness and
photosynthesis are connected and what processes they answer to. However, the manuscript does not elaborate these
questions yet, and I encourage the authors to do so to fuel future research and a wider discussion. 
General remarks 
Abstract: The abstract summarizes the study well. Maybe you add that your study illustrates adverse effects of autumnal
temperature on the annual carbon uptake? 
Introduction: Well structured and easy to read, this section nicely introduces the concepts behind the end of the growing
season marked by leaf senescence versus by the end of photosynthesis. However, I am unhappy with this differentiation,
because the beginning of senescence may well relate to the end of photosynthesis. Therefore, I prefer “leaf coloration”
instead of “leaf senescence”, (a) because you define it based on a change in greenness (e.g., L42), and (b) because
senescence is a process rather than a stage (Lim et al., 2007, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.arplant.57.032905.105316). 
Materials and methods: The materials and methods are nicely introduced and generally well written. However, certain points
need clarification, such as the identification of the peak, 25%, and 75% of the phenological variables, the possible
interpolation of the phenological data, and the effect of possible considerations/ignorance of conditions that occurred
after/before the assessed EOS (see my comments to your lines 109–125, 171–179, and 191–197). Because I am not very
familiar with both random forest models and structural equation models, I cannot comment much on their applicability and
implementation. However, I would have expected the same explanatory variables to be analyzed in sections 2.3 and 2.4

(i.e., the analysis of the factors driving ΔEOSNDVI,GOSIF changes and the exploitation of the mechanisms underlying
ΔEOSNDVI,GOSIF change). Moreover, while I feel that the contribution of the random forest models is neglectable, I have
the impression that the structural equation models would be more informative if they would be slightly more complex. In
particular, I would welcome to see structural equation models with the parallel starting variables SOS, Tearly, and Taut,
which are the mos relevant variables identified earlier (see also my comment regarding L429–550). 
Results: Key results are well emphasized. Please use the word “significant” only in either a qualitative or a statistical context
(i.e., either as synonym to “notable” and “considerable” or with reference to a significance level α; e.g., L268). Also, please
be careful not to start discussing your results in this section but do so in the “Discussion” section (e.g., L397–398). 
Discussion: The present rather than past tense is used to summarize the results, which creates a confusion between
summary and discussion. Further, the content of some references is wrongly cited, e.g., in lines 438–440, where only one of
the three cited references partially backs the content of the sentence, or lines 547–550, where a hypothesized effect of
photosynthetic activity is backed by a study that observed changes in biomass. As I did not have the time to check all
references, I encourage the authors to carefully do so. Finally, the hypothesized relationship of pre-solstice photosynthetic
activity and EOS is overly present, being repeated in several paragraphs of the discussion. On the one hand, I feel it is
important to state that this is a hypothesis (that basically goes back to two studies from one research group, i.e., Zani et al.,
2020; Zohner et al., 2023), which is highly controversial (Norby, 2021, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abg1438; Lu and
Keenan, 2022, https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.16104; Marqués et al., 2023, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-022-01946-1; Van
der Meersch and Wolkovich, 2025; https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2506796122). On the other hand, I would like to see the
concerned results to be discussed from additional view points than just this single one. As an alternative, I would like to read
about possible consequences of the differences between EOSNDVI and EOSGOSIF for science: What should be
researched and how? What do these differences imply? Are EOSNDVI and EOSGOSIF two disconnected stages of the
growing season that answer to different processes or are they connected in some way? 
Conclusion: The conclusion is well written. Please add that the mentioned possible autumnal carbon loss in the future
lessens the annual carbon uptake, in order not to give the impression that there may result an annual carbon loss.

Yu et al., examines how climate change is altering the end of the growing season (EOS) 
across northern ecosystems (≥30°N) from 2001 to 2021, using satellite indicators of 
greenness (EOSNDVI) and photosynthesis (EOSGOSIF). The researchers found a 
growing discrepancy between the timing of leaf senescence and photosynthetic decline, 
with EOSNDVI occurring later than EOSGOSIF. This discrepancy is largely driven by 
earlier spring onset (SOS) and warmer autumn temperatures, which differently influence 
the two EOS measures. These findings highlight the complex and asynchronous 
responses of vegetation structure and function to climate change, with important 
implications for carbon cycle modeling.  
This study does not clearly address how the increasing discrepancy between EOSNDVI 
and EOSGOSIF affects the overall length of the growing season and carbon balance. 
The assumption that SOSNDVIand SOSGOSIF are the same may oversimplify the 
dynamics. In reality, SOSGOSIF often occurs earlier than SOSNDVI, suggesting that 
the growing season for photosynthesis may be longer. This raises an important 
question: while a longer NDVI-based season could imply increased respiration, an 
extended GOSIF-based season may enhance carbon uptake. Whether the net effect 
results in a stronger carbon sink depends on whether the increase in photosynthesis 
outweighs the increase in respiration. Exploring how the widening ΔEOSNDVI,GOSIF 
relates to these opposing fluxes would provide valuable insight into ecosystem carbon 
dynamics under climate change. 
This study provides valuable analysis of the underlying mechanisms in Figures 3, 4, and 
5. However, it lacks a clear discussion on how the full seasonal sequence—SOS, Tearly, 
Taut, and EOS—or the combination of SOS, GPP , and EOS—interacts to influence 
EOS. It would be helpful if the authors clarified whether Tearly and Taut are considered 
key mediators of GPP dynamics across the growing season and how they contribute to 
ΔEOSNDVI,GOSIF.
