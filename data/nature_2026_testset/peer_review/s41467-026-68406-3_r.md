(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript tries to deliver a new concept on self-assembly dislocation and strain field control to tune properties of
BFMO films. Although the authors carried out comprehensive studies on the dislocation effects on strains and ferroelectric
properties, I do not think this manuscript provides sufficient novelty that readers of Nature Communicaions expect. In
addition, some of the statements are rather speculative. The detailed comments are as follows. 
I am convinced that the manuscript presents the consistency that the BFMO/LNO/Ni-Cr film is less dislocation density than
the BFMO/Ni-Cr film and demonstrates the consequent stress uniformity and improvement of ferroelectric properties.
However, I do not see how the authors engineered and leveraged the dislocations to control the strain field and improve the
properties besides reducing the dislocations. Although the authors bring up "reduced dislocation self-assembly mediated
strain engineering", the conclusion appears to be that a film with lower dislocation density (Fig. 4g and h) is better (Fig. 2c,
Fig. 5g, h, and i), which is not a new concept. People have already developed high quality BFO films with lower coercive
field and higher polarization (which is not easy to compare due to the orientation difference) even with chemical solution
method [10.1002/smll.201903663]. 
Also, the dislocation self-assembly at grain boundaries is not fully convincing based just on Fig. h and f. To prove the self-
assembly, the authors need more data for statistical analysis for any scientific publication. 
I am not fully convinced with the lattice matching mechanism for the dislocation reduction for those specific polycrystalline
films. Firstly, I do not see the “discontinuous columnar grains” from Fig. 4b and c. To me they look more like granular
microstructure. In case of granular grains, the lattice mismatch discussion that the author mention is invalid since the grains
at the BFMO/LNO interface are only influenced. Next, even if they are columnar growth, the effects on the dislocation density
at grains inside the film are questionable. The lattice mismatch induces misfit dislocations at the interface but does not
necessarily produce them in the bulk. 
I am concerned with the dislocation effects on the aging and degradation effects (by the way, that experimentation is not for
retention study). The films have neither same interface nor crystallinity, which may also affect the degradation after aging.
The authors reason the dislocation contribution based on the Ec drop after aging, but it is invalid evidence because the P-E
loops after aging (especially for 60 C) do not show proper ferroelectricity anymore. 
The statement “This distinct aging
behavior directly correlates with dislocation configurations” is speculative.

Comment 1: One important challenge for BiFeO 3 (BFO) and related compounds in 
electronic device applications, including memory devices, is the issue of large leakage 
current (as described in line. 101). It would strengthen the manuscript if the authors 
could clarify whether the proposed ''reduced-dislocation self-assembly strategy'' also

Response: Thanks to the reviewer for the valuable suggestions. We fully agree that 
high leakage current is a critical issue in BiFeO3 (BFO)-based electronic devices [Adv. 
Mater. 23, 669–672 (2011) and Adv. Funct. Mater. 32, 2112944 (2022)]1,2. 
In response to the reviewer's comment, we have conducted additional electrical 
measurements to compare the leakage current between BFMO thin films with 
high-dislocation (without self-assembly) and the one with reduced-dislocation 
self-assembly. The leakage current density was measured as a function of the applied 
electric field to obtain I-E curves for BFMO thin films on Ni-Cr and LNO/Ni-Cr, as 
shown in Fig. R1 (Revised Supplementary Materials marked Fig. S10). It is 
interesting to note that the leakage current density of BFMO thin film on LNO/Ni-Cr

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

is nearly one order of magnitude lower than the one on Ni-Cr under the same electric 
field of 600 kV/cm. The results confirm that the BFMO thin film on LNO/Ni-Cr with 
reduced-dislocation self-assembly maintains a significantly lower leakage current 
density. The results clearly indicate that our ''reduced-dislocation self-assembly 
strategy'' contributes to a notable reduction in leakage current density. On one hand, 
the strategy significantly reduces the dislocation density, thereby diminishing the 
number of defects that can serve as leakage current pathways [Nat. Commun. 11, 1762 
(2020) and Nat. Commun.  15, 479 (2024)] 3,4. More critically, the self-assembly 
process drives the dislocations to transition from a chaotic, random distribution into a 
highly ordered, isolated, and periodic arrangement. This ordered structure effectively 
disrupts the continuous percolation network that readily forms between 
randomly-distributed dislocations. Consequently, even if charge carriers enter a 
specific dislocation line, they cannot find a complete conductive path spanning the 
entire thin film, leading to a significant suppression of leakage current. In other words, 
self-assembly not only reduces the number of leakage paths but also prevents the 
formation of an "interconnected network" by reorganizing the pathway, thereby 
effectively blocking leakage current. 
 
Fig. R1 (Revised Supplementary Materials Fig. S10) Leakage current density 
versus applied electric field ( I-E) curves of BFMO thin films on Ni-Cr and 
LNO/Ni-Cr. 
Following the reviewer's suggestion, the I-E curves have been added into the 
revised Supplementary Materials marked Fig. S 10 considering the limited space 
availability. Additionally, we have added discussions, reads as: '' Additionally, the 
reduced-dislocation self-assembly strategy also helps in reducing the leakage current 
density in BFMO on LNO/Ni-Cr, being one order of magnitude lower than its 
counterpart on Ni-Cr substrate (Fig. S10).''.

(2) About the motivation regarding the choice of Ni-Cr substrate

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

The reviewer's concern regarding the relatively limited application of Ni-Cr 
substrates in Si-based technology makes really good sense. However, our choice of 
Ni-Cr substrate is driven by a specific scientific motivation that differs from 
conventional approaches, which also represents one of the key innovations of this 
study: 
I. It allows us to introduce a specific strain state and a high density of defects. 
There exists a significant lattice mismatch and difference in thermal expansion 
coefficients between the Ni-Cr substrate with a face-centered cubic ( FCC) structure 
and BFO thin film with rhombohedral ( R3c) structure. We intentionally leverage this 
substantial mismatch to introduce a relatively high density of dislocations and strain 
fields into the BFO thin film. As demonstrated in our study, this distinctive 
microstructure is not an indicator of "poor quality". Instead, it serves as an essential 
prerequisite for controlling dislocation density, enabling dislocation reconfiguration, 
and consequently investigating the physical mechanisms of interaction between 
dislocations and ferroelectric polarization. Our objective is to explore the influence of 
defect engineering on ferroelectric polarization, rather than merely pursuing low 
defect density. 
II. It enables the possibility for subsequent device integration. Although Ni-Cr is 
not compatible with Si, it can serve as a bottom electrode directly for devices given its 
nature as a metallic alloy substrate. This allows us to construct a simple 
metal/ferroelectric prototype device for studying physical processes such as charge 
injection and interfacial transport, a functionality not directly attainable using 
insulating substrates like STO. 
III. It provides a way to explore potential pathways for BFO-based applications 
in flexible electronics. Ni-Cr offers favorable mechanical strength and flexibility. 
Compared with rigid substrates (such as STO), integrating functional oxide thin films 
on flexible Ni-Cr represents an emerging research direction in the field of flexible 
electronics. Our work provides a foundational technical exploration in this regard. In 
future studies, we will further investigate the mechanical properties of Ni-Cr (such as 
elastic modulus and yield strength) and their influence on the microstructure, 
ferroelectric domain switching, and the stability of the piezoelectric response of BFO 
thin film under repeated bending deformation. This ongoing work aims to clarify the 
failure mechanisms of thin films on flexible substrates under mechanical stress and to 
establish a foundation for developing flexible ferroelectric devices with superior 
performance and exceptional reliability. 
In summary, the choice of Ni-Cr substrate represents a non-conventional design 
strategy. The core concept is to "turn a drawback into an advantage" by leveraging the 
significant lattice mismatch to deliberately introduce defects and strain, thereby

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

enabling the exploration of novel physical phenomena that cannot be observed in 
single crystals or epitaxial thin films without defect (or with low defect density).  
We have included this explanation regarding the motivation for substrate 
selection in the Introduction of the revised manuscript, reads as: '' Here, we have 
developed an innovative strategy termed "reduced-dislocation self-assembly mediated 
strain engineering" to overcome the limitations of conventional defect-control 
approaches in Bi(Fe,Mn)O 3 (BFMO) thin films. Unlike previous studies that 
employed SrTiO3 (STO) substrates to achieve low dislocation densities and improve 
ferroelectric properties25–27, we selected a face-centered cubic ( FCC) Ni-Cr substrate 
to deliberately introduce lattice and thermal expansion mismatch with the 
rhombohedral BFMO film. This mismatch is not a defect but a design feature, 
enabling strain engineering and generating a relatively high density of dislocations. 
These dislocations provide a unique way of probing dislocation-polarization 
interactions and strain-mediated ferroelectric behaviors. Furthermore, the metallic 
nature of Ni-Cr substrate offers inherent potential as a built-in electrode for future 
studies on interfacial charge behavior, while its mechanical properties also make it a 
promising candidate for explorations into flexible ferroelectric electronics. ''. We hope 
this content can effectively addresses reviewer's concern and better highlights the 
novelty and contribution of our work. Once again, we sincerely thank the reviewer for 
helping improve the quality and clarity of our paper.

(1) The reason for the selection of Mn as the doping element 
The selection of Mn is grounded in its established role as an effective dopant for 
mitigating the high leakage current in BFO, a necessity for measuring ferroelectric 
properties. This is based on two key mechanisms supported by literature [ Ceram. Int. 
41, S234-S239 (2015) and J. Alloy. Compd. 886, 161168 (2021)]1,2. 
I. Charge compensation. Mn ions substitute on Fe-site, and their mixed valence 
states effectively compensate for the charge from oxygen vacancies, significantly 
reducing leakage current, an effect robustly demonstrated in prior works [ Ceram. Int. 
41, S234-S239 (2015)]1.

II. Structural stabilization. The strong Mn-O bond energy can enhance the 
stability of the perovskite structure [J. Alloy. Compd. 886, 161168 (2021)]2. 
Consequently, Mn doping was chosen to solve the fundamental problem of high 
leakage in the sol-gel derived BFO thin films, enabling ferroelectric characterization.
