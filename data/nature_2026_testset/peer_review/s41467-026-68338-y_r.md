Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Huang and colleagues have demonstrated a water-activated adhesive tissue patch based on a bilayer of gelatin sponge and
a second layer having PEGDA and 4-arm PEG-acrylate. The adhesion mechanism is driven by diffusion, followed by
crosslinking using a redox reaction mediated by a mixture of ferrous glucose and potassium persulfate added to the PEG
part of the patch. The concept of a patch being able to provide adhesion over wet tissues is not new. However, the material
combination which the authors have demonstrated is new and could be beneficial to furthering research and development
towards hemostatic patch materials in the clinics. However, the manuscript in its current state is not publishable in Nature
Comm and a major revision is necessary to be suitable for publication. See details below. 
1. Some studies on hydrogel patches offering wet adhesion have been missed. It is recommended to expand the
introduction section and include seminal papers from the recent years which have been included in the recent review by
Yuen and colleagues (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cej.2021.133372). Also see the list below. The authors should then highlight
the key advantages and differences with their work. 
a. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-45147-9 
b. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-48980-0 
c. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aay5394 
d. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scitranslmed.abh2857 
2. In figure 2, how was the integrity characterized? What is the “Completeness” aspect that the authors refer to in the Figure
2C? Its not mentioned in the manuscript text. 
3. Reaction schemes should be illustrated in Figure 1, and relevant time scales and nuances such as pressure application
over the patch should also be illustrated. 
4. The shear strength tests reported in Figure 2 or the burst pressure measurements have been benchmarked to existing
literature, which is not convincing. The authors should test the TISEEL fibrin glue and other commercially available
adhesives which they have mentioned in their work, with the mechanical testing setup they have used for characterizing the
shear strengths. 
5. Instead of the shear strength tests, interfacial toughness or force using peel off tests can allow benchmarking with existing
studies, which the authors can consider performing (see some relevant references from point #1). 
6. Appropriate videos and images should be appended to the manuscript highlighting the interfacial adhesion of the patches
to tissues under varying degrees of hemorrhage. 
7. Currently, the animal studies (e.g., porcine model undergoing open heart surgery) only show a few seconds after the
patches are applied. This is unfortunately not sufficient to support the claims that the patch can successfully stop bleeding
over long term administration. Here, a prolonged video of the patch over the heart tissue could demonstrate whether the
material is able to conform to the dynamics of the heart tissue and successfully stop bleeding.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors have fabricated a water-activated tissue adhesive patch (WAP), which is composed of PEG
derivative and gelatin sponge, for high-efficient adhesion to blood vessel and heart tissue surfaces after surgery. They have
demonstrated the good performance of the patch using a series of animal experiments, and indicate that this patch material
might be an ideal candidate for the next generation of vascular and transapical closure devices. 
The adhesive patch reported in this work is well-designed and the experiments and characterizations are systematically
performed. The manuscript is well written and the figures and references are reasonably organized. The discussions are in-
depth and informative. Overall, this paper is of high quality and would be interesting to the wide scope of readers in polymer,
biomaterial and medical communities. As such, I would recommend the publication of this manuscript in Nature
Communications after some revisions. 
My specific comments are as follows: 
1) Water-activation represents a novel and effective strategy for fabricating high-performance adhesives used in water-rich
environments. In this context, the adhesion mechanisms should be the highlight of the work. In the current manuscript, the
authors used the linear PEGDA and 4-arm PEGAA as the key components of the patch, whereas in the figure titled
“Application of WAP” and “Adhesion and Hemostatsis”, it seems only the linear polymers are shown. Moreover, the ionic
species usually exhibit good solubility in an aqueous environment due to the solvation effect, whereas in the figure titled
“Adhesion and Hemostatsis”, it seems that the ionic components exhibit microphase separations. I may wonder whether
there exist microphase separations during the adhesion, and it would be instructive if the authors could provide the
schematic illustration in Figure 1 in a more readable way. 
2) The authors summarized mechanisms in line 118-line 138. In ionic systems, there exist strong, long-ranged electrostatic

interactions between cationic and anionic components (particularly between the multivalent ions). In addition, the cation and
EO monomer usually exhibit strong binding effects, such as the Li+/EO complexation, of which the origin is still the
electrostatic interactions between cations and the partial charge of EO monomers. In ion/polymer mixtures, the electrostatic
interactions can also couple with some short-range interactions, such as hydrogen-bonding or hydrophobic interactions, and
synergistically result in cross-linkings or microphase separations (Nat. Commun., 2023, 14: 6563; Macromolecules 2021, 54,
9053, et al.). The authors emphasize oxidation-reduction reactions in the discussions for mechanisms, and I may wonder
whether the couplings between lang-range and short-range interactions also play a key role in the adhesions. 
Typos: 
1) “…have been documented which greatly absorbs water and temporarily push away”. “push” should be “pushes”. 
2) “Application of WAP as a transapical closure devices”. “devices” should be “device”.

11. Differences in the degradation of their patc hes from subcutaneous implantations and the 
femoral artery sealing study in figure 6 are substantial, and should be explained in the main text. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this important comment. The observed differences in 
patch degradation between subc utaneous implantation and the femoral artery sealing study 
(Figure 6) can be attributed to variations in  material form, crosslinking degree, and local 
microenvironment. Specifically, the subcutaneous patch was fully pre-formed and crosslinked 
prior to implantation, whereas in the arterial study, crosslinking occurred in situ and was 
therefore less complete initially. Moreover, the arterial site is exposed to blood flow and 
enzymatic activity, which accelerates degrada tion compared with th e relatively stable 
subcutaneous environment. It should also be noted that the subcutaneous study was primarily 
designed to evaluate material biosafety and to  assess the longest potential degradation period 
under controlled conditions, which is important fo r translation. In the revised manuscript, we 
have added the following description to clarify these differences (Line 420-425): Notably, the 
degradation of the material after arterial puncture was faster than that observed in subcutaneous 
implantation. This difference is likely due to two factors: the arterial material underwent in situ 
water-triggered crosslinking, which was less complete than the pre-formed hydrogel used 
subcutaneously, and the arterial site provide d greater exposure to blood and degradative

polymerization.
