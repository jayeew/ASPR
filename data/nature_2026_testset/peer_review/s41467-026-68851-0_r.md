Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript, entitled “The Physiological basis of high acuity vision” by Ramsey et al. examines the neural basis of high-
acuity foveal vision in primates. The authors address a longstanding and unresolved question related to the convergence of
central retinal cones in higher-order brain centers, as assessed in single-unit measurements in the lateral geniculate
nucleus (LGN) in primates. Despite decades of anatomical and physiological studies, direct functional mapping of LGN
neuron receptive fields corresponding to the central retina at single-cone resolution has remained elusive, mainly due to
technical limitations. The authors leverage state-of-the-art adaptive optics to overcome previous technical barriers in
combination with direct electrical measurements from single LGN units. This powerful approach enables them to directly test
the convergence of central cones, as read by midget parasol ganglion cells projecting to individual parvocellular LGN
neurons. The study provides compelling evidence that the receptive field centers of most parvocellular LGN neurons (up to 4
degrees of eccentricity) originate from single cones. The technical sophistication of the approach —combining high-
resolution imaging, targeted stimulation, and simultaneous extracellular recording from the LGN in primates— represents a
significant advance for the field. 
These findings are of broad interest to visual neuroscientists working in either the retina or
higher-order brain centers. There are three major comments and a list of minor comments. 
Resolving these issues will
further enhance the presentation and clarity of the study.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Ramsey and colleagues employed an adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscope (AOSLO) to correct high-order ocular
aberrations, allowing them to utilize cone-resolved images for mapping the receptive fields of LGN neurons. They found that
the receptive field centers of many parvocellular (p) LGN neurons in the foveal and parafoveal regions were driven by input
from single cone photoreceptors. Furthermore, they demonstrated that LGN neurons respond to corrected images at spatial
frequencies (SF) significantly higher than those previously reported without aberration correction, strongly supporting their
finding of single-cone drive for most parvocellular LGN neurons. This finding is intriguing and provides important evidence
for a pre-cortical mechanism underlying high-acuity vision, thereby reconciling discrepancies between anatomical and
perceptual data. The manuscript is well-written, with well-designed and carefully conducted experiments and analyses.
However, I have several comments and suggestions for clarification and discussion: 
1. While the discovery of single-cone input enables LGN neurons to convey high spatial frequency (acuity) information, such
information could also be partially encoded by pre-cortical population coding, even if individual neurons lack single-cone
input (e.g., as in hyperacuity theories). In this scenario, cortical neurons might employ strategies to decode high-acuity
information from a population of pre-cortical inputs. Studies indicate that neurons in V1 output layers can exhibit higher cut-
off spatial frequencies than those in input layers (e.g., Nonuniform and pathway-specific laminar processing of spatial
frequencies in the primary visual cortex of primates, 2024), suggesting cortical mechanisms for acuity enhancement. To
better understand the relative contributions, the authors should consider simulations estimating how much the pre-cortical
mechanism (single-cone input) contributes to visual acuity when cortical mechanisms (population coding) are also involved.
At minimum, this point warrants further discussion. 
2. The authors compared the SF tuning of LGN neurons in their aberration-corrected dataset to values reported in the
literature without correction, showing a striking increase in preferred/cut-off SF after correction. A direct comparison of SF
tuning before and after optic correction in the same animals at matched eccentricities would be ideal. If the authors did not
measure SF tuning prior to correction, did they acquire retinal images without AOSLO? If so, could the SF tuning be
estimated from these pre- and post-correction retinal images? Additionally, including an example retinal image before
correction would be beneficial. 
3. The title "Physiological basis of high acuity vision" is overly broad. A more specific title, perhaps emphasizing the pre-
cortical mechanism identified, would be preferable. 
4. On page 5, lines 5-6, the phrase “(each one being a set of 4 × 4 imaging pixels)” is unclear. Please clarify the intended
meaning (e.g., specifying what "one" refers to and the context of the 4x4 pixels). 
5. On page 8, lines 18-19, the sentence "Unsurprisingly, these data also lie near the minor axis of the 2 cone simulations." is
ambiguous. What does the authors want to express here?

Minor: 
 
1. Robustness and sensitivity of the Biophysical Modeling (Fig. 3 & Extended Data Fig. 3): The 
authors should show or comment the robustness of their conclusions to variations in key 
parameters, such as cone optical density and refractive indices. 
 
The light propagation model contains many variables that can affect the size of the simulated 
receptive fields. We went through all the variables used and identified those most likely to alter 
receptive field size if they were to be varied by biologically reasonable amounts. These included 
(1) the initial electric field size, (2) photopigment extinction coefficient (equivalent to optical 
density), (3) refractive index, and (4) cone outer and inner segment diameters. We have now 
discussed these robustness factors in a new paragraph in the Methods (lines 920-941). 
The initial electric field (as shown in Fig. 4a) will be the primary determinant of receptive 
field size.  The best way to consider this is outcome is to know that the receptive field size will 
be the size of the initial electric field (Airy disc) convolved with the cone diameter (Figure R1).  
This figure shows that large electric fields will produce large receptive fields, but electric fields 
smaller than the cone will produce receptive fields that always span the cone size. In response 
to the reviewer’s request, we examined two parameters that would most likely increase the 
initial electric field size. One is eye length, for which there is some uncertainty. We calculated 
that a 10% increase in eye length (a large difference) would widen the Airy disc by 10%, which 
was ~0.3 um for 543 nm light. Since our stimulus pixel size was ~2.2 um, this means the Airy 
disc size difference would be hard to detect in our STAs, suggesting that eye length differences 
would not affect our results substantially. The other major parameter is wavelength, which 
proportionally alters the Airy disc size. This variation is already considered in Fig. 4, where we 
simulated both of the wavelengths used in the experiment.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

The second parameter examined was the extinction coefficient. If this were to be 
increased or decreased, the amount of light absorbed will likewise be changed. This turns out to 
affect only the amplitude of the STA representing the receptive field, not its diameter. Thus 2D 
Gaussian fitting of the simulated receptive fields does not shift significantly. We note here that 
the extinction coefficient difference varies by a factor of 149 between the 543 and 710 nm light, 
yet this scarcely affects receptive field size (compare Fig. 4c and d).   
Third, we tested the effect of changing the refractive index inside the cones, reducing 
the index substantially by 0.0046 (the difference in refractive index between 543 and 710 nm in 
water). This shift did not change the mean STA diameter between the original (3.28 ± 0.40 μm) 
and reduced refractive indices (3.26 ± 0.42 μm) (see Fig. R2a below).  
Finally we tested variation in the diameter of the cone outer segment, changing its size 
by ±20%. This yielded shifts in simulated STAs that were ~1/3 of the SD in the original 
distribution of widths (Fig. R2b below). Analogously, we varied the inner segment diameter by 
±10%, and this shifted the simulated STAs by ~1/4 of the SD in the original width distribution 
(Fig. R2c below). These results show that varying cone shape does change the size of the STA, as 
assessed by the Gaussian fit, but the effect of these changes is modest. We are not likely to 
have errors in the measured cone sizes greater than 20% given the averaged cone size 
estimates computed in Extended Data Fig. 2, where the error is <10%.  We conclude that while 
changes in simulated STA sizes are real, the variation is unlikely to cause significant changes to 
the results in the absence of exotic effects such as optical coupling.

effects of this short-term anesthetic will have worn off by the time of our first recordings.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
Ramsey and colleagues employed an adaptive optics scanning laser ophthalmoscope (AOSLO) to 
correct high-order ocular aberrations, allowing them to utilize cone-resolved images for 
mapping the receptive fields of LGN neurons. They found that the receptive field centers of 
many parvocellular (p) LGN neurons in the foveal and parafoveal regions were driven by input 
from single cone photoreceptors. Furthermore, they demonstrated that LGN neurons respond to 
corrected images at spatial frequencies (SF) significantly higher than those previously reported 
without aberration correction, strongly supporting their finding of single-cone drive for most 
parvocellular LGN neurons. This finding is intriguing and provides important evidence for a pre-
cortical mechanism underlying high-acuity vision, thereby reconciling discrepancies between 
anatomical and perceptual data. The manuscript is well-written, with well-designed and 
carefully conducted experiments and analyses. However, I have several comments and 
suggestions for clarification and discussion: 
 
1. While the discovery of single-cone input enables LGN neurons to convey high spatial 
frequency (acuity) information, such information could also be partially encoded by pre-cortical 
population coding, even if individual neurons lack single-cone input (e.g., as in hyperacuity 
theories). In this scenario, cortical neurons might employ strategies to decode high-acuity 
information from a population of pre-cortical inputs. Studies indicate that neurons in V1 output 
layers can exhibit higher cut-off spatial frequencies than those in input layers (e.g., Nonuniform 
and pathway-specific laminar processing of spatial frequencies in the primary visual cortex of 
primates, 2024), suggesting cortical mechanisms for acuity enhancement. To better understand 
the relative contributions, the authors should consider simulations estimating how much the 
pre-cortical mechanism (single-cone input) contributes to visual acuity when cortical 
mechanisms (population coding) are also involved. At minimum, this point warrants further 
discussion. 
 
Our study is limited to the basic response properties of LGN parvocellular neurons, which we 
know provide the primary retinal input to neurons in layer 4Cβ of V1. At present, it is not 
known how many LGN parvocellular cells provide signals to individual layer 4Cβ cells (setting 
the stage for pooling). Such anatomical data would be required to adequately model different 
theories about the relative contributions of single neurons versus populations to acuity. Thus 
we feel simulations estimating the relative contributions are beyond the scope of this paper.  
The peak spatial frequencies of V1 neurons in macaques is lower than 10 cycles/deg 
(e.g. DeValois et al. 1982 Vis Res, and the paper cited by the reviewer above), which indicates 
that these neurons are not using signals from individual LGN neurons to perform spatial 
frequency detection. However, spatial frequency measures of V1 neurons have not been 
reported with adaptive optics correction, so it is not clear how an animal would perform under 
conditions like that used in our study.  
In response to Reviewer #1’s comments about implications for visual acuity processing, 
we have added more material in the Discussion (see above and lines 239-261).  We have now 
pointed out that our findings can better frame theories about how hyperacuity tasks are

accomplished by substantiating the assumption that “private line” signals from midget retinal 
ganglion cells can reach V1 via the LGN. 
 
2. The authors compared the SF tuning of LGN neurons in their aberration-corrected dataset to 
values reported in the literature without correction, showing a striking increase in preferred/cut-
off SF after correction. A direct comparison of SF tuning before and after optic correction in the 
same animals at matched eccentricities would be ideal. If the authors did not measure SF tuning 
prior to correction, did they acquire retinal images without AOSLO? If so, could the SF tuning be 
estimated from these pre- and post-correction retinal images? Additionally, including an 
example retinal image before correction would be beneficial. 
 
As we explained in our response to Reviewer #1’s point 2, we did not record responses with and 
without adaptive optics in the same cell, which we agree would have been ideal.  
The best way to examine how spatial frequency tuning would be affected by the optics 
is to estimate the modulation transfer function (MTF), which we have done for our system (Fig. 
R3). Using 50 cycles/deg stimulus as an example, under AO corrected conditions the MTF for 
543 nm light is 0.67, which is the condition for our grating experiments. Without AO correction 
but having the best defocus correction possible, the MTF is 0.22 (Fig. R3d). Since manual 
refraction would have been done in all prior macaque studies, there is likely to be some 
residual defocus error, up to 0.25 D, and this would degrade the MTF to 0.05 (Fig. R3e). Thus, 
these degraded conditions would lower stimulus contrast by at least a factor of 4, driving cells 
less well by that factor. This means one would expect spatial frequency tuning curves to be 
shifted to lower spatial frequencies by about that amount, which agrees with what we report in 
Fig. 5 and on Line 209. When human contrast sensitivity to monochromatic light was examined 
with and without AO correction previously, the amount of improved sensitivity varied by spatial 
frequency (as expected) and ranged across a factor of 2 to 6 (Liang & Williams 1997 JOSAA; 
Yoon & Williams 2002 JOSAA). We have now noted this improvement factor is consistent with 
our results (Lines 210-212).
