Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study introduces HyDrop v2, an improved, open-source microfluidic droplet platform for scATAC-seq. Compared to
commercial systems, HyDrop v2 enhances sensitivity, reproducibility, and cost-efficiency, enabling the generation of large-
scale single-cell chromatin accessibility atlases required for training S2F deep learning models. The method represents a
valuable, accessible alternative to commercial platforms, particularly for labs with limited resources. However, several
critical issues need to be addressed before further consideration. For example, the current version lacks a clear scientific
narrative, does not control for key confounders in the AI evaluation, and misses the level of rigor expected for claims about
model performance. The work could be valuable if reframed as a carefully controlled benchmark study of platform-generated
data for AI tasks. To reach that level, it needs tighter experimental design, cleaner definitions, and more disciplined analysis.
Major Concerns 
1. On line 192. It is unclear whether the authors ensured a fair comparison between methods regarding cellular diversity.
Given that more cells were processed with HyDrop v2, it is unsurprising that more diversity was captured. The authors
should explicitly state whether subsampling or normalization was performed to ensure comparability. A controlled analysis
showing that HyDrop v2 captures equivalent diversity to 10x v2 given the same number of cells would be more convincing. 
2. Lines 215 & 307, Fig. 2b & 4d. The current figures only show examples at single loci, which are not representative of
genome-wide performance. The authors may consider includING genome-wide comparisons of DARs and fragment
distributions, and reporting the total number of DARs and TFBS identified by each method. Extend the comparison beyond
isolated regions and include summary statistics across the genome. 
3. Lines 240–241. While the authors claim that HyDrop v2 models perform similarly to those based on 10x data, the visual
evidence (Figure 3b) suggests that the 10x model performs better in certain classes. The authors should acknowledge this
difference and provide quantitative metrics to support or qualify their claims. 
4. Line 308, Fig. S5d. The HyDrop v2 peak within the hemocyte data differs notably from 10x v2, with an apparent lack of
read coverage in specific regions. This discrepancy warrants further investigation—are these technical artifacts, biological
variability, or data quality issues? Additionally, the sciATAC profile appears irregular and may suggest underlying quality
issues. 
5. Line 315, Fig. 4c. The FRIP is a key quality metric in scATAC-seq. The lower FRIP scores observed in HyDrop v2 (mostly
between 0.3–0.4) compared to 10x v2 (most above 0.4) raise concerns about data quality and signal-to-noise ratio. 
6. Line 473. The claim that HyDrop v2 footprints ‘largely correspond’ to those in 10x data lacks concrete support. The
examples are scattered across several figures, making it difficult to assess. The authors may consider providing quantitative
comparisons of footprint across genome, and consolidating results into a single comprehensive panel or summary figure
other than from panels from different figures. 
Other major concerns about the approaches

1. The paper’s core scientific focus is unclear. The methods section centers on improving the HyDrop v2 platform, while the
results section shifts gears to evaluating deep learning (S2F) models trained on the data. It’s not clear whether the main
contribution is engineering or modeling, and right now it reads like two separate papers stitched together. If the S2F
performance is meant to validate the platform, the authors need to make that reasoning explicit. 
2. The comparison between S2F models trained on HyDrop v2 and 10x Genomics data lacks basic experimental rigor.
Model performance can be affected by architecture, training strategies, label definitions, and class imbalance—not just input
data. Without proper controls or ablation, it's not convincing to attribute observed performance differences (or similarities) to
the data source. A side-by-side setup with identical models, cross-dataset evaluation, and clear statistical analysis is
necessary. 
3. Generalization is not tested. The S2F models are trained and evaluated on HyDrop v2 data from only two settings: mouse
cortex and fly embryo. If the claim is that HyDrop v2 enables training of robust S2F models, this should be tested on
additional tissues, organisms, or at least some external datasets. Otherwise, it’s hard to assess the practical range of the
platform’s utility. 
4. Terminology is used inconsistently. “HyDrop v2” is sometimes the platform, sometimes the data, and sometimes the
model. That’s a problem. The authors should make a clean distinction between the system, the dataset (scATAC-seq
profiles), and the models (S2F predictors). Right now the language adds unnecessary ambiguity. 
5. The modeling section lacks key technical details. There’s no architectural description, no mention of input processing,
hyperparameters, or training-validation splits. For a paper that makes claims about model performance, this is not
acceptable. Even though the authors used CREsted, readers still need enough information to reproduce or evaluate the
results. Code and weights should be released as well. 
6. There is no methodological innovation on the AI side. The authors rely on existing pipelines (CREsted, tfmodisco,
seq2PRINT), and that’s fine, but it should be clearly acknowledged. The value here lies in platform-driven application, not
algorithm design. Right now the manuscript doesn’t make that distinction clear. 
7. The authors argue that the combination of deep learning’s data requirements and the sparsity of scATAC-seq data
necessitates large-scale datasets “across species, genotypes, conditions, and developmental stages.” However, this
reasoning is not sufficiently justified. While it is true that deep learning models benefit from large datasets and that scATAC-
seq data is inherently sparse, this does not logically entail that diversity across such biological axes is required. In fact,
integrating data from multiple species or genotypes introduces potential domain shifts that may complicate model training
rather than ameliorate sparsity or data scarcity. Unless the authors' goal is to build cross-species generalizable S2F models
(which is not the stated focus here), the need for such heterogeneous data sources should be either better motivated with
empirical or theoretical support, or more precisely framed as an optional advantage rather than a core requirement. 
Minor Concerns 
1. Figure placement and citation are disorganized. For example, Figure 1b appears after 1d, and Figure S1f is cited before
S1a. Similarly, Figure 3g is never cited. Please review the entire manuscript and ensure figures are cited sequentially and
clearly aligned with the text. 
2. Fig. 1f. Surprisingly, 10x v1 shows higher FRIP than 10x v2. This observation needs explanation. Could this be due to
batch effects, sequencing depth, or differences in preprocessing? 
3. Fig. 2d. Some cell-type-specific ACRs do not show high overlap between platforms. Please comment on potential causes.
4. Line 235. The decision to forgo batch correction needs justification. Given the number of batches and platforms compared,
batch effects could confound the interpretation. 
5. Line 265. This paragraph lacks a visual aid reference. A figure or table should be cited to support the results presented. 
6. Fig. S5a. For clarity, split the comparison plots between HyDrop v2 and 10x into separate panels, as overlapping density
plots are hard to interpret. 
7. Lines 313, 411. Typo noted: "Figure 43" and "Figure S73" should be corrected. 
8. Lines S6a–c. The color bar scales for correlation values should be standardized across comparisons to enable direct
visual interpretation.

This study introduces HyDrop v2, an improved, open -source microfluidic droplet platform for scATAC -seq. 
Compared to commercial systems, HyDrop v2 enhances sensitivity, reproducibility, and cost -efficiency, 
enabling the generation of large -scale single-cell chromatin accessibility atlases required for training S2F 
deep learning models. The method represents a valuable, accessible alternative to commercial platforms, 
particularly for labs with limited resources. However, several critical issues need to be addressed before 
further consideration. For example, the current version lacks a clear scientific narrative, does not control for

We thank Reviewer 1 for the constructive suggestions. We significantly rewrote the manuscript, improved 
the narrative, added many additional analyses, and focused more on a carefully controlled benchmark study 
as suggested. We now trained models with 10-fold cross-validation to address model variability. We believe 
that our new structure provides a clearer story and we hope that the reviewer agrees.  
 
Major Concerns 
1. On line 192. It is unclear whether the authors ensured a fair comparison between methods regarding 
cellular diversity. Given that more cells were processed with HyDrop v2, it is unsurprising that more diversity 
was captured. The authors should explicitly state whether subsampling or normalization was performed to 
ensure comparability. A controlled analysis showing that HyDrop v2 captures equivalent diversity to 10x v2 
given the same number of cells would be more convincing. 
 
We have addressed this concern by subsampling the mouse cortex data to only focus on M1 motor cortex. 
We trained models with identical model architectures and hyperparameters on HyDrop v2 - and 10x-based 
data, and we varied the training data in terms of the  number of cells and the number of reads in the 
preprocessed data. For each scenario, we performed baseline model training on all peaks, followed by a 
fine-tuning stage on cell-type-specific peaks. Additionally, we performed k -fold cross validations (k=10) for 
each model, wherein each fold model was validated on different test regions, ensuring the same train -test 
splits between HyDrop v2- and 10x-based data. When investigating these models with respect to the inputs, 
such as in the case of the sequence con tribution plots, or, when validating on different datasets, we used 
the average predictions over the ten folds to reduce variability.  
In total, we trained 220 models starting from the same hyperparameters (11 scenarios x fine tuning and 
baseline x 10 folds). Reducing the number of cells of the training data set mainly impacts the average 
performance across all predictions (macro AP), whi le reducing the sequencing depth per cell does not 
impact the precision and recall (Fig. S3a). Based on these findings, we determined the number of additional 
HyDrop v2-based cells needed to generate a model comparable to one trained on 10x-based data (Fig. 2d).

In Dickmänken et al, the authors benchmark an improvement on their previous technology (Hydrop v1, De 
Rop 2022) and use it to perform snATAC-seq and machine learning on the subsequent accessible chromatin
