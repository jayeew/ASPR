Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper investigates the distinct roles of two quantum resources---entanglement and ancilla qubits---in enabling
exponential efficiency gains in Pauli channel learning, the goal of which is to estimate Pauli eigenvalues of an unknown
channel acting on $n$-qubit systems. Classically or without memory, learning such channels requires exponentially many
samples. Prior work showed that quantum memory---typically realized through entangled Bell pairs---reduces the complexity
to polynomial. However, the precise quantitative contributions of entanglement versus memory size had remained unclear. 
The authors prove two central results. Firstly, they show that only a vanishingly small amount of entanglement suffices: with
$k=n$ ancilla qubits, input states possessing just inverse-polynomial entanglement entropy ($n\alpha$ scaling for inverse-
polynomial $\alpha$) still allow polynomial sample complexity $O(n^{\alpha-2})$. They also provide explicit construction of
the input state by considering the superposition of separable states and Bell-pairs. This demonstrates that large-scale
entanglement is unnecessary for exponential advantage, as long as sufficient ancilla qubits are available. Secondly, they
establish that the number of ancilla qubits is the decisive resource. If the ancilla register is too small, then even estimating a
restricted subset of parameters (low-weight Pauli eigenvalues of weight at most $w$) demands exponential samples. They
sharpen previous lower bounds at $k=n$ and show a transition at $w=n/2$. Complementing this, they provide matching
upper bounds at $k=0$ using stabilizer coverings, a combinatorial construction from quantum error correction. They also
provide upper bounds at $k\neq 0$ extending the concept of the uniform stabilizer covering. 
This paper makes a clear and meaningful contribution to the theory of quantum learning by disentangling the quantitative
roles of entanglement and ancilla qubits in enabling exponential advantage. From a technical perspective, while Theorem 1
and Theorem 2 can be obtained by extending the prior works, the idea of using the superposition of a Bell pair and a
separable state in Theorem 1 and of adding a carefully crafted probability distribution to the alternative hypothesis in the
distinguishing task in Theorem 2 are nontrivial. The authors further introduce stabilizer coverings into quantum learning
theory. The paper is well-written, and the results are presented clearly. I thus think this work is a natural fit for nature
communication after addressing the following comments: 
1. While the previous work [21] showed that the Bell-pair input algorithm (or the partially Bell-pair input algorithm when the
number of ancillas is bounded) is robust against state-preparation and measurement (SPAM) noise, it is unclear whether the
algorithms proposed in Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are SPAM robust. I would thus like to suggest the authors providing
further discussion on the robustness of the proposed algorithms. 
2. In the proof of Theorem 1, the authors construct the input by considering the superposition of Bell pair and a carefully
crafted product state. I am thus curious if it is possible to replace the specific product state in Eq. (3) with, say, a family of
other separable quantum states. Also, can we characterize what kind of separable quantum states can (not) be potentially
used to achieve polynomial sample complexity with bounded entanglement entropy? 
3. In the Supplementary Material, the authors also compute the sample complexity upper bound of Pauli channel learning
provided by stabilizer covering with eigenvalues corresponding to Paulis of weight at most $w$ if the protocol has $k$
ancillas. However, the upper bound here does not match the lower bound in Theorem 2. The authors then suggest that there
is an improved proof technique for the lower bound may lead to a tighter bound that matches the upper bound. I am
wondering why it is impossible that the upper bound provided by stabilizer covering is not tight. I would like to invite the
authors to explain more above this claim (conjecture).

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper studies the question of learning Pauli channels and focuses on the source of the exponential advantage
discovered in recent works. More precisely, the paper asks whether this advantage comes from the ancillary qubits or from
entanglement in the input state. The first result shows that when n ancilla qubits are available, then exponential advantage
occurs even with input states of very low entanglement (e.g., 1/poly(n)). The algorithm is a slight modification of Bell
sampling. 
The second result provides lower bounds on the sample complexity for various numbers of ancilla k \le n and Pauli weight w
\le n. Interestingly, the lower bound 2^(n-k) (for w = n) was established, improving prior bounds and matching the existing
upper bound. The proof uses the Le Cam method along with the SWAP trick. 
The third result provides upper bounds on the sample complexity when no ancilla qubits are allowed. The sample
complexity depends on the weight of the Pauli and is optimal up to polynomial prefactors in n. The algorithm uses a new
method to construct Pauli stabilizer coverings, generalizing the one used by Flammia and Wallman for general Pauli
channel learning. 
These results improve our understanding of Pauli channel learning, and in particular, the cause of the exponential
advantage.
