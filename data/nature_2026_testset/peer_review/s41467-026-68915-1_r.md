Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript “Counteracting FOX proteins epigenetically control the herpesvirus lytic-latent balance” by Xiang et al.
describes the role of FOX pioneer transcription factors in HSV-1 replication. The family of FOX transcription factors consists
of several members in humans that are known for their role in embryonic development. FOX proteins consist of a forkhead
box (winged helix) that mediates binding to DNA. The authors convincingly show that some family members have either an
antiviral or proviral role in HSV-1 replication, which is an important finding per se. The manuscript is well-written and well-
structured in general. There are too many jumps from one FOX protein to another between the individual images which is
somehow confusing. In my opinion, the manuscript would have benefited from focusing on maybe two FOX proteins, one
promoting viral replication and one hampering viral replication. The data about how FOX proteins influence viral replication
in vitro and in vivo are convincing. However, the mechanistic studies would benefit from additional experiments, in particular
in regard to ChIP / ChIP-seq. experiments. Most experiments were performed with overexpressed proteins, which is not the
best setting to study transcription factor binding to DNA. 
Major points: 
- Throughout the manuscript the authors use a ICP0-null HSV-1 virus based on an initial experiment in Figure 1 where they

ICP0 virus in cells that have endogenous expression of most of the FOX proteins (e.g. BJ fibroblasts and HepG2). This

genome upon FOXF1 overexpression with more physiological settings e.g. from Cancer Discov (2018) 8 (2): 234–251. A list

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors investigate the role of FOX family transcription factors in regulating the lytic-latent balance of
herpesviruses, with the primary focus on herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1). The study identifies specific activating (FOX
A, C, E, and F family members) and repressive (FOX K1 and K2) FOX proteins and investigates their mechanisms of action
through a range of sequencing and molecular assays. While the majority of the work is in mouse cells, some of the activity
could be recapitulated in human cells to varying degrees between cell lines, indicating a general but cell type-dependent
effect. 
RNA-seq indicates that repressive FOXK proteins are widely expressed while the activating proteins are more
abundant in non-neuronal cells, but expression of activating FOX proteins increases in neurons after infection or cellular
stress. Focusing on latency, reducing the basal levels of repressive FOXK1 and 2 in trigeminal ganglia of infected mice with
siRNAs was sufficient to induce reactivation along transduction of activating FOX proteins. Furthermore, FOX proteins were
found to bind the viral genome extensively, even at sites lacking the RAAACA motif seen for host genes. The functional
activating/repressive activity was found through screening of FOX mutants to depend on backbone interactions rather than
the recognition of this motif. Using timepoints and chemical inhibitors of viral DNA replication, the role of FOX proteins was
shown to occur early in the viral lifecycle. 
Interestingly, expression of FOXF1 even dispersed silencing PML bodies,
mimicking one of the major roles of the viral ICP0 protein. 
Through a series of IP assays and proximity labeling assays, the
authors identify activating FOX proteins interacting with chromatin remodelers, namely CBP/P300, and that this leads to
increased H3 acetylation and opening of viral chromatin. 
On the other hand, repressive FOXK1 interacts with MAX and
SIN3A to provide the repressive role through the opposite effect. 
Overall, the manuscript provides a thorough investigation of FOX proteins and their roles in viral replication through of a
number of techniques and settings. It links binding of these proteins to the viral genome to functional roles in chromatin
formation of viral DNA and outcomes on viral gene expression and titers, however it would be helpful to have the ChIP and
ATAC-Seq done on latently infected neurons before and after induction of FOX members. The techniques are well
described and the findings presented well, and the findings should be on interest to researchers investigating neurotrophic
DNA viruses.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their manuscript, Xiang et al. investigate the role of FOX proteins — a family of transcription factors – in HSV-1 replication.
The authors use a variety of approaches and sophisticated methods to decipher the functions of FOX proteins in controlling
HSV-1 latency, including proximity labelling experiments to identify potential FOXF1- and FOXK1-interacting proteins with
roles in gene regulation. 
At the editor’s request, this review focuses exclusively on the proteomic experiments presented in the manuscript. Overall,
the proteomic studies are well designed, appear to have been conducted by experienced proteomics researchers, and are
analysed using appropriate cut-off criteria. Please see my specific comments below. 
Major comments 
1. The authors state that their proteomic data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange repository under the dataset
identifier PXD059232, and that the dataset should be publicly available. However, I was unable to locate the dataset using
this identifier, or by searching relevant keywords or author names. I urge the authors to verify the accuracy of the dataset ID
and ensure that the data are publicly accessible at the time of review. If the dataset is still private, reviewer credentials
should be provided to allow proper evaluation of the proteomic data. 
Minor comments 
1. I commend the authors for clearly distinguishing between proteins that are proximal to the bait protein and those that
directly interact with it — a distinction that is often overlooked in proximity labelling studies and in many manuscripts I have
reviewed previously. 
2. The authors should provide more detailed information on how the proteomic data were processed, including steps such
as the handling of missing values, removal of potential contaminants, normalization procedures, and any filtering criteria
applied. Additionally, specify which software tools or statistical packages were used for the analysis. 
3. The authors may consider validating the nuclear localisation of the TurboID-tagged FOX constructs using IF to confirm
their proper subcellular distribution, and include these data in Fig. S8. 
4. Fig. 6: It would be helpful to include a Venn diagram in to highlight the 11 proteins enriched by more than 2 log2 in all
pairwise comparisons. This addition would improve the overall flow of the figure and help readers more easily understand
the rationale behind validating the interaction with CBP in panels C–E, as well as the selection of genes shown in panel F
for silencing experiments. 
5. Fig. 7A: Would MAX, KIN, GTF2A1, SIN3A, and ARID4B still be considered hits if a more stringent cut-off of log
₂
 fold-
change > 2 were applied, as was done for the FOXF1 dataset? This may be particularly relevant given that MAX and SIN3A
were not successfully validated in the co-IP assays, which—beyond the possibility of a temporal or indirect interaction, as

detection, which might be worth considering if validation has been challenging.
