Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Using an integrative structural biology approach, Boniardi et al. revealed that sortilin interacts with an unstructured C-
terminal peptide of the thyroglobulin monomer. Furthermore, the authors characterized the interaction of the thyroglobulin C-
terminal peptide in the sortilin pocket and provided a predictive consensus motif for cargo binding to the sortilin pocket.
Overall, this study provides novel insights into the mechanism of thyroglobulin endocytosis during thyroid hormonogenesis
and expands our understanding of how sortilin recognizes cargo. 
The manuscript lacks experimental data in thyroid cells demonstrating the importance of the sortilin-thyroglobulin binding
site (Figure 2F) in thyroglobulin endocytosis and the inhibition of thyroglobulin endocytosis in the presence of the sortilin
inhibitor AF38469. 
The discussion of the article fails to address the impact of its findings in the context of thyroglobulin mutants present in
patients with congenital hypothyroidism. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary: 
In this study, Boniardi investigate recognition of thyroglobulin (Tg) by the extracellular peptide-binding domain of sortilin, a
membrane receptor which recognizes & facilitates endocytosis of thyroglobulin, amongst other cargo. The authors
convincingly identify the peptide motif on thyroglobulin which is recognized by thyroglobulin, using an elegant approach
involving both crosslinking mass spectrometry and Alphafold prediction of the complex, and validate it by structural analysis
using cryoEM. Finally, they extend the Alphafold-based approach used to identify the Tg sortilin motif to characterize other
potential sortilin cargo motifs. 
Main Impressions: 
This is an excellent manuscript on an important topic. The structural analysis is well executed and the Tg peptide density
bound to sortilin is convincing. I have only some relatively minor comments/queries for the authors to consider: 
Specific Comments:

Also, there does not appear to be a panel d. E.D. Fig 7 is missing panel labels (a & b not labeled).

the cryoEM structure)?

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Thyroglobulin (Tg) is a protein complex responsible for thyroid hormone synthesis and iodine storage in mammals. Its apo-,
iodinated-, and prohormone-containing structures have been resolved over the past four years, yet the molecular
mechanisms underlying Tg trafficking, hormone release, and recycling remain poorly understood. 
In this study, the authors identified and structurally characterized a protein–protein interaction site that mediates recognition
between human Tg and the putative Tg membrane receptor Sortilin, previously proposed as a trafficking regulator for Tg. 
Using cryo-EM, a range of biophysical methods (mass photometry, cross-linking mass spectrometry, nanoDSF), and
fluorescence microscopy, the authors provide further evidence supporting the role of Sortilin in Tg trafficking. They precisely
identify a Tg sequence responsible for Sortilin binding and solve the structure of Sortilin in complex with the Tg-derived
peptide. However, they were unable to resolve the structure of Sortilin bound to the full Tg monomer. 
The authors demonstrate that Tg, which primarily exists as a homodimer, must dissociate into its monomeric form to interact
with Sortilin. Furthermore, they suggest that the monomeric state, rather than the iodination status, is the key factor
modulating Tg–Sortilin interaction. Finally, they used AlphaPulldown (AlphaFold-based prediction) to analyze Sortilin’s
interaction modes with binding partners and predicted a consensus Sortilin-binding motif. 
The results presented in this study are supported by robust structural and biophysical data, particularly supporting the
hypothesis that Sortilin selectively binds monomeric Tg, the identification of the interaction motif and the sortilin-Tg-peptide
binding mode. The overall methodoly is well documented. These findings represent a step forward in understanding the
regulation of thyroid hormone biosynthesis. However, some minor methodological aspects raise concerns, and I suggest
further elaboration regarding the impact of Tg iodination. 
I recommend publication of this study after minor revisions and discussion with the authors regarding the following points: 
Major Comments 
1. The production of monomeric Tg: 
The 2D classes and EM density maps of the mTg–Sortilin complex suggest significant flexibility of mTg, as noted by the
authors, but this could also reflect poor folding of mTg. I am concerned about the structural integrity of mTg following the heat
shock treatment at 60°C for 2 minutes, which may have contributed to the difficulty in resolving the complex. Given that
extracellular cathepsins may be implicated in Tg partial degradation, what was the rationale for using heat shock instead of
limited proteolysis with cathepsins? 
Another possible explanation for the failure to obtain a Sortilin-mTg structure could lie in sample behavior during grid
preparation. The authors used graphene oxide grids, which were employed in the first human Tg structure. While continuity
in sample preparation is understandable, other strategies (e.g., detergent use, affinity grids) have been successfully applied
to Tg. Were alternative sample preparation methods tested? 
2. Sortilin binds a short disordered C-terminal Tg peptide, independent of iodination status: 
The iodination level or mapping of iodination sites should be measured or reported for the samples used in the interaction
assays comparing unmodified and iodinated Tg.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

No dissociation constant (Kd) is provided for the Tg peptide–Sortilin interaction. 
Tyrosine 2766 (Y2766), identified here as involved in Sortilin binding, was previously reported as a hormonogenic and
iodination site (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-1995-4). As the authors propose that Tg quaternary structure, rather than
iodination status, governs Sortilin recognition, it would be important to discuss the potential consequences of Y2766
iodination and hormone formation at this site. For instance, mono- or diiodination may affect binding affinity, while
hormonogenesis would lead to the formation of a bulky prohormone group on the acceptor tyrosine and dehydroalanine on
the donor tyrosine. Comparative binding studies using native, iodinated, hormonogenic, and dehydroalanine-containing Tg
peptides would provide valuable insights. 
Additionally, is the Tg–Sortilin interaction preserved if the identified peptide sequence is deleted or mutated?

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper presents an integrative structural analysis of the ST–TG complex. The findings suggest that ST preferentially
interacts with monomeric TG rather than its dimeric form, and that this interaction is independent of TG’s iodination state.
Cross-linking mass spectrometry identified contacts between ST and the C-terminal region of TG. Combined with cryo-EM
and AlphaFold-based predictions, a structural model of the ST–TG peptide complex was proposed. While the study is
interesting, it does not provide direct evidence for the biological significance of the observed interactions, especially given
that the findings contradict current knowledge. In addition, the results do not seem to directly reveal the role of sortilin in the
thyroid. Thus, additional analyses would be needed to substantiate the conclusions. Here are the detailed points that need
to be clarified. 
1. The authors stated that sortilin-TG interaction only relies on the conversion of TG to its monomeric state. Since TG forms a
stable dimer naturally and does not disassemble into monomers in cells, it is unclear whether the interactions between
sortilin and monomeric TG are biologically relevant. Therefore, it would be important to demonstrate the biological
significance of the observed interactions. 
2. The authors proposed that extracellular degradation could induce the conversion of TG to monomer. Any biological
evidence to support that? 
3. Given the limited resolution of SEC separation, it is difficult to unambiguously distinguish the binding of ST to TG
monomers versus dimers, both with and without iodination. Thus, it is critical to provide orthogonal validation using other
approaches such as native mass spectrometry to support the claim that the Sortilin-TG interaction is independent of
iodination. In addition, measuring the binding affinities of Sortilin for TG monomers and dimers could further support the
proposed preferential binding. 
4. In this work, 5% FDR was applied for cross-link identification, which is much higher than the commonly used 1% FDR,
compromising the identification accuracy. Please clarify. 
5. How many biological replicates were performed for cross-linking analyses? 
How many cross-links were identified in this
work? How many represent intra-protein and inter-protein interactions? What is the reproducibility for cross-link

chaperone" area of the protein. Therefore, we hypothesise that ChEL mutations may 
affect not only TG folding but also its internalization by sortilin. Concerning other TG
