Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript provides a detailed structural analysis of the bridge helix (BH) in Cas12a activation, offering insights into its
role in allosteric regulation. The cryoEM data are well-analyzed, and the findings contribute meaningfully to the
understanding of Cas12a’s conformational transitions. The study is of interest to researchers in CRISPR biology and
genome editing. While the manuscript is generally well-prepared, some points require further clarification and discussion to
strengthen the conclusions. 
Major Comments 
1. The study suggests that BH’s loop-to-helical transition directly influences RuvC activation. However, it remains unclear
whether this is a cause or a consequence of R-loop formation. Additional discussion, or reference to supporting biochemical
evidence, would help clarify this point. 
2. The manuscript suggests that the impaired BH prevents trans-cleavage, but no direct biochemical validation is provided. If
prior studies have addressed this, a more explicit reference would be beneficial. Otherwise, acknowledging this limitation
would improve clarity. 
3. The role of BH in Cas12a is emphasized, but how this differs from Cas9 remains unclear. Since Cas9 does not require a
major RuvC conformational shift, a brief discussion on this evolutionary divergence would add depth to the paper. 
4. Cas12a’s ability to recognize mismatches is only briefly mentioned. A more detailed discussion would be useful,
especially given its implications for high-fidelity genome editing. 
Minor Comments 
1. In the abstract, the authors state, “Overall, our study enables the development of high-fidelity Cas12a and Cas9 variants
by BH modifications.” However, the study does not provide direct data on BH regulation in Cas9, making this claim difficult to
support. Clarification or a more cautious phrasing would be advisable. 
2. Suggest the authors to enhance the figures clarity further. In many figures, the information is not sufficient. For example,
Figure 2, what color indicates what component? What the state it is for Panel D to G. I noticed the authors mentioned it in the
figure legend, however, this will affect the reading experience for the figure. If the author can label the component in the
figure according to the color, that would be great. Many figures have this similar issue. 
3. In the Figure 2, the display of W971 is unclear. Because this is very important point, A clear cryoEM density of the
residues to show the interaction distances would be helpful.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Ganguly et al. describes the potential importance of the bridge helix (BH) domain of the Cas12a from Francisella novicida
(FnoCas12a) in mediating conformational state transitions throughout initial R-loop formation, implicating it as an allosteric
activator of REC domain movement and in achievement of the enzyme’s final catalysis-primed conformation. The authors
present a series of five (very nice, often high resolution) conformationally unique structures of a mutated FnoCas12aKD2P
model system. These demonstrate the domain movements associated with the extension of the R-loop as it forms and how
the CRISPR effector undergoes a large scale ‘opening’ in order to accommodate this extending heteroduplex. The
structures are of varying quality, the authors interpreted the density reasonably. 
Suitability for Nature Communications 
• Given the structural focus of this manuscript, I would recommend publication in a more suitably specialized journal (i.e.
Structure). 
This manuscript is purely focused on structural biology and, as such, would be a better fit for journals more so
catered to this focus. More biochemical analysis would be required for a broader audience. 
Major Comments 
• The authors rightly bolster their results through comparison to similar structures in Stella et al., 2018, and Strohkendl et al.,
2024, which describe conceptually similar findings focused on FnoCas12a and AsCas12a, respectively. However, it is
unclear in this manuscript what is the new intellectual contribution aside from the role of this mutated bridge helix. The
manuscript would benefit from a clearer presentation of what is new. . 
o Further, the two referenced manuscripts described above resolve structures of WT enzymes, whereas this manuscript
focuses on a Cas12 variant with a modified bridge helix domain thought to slow kinetics of conformational transitions. While
this mutant system may be descriptive of WT mechanisms, the lack of biochemical confirmation of their findings makes it
challenging to distinguish if these lessons learned are broadly applicable or simply idiosyncrasies of an impaired system. 
o While the authors have made efforts to biochemically characterize this specific mutant of FnoCas12a (Priya et al. 2021),
and indeed some results from this previous manuscript strengthen claims made here, their previous work was largely
focused on catalysis with little biochemical evidence to directly support the claims made in this manuscript which implicate
the bridge helix in pre-catalysis conformational changes. More biochemistry is required here. 
• The figures in this manuscript are simplified to the point where it makes their interpretation challenging. Figures containing
protein structures are under-labeled and stripped of context, requiring frequent reference to the manuscript, figure legend,
and occasionally the provided structures, to properly understand. Increased labeling (as seen in figure 5B) and structural
context callouts (as seen in figure 2A) would go a long way in increasing the clarity of the images to the reader. 
Minor Comments 
• The authors note on page 17 that their enzyme FnoCas12aKD2P is unable to perform trans-cleavage. Priya et al. 2021
demonstrates that while trans-cleavage is dramatically inhibited in FnoCas12aKD2P it does still occur if incubated for long
enough – at least with M13 ssDNA. 
• While the bioinformatics was well done, I thought that, in line with my comments above, the associated figures highlighting
structural similarities were unclear (though these were much better than previous figures). Further, the commentary on the
bioinformatics portion of this manuscript was surface level and somewhat vague with little discussion beyond how they are
structurally (and thus potentially mechanistically) similar. We suggest that the implications of this bioinformatic analysis be
explored in the discussion section. For example, how frequently do these domain insertions occur across similar systems? 
• We found typos/grammatical errors in the manuscript, please proofread carefully.

offering insights into its role in allosteric regulation. The cryoEM data are well-analyzed, and the findings

Major Comments  
1. The study suggests that BH’s loop -to-helical transition directly influences RuvC activation. However, it 
remains unclear whether this is a cause or a consequence of R -loop formation. Additional discussion, 
or reference to supporting biochemical evidence, would help clarify this point.  
 
To clarify whether BH’s loop -to-helical transition is a cause or a consequence of R -loop formation and 
its influence on RuvC activation, we have conducted additional biochemical , structural,  and 
computational analyzes. The results now clearly establish that BH’s loop-to-helical transition is a cause 
since without this transition, R -loop formation is inhibited mid -way through, and the formation of the 
helical structure of the hybrid is impaired. We also show that, with a variant able to undergo the loop-to-
helical transition, BH –RuvC-lid interactions are only secondary as the variants maintain efficient on -
target DNA cleavage without the BH–RuvC-lid interactions.

Biochemical Analysis:  
To probe the allosteric mechanism, residues that mediate BH-lid interactions to convert the lid from a 
loop to a helical form at different stages of the activation process, thereby mediating the exposure of 
RuvC active site, were identified based on wild-type (FnoCas12aWT) and BH -variant (FnoCas12aKD2P) 
structures. Residues R964 of the BH  and E1020 and K1021 of the RuvC -lid were substituted with 
alanine, along with  a double variant E1020A/K1021A in both FnoCas12aWT and FnoCas12aKD2P 
backgrounds. In vitro plasmid DNA cleavage assays using both matched (MCH) and mismatched (MM) 
DNA substrates (MM2, PAM proximal mismatch and MM13, mid R -loop mismatch) were performed to 
assess the effects of the alanine substitutions. In the FnoCas12aWT background, single substitutions do 
not show prominent impairments, except for MM13-R964A combination, consistent with the stabilizing 
effect that the BH offers for the allosteric interactions. On the other hand , in the FnoCas12aKD2P 
background, where BH stability is impaired by the proline substitutions, the substitutions at R964 of the 
BH and the double variant (E1020/K1021) of the RuvC-lid, impacted both TS and NTS cleavage s. In 
comparison, the only significant impact seen in FnoCas12aWT is the accumulation of nicked intermediate 
in R964A-MM13 combination ( Figures 3e-f, Supplementary Figs.17,18, and Supplementary Table 
11). These data clearly show that DNA cleavage is an additive process with combinations from effects 
from the BH’s loop -to-helical transition, opening of the RuvC active site by the helical transition of the 
lid, and the strength of complementary pairing bet ween the RNA and DNA. Of these three, BH’s 
transition has the most significant contribution since in the wild -type backbone, all substitutions show 
minimal effect, except in R964 -MM13, which indicates that the second contributor is the strength of 
complementary base pairing that is also dictated by the position of the mismatch between the RNA and 
DNA. The new results are presented in pages 12-15, with a summary in pages 14 (lines 23-26, and 
15 (lines 1-10) which is copied below.   
 
“Our findings show that contacts between the BH and RuvC-lid (R964-E1020, E961-K1021) are 
only secondary to the BH’s helical transition for orchestrating the conformational transitions required for 
double-strand cleavage and mismatch sensitivity. The wild -type protein with an intact conformational 
change in the BH can linearize (i.e., both  NTS and TS cleavages) despite disrupting the BH –RuvC-lid 
interactions, with the only significant reduction being in the second strand cleavage for MM13 substrate 
(Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Table 11 ). On the contrary, when BH’s sturdiness is 
restricted, as in the FnoCas12aKD2P variant, the enzyme becomes highly dependent on these interactions 
to provide RuvC active site access to ssDNA. In the FnoCas12aKD2P background, while R964A and 
E1020A/K1021A substitutions impair both strand cleavages, single substitutions (E1020A or K1021A) 
impede second -strand cleavage ( Supplementary Fig. 18a and Supplementary Table 11 ). These

Structural Analysis:  
Structural analysis showed that R964 and R 968 interact with nucleotides dG(-7), dT(-8), and U10 of 
crRNA/DNA in state I4 of FnoCas12aWT (Supplementary Fig.15), while the corresponding interaction 
is absent in FnoCas12aKD2P (a maximum of 11 base pairs is visible in State 4a). While position 11 of 
crRNA and TS DNA move closer to the BH in FnoCas12aWT, this interaction stays at around 20 Å 
FnoCas12aKD2P. Previous studies have shown that 16 nucleotides of base pairing of crRNA and DNA is 
a checkpoint for Cas12a activation  (Stella et al., 2018 and Strohkendl et al., 2024 ).  Thus, it appears 
that BH’s transition is essential for R -loop propagation, making BH ’s transition the cause for R -loop 
propagation, especially past 10-nucleotides of base pairing. The bending of BH towards R -loop and 
latching into REC2 domain in parallel with the growing RNA-DNA hybrid appears to give the RNA-DNA 
hybrid a double-helical structure, implicating the importance of BH in R -loop propagation and stability. 
This stable configuration of the BH and the R-loop seems to facilitate a concomitant opening of the RuvC 
active pocket through RuvC-lid remodeling. These points have been added to the Discussion section 
(page 19 (lines 14-26), which is copied below). 
  
“In addition, the elongation of the BH due to the addition of the new helical turns and its bending towards 
the RNA-DNA hybrid appears to be critical in providing an anchorage for R -loop and to complete the 
RNA-DNA hybrid formation. In the FnoCas12aWT structures, states I2 and I3 20 have BH in a loop state 
prior to its the helical conversion. In these structures, BH is at a distance of 17 Å from the RNA-DNA 
hybrid with a total of 8 -nucleotides of base pairing between the crRNA -guide and TS  DNA. In 
comparison, in state I4, we see a fully formed and bent BH that is closer to the RNA-DNA hybrid (6 Å). 
In FnoCas12aKD2P, which is not capable of undergoing the loop-to-helical transition, we only see up to a 
maximum of 11-nucleotides of base pairing between the crRNA -guide and TS DNA, with a distance of 
~20 Å from the BH. Interestingly, we also see stacking and distortion of bases towards the end of the R-
loop, implicating that the strands are not aligned well with an impaired BH, hinting at the essentiality of 
the stability provided by the BH in these intermediary stages to drive R -loop propagation. The slowing 
down of the structural transitions is supported by the lower reaction kinetics of FnoCas12a KD2P when 
compared to FnoCas12aWT14..”

Computational Studies:  
⁠Our MD simulations indicate that the BH’s loop -to-helical transition actively shapes the conformational 
landscape of Cas12a by exerting allosteric influence over the RuvC-lid, crRNA, and DNA. Cross -
correlation analyses show a shift from anti-correlated to positively correlated interactions across states, 
such as S2 to S4b to I4 (especially for nucleotides at position 6 and 8 of crRNA and -8 to -4 of DNA). 
This reorganizatio n coincides only with the helical transition of the BH, which in turn facilitates the 
emergence of strong BH –crRNA, BH–DNA, and BH –RuvC-lid couplings in the pre -catalytic state. 
Overall, these data show that BH’s helical transition is an activating trigger regulating R-loop propagation 
and opening of RuvC active site pocket (Points have been added in the Results section, page 16 (lines 
8-24), which is copied below, and Supplementary Methods 3). 
 
“To delineate potential allosteric communication between BH and nucleic acid, we performed correlation 
analyses of the residues E961, R964, R968, K981, E1020, K1021, and N1022 of the BH, RuvC-H1, and 
RuvC-lid, as well as nucleotides in crRNA and DNA ( Fig. 4c-e). The complete correlation analysis is 
available in Supplementary Methods 3. Results reveal that BH–RuvC-H1 and RuvC-lid conformations 
exhibit strongly correlated motions with those of the crRNA and DNA, and that the motions are different 
between the different states. FnoCas12a KD2P S2 has fewer correlated motions (i.e., components move 
in the same direction) between the BH –RuvC-H1 and RuvC-lid, while there are several anti -correlated 
motions (i.e., components move in the opposite direction) between BH–RuvC-H1 and the RNA and DNA 
(Fig. 4c). In the intermediate state S4b, however, this correlation pattern shifts; there is an increase in 
the correlated motions between BH –RuvC-H1 and RuvC -lid and a few new motions with the crRNA, 
while there still is a substantial number of anti-correlated motions with crRNA and DNA (Fig. 4d). In I420 
of FnoCas12aWT, we see only correlated motions between BH –RuvC-H1, RuvC-lid, crRNA, and DNA 
(Fig. 4e). Interestingly, some of the interactions with crRNA and DNA include nucleotides near the middle 
of the R-loop (~8) providing mechanistic clues on why mismatches between the crRNA and DNA in the 
R-loop away from the PAM are particularly sensitive in FnoCas12a. This reorganization of the correlation 
network in different states of the protein points to the pivotal role that the BH –RuvC-H1 transitions play 
in organizing the cleavage-competent form of Cas12a.”

3. The role of BH in Cas12a is emphasized, but how this differs from Cas9 remains unclear. Since Cas9 
does not require a major RuvC conformational shift, a brief discussion on this evolutionary divergence 
would add depth to the paper.  
 
We thank the reviewer for this comment. It prompted us to  include Cas9 proteins in our structural 
analyses. Further analyses by including the available structures of Cas9 subtypes (II-A, II-B and II-C) in 
using the pairwise 3D structural comparison of the BH-RuvC domain of FnoCas12a and various Cas12 
subtypes (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 22 ) suggest that the Cas9 BH do not align completely with 
that of FnoCas12a, whereas a helix in the RuvC motif -II is  structurally positioned in a similar 
arrangement as the RuvC-H1 of Cas12a. As the BH and the RuvC motif-II are placed farther away from 
each other due to the insertion of the REC lobe in between in Cas9, it may not be feasible to translate 
or couple the conformational changes in the BH to the RuvC motif-II’s helix or vice versa, similar to the 
coordinated transitions that we observe in Cas12a’s BH and H1 (Supplementary Fig. 23). In Cas12a, 
the BH and the RuvC-H1 of the RuvC motif -II are located close to each other both in the primary

The molecular dynamics (MD) simulations complement the experimental structures by examining the conformational behavior of Cas12a across several experimentally defined functional states. The study focuses on how the bridge helix (BH) transition from a loop to a helix may contribute to the enzyme’s activation mechanism. The authors simulated four states: wild-type (I2 and I4) and mutant (S2 and S4b), each for 1 μs. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct conformational clusters for each state, which the authors interpret as a progression from early (S2/I2) to pre-catalytic (I4) conformations, with S4b representing an intermediate. Cross-correlation analyses were further used to probe potential allosteric coupling between the BH, RuvC-H1, RuvC-lid, and nucleic acids. According to the authors, these correlations become stronger in the active state, whereas the KD2P mutant shows disrupted and anti-correlated motions, which they propose explains its reduced catalytic efficiency.  The molecular dynamics (MD) component of this study provides valuable mechanistic context to the experimental findings by exploring the conformational dynamics of Cas12a in different functional states. Overall, the MD protocol appears well-designed and appropriately detailed. The selection of trajectories and analyses is well aligned with the study’s stated goals. However, several points related to clarity, consistency, and methodological transparency could be improved to further strengthen the computational section.  The description of the cross-correlation analysis lacks sufficient methodological detail. It is not clear which software, module, or specific algorithm was used to compute the correlation matrices (e.g., CPPTRAJ, Bio3D, or an in-house script). The reference frame, atom selection, and specific trajectory segments used for this analysis should also be specified for reproducibility. Additionally, it is not mentioned which frames of the MD trajectories were analyzed. Since the initial part of the simulation often represents equilibration rather than equilibrium dynamics, clarification is needed on whether only equilibrated frames (e.g., after RMSD or energy stabilization) were included, which is standard practice. This information is important to ensure that the reported correlations reflect genuine conformational dynamics rather than transient relaxation artifacts.  In interpreting correlation results, correlation should not be equated with direct physical interaction. Statements such as “interaction network” , ' I4  state shows strong interactions with A3', and "Lid also supports DNA positioning in I4" could be softened to emphasize changes in correlated motion rather than binding.   Consistency in the labeling of simulation states would improve readability. Throughout the MD section, state identifiers alternate between full notation (e.g., FnoCas12aKD2P–S2/S4 and FnoCas12aWT–I2/I4) and abbreviated forms (S2/S4, I2/I4). Standardizing this nomenclature across the text and figures would enhance clarity.  Correlation values are not crystal clear in Fig. 4 and Fig. SF20. (Page 28) The following sentence could be revised for clarity: “Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using AMBER20 with appropriate force fields, including 1 μs production runs under NVT conditions and PCA on 4 μs of total simulation time using CPPTRAJ⁶⁶.” As currently written, the sentence combines multiple methodological details in a way that may be confusing. It could be clarified to specify that each system underwent a 1 μs production run under NVT conditions, and that the total 4 μs of trajectories were subsequently analyzed by principal component analysis (PCA) using CPPTRAJ.  (Page S8)In general, the simulation workflow is well-constructed, but some explanations could benefit from rephrasing for greater clarity. For example, the phrase “Pressure was maintained at 1 atm using the Berendsen barostat under periodic boundary conditions during 200 ps” is vague and does not clearly indicate that this step corresponds to the pressure equilibration phase. It could be revised to more explicitly describe its role in the equilibration process.  (Page S9) The statement “In PCA, the eigenvalues reflect the RMSD of Cα atoms” should be corrected for accuracy. In PCA of molecular dynamics simulations, the eigenvalues represent the variance (mean-square fluctuation) of the atomic coordinates along each principal component, rather than the root mean square deviation (RMSD).  (Page S9) The sentence “In contrast, S4b represents a unique intermediate conformation between I2 and I4” seems redundant, as this point has already been discussed earlier.  (Page S10)Furthermore, the reference to nucleotide G7 in the circular correlation plot appears inconsistent with the figure and should be verified.  Finally, regarding data presentation and interpretation, the authors discuss correlations between the R-loop (crRNA–DNA hybrid) and DNA: " It is notable that Cas12a’s off-target sensitivity is more towards the mid-regions of the R-loop and these positions show a strong positive correlation in FnoCas12aWT-I44 with an on-target DNA, disruptions of which may reduce off-target cleavage.
