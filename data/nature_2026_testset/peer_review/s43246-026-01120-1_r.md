1. Could Cu substitution during morphotaxial cation exchange induce (or
modify the density of ) sulfur vacancies?
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful question. While Cu substitution
during morphotaxial cation exchange could influence sulfur vacancy density, our experimental
results from Raman, PL, XPS, and optoelectronic measurements consistently indicate that
sulfur vacancies are not modified by the cation exchange process.
To address this question, we consider two possible scenarios:
(i) introduction of sulfur vacancies
(ii) passivation of pre-existing sulfur vacancies during morphotaxial cation exchange.
(i) Does morphotaxial cation exchange induce sulfur vacancies ?
If the morphotaxial cation exchange induces sulfur vacancies, Raman spectroscopy expects
to show a broadening of the phonon modes due to increased defect density, as previously
reported in ion-bombarded MoS 2 and Ar plasma-treated MoS 2. 1,2 However, after cation
exchange, we observe a decrease in the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of both Raman
modes, which is inconsistent with an increase in sulfur vacancy density. In addition, sulfur
2

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

vacancies are known to cause a red shift of the E 1
2g mode, 1,3 whereas the E 1
2g peak position
remains unchanged after cation exchange.
Moreover, it has been reported that an increase in sulfur vacancy concentration leads to
a less pronounced blue shift of the A 1g mode, accompanied by an increased FWHM. 2,3 In
our case, although a pronounced blue shift of the A1g mode is observed, it is accompanied by
a reduced FWHM. Since the A 1g mode is highly sensitive to doping, whereas the E 1
2g mode
remains unaffected. 4 Cu doping is reported to induce p-type doping in MoS 2. 5 Therefore,
the reduced FWHM and blue shift of the A 1g peak, together with no change in the E 1
2g peak
after cation exchange, are more consistent with Cu-induced doping than with an increase in
sulfur vacancy concentration.
In addition, the contribution of the trion peak in the PL spectra decreases after morpho-
taxial cation exchange, whereas an increase would be expected if additional sulfur vacancies
were introduced during the morphotaxial reaction.
(ii) Does morphotaxial cation exchange lead to vacancy passivation?
Another possible scenario is that morphotaxial cation exchange passivates pre-existing
sulfur vacancies, which could result in a faster photoresponse. Sulfur vacancies are known to
introduce both shallow and deep trap states inside the bandgap, 6–8 with a higher density of
deep-level traps.9 Therefore, passivation of sulfur vacancies could enhance the photoresponse
speed.
However, sulfur vacancy passivation has been reported to result in a blue shift of the E 1
2g
mode and a red shift of the A 1g mode, 10 which are not observed after cation exchange. XPS
analysis shows no change in the S 2s peak area, indicating that the overall sulfur content
remains unchanged. Furthermore, sulfur vacancy passivation is expected to shift XPS peaks
toward higher binding energy, 10 whereas we observe a shift toward lower binding energy,
which is inconsistent with sulfur vacancy passivation.
These results allow us to rule out the modification of sulfur vacancies after morphotaxial
cation exchange.

2. The authors attribute p-type behavior to Cu substitution at Mo sites. Could the
authors elaborate on the microscopic mechanism by which this substitution induces p-type
doping?
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this point. First-principles calculations
have shown that Cu substitution at the Mo site in MoS 2 is energetically favorable, as indi-
cated by its negative formation energy, confirming the thermodynamic stability of Cu-doped
MoS2. 11
p-type doping arises from the difference in valence states between the host and dopant
atoms. Substituting Mo 4+ with Cu2+ results in a deficiency of electrons.12 Density functional
theory (DFT) calculations further reveal that Cu substitution at the Mo site introduces
acceptor-like defect states near the valence band maximum, accompanied by a downward
shift of the Fermi level toward the valence band edge, which is characteristic of p-type
behavior. 11,12
Experimentally, this p-type doping is supported by XPS measurements. After morpho-
taxial cation exchange, the observed shift of Mo and S core-level peaks toward lower binding
energies reflects the lowering of the Fermi level. Similar binding-energy shifts associated
with p-type behavior have been widely reported in previous studies. 13,14

5. The authors state that “we did not observe any qualitative difference in the nature
of hysteresis after the Cu-doping,” yet they attribute the change in photoresponse time to
trap states. Could the authors clarify this apparent discrepancy?
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. Hysteresis in the
transfer characteristics of MoS 2 field-effect transistors arises from both extrinsic factors,
such as adsorbates and charge trapping at the MoS 2/dielectric interface, 15–17 and intrinsic
factors such as defects within the MoS 2 lattice. Several studies report that hysteresis is pre-
dominantly governed by extrinsic factors. 16–18 Consequently, hysteresis can be significantly
reduced by vacuum measurements, high-temperature annealing, 19 substrate engineering, or
encapsulation of MoS 2. 15,16,18,20
On the other hand, some reports suggest that, besides external factors, intrinsic defects
such as sulfur vacancies and other structural imperfections largely affect the hysteresis. 21,22
After cation exchange, the extrinsic conditions, including the SiO 2/MoS2 interface and
the ambient environment, remain unchanged. Hysteresis in our devices is likely dominated
by extrinsic factors, which explains why no qualitative change in the hysteresis behaviour is
observed after cation exchange.

In contrast, long-lived photogenerated carriers are predominantly associated with intrin-
sic factors, while extrinsic factors play a comparatively minor role. 23 The photoresponse
dynamics are governed by the trapping, de-trapping, and recombination processes of pho-
togenerated carriers. Trap states are generally classified as shallow traps, deep traps, or
recombination centers based on their position within the bandgap relative to the quasi-Fermi
levels. Under illumination, defect states located between the electron and hole quasi-Fermi
levels act as recombination centers, whereas states below the hole quasi-Fermi level act as
hole traps and states above the electron quasi-Fermi level act as electron traps. 24 Shallow
traps are located close to the band edges, while deep traps are energetically distant from the
conduction or valence band edges. 25

recombination.

Structural defects in MoS 2, such as sulfur vacancies and grain boundaries, introduce a
large number of localized states within the bandgap. 23,28 In CVD-grown MoS2, sulfur vacan-
cies are the most prominent defects and are known to introduce both shallow and deep trap

Consequently, the faster photoresponse observed after cation exchange can be attributed to
the increased contribution of shallow trap states.
6. In Fig. 5a, shallow traps, deep traps, and recombination centers are shown schemat-
ically. Could the authors clarify the actual physical origins of these levels in MoS2 (e.g.,
sulfur vacancies, grain boundaries, interface states, or impurities)?
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comment. The shallow traps, deep
traps, and recombination centers schematically illustrated in Fig. 5a can originate from mul-
tiple intrinsic and extrinsic sources in MoS 2-based devices. Previous studies have reported
the presence of band-tail states arising from both structural defects in MoS 2 and interface-
induced disorder. 32,33
Intrinsic defects in MoS2, such as sulfur vacancies, antisite defects, dislocations, and grain
boundaries, are known to introduce localized states within the bandgap. Sulfur vacancies,
in particular, can give rise to both shallow and deep trap levels. 25,29,30 Grain boundaries
typically contribute to midgap states. 31
In addition to intrinsic defects, extrinsic factors also contribute to the formation of trap

states. The MoS 2/SiO2 interface is a well-known source of trap states 32,34
Moreover, exposure to ambient conditions can lead to partial surface oxidation of MoS 2,
resulting in the formation of MoO 3 or oxygen-related defect complexes. Such surface oxida-
tion introduces structural disorder and additional in-gap states. 32
Finally, extrinsic dopants or unintentional impurities introduced during growth or pro-
cessing can also give rise to localized in-gap states. 34
Therefore, the trap levels depicted in Fig. 5a represent a simple schematic description
of the combined contributions from intrinsic defects, interface states, environmental effects,
and impurity-related states in the MoS 2 device.

Response:
We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful comment. Based on our experimental results
from Raman, PL, XPS, and optoelectronic measurements, we do not observe any signature
of modification of sulfur vacancies after Cu doping (also discussed above). Instead, substitu-
tional of Cu at the Mo site leads to introduction of new impurity levels closer to the valence
band edge. 11,12 Under illumination, these levels predominantly act as shallow traps. The
more dominance of shallow traps, leads to the faster photoresponse after Cu doping.

Response:
We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. The schematic in Fig. 5a provides
a simple schematic of carrier recombination and trapping. Photoresponse is governed by

shallow traps, deep traps, and recombination pathways illustrated in the band diagram.
The transient photoresponse decay is fitted using the biexponential function
Iph(t) = A1 exp(−t/τ1) + A2 exp(−t/τ2), (3)
where the fitting parameters can be explicitly linked to the trap states as follows.
The fast decay component, with time constant τ1, arises from detrapping via shallow
trap states near the band edges, as shown in Fig. 5a. Due to their relatively low activation
energies, carriers in these shallow states are thermally released quickly. The corresponding
amplitude A1 represents the contribution of shallow-trapped carriers to the overall photore-
sponse.
The slow decay component, with time constant τ2, originates from carriers captured by
deep trap states located farther from the band edges. These deep traps strongly localize
carriers, suppress direct recombination, and lead to slow thermal detrapping, producing a
long-lived photocurrent tail. The amplitude A2 reflects the contribution of deep trap states
to the overall decay process.
Before Cu doping, the amplitude ratio A1/A2 is 1.22, indicating that the contributions
from shallow and deep trap states are comparable. After Cu doping, this ratio increases
significantly to ∼ 2230, demonstrating that the photoresponse is largely dominated by the
fast decay component associated with shallow traps.
