Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors investigated the fracture behaviors of topological mechanical metamaterials (TMMs) and
compared their behavior with that of continuum material and regular Kagome lattices. They demonstrated the directional
asymmetry in the fracture behavior of TMMs, which contrasts with the symmetry behaviors of continuum materials and
regular Kagome lattices. Overall, the novelty of the current study is limited, as the authors primarily examined the fracture
behaviors of an existing structure TMM, which has already been extensively studied (e.g., refs. [48-52]) including its fracture
properties (ref. [56]). Furthermore, the current study lacks the depth, as it merely presents qualitative phenomena without
providing a quantitative comparison of fracture toughness. The reported phenomena of directional asymmetry in fracture
behavior are also not novel (PRL, 126, 025503, 2021). Furthermore, some claims appear to be overstated (see comment #4
below). Therefore, the current manuscript cannot be recommended for publication in Nature Communications. Specific
comments are given below: 
1. The novelty of the study is limited. There are many existing studies on TMMs, including those from the same authors (refs.
[48-52, 56-60]). Regarding fracture properties, it is not surprising that such an asymmetric structure exhibits directional
asymmetry in fracture behavior, as in one direction the crack tip is sharp and the structure is weaker while in the other
direction the crack tip is blunt and the structure is stronger. The concept of “fracture unidirectionality feature” is not new either
(ref. [39] PRL, 126, 025503, 2021). The current study simply demonstrates the directional asymmetry in fracture behavior for
an existing structure without providing new insights into how such designs could achieve superior fracture resistance. 
2. The current study does not provide any quantitative comparison beyond showing the phenomena. For example, in Fig. 1E
and Fig. S6, only the normalized force (force/peak force) is reported. The authors are suggested to provide the absolute
force-displacement curves and calculate the fracture toughness of TMMs, and compare these results with those of regular
Kagome lattices and other benchmark lattices, such as triangular lattices. Because it would be better to confirm whether
TMMs achieve such fracture properties at significant expense of load-bearing capacity. 
3. Even though using the normalized force, it seems that the regular Kagome lattices experimentally outperforms the TMMs
in terms of fracture resistance (Fig. S6C). Furthermore, the discrepancy between simulations and experiments should be
addressed. 
4. In the Introduction Line 26, the claim of “Despite this abundance of approaches, a robust general principle for using
geometry to guide the direction of fractures is still lacking.” seems overstated. The current study does not seem to provide a
robust general principle either. In Line 50, it is stated that “the TMM is light-weighted and features material-invariant and
scale-invariant properties.” Although it may be true for “material-invariant and scale-invariant”, there are no results in the
manuscript that directly support this. 
5. In Fig. 4, it is seen that the upper gripper rotated during loading. The authors are suggested to improve the mechanical
testing setup to ensure uniaxial loading and provide the loading-displacement curves to make the results more quantitative.
Moreover, the demonstration in Fig. 4 is too simple. It would be better to show how to engineer a tortuous crack path that
leads to superior toughness through such asymmetry design.

The paper by Wang et al. uses topological metamaterials to control fracture. In particular, since topological metamaterials
are polarized---they host asymmetric floppy modes, the fracture propagation becomes unidirectional and leads to large
deformations before failure. As a consequence, the metamaterial appears ductile even though it is made from a brittle
material. 
As clearly described in the manuscript, the mechanism at stake is stress asymmetry, that comes from the topological
polarization, the distorted Kagome unit cells are floppy on one side, therefore tend to deform without stretching any of the
bonds on this side. In contrast, the opposite size exhibits no such zero energy deformation and the bonds tend to stretch
when load is applied. What the authors 
show is that this simplified picture of the balls-and-springs holds even when the
metamaterial is made from a continuous material rather than discrete springs, i.e. when the geometry is made from real
hinges. Whereas the floppy hinges exhibit low stress, the opposite hinges undergo high stresses and hence break first. This
is the reason why the fractures tends to propagate in one direction and, as the authors show, even turn around when the
notch is created on the side with high stresses. 
The main take away from this work is that topological metamaterials offer a new paradigm to control fracture, in a way that is
very different from classical fracture mechanics. Fracture metamaterials have recently emerged as a promising area of
research, but so far broadly applicable design concepts are missing. The present work establishes topology as a promising
approach and hold the potential to become a seminal work. Therefore I think this paper is potentially an excellent fit for
Nature Communications. I however think that the control of fracture displayed in the manuscript in its present form remains
rather limited. The key attribute of metamaterials is their tunability. I think that the manuscript would reach a much higher
impact if the authors were able to provide more compelling demonstrations of fracture control by utilizing further the design
freedom at their disposal and topological floppy modes as a guide. Besides this main concern (which I believe the authors
are well equipped to address), I list below some comments, which might help the authors further improve their manuscript. 
*1 The authors argue that the unidirectionality of the fracture is protected by topology and is therefore less sensitive to
defects and imperfection. It would perhaps be worth to back up that statement, especially since fracture is a phenomenon
that is known to be extremely sensitive to imperfections. In particular, to which extend is the approach more robust that the
asymmetric fracture reported in ref [39]? 
*2 Is topology really essential here? What happens if one take a twisted (undistorted) Kagome instead? Would such
metamaterial exhibit symmetric fracture instead? 
*3 Likewise, does the magnitude of the topological polarization matter for unidirectional facture propagation? I would expect
a topological metamaterials with a small polarization/asymmetry to be less robust to imperfections? 
*4 Section 2.2 can be understood by physicists well versed in topological metamaterials but less so by scientists with no
background in this area. The authors might want to make that section a bit more pedagogical to extend the reach of their
paper. 
*5 The authors often refer 
to supplementary figures from the Main Text, which make the read somewhat cumbersome and
gives the impression that key information is given in the SI rather than in the Main Text. For instance, the comparison with
continuous media in Fig. 1 is somewhat of a naive benchmark. From the text it seems that comparing with the undistorted
Kagome would be more meaningful (and would allow less back and forth between Main Text and Supplementary
Information). 
*6 Topological metamaterials can host states of self-stress. What is the role of those states of self stress on the fracture
propagation? 
*7 Higher order topological metamaterials can host corner modes, would such corner modes be beneficial for the control of
fracture? 
*8 More generally, to reflect my main concern above, it would be important to know what the range of control one can
achieve with topological metamaterials as well at the limitations.

(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciated the authors' efforts in revising the manuscript and carefully addressing the concerns raised in the first-round
review. The revision demonstrates substantial work and improvements. However, some fundamental concerns remain
unresolved, particularly regarding (1) the level of novelty and significance, and (2) overstated claims. Given the high
standards and broad scope of Nature Communications, the manuscript cannot be recommended for publication in its current

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

form. Detailed comments are provided below. 
1. Novelty and significance. The concept of unidirectional fracture is not new and has already been demonstrated in the
literature. It can also be easily achieved in twisted Kagome as shown in this manuscript. The authors try to emphasize the
novelty of the TMM in enabling “robust” one-way fracturing that is insensitive to notch geometries and imperfections.
However, this claimed novelty seems very limited. It is not surprising that fracture propagates preferentially in one direction
when one side undergoes higher stress. This is achieved at the cost of substantial reduction in fracture toughness.
Moreover, the significance of achieving such insensitivity is also limited. (see detailed comment below) 
2. To demonstrate the “robust” one-way fracturing of TMM, the authors introduced “triangular cracks” (Fig. 2D-G), which is an
unusual and rarely used configuration in fracture studies. If the TMM only shows an advantage in this special case, it means
the significance is limited. 
In Movie S7, in TMM with “triangular crack”, after a hinge on the right breaks, the subsequent fracture occurs on the left side
—this contradicts the claimed robustness. 
In Movie S6 and Fig. 2F, the twisted Kagome sample appears to have thicker hinges. If the hinge thicknesses were reduced,
would the fracture direction change? When comparing different structures in Fig. 2, were the hinge thicknesses controlled
consistently across the samples? Can the fabrication method consistently produce the desired hinge thickness? 
Additionally, in the context of fracture mechanics, “crack” typically refers to sharp flaws or narrow slits. Using the term
“triangular cracks” to describe these holes is misleading. It would be more appropriate to refer to them as defects or notches. 
3. Overstatements. The manuscript contains several claims that are overstated or insufficiently supported, which reduce the
overall credibility. Below are just a few illustrative examples: 
1) The authors state that the TMM “can be used to robustly guide fractures to propagate 
in a unidirectional and sequential way throughout the nonlinear fracturing process”. However, in Fig. 1 and Movie S2, after
two hinges break on the right, a hinge on the left also fractures, indicating that the fracturing behavior is not entirely robust in
the propagation stage. Nevertheless, lines 130-133 still describe the fracture as a fully formed one-way cracking pattern,
ignoring the broken hinges on the left. 
2) Statements such as “regardless of the crack shape” and “invariant to notch shapes” are too strong, especially considering
that only triangular notches were tested. 
3) The TMM achieves directional fracturing at the cost of significantly reduced load-bearing capacity and fracture toughness.
Fig. 2 shows that, compared to the triangular lattice, the peak force and fracture toughness of the TMM drop by over 80%.
Despite this, the manuscript claims “without significantly sacrificing load-bearing capacity” and “without a significant
reduction in fracture toughness,” which are clearly misleading. 
4) In line 60, the authors claim “This study also aims at filling important gaps in efforts to apply TMMs to real-world
engineering problems.” This is overly ambitious, as no practical application or engineering integration is presented in the
paper. 
4. The experimental setup is not fully convincing. Although the authors claim minimal influence from gripper rotation, such
deformation can introduce mixed-mode loading, which is known to affect fracture paths. The authors argued that the force-
displacement curves were measured using center-cracked samples and “the samples can remain connected at left and right
edge for the full loading period.” which is not accurate. As shown in Movie S2, after failure on the right, the upper gripper
rotates, and the crack does not propagate straightly on the left side (likely due to the loading condition), in contrast to the
simulation results. 
Additionally, since the samples have only ~4 unit cells for crack propagation on each side, the authors should clarify the
influence of the boundary on the crack tip. 
In Fig. 2B, the initial nonlinearity in the curve of TMM should be clarified. 
5. Fig. 2C, please avoid including a data table as a figure panel. Consider using a column chart or another graphical
representation.

(3) Compared with other fracture-control approaches (e.g., Ref. [39]), the TMM is more general
and robust. Its crack propagation direction is less sensitive to unavoidable fabrication imperfections
and remains unaffected by notch shapes. In fact, Ref. [39] (PRL, 126, 025503, 2021) presents a

element analysis (FEM) in the linear elastic regime; the resulting stress distribution is presented in Fig.3.
As illustrated in Fig.3(A), the left notch tip experiences higher stress than the right tip, which explains
why forward cracking is systematically favored.
Small defects and system imperfections can act as stress concentrators, influencing both crack
initiation and propagation (T. L. Anderson, Fracture Mechanics: Fundamentals and Applications). This
sensitivity might explain the indeterminacy observed in PRL 126, 025503 (2021), where approximately

account for potential fabrication imperfections, we introduce a slight geometric modification by rounding
a sharp corner, as illustrated in Fig. 3B. In this modified case, the highest stress concentrates at the
rounded corner, where crack initiation is expected. This demonstrates that even minor fabrication
imperfections can significantly alter the crack propagation path. In contrast, TMMs exhibit notably
more robust behavior experimentally. Despite uncontrollable fabrication imperfections in our TMM
experiments, cracks consistently initiate on the right side of the notch in all 40 tested specimens (provided
that no hinges were accidentally broken before testing), as clearly illustrated by the histogram in Fig. 3C.
Thus, TMMs are less sensitive to fabrication errors and provide a more robust approach to crack control.
Moreover, TMM exhibits topologically protected properties that remain invariant of crack shapes. If
the directional asymmetry arises solely from the geometry, the directional cracking can be easily altered.
Fig. 3C and D show two different notch shapes applied to the same specimen: Fig. 3C can preserve
the predicted cracking direction, whereas Fig. 3C does not. In addition, the twisted kagome structure,
though not topological, also features asymmetric geometry. Like the TMM, twisted kagome is blunt

to the right. However, for other crack shapes, twisted kagome sometimes directs the crack to the left
