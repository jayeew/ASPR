Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this interesting work, Li and colleagues present a thorough study of the development and testing of novel inducible,
localizable, bifunctional ecto-enzyme combinations for the degradation of proinflammatory extracellular ATP to
immunosuppressive adenosine. This innovative study further involves the use of an ATP-sensing nanodevice (ND) that
appears to expose the active domains of the ectonucleotidases, ENPP1 and CD73, in the presence of pathophysiologically
relevant ATP concentrations. 
Overall Results: 
Noteworthy, in this work, the authors report on the design, construction, and testing of a unique ND where “ENPP1 and
CD73 were chemically conjugated with two thiolated DNA single 184 strands (SH-ssDNA1 and SH-ssDNA2, respectively;
Supplementary Fig. 5)”. 
By positioning the ectoenzymes ENPP1 and CD73, the device can sense and degrade extracellular ATP into AMP and
ADO, thereby regulating purinergic signaling and switching from pro-inflammatory ATP-mediated responses to
immunosuppressive adenosinergic signaling, which in turn modulates immune homeostasis. 
This ND approach hopefully allows further in vivo localization of this device to sites of experimental inflammation, and does
not perturb, e.g., intracellular purine metabolism, as in the liver. The targeted approach can be further enhanced via
anchoring ND to monocytes to allow inflammation-directed chemotaxis and tissue-targeting ability of these cells to more
actively deliver the payloads. The claim of local modulation of an immune response is tested and examined within models of
cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury (and others) and LPS-induced acute lung injury (Fig. 5). Their applied strategy exhibits
anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects in several acute experimental injury models. 
Critique. 
MAJOR CONCERNS: 
1. The manuscript emphasizes that the ND specifically senses extracellular ATP at pathophysiological concentrations and
responds to these. Please also include data showing the response to structurally related molecules (such as GTP, UTP,
ADP, AMP, and others) to confirm selectivity and specificity, thereby enhancing the rigor of the study. 
2. Are there any relative constraints of the ND construct on ENPP1 activity or that of CD73? Was there uptake by the liver to
any significant extent, as intracellular ATP levels could aberrantly activate ENPP1 or CD73 release and impact energy
charge and modulate AMPK activity? Why was ENPP1 chosen and not CD39 as a nucleoside triphosphate
diphosphohydrolase – see: doi: 10.1152/ajpcell.00430.2020. 
3. In the model of cisplatin-induced kidney injury, more general effects of cisplatin have been shown with extrarenal toxicity
and systemic compromise. Was there both recapitulation and rescue of any of these other defects with the use of the
designed ND? See: doi: 10.1155/2018/1462802. Curiously, other work has shown that monocyte-macrophage targeting and
inhibition have no effects on cisplatin nephrotoxicity. Please address: and see doi: 10.1124/jpet.107.130161. Other renal
injury models are less well described, e.g., ureteric ligation unilaterally with no provided reference. Please add in serum
creatinine values; as well as the urea concentration numbers. 
4. Please provide the required time points and full description of the models of inflammatory diseases for interested
colleagues to be able to recapitulate these findings.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Li et al. describe the development of a sophisticated DNA-origami nanodevice engineered to sense
extracellular ATP and trigger a catalytic cascade that converts ATP into anti-inflammatory adenosine. The device can be
anchored to monocytes for targeted delivery. The conceptual framework is innovative and addresses a significant challenge
in inflammation control. The manuscript is clearly presented and supported by a solid body of experimental data.
Nevertheless, several critical issues regarding the mechanism of action, quantitative validation, and preclinical safety remain
unresolved. I therefore recommend major revision before the work can be considered for publication in Nature

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

Communications. 
Major concerns 
1.The core scientific hypothesis is that the therapeutic effect is driven by ATP-responsive unlocking and catalysis. However,
in the pivotal in vivo animal experiments (Figure 5), the authors failed to include an "ATP-nonresponsive" control group (e.g.,
using the non-binding lock1 sequence mentioned in the manuscript). Without this control, it is impossible to exclude the
possibility that the observed therapeutic effects are derived from the biological effects of the DNA scaffold itself or other
impacts of the monocyte delivery system. Thus, the revision needs to prove that ATP sensing and unlocking are
prerequisites for the therapeutic effect. 
2.While the manuscript emphasizes NF-κB readouts, it does not examine the canonical A2A/A2B receptor-cAMP-p-CREB
anti-inflammatory axis. Although AB928 antagonism provides supportive indirect evidence for receptor involvement, the
signaling investigation remains incomplete without direct biochemical measurements. The reviewer suggests quantifying
ATP/adenosine contents in the local microenvironment of cells or tissue slices by LC/MS, measuring downstream activation
(cAMP levels and p-CREB by Western blot) with or without the nanodevice treatment, and ideally confirming reversibility
with A2A/A2B receptors blockade. 
3.In the functional cell-based assays (Figure 3), the authors used a high ATP concentration of 5 mM for stimulation. This may
far exceed the pathophysiological concentrations reported in most inflammatory injury models (typically in the µM range).
This disconnect raises questions about whether the in vitro conclusions can be directly extrapolated to the in vivo situation. It
is recommended that the authors establish dose-response relationships at more physiologically relevant ATP concentrations
(e.g., 100 - 500 µM) to validate the device's function under realistic pathological conditions. 
4.The ND device is designed to function in the extracellular space via anchoring to monocytes. However, monocytes are
likely to internalize a fraction of the surface-anchored devices. High intracellular ATP concentrations and the acidic
endosomal environment could lead to nonspecific unlocking, which would confound interpretation. The authors might
perform experiments, for example immunofluorescence colocalization assays, to quantify the proportion of surface bound
versus internalized devices and to assess the potential impact of internalization. 
5.In Figure 4d, fluorescence imaging indicates in vivo enrichment of ND@Monos at target sites, but lacks quantitative
characterization of biodistribution dynamics. Please complement imaging with quantitative methods—such as device-
specific DNA barcodes quantified by qPCR or other suitable tracers to profile compartmental distribution across tissues over
time and to estimate relative retention and clearance rates. 
6.Although the manuscript demonstrates biodistribution of the ND@Monos to injured tissues in Figure 4e (mistakenly cited
as 4f in the manuscript text) and Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16 show ND@Monos biodistribution to injured tissues, direct
evidence of in vivo unlocking in complex tissue is still lacking. The reviewer suggests adapting the Fig. 4d FRET-lock
reporter for ex vivo imaging of harvested tissues to visualize the activation state and confirm ATP-specific unlocking in
injured regions compared to other organs. 
7.The omics analysis in Figure 6 is currently correlational. Key findings related to the proposed mechanism (e.g., the NFkb,
or cAMP/CREB pathways) should be validated at the protein level via Western Blot to strengthen the conclusions. 
Minor comments 
1.Although catalytic activity of ENPP1/CD73 pairs is shown, standard enzyme kinetics and a consolidated summary are
missing. The reviewer suggests adding a brief table reporting enzyme copies per device with variance, post-conjugation Km
and Vmax with assay conditions and substrate ranges, and batch-to-batch variability to document reproducibility. 
2.The study does not address the heterogeneity of the device loading process on monocytes. The per-cell distribution of the
nanodevice should be quantified (e.g., via MFI from calibrated flow cytometry) to properly assess the variability of this cell-
based therapeutic. 
3.Although Fig. 4d qualitatively shows ND unlocking on monocyte surfaces in response to ATP, this critical step lacks
quantitative characterization. The reviewer suggests quantifying fluorescence over time to extract activation kinetics (e.g.,
activation t½) and generating a dose–response by repeating the assay across physiologically relevant ATP concentrations
to confirm that the solution-phase dose dependence is preserved at the cell surface. 
4.The concluding paragraphs of the manuscript read more as a summary of the findings than a critical discussion. The
reviewer suggests expanding this section to situate the work within the broader field by comparing it to existing therapeutic
strategies and to address the platform's practical translational challenges. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents an advance in DNA nanotechnology for immunomodulation. The ND@Monos system elegantly
combines sensing, catalysis, and targeted delivery to resolve inflammation with high spatial precision. While scalability and
intracellular stability require optimization, the work establishes a robust strategy for "smart" nanotherapies targeting
dysregulated metabolic signaling. The followings are a few concerns: 
1. High intracellular ATP may prematurely unlock ND after monocyte endocytosis. They should study endosomal escape
mechanisms or dual-lock systems. 
2. ND’s ATP→ADO conversion kinetics vs. endogenous enzymes were unquantified. They may compare 
Kcat/KM values. 
3. ND@Monos retained ~40% surface display after 12 h in serum. Is there a way to improve the stability?
