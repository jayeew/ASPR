Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, the authors described Raichu, a new Hi-C data normalization tool. Comparing to the widely-used matrix
balance-based algorithms ICE and KR-norm, Raichu has the clear advantage of improving the sensitivity of detecting
enhancer-promoter interactions. The authors performed comprehensive benchmark studies and clearly demonstrated the
appealing performance of Raichu in Hi-C, HiChIP and single cell Hi-C data. The results are solid and convincing. The paper
is also well written. The authors also developed a user-friendly software package in GitHub with detailed manual and
sample scripts. Overall, this work addressed a critical problem in Hi-C data analysis, and Raichu has a potential to become
a widely-used tool in 3D genome research. I just have a few minor comments and suggestions which may further improve
the current manuscript. Here are my specific comments. 
Minor comments: 
1. Most of benchmark analyses are based on GM12878 cells. I would suggest to also consider mouse ESC Hi-C data
(PMID: 29053968). It would be very interesting to validate Raichu-specific loops identified from mESC Hi-C data, using
loops from other ligation free technologies such as GAM, SPRITE and super-resolution imaging. 
2. Micro-C and region capture Micro-C (PMID: 32213324, 37157000) provides nucleosome resolution of 3D genome map,
and shows better signal-to-noise ratio compared to traditional Hi-C. Is it possible to apply Raichu to Micro-C and region
capture Micro-C data and further improve sensitivity of calling enhancer-promoter loops? 
3. When applying Raichu to Hi-C data with multiple biological replicates, whether the Raichu normalized data show high
reproducibility between biological replicates? 
4. Fig 5a and Fig S9 show a few examples of Raichu-specific loops. Is it possible to perform CRISPR experiments in the
engineered GM12878 cells, and demonstrate such enhancer-promoter loops are functional?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Wang et al. introduce a novel Hi-C data normalization method called Raichu, which is based on solving an optimization
problem by modeling Hi-C data under specific assumptions. Unlike conventional approaches, Raichu relies solely on the Hi-
C interaction map for normalization and does not require additional sources of information such as mappability, GC content,
or restriction fragment frequency. This design makes Raichu computationally efficient and straightforward to implement. 
The authors first provide a brief overview of their method. Raichu normalizes Hi-C data by fitting the observed Hi-C matrix as
a product of two bias vectors and a scaling function, P(s), which describes the well-characterized dependence of contact
probability on genomic distance—an emergent property of Hi-C data due to the polymer nature of chromatin. The
normalization is then performed using the two derived bias vectors.

To assess the performance of Raichu, the authors conduct extensive benchmarking against classical Hi-C normalization
methods, including Iterative Correction (IC) and Knight-Ruiz (KR). Their evaluation strategy involves normalizing a Hi-C
dataset from GM12878 cells using different methods, identifying enriched interactions (also called loops or dots) with the
HiCCUPS algorithm, and performing statistical analyses on sets of dots unique to Raichu or IC. Additionally, they evaluate
Raichu’s performance under various conditions, such as downsampling Hi-C data and applying the method to capture-
based enrichment techniques, including HiChIP and ChIA-PET. Lastly, the authors demonstrate the applicability of Raichu
for normalizing HiChIP and single-cell Hi-C (scHi-C) data. 
After reviewing the manuscript, I have several concerns regarding the validity and interpretation of the findings. 
1] In the introduction, the authors state that Raichu does not assume equal visibility of genomic regions. However, the
computed weights in Raichu appear to be very similar to those of IC and KR—methods that explicitly assume equal visibility.
Could it be that, while Raichu does not impose this assumption, it nonetheless produces a normalization outcome that
closely aligns with equal visibility? 
To clarify this point, I request the authors to calculate and present the sum of interactions for each genomic region after
Raichu normalization and compare these values to those obtained using IC and KR. Specifically, are the summed
interactions significantly different across genomic loci? If so, which regions deviate the most, and by what magnitude? 
Moreover, given the observation that Raichu’s bias estimates closely resemble those from IC and KR (Figure 1, SFig. 2), it
seems plausible that the deviation from equal visibility is minimal. How do the authors reconcile this observation with their
claim that Raichu does not assume equal visibility? 
2] Raichu’s Hi-C normalization model comprises three key components: the bias for region 1, the bias for region 2, and the
global scaling decay function P(s), where s represents the genomic distance between regions 1 and 2. Consequently,
Raichu inherently assumes that all genomic regions follow the same scaling behavior. In other words, the model presumes
that the looping profile of any given genomic region aligns with the average looping behavior across the genome. However,
certain regions, such as enhancers and Polycomb-bound domains, are known to engage in long-range interactions that
deviate significantly from the global scaling expectation. 
How do the authors justify the assumption of uniform scaling across all genomic regions? Does this assumption hold when
applied to real biological data? 
I encourage the authors to consider prior studies that have demonstrated deviations in scaling for specific genomic contexts,
such as Spracklin et al. (2022, Nat Struct Mol Biol)—which highlights distinct scaling behaviors in the B4 compartment
(Figure 4a, PMID: 36550219)—and Boettiger et al. (2016, Nature), which reports differences in radii of gyration and volumes
between active, inactive, and repressed chromatin domains (PMID: 26760202). 
To further investigate this issue, I suggest analyzing the scaling behavior of (1) different chromatin compartments and (2)
distinct domain types before normalization, after Raichu normalization, and after IC/KR normalization. Which normalization
method best preserves the expected scaling behavior? Furthermore, how do these results align with independent orthogonal
data, such as imaging-based measurements? 
3] As part of its modeling approach, Raichu estimates both the bias factors and the global scaling decay function, P(s).
However, during the normalization step, the scaling decay component is not explicitly utilized—only the bias factors are
applied for normalization. This raises an important question: what is the actual effect of approximating the scaling decay in
the first place? How does the inferred scaling behavior compare before and after Raichu normalization? Furthermore, does
the final scaling plot resemble that produced by IC/KR normalization methods? 
A key concern is whether Raichu-normalized Hi-C matrices adequately preserve the expected scaling properties,
particularly scaling humps that are indicative of cohesin-mediated chromatin organization. To evaluate this, I recommend
analyzing datasets where contact probability exhibits well-characterized behaviors associated with cohesin activity. For
example, previous studies have demonstrated the removal of short-range contact peaks due to cohesin extrusion and
looping dynamics (Rao et al. 2017, Cell, PMID: 28985562), as well as the effects of cohesin loading (Schwarzer et al. 2017,
Nature, PMID: 29094699) and unloading (Hsieh et al. 2022, Nature Genetics, PMID: 36471071). 
Does Raichu normalization allow for the detection of these known changes in scaling behavior? Specifically, can it
accurately capture variations in the position and height of the short-range hump associated with cohesin activity? A
systematic comparison of Raichu-normalized matrices against IC/KR-normalized matrices in these contexts would provide
valuable insight into the impact of Raichu on biologically relevant scaling properties. 
4] While Raichu does not assume equal visibility across genomic regions, does it implicitly assume that all regions follow
the same scaling decay function? If so, is this assumption necessary for the method to function correctly? I encourage the
authors to explicitly address this point in the manuscript and clarify whether Raichu relies on uniform scaling decay across
genomic loci. 
5] The authors benchmark Raichu using several approaches, including: (1) comparing loop (dot) calls from Hi-C processed
with different normalization methods, (2) downsampling Hi-C data, (3) analyzing Hi-C in different experimental conditions,
(4) applying Raichu to capture-based enrichment techniques such as Hi-ChIP and ChIA-PET, (5) applying Raichu to single-
cell Hi-C (scHi-C), and (6) correlating Raichu-normalized Hi-C data with ChIP-Seq profiles of activity-associated factors
such as CTCF and H3K27ac. 
However, benchmarking strategies (1–5) rely on chromosome conformation capture-based techniques, which inherently
share—or may introduce—similar biases to those present in Hi-C, thereby limiting their ability to independently validate
Raichu’s performance. Similarly, benchmarking with ChIP-Seq (6) may be confounded by the fact that these epigenetic
markers are correlated with chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity, key factors that contribute to the unequal
visibility of genomic regions in Hi-C. Consequently, none of these benchmarks provide direct evidence that Raichu more
accurately recovers the true contact probabilities of chromatin interactions.

To strengthen the validation of Raichu, I recommend incorporating benchmarking with orthogonal, non-capture-based
methods such as Oligopaint (Bintu et al., 2018, Science, PMID: 30361340) or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)
(Fudenberg et al., 2017, Nature Methods, PMID: 28604723). 
One potential experimental design for an orthogonal benchmark is as follows: Identify an extended genomic region
containing (i) CTCF-mediated interactions (dots) detected by Raichu, (ii) promoter-enhancer interactions detected by Raichu
but not by IC/KR, and (iii) neutral interactions that serve as a control. Apply Oligopaint or FISH to measure the 3D spatial
proximities of these regions. Specifically, are CTCF-occupied loci found in closer spatial proximity to each other compared
to neutral interactions? Similarly, are promoter-enhancer pairs identified by Raichu in closer spatial proximity than neutral
interactions? 
A benchmark of this kind would provide a more direct evaluation of how well different normalization methods reflect the true
spatial organization of the genome, independent of visibility biases that are inherent to chromosome conformation capture
methods. 
6] In Figure 5f, the authors compare loop (dot) calls between wild-type (C/C) GM12878 cells and a genetically engineered
GM12878 cell line with induced GATA3 activity (A/A). The analysis shows that dots identified exclusively by Raichu in C/C
cells exhibit strong enrichment for GATA3 binding in ChIP-Seq data (~2.2 units of peak height). However, this result is
unexpected, as wild-type C/C cells are presumed to have limited GATA3 activity. Conversely, Raichu-specific dots in A/A
cells—where GATA3 is actively expressed—display only a modest increase in GATA3 binding (~1.3 units of peak height). 
How do the authors explain why the GATA3 ChIP-Seq peak height is significantly higher in wild-type C/C cells compared to
the engineered A/A cells, despite the expectation that GATA3 binding should be stronger in the latter? 
A key concern is that this effect may arise due to the non-specificity of Raichu-identified dots. Prior analyses indicate that
Raichu detects a greater number of dots overall compared to alternative methods. Could it be that Raichu-detected dots tend
to localize within regions of generally open chromatin, rather than marking specific, functionally relevant interactions? 
To address this concern, I encourage the authors to demonstrate that Raichu-identified dots are specific to meaningful
chromatin interactions rather than merely reflecting enrichment in open chromatin regions. A possible approach could
involve analyzing Raichu-identified dots in relation to chromatin accessibility data (e.g., ATAC-Seq or DNase-Seq) and
assessing whether these dots are disproportionately associated with general open chromatin rather than being functionally
linked to GATA3 binding. 
7] Figure 1d presents saddle plots—2D heatmaps depicting the average observed-over-expected Hi-C interaction
frequencies between genomic regions classified by different factor enrichments (e.g., CTCF, Rad21, PolR2A, H3K27ac).
The authors use these plots to highlight the increased contrast in Raichu-normalized interactions, particularly in high-high
enrichment groups (upper-right quadrant). For instance, interactions between high-CTCF regions reach a value of 2.34 after
Raichu normalization, compared to 2.07 with IC normalization. 
However, a closer inspection suggests that the increase in interaction frequencies is not restricted to the upper-right
quadrant. Instead, it appears that interactions across the entire saddle plot are elevated after Raichu normalization. How do
the authors explain this global increase in interactions? 
By design, observed-over-expected normalization adjusts interactions relative to the mean signal, typically yielding some
regions with values above 1 and others below 1. However, in the presented Raichu-normalized saddle plots, nearly all
values appear to be greater than 1. Why is this the case? 
Additionally, it seems that high-CTCF regions in Raichu-normalized data interact more frequently not only with other high-
CTCF regions but also with non-CTCF regions (e.g., upper-left quadrant: 1.63 in Raichu vs. 1.5 in IC). Does this suggest that
Raichu enhances non-specific interactions rather than strengthening specific, functionally relevant contacts? If so, how do
the authors reconcile this with the claim that Raichu improves interaction contrast? 
A clearer explanation of these trends, along with additional validation analyses, would help determine whether Raichu
normalization differentially affects specific versus non-specific interactions and whether these effects align with expected
biological properties. 
Minor Concerns: 
1] At the beginning of the manuscript, the authors state that they “… focus on implicit normalization methods for Hi-C data, as
explicit and hybrid methods require additional external data (e.g., mappability, GC content, fragment length), which limits
their applicability to new genome assemblies where such data may be unavailable.” However, Raichu is tested exclusively
on the well-characterized human genome. To substantiate this claim, I encourage the authors to demonstrate Raichu’s
performance in cases where explicit and hybrid methods are indeed inapplicable. 
Additionally, while the authors argue that new genome assemblies may lack necessary external data for explicit
normalization, most new assemblies include genomic sequences for each Hi-C mappable bin, allowing straightforward
computation of mappability, GC content, and restriction fragment distribution. What specific concerns do the authors have
regarding the reliability of these calculations? How did they conclude that such information would be unavailable or
unreliable for new genome assemblies? 
2] SFig. 11, Virtual 4C Analysis: 
– The Y-axis is truncated, making it impossible to determine the relative peak heights on the opposite side of the functional
interaction. Could the authors adjust the plot to show the full range of values? 
– What bin size was used for Hi-C and Virtual 4C analysis in this figure and throughout the manuscript? 
– What was chosen as the anchor point for Virtual 4C—was it a functional enhancer or a promoter? 
The authors present results for only one viewpoint. However, to confirm the specificity of interactions, it is essential to
demonstrate reciprocal interactions, i.e., that enhancers interact specifically with promoters, and vice versa. Could the
authors provide a complementary viewpoint analysis?

3] Stripe Signal in Raichu-Normalized Maps (Fig. 6g): 
– The authors state that Raichu-normalized maps exhibit fewer stripe signals than raw maps, leading to a slightly higher
enrichment of functional interactions over control interactions (z-scores: 1.73 vs. 1.55), whereas raw maps show
indistinguishable enrichment (z-scores: 2.21 vs. 2.23). However, this conclusion is not supported by any stripe-calling
algorithm and may be an artifact of the averaging procedure. Could the authors confirm this observation at the single-
genomic-region level? 
– While the manuscript discusses the reduction of stripe signals in Raichu-normalized maps compared to raw data, it does
not address why Raichu-normalized maps exhibit more stripes relative to IC-normalized maps. Could the authors clarify this
discrepancy? 
4] Choice of Normalization Method (KR vs. IC): 
– The manuscript discusses KR normalization early on, yet most results focus on IC normalization, with no clear justification
for this choice. What led the authors to prioritize IC over KR? 
– While SFig. 1 and 2 show some degree of similarity between IC and KR results in select examples, there is no systematic
evaluation of their convergence. Given that these normalization methods are not necessarily expected to produce identical
results, could the authors provide a broader comparison of their effects on Raichu’s output? 
5] Computational Performance of Raichu: 
– How does Raichu compare in computational efficiency to IC and KR normalization methods? Is it feasible for large-scale
Hi-C datasets? 
6] Scalability to High-Resolution Hi-C Data: 
– The authors evaluate Raichu at a 10 Kb resolution, which is relatively coarse-grained. However, an increasing number of
Hi-C datasets are being generated at sub-kilobase resolution. Is Raichu computationally scalable to such fine-scale data? If
so, could the authors provide an example of its performance at higher resolution?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study introduces Raichu, a novel normalization method for chromatin interaction data, addressing the limitations of
existing approaches like ICE in detecting transcription-related chromatin loops. While ICE pioneered bias correction in Hi-C
analysis by assuming uniform genomic visibility, Raichu advances the field by dynamically modeling locus-specific biases
and retaining low-frequency regulatory interactions and enables the detection of more loops. The authors validate Raichu’s
superiority across multiple platforms and demonstrate its biological relevance using orthogonal datasets. However, rigorous
validation of its assumptions and broader biological testing are essential to establish it as a community standard. We are
open to revisions of this manuscript, but several major critiques should be addressed before we can recommend it for
publication. 
Major Critiques 
1. The manuscript does not explicitly clarify the biophysical rationale underlying the design of the Raichu algorithm. What is
the justification for the sliding window parameters (e.g., window size, step size) used to calculate interaction frequencies?
Are these choices grounded in physical principles of chromatin folding? Please provide a detailed explanation of how the
Raichu model aligns with known biophysical mechanisms of 3D genome organization. 
2. The methodological robustness of Raichu is hindered by the absence of systematic evaluations for critical parameters,
including sliding window dimensions and bias boundary constraints. To address this, sensitivity analyses—such as
quantifying loop detection consistency across parameter ranges—must be integrated into the framework. Furthermore,
comprehensive benchmarking on alternative chromatin conformation capture platforms (e.g., higher-resolution methods like
Micro-C, its locus-targeted variants like RCMC, and locus-tiling Capture Hi-C) is imperative to establish its generalizability
across diverse experimental paradigms. 
3. A comparative analysis of the scaling relationship for both raw and corrected data should be included to validate whether
the normalization preserves biologically relevant distance-dependent decay patterns (e.g., 1/L scaling) while eliminating
technical artifacts (as shown in PMID: 22941365). 
4. The functional significance of Raichu-specific chromatin loops—particularly those associated with transcriptional
regulation, such as the SUPT16H-enhancer interaction—should be validated to confirm their biological relevance. 
5. While Raichu’s performance has been rigorously benchmarked in GM12878 and K562 cell lines, its generalizability
across diverse biological contexts remains unproven. To establish broader applicability, systematic evaluation should be
expanded to cell types with distinct chromatin organization patterns—such as neurons or developing embryonic cells.
Cross-species validation, leveraging model organisms like mouse cortical neurons where chromatin folding mechanisms
are evolutionarily conserved, would further strengthen claims of methodological universality. 
6. Inconsistency in Raichu specific chromatin loop sizes/distributions in GM12878 data (Figures 2b & 5c). What factors

explain this discrepancy? Please validate whether these differences reflect biological reality or technical artifacts. How can
the biological validity of chromatin loop size differences detected by KR versus ICE normalization methods in Figure 2b and
Supplementary Figure 5b be verified? 
7. The use of ICE and Raichu for HiChIP data normalization raises concerns: ICE assumes genome-wide uniform visibility,
but HiChIP data is inherently targeted and sparse. Recent studies (e.g., PMID: 38213897) emphasize that HiChIP requires
specialized normalization. Why was Raichu chosen over established HiChIP-optimized methods to normalize and analyze
HiChIP data? It seems like the wrong tool for this purpose. We recommend focusing on Hi-C variant methods in which the
matrices can approach full coverage rather than being populated with data centered at select capture viewpoints. 
8. How does bin size influence the detection of insulation boundaries, compartmentalization, and cross-compartment
interactions with Raichu?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have addressed my previous questions regarding the justification of sliding window parameters used to
calculate interaction frequencies by systematically comparing a range of parameter settings and evaluating their impact on
loop detection performance. Additionally, they have extended their analysis to alternative chromatin conformation capture
platforms, including Micro-C, to demonstrate Raichu’s broader applicability. Importantly, they have also incorporated
analyses from additional cell types and cross-species datasets, which helps strengthen the generalizability and robustness
of their approach. These additions are appreciated and resolve the concerns in the previous review. The validation against
orthogonal technologies such as SPRITE, GAM and seqFISH also strengthened the paper. Overall the manuscript is
substantially improved and I am generally supportive of publication of this article as long as the following points can be
addressed. 
1. I appreciate the authors' transparent acknowledgment of their group's current experimental limitations. However, I remain
concerned about the strength of the biological conclusions drawn from Raichu-specific loops. In particular, the orthogonal
validation using ChIA-PET and HiChIP data (Fig. 2g) revealed that nearly half of the Raichu-specific loops lack detectable
binding of key regulatory factors (CTCF, Pol II, H3K27ac, or H3K4me3). This raises questions about the functional relevance
of a substantial portion of the predicted interactions. Although aggregate ChIP-seq signal analyses show overall enrichment
at Raichu-specific loop anchors, the discrepancy between aggregate signal and the absence of binding at many individual
loops warrants a more tempered interpretation of these findings. 
2. In evaluating the impact of normalization parameters—such as sliding window dimensions and bias boundary constraints
—on Raichu’s performance, the authors focus primarily on the number of loops identified under each condition. While loop
count is a downstream outcome, it is not a direct or reliable metric for assessing the quality of matrix normalization. To more
rigorously assess the effects of parameter changes on normalization, I suggest the authors report correlation metrics
between normalized matrices. These provide a direct measure of how consistent and stable the normalization output is
across different parameter settings. 
3. In the newly added Fig. 3d, I noticed that the proportion of transcription-related loops (P-P, E-E, and E-P) is unexpectedly
low—below 3%, which is substantially lower than in comparable datasets. This discrepancy raises questions about the
annotation strategy or the quality of the underlying loop calls. In addition, the current bar chart format may be misleading, as
the combined proportions of loop types appear to exceed 100%, suggesting possible overlaps or non-exclusive
categorization. To more clearly illustrate the composition of loop types, I suggest the authors use a pie chart or a stacked bar
chart with normalized values, and explicitly indicate whether loops can be counted in multiple categories (e.g., a loop
involving two enhancers and a promoter). 
4. The authors mentioned that transcription-related loops tend to span shorter distances than structural CTCF-mediated
loops, which is a well-supported observation in the field. However, in the newly added Fig. 3f using DNA seqFISH+ data, the
spatial distances measured between CTCF-anchored loci appear longer than those observed between enhancer-promoter
pairs. 
5. I remain concerned about the application of Raichu to HiChIP datasets. In particular, the authors apply ICE normalization
for comparison, which is not appropriate for these types of targeted, non-uniform interaction datasets. As clearly illustrated in
Supplementary Fig. 17a, ICE and Raichu introduces artifact signals in regions that lack probe coverage. This issue has
been previously documented before(e.g., PMID: 37157000). Given this, I think the current application of Raichu to HiChIP
are insufficiently supported. It should also be made clear in the manuscript if the ICE and Raichu normalizations were
applied to RCMC data strictly within the region of interest as in Goel et al., or genome-wide, which introduces artifacts. 
6. In Supplementary Fig. 8a, the authors report that Raichu detects nearly twice as many TAD boundaries as ICE. However,
this raises a critical question: are these additional boundaries biologically meaningful or potentially spurious? Without
orthogonal validation (e.g., enrichment for boundary-associated features such as CTCF, cohesin, or sharp insulation drops),
it is difficult to assess the accuracy of these extra boundaries. Moreover, Supplementary Fig. 7d presents an insulation score
comparison only at 25 kb resolution. Given that TAD detection is sensitive to resolution, additional analyses across multiple
resolutions, as well as comparisons of boundary strength, reproducibility, or biological enrichment, would help clarify
whether Raichu’s increase in TAD calls reflects improved sensitivity or over-fragmentation.

(Remarks on code availability) 
My attention was brought to the fact that Raichu loads whole-chromosome data into memory before parallelizing. The
algorithm's performance and memory usage could likely be improved by switching to a piping approach, such as that
implemented in cooler (e.g., cooler._balance.py). Abstracting Raichu as a pipeline that leverages cooler's existing API for
fast, parallelizable contact fetching could be a powerful optimization. Please note, I see this as a direction for future
development, not as a requirement for publication.
