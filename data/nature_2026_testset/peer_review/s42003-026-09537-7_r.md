Dear Reviewers, 
 
We sincerely thank you for your valuable comments, which have helped solidify our findings. We have 
carefully addressed your suggestions and incorporated the new data into the revised manuscript 
entitled “WDR62 is required for proliferation and early differentiation of skeletal myoblasts”. 
 
WDR62 is a centrosome and microtubule -associated protein that plays vital roles in maintaining 
centrosome integrity, as well as spindle assembly and stability during mitosis. In this study, we deleted 
Wdr62 in vivo in both mouse and Drosophila, and in vitro in C2C12 mouse myoblasts. Our findings 
demonstrate that WDR62 is essential for centrosome integrity, which is required for proper myoblast 
proliferation and for preventing premature differentiation during myogenesis. 
 
To assess proliferation, we performed BrdU labelling and trypan blue cell counts, which revealed that 
loss of WDR62 reduces C2C12 myoblast proliferation. Additionally, PAX7 and laminin wholemount 
staining of myofib ers showed no difference in the basal satellite cell population between Wdr62+/+ 
and Wdr62STOP/STOP muscles. We further examined differentiation by performing PAX7 and MYOD 
immuno-staining in untreated muscles and at days 2, 4, and 7 post -cardiotoxin (CTX)  injection; 
devMHC and laminin immuno -staining at day 7 post -CTX; and MYOG and MHC immuno -staining in 
Wdr62 siRNA-transfected C2C12 cells during differentiation. These data indicate that WDR62 
suppresses premature differentiation.  
 
To explore the underlying mechanism, we conducted CEP170 staining in Wdr62 and scrambled siRNA-
transfected C2C12 myoblasts. We observed reduced recruitment of CEP170 to both the centrosome 
and perinuclear region following WDR62 knockdown. Since CEP170 is k nown to anchor and nucleate 
microtubules, we speculate that reduced CEP170 levels may lead to early centrosome reduction, 
thereby promoting premature differentiation.  
 
To accommodate these changes, some of our original figures were rearranged. The updated  figure 
organisation is outlined in the tables appended to our response to the reviewers' comments. The 
results presented in this study provide new insights into the role of WDR62 in myogenesis and should 
be of interest to researchers in the fields of centrosome biology and the molecular genetics of skeletal 
muscle development. 
 
Thank you for reviewing this manuscript.

Reviewers' comments: 
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This is an interesting study presented by an experienced group on a protein WDR62 that associates 
with the centrosome and microtubules. The work is done well although some of the data needs 
refinement to support the claims. Loss of function studies and the use of the distinct animal models 
are well appreciated, however the cellular work and some of the biochemical/molecular analysis 
could be more rigorous in support of the conclusions drawn. 
 
1) The claim that WRD62 impacts muscle differentiation needs to be better documented. Myotube 
formation and fusion index calculations in C2C12 cells would help boost the differentiation claim. 
Response: 
Thank you for this suggestion. We have performed Myogeni n (MYOG) and MHC (all isoform) co -
staining at day 0, 3 and 5 of differentiation in siCon vs siWdr62-treated C2C12 (new Figure 4a) and 
showed a higher percentage of MYOG+MHC+ mononucleated cell in siWdr62 compared to siCon at day 
3 of differentiation (new F igure 4b). In addition, an increasing trend in the percentage of myotubes 
(myotubes with 3 or more nuclei) (new Figure 4b) and fusion index (number of nuclei in these 
myotubes/total nuclei) (new Figure 4c) were observed in siWdr62 at day 3 of differentiation compared 
to siCon. These data suggest that WDR62 deficiency may lead to premature myoblast differentiation. 
We have added a paragraph describing this data in lines 311-323 of the manuscript.  
 
2) Gene expression/RNA seq data would help support the claim s made 
(differentiation/proliferation) 
Response:  
We agree that RNA seq analysis would provide a broader, more comprehensive profile of genes 
involved in differentiation/proliferation. However, due to constraints of limited funding, we were 
unable to perform large data study of this type, requiring repeated analysis at multiple developmental 
time points. As an alternative, we have made an attempt at a more detailed analysis of C2C12 
differentiation/proliferation states. We have performed BrdU labelling experiment (new Figure 5b and 
c) and trypan blue cell count (new Figure 5a) to show that WDR62 loss reduced C2C12 myoblast 
proliferation. We have also performed PAX7 and laminin wholemount staining on myofibers which 
showed no difference in the basal level of satellite cells between Wdr62+/+ and Wdr62STOP/STOP muscles 
(new Figure 2f -h). Furthermore, we have carried out  immuno-staining for  PAX7 and MYOD in 
untreated, day 2, 4 and 7 post-CTX (new Supplementary 3); devMHC and laminin co-staining at day 7 
post-CTX (new Figure 2e);  and MYOG and MHC co -staining in Wdr62 and/or scrambled siRNA 
transfected C2C12 during differentiation  (new Figure 4) . The data obtained indicate that WDR62 
prevents premature differentiation. 
 
3) Additional means to measure impact on proliferation would really support the conclusions. 
Response:

To address this comment, we have performed trypan blue cell count, which showed reduced cell 
proliferation in siWdr62-treated C2C12 compared to siCon-treated (new Figure 5a). We have also 
performed 2h BrdU labelling experiment at 24h after siCon or siWdr62 transfection, which showed 
decreased BrdU+Ki67+ fold change in siWdr62-treated compared to siCon-treated C2C12 (new Figure 
5b and c).  
 
We have added a subsection in the Results, entitled “WDR62 d eficiency leads to reduced myoblast 
proliferation” (lines 346-360) describing the proliferation data in revised Figure 5.  
 
4) Proteomic analysis of WRD62 associations with centrosome/microtubules would also help the 
study. 
Response: 
We have previously per formed a BioID assay coupled with mass spectrometry analysis of WDR62 
interacting partners in AD293 cells which will be published in a separate manuscript and currently 
available as a preprint (Morris et al., 2024, doi: 10.1101/2024.07.01.601630). Our top WDR62 binding 
partners identified were JNK1/2, AURKA, CEP170, MAPKBP1 and BAG2. We have previously shown 
that AURKA and JNK1/2 phosphorylate WDR62, which affect its association and disassociation at the 
spindle poles respectively (Lim et al., 2015, doi: 10.1242/jcs.157537). Recently, WDR62-CEP170-KIF2A 
interaction has been implicated in primary cilia disassembly (Zhang et al., 2019, doi: 10.1038/s41467-
019-10497-2). We have also shown that WDR62 recruits CEP170 and MAPKBP1 to stabilise mitotic 
spindles and maintain centrosome integrity (unpublished data). CEP170 localises at the subdistal 
appendages of the mother centriole (Chong et al., 2020, doi: 10.7554/eLife.53580 ) to anchor 
microtubules (Huang et al., 2017, doi: 10.1038/ncomms15057 ; Barenz et  al., 2018, doi:  
10.1091/mbc.E18-02-0115). Therefore, we have decided to examine CEP170 localisation in siCon vs 
siWdr62 C2C12 at day 0 and 5 of differentiation. We have shown that CEP170 was localised to the 
centrosomes at day 0 and translocated to the pe rinuclear region in MHC + mononucleated siCon cells 
at day 5 of differentiation ( new Figure 7). Upon Wdr62 siRNA knocked down, reduced CEP170 at the 
centrosomes were observed, along with reduced CEP170 translocation to the perinuclear region in 
MHC+ mononucleated siWdr62 cells ( new Figure 7). These preliminary data suggest that WDR62 
recruits CEP170 in both undifferentiated and committed myoblasts, possibly for microtubule 
anchoring and nucleation.   
 
We have added a subsection  that contains the above inform ation in the Results, entitled “WDR62 
recruits CEP170 to the centrosomes and perinuclear region” (lines 386-402). This inserted section also 
describes our rationale for examining CEP170 and the results in Figure 7.     
 
5) A mechanistic insight would also help in terms of how WRD62 impacts these activities. 
Response: 
Based on our results, we propose that the WDR62 loss disrupts centrosome integrity, leading to 
increased centriole numbers and increased centriole cohesion in proliferating myoblasts. WDR62 has  
an established role in mitotic spindle assembly and stability. We hypothesised that the increased

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

centriole numbers would lead to mitotic spindle instability, resulting in mitotic delay. The overall 
outcome is reduced myoblast proliferation.  
WDR62 deficient myoblasts also showed premature differentiation, possibly caused by asymmetrical 
division of myoblasts. WDR62 loss also reduced CEP170 recruitment to the centrosomes at day 0 and 
perinucleus at day 5 of differentiation in vitro. We hypothesised the red uced CEP170 recruitment in 
WDR62 deficient myoblasts accelerated centrosome reduction by decreasing microtubule nucleation 
and promoting centrosomal protein release. The overall outcome is early myoblast differentiation 
initiation.   
Finally, WDR62 recruited CEP170 in differentiating myoblasts most likely to reform the non-centriolar 
microtubule organisation centre (ncMTOC) at the perinuclear region.  
 
We have updated our abstract, introduction, methods, results and discussion to include the above 
information. These new sentences are highlighted in yellow in the tracked version of our revised 
manuscript. For a summary of the revised figures, please see the tables at the end of this document.  
 
Data from above studies would be needed to make this MS publishable in my view.
