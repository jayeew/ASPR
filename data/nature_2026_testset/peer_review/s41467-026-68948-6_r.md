consequences of preterm birth in a mouse model.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors investigate how preterm birth affects cognitive function and visual information processing by
recording neural activity in the V1 and ACC of mice during a Go/No-go task using electrophysiological techniques. They
demonstrate that preterm mice exhibit a higher FA rate in the Go/No-go task, despite showing comparable orientation
selectivity in V1 neurons to term controls. Furthermore, they show that both V1 and ACC neurons in preterm mice respond
differently to visual stimuli during the task. Using optogenetic tagging, they also identify abnormalities in the visual
processing of V1-projecting ACC neurons and ACC-FS neurons. The authors conducted experiments using both male and
female mice, employed appropriate control groups, and used suitable methods and analyses to address the important
question of how preterm birth impacts cognitive function. However, as described more in detail below, there are significant
limitations to the interpretation of the data. Notably, the study lacks an investigation of causality, which diminishes its impact. 
Major comments: 
1) The study does not provide any causal link between neural activity changes of studied circuit (V1 projecting ACC neurons
or FS-ACC neurons) and behavioral changes (increased FA) in pre-term mice. A key hypothesis in this manuscript is that
overactivation of ACC-FS neurons leads to decreased activity of V1-projecting ACC neurons, resulting in reduced V1
activity in No-go trial and an increased FA rate. However, none of these causal relationships have been demonstrated,
leaving the study less mechanistic. Without a demonstration of direct manipulations of neural circuits during behavior
impacting behavior, observed neural changes may not be related to behavioral changes, leaving the study descriptive. 
2) It is unclear if the observed neural and behavioral deficits in preterm mice reflect deficits in learning or/and discrimination.
Throughout the manuscript, these two concepts are not clearly considered separately. Due to the trajectory difference
(especially FA) during training phase, it is highly likely that there is a deficit in learning. Demonstration of neural correlate of
learning trajectory would be informative to enhance the mechanistic significance of this study (especially if the study does
not provide causal link between circuit activity and behavior). 
3) Given that V1 activity is known to be enhanced by locomotion, extent and timing of locomotion on the treadmill during the
task should be analyzed to rule out that neural changes are not confounded by locomotion. 
4) Preterm mice show a lower Hit rate even in the naïve state (Fig 2G). This raises questions about potential motivational
deficits, which can be assessed by different tasks such as progressive ratio test. 
5) While the activity of FS and RS neurons is analyzed separately in Fig 4, the activity of V1 neurons in Fig 3 is not divided
into these categories. It is unclear why FS and RS neurons were not differentiated for V1 activity, particularly as the
distinction between excitatory and inhibitory neurons in V1 could provide critical insights into the observed changes. 
6) Inclusion of “Adolescent-like” in the title misleading. While author cited relevant literatures, they did not examine the
neural activity nor behavior using the same task used in this study. Authors should consider conducting experiments in
adolescent mice to support the claim, or “Adolescent-like” should be removed from the title, result section, and limited to the

discussion section. 
Other comments: 
-In Fig 2C, the Miss marker (water droplet) is present, but the cross marker is missing. 
-How were trained mice defined in Fig 2G? Were only animals that passed the criteria included, or were all animals
analyzed at their final session? If the latter, it would be important to compare only those that passed the criteria to see

-In Fig 3G, V1 activity during FA trials does not appear to increase significantly in the Term group. The sustained activity in

-The authors claim that elevated firing rates in preterm mice are unlikely due to licking during the task (L312), but
Supplementary Fig S3 shows that Preterm mice have significantly more licks per trial than Term mice. Without investigating
the correlation between lick count and sustained activity, this conclusion is premature. Correlating lick count with sustained
activity and demonstrating a lack of correlation would strengthen this argument. 
-Fig 3D shows a distribution of MI values where some V1 neurons exhibit higher MI and others lower MI in Preterm mice. It is
unclear whether the average MI for V1 neurons in Preterm mice is genuinely higher than for neurons in Term mice. Showing
vertical lines indicating group means would clarify this point. 
-Are the data in Fig 4E limited to RS neurons? Clearly separating RS and FS neurons and presenting the data would
improve clarity. 
-Figure 4K is missing. 
-The statement on L427 ("stronger positive modulation by the rewarded cue despite no change in the firing rates") is
confusing. Since MI is calculated using firing rates, the claim of "no change in firing rates" is misleading. While statistical
significance may not have been detected, differences are evident in the traces. 
-The claim in L443 that deficits are "not as severe as in the V1" (Fig 3) is unclear. Based on the MI values, changes in V1-
projecting ACC neurons appear larger. Moreover, throughout the manuscript, the overlap between the bars of MI histograms
for each group makes it difficult to interpret the data. Adjusting the colors or the presentation style of the graphs could
improve readability. 
-The degree of overlap between opto-tagged FS neuron data in Fig 6 and FS neuron data classified by spike width in Fig 4
should be presented in the supplementary figure. If discrepancies exist, these should be discussed. 
-In Fig 7B, the authors claim that environmental enrichment (ENR) accelerates learning in the Term group compared to the
standardly housed (STD) condition. However, the initial performance on Day 0 appears worse, and there seems to be no
difference in final performance or days to reach criterion. 
-Since both male and female data were collected, the authors should explicitly show whether there were any sex differences
in the supplementary figures.

POINT-BY-POINT RESPONSE TO REVIEWS 
Introduction and response to general concerns 
We greatly appreciate the Reviewers’ support, critiques and suggestions, and we think they 
significantly improved the manuscript and the quality of our analyses. Before we address the 
concerns point by point below, we would like to highlight major revisions to our study. Major 
textual revisions are highlighted in light grey throughout the revised manuscript.  
1. We consulted Dr. Xin Cynthia Tong, a mathematician in our Department, who is an 
expert in quantitative approaches. She helped reanalyze all our data, which we hope 
addressed concerns about data analysis. New analysis approaches made some of the 
differences between experimental groups even more pronounced, especially in the cue-
evoked firing and the distribution of selectivity indices. While our findings remain the 
same, i.e. the representation and processing of task cues in preterm mice is altered 
compared to term mice, some of our conclusions are revised considering the new 
analyses.  
2. Revised analysis revealed that the N of units significantly responsive to the non-
rewarded cue when split by behavioral outcomes for optotagging experiments was too 
low to comfortably draw conclusions. We therefore focused the results derived from 
optotagged neurons solely on general cue representation, and we moved that data to 
the supplementary material. Of note is that the data for fast-spiking neurons isolated 
through bulk electrophysiology and the data for optogenetically identified fast-spiking 
neurons overlaps to a large degree (new Figure 4 and Supplementary Figures 9 and 10).  
3. We separated the data for the visual cortex neurons into regular and fast-spiking 
neurons and to our surprise found that both neuronal populations exhibit similar task-
related hyperactivity. We revised our discussion to incorporate these new findings, 
especially since the prefrontal cortex shows a different pattern of activity in RS and FS 
neurons during the task.  
4. To give more credence to our previous suggestion that preterm PFC resembles 
adolescent PFC, we trained adolescent term mice in our task, recorded their prefrontal 
activity and compared the cue representation to that of preterm mice (new Figure 6 and 
Supplementary Figure 11). While our results haven’t fully recapitulated the Nieves and 
Liston preprint we cite in the original submission (likely due to methodology differences), 
we did find remarkable similarities between adolescents and adult term mice in the 
representation and processing of the non-rewarded cue. We discuss these in the revised 
manuscript, and we think that the adolescent data will be a valuable resource to the field.  
5. In the original manuscript, we use the term “Modulation Index” to determine the degree 
of responsivity of neurons to task cues. We revised this term to “Selectivity Index” as our 
literature search found that Modulation Index is typically used to denote firing rate 
alterations by behavioral states, such as attention. To emphasize that we measure 
responsiveness to the sensory cues, we chose to use Selectivity Index instead. While 
this was not an issue brought up by the reviewers, it was brought up by the 
neuroscience community as the submission was accessible as a preprint.
