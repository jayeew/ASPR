Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary: 
This manuscript from Fei Meng et al presents a mechanistic study uncovering a positive feedback loop between UFL1 and
AKT1 in triple-negative breast cancer. The authors show that UFL1 promotes AKT1 activation through UFMylation at lysines
K189, K276, and K297, facilitating AKT1’s ER localization and interaction with upstream kinases PDK1 and mTORC2. In
turn, AKT1 phosphorylates UFL1 at T426, enhancing UFL1 activity and thereby amplifying UFMylation. This regulatory axis
is shown to control lipid metabolism and tumor growth, and the study further demonstrates that pharmacological inhibition of
UFMylation sensitizes cells to AKT inhibition and chemotherapy. These mechanistic findings came from a comprehensive
and rigorous experimental design, in which the authors combined in vitro biochemistry, genetic models and in vivo xenograft
mouse model. Moreover, the identification and functional dissection of UFMylation (3K/R) and phosphorylation (T426) sites
are well executed and supported by multiple lines of evidence. The use of AKT and UFMylation inhibitors, and the
demonstration of synergy, provide a translational angle that strengthens the manuscript. Finally, the emphasis on AKT1-
mediated lipid accumulation connects this signaling axis to a well-established hallmark of cancer. However, several key
points need to be addressed to fully support the proposed model.

Major Points for Revision: 
+ Biochemical Evidence for AKT1 UFMylation in AKT activation: A key mechanistic claim of the manuscript is that
UFMylation of AKT1 at K189, K276, and K297 is required for its phosphorylation at T308 and S473. However, in standard
cellular models (including our own lab’s experience and widely published studies), insulin or IGF-1 stimulation induces
robust AKT1 phosphorylation without any observable upward shift in AKT1 molecular weight on Western blot with the
phospho-AKT1 specific antibodies. This raises an important question: if UFMylation were required for AKT activation, why is
the fully activated AKT with both p-T308 and p-S473 readily observed in the absence of a UFM1-dependent band shift in
these common settings? Please clarify whether the proposed UFL1-AKT1 UFMylation axis is prior to AKT activation and
whether the de-UFMylation of fully activated AKT1 happened. 
+ Breast cancer subtype specificity: While experiments include multiple breast cancer subtypes, the core mechanistic and
therapeutic data focus largely on TNBC cell lines. Some conclusions imply pan-breast cancer relevance, but the
experimental data supporting this are relatively limited. Please clarify whether the UFL1-AKT1 feedback loop and its impact
on lipid metabolism are broadly active across subtypes, or primarily relevant in TNBC. 
+ Clinical relevance and correlation analysis: The manuscript refers to a positive correlation between pUFL1 and pAKT1 in
breast tumor tissues. However, the correlation coefficient (r) and significance (p-value) are not clearly presented in the main
text or figure legends. Please include the quantitative data (r, p-value) and consider moving the correlation plot to a main
figure to better support the clinical relevance of the proposed axis. 
+ Functional role of UFMylation beyond AKT1: The focus is tightly centered on AKT1, but the authors note that other
components of the lipid metabolism and PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway may interact with UFL1. Please comment more fully on
whether UFMylation of other components (e.g., ACLY, SREBP1 regulators) may contribute to the lipid phenotype, or
whether this is uniquely AKT1-driven.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Meng et al reported the critical role of the UFL1-AKT axis in breast cancer progression and drug
resistance. Specifically, the authors found that AKT1 was UFMylated by UFL1, an UFMylation-specific E3 ligase, in breast
cancer cells. AKT1 UFMylation promoted its ER recruitment and activation by both PDK1 and mTORC2, which in turn
enhanced lipid biogenesis and cancer progression. Moreover, the authors showed that AKT1 phosphorylated UFL1 and
boosted UFL1’s activity. Finally, UFL1 expression was elevated in breast cancer samples and associated with poor
prognosis in human patients. 
Targeting both UFMylation and AKT1 had a synergetic effect on TNBC in xenograft models. 
Overall, the finding of the positive feed-back regulation between AKT1 and UFL1 appears to be novel and may represent a
potential target for cancer therapy. However, there are some significant flaws in data analysis and interpretation that should
be addressed. As described below, this reviewer failed to duplicate some of the results as claimed by the authors regarding
the association between UFL1 mRNA level and overall survival of human BC patients. In addition, a few pieces of
experimental data appeared to conflict to each other, and some results were difficult to explain. Finally, the evidence to
support the role of lipid metabolism in tumor progression is relatively insufficient.

Response: We thank the reviewer for these constructive suggestions.  
(1). Based on our findings that UFL1 depletion, UBA5 ablation, and mutation of AKT’s UFMylation 
sites (3K/R) all significantly decreased AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 4A, B; Supplementary Fig. 4A-C 
in the revised manuscript), we concluded that AKT UFMylation occurs prior to AKT phosphorylation.   
(2). We agree that insulin or IGF-1-activated AKT1 displays no changes to its molecular weight, which 
seems to conflict with the fact that UFMylation is required for AKT activation and UFM1’s molecular 
weight is approximately 9 kDa. We hypothesized that AKT phosphorylation promotes its 
deUFMylation. To address this, we performed the following experiments, and these results support 
our prediction from different angles.  
(i). Similar to other post-translational modifications, UFMylation is dynamic and reversible, and this 
feature ensures that UFMylated AKT1 accounts for a tiny portion of total AKT1. Therefore, 
endogenous AKT1 and p-S473 were examined using an anti-AKT1 antibody or an anti-p-S473 antibody 
in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells with or without UFL1 depletion. After a long exposure, 
weak p-S473 and AKT1 bands, whose molecular weights were approximately 27 kDa greater than that 
of their corresponding lower bands due to UFMylation at K189, K276, and K297, were observed in 
shSC cells, and these upper bands nearly disappeared in UFL1 knockdown cells (Supplementary Fig. 
4K in the revised manuscript). Hence, a tiny amount of AKT1 is maintained in a UFMylation state, 
perhaps due to deUFMylation.

(ii). Covalently bound UFM1 is primarily cleaved from substrates by the cysteine protease UFSP2
(Christopher P. Walczak et al., 2019 PNAS. PMID: 30626644; Jessie Kulsuptrakul et al., 2021 Cell 
Rep. PMID: 33730579) (Fig. 1 in this letter). Thus, UFSP2 was depleted by shRNA in MDA-MB-231 
and MDA-MB-468 cells to assess whether inhibited deUFMylation leads to an increase in AKT1 
UFMylation and an upward shift of the p-S473 band. As expected, the intensity of the upper p-S473 
band and the UFMylated AKT1 band was both significantly increased in UFSP2 knockdown cells (Fig. 
4H in the revised manuscript). These findings indicate that AKT1 undergoes deUFMylation. 
(iii). p-T308 and p-S473 result in full AKT1 activation. A plasmid encoding a mutant AKT1 (referred 
to as AKT1-DM), which harbors T308A and S473A mutations, was generated. MDA-MB-231 cells

(2). Synergistic combinations of two or more agents can overcome toxicity and other side effects 
associated with high doses of single drugs by countering biological compensation, allowing reduced 
dosage of each compound or accessing context-specific multitarget mechanisms (Joseph Lehár et al., 
2009 Nat Biotechnol. PMID: 19581876; Naiara Perurena, Lisa Situ, and Karen Cichowski, 2024 Nat 
Rev Cancer. PMID: 38627557). Multiple promising combinatorial strategies have been developed to 
achieve synergistic effects, such as vertical inhibition, disruption of positive feedback loops, blocking 
parallel signaling pathways, and preventing compensatory bypass signaling. ① Vertical inhibition 
consists of inhibiting the main oncogenic driver, which is genetically altered, often mutated or 
amplified, together with an upstream or downstream component of the same oncogenic pathway, to 
achieve a deeper suppression of the pathway (Naiara Perurena, Lisa Situ, and Karen Cichowski, 2024 
Nat Rev Cancer. PMID: 38627557; David Shahbazian, Joshua Sznol, and Harriet M Kluger, 2012 Adv 
Pharmacol. PMID: 22959021). From this perspective, AKT1 is the main oncogenic driver, UFL1 (or 
other components of the UFMylation system) is an upstream component of the AKT 1 pathway; the 
combination of an AKT1 inhibitor and a UBA5 inhibitor achieved a deeper suppression of the AKT1 
pathway (Fig. 7 in the revised manuscript). Thus, the combination of an AKT1 inhibitor and a UBA5 
inhibitor constitutes a vertical inhibition. Similarly, PDK1 phosphorylates AKT1 at T308, and a PDK1 
inhibitor (GSK2334470) and an AKT1 inhibitor (Ipatasertib and MK-2206) achieve a synergistic effect 
(Emily K Slotkin et al., 2019 Cancer Discov. PMID: 30877085; Kristyna Kupcova et al., 2024 Exp 
Hematol Oncol. PMID: 39487517). PRMT5 methylates and subsequently activates AKT1, and a 
PRMT5 inhibitor (GSK3326595) and an AKT inhibitor (MK- 2206) display a synergistic effect on 
promoting cell death ( Shasha Yin et al., 2021 Nat Commun. PMID: 34103528). ② When Protein A 
and Protein B directly or indirectly activate each other (forming a positive feedback loop), their 
inhibitors frequently exhibit strong synergistic effects. This occurs because blocking either protein

summary, the UBA5 inhibitor sensitizes breast cancer cells to the AKT 1 inhibitor, and their 
combination achieves a deeper suppression of AKT1 signaling and breast tumor growth, thereby 
exhibiting a synergistic effect.
