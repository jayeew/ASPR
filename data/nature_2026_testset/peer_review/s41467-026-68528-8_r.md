Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports a novel solid-flame technique for the rapid upcycling of waste carbon fiber-reinforced epoxy
composites (CFRECs). The waste CFRECs were mixed with Mg/CaCO
₃
 powders, and the mixture was electrically ignited in
a vacuum to produce graphene-grafted carbon fibers (GCFs) and graphene powders. Using SEM images, high-resolution
TEM images, and molecular dynamics simulations, the authors demonstrate that the surfaces of all GCFs are grafted with
well-distributed graphene flakes. The authors also provide insight into the mechanism by which these graphene flakes form
and graft onto the carbon fiber surface. 
Additionally, the sustainability of this solid-flame upcycling technique is evaluated. The GCFs have shown to be excellent
reinforcements in graphite materials, indicating significant potential for enhancing various polymeric and metal matrices.
This manuscript is well-written, well-organized, and supported by convincing data. I therefore recommend its publication in
Nature Communications after minor revisions. Several suggestions are listed below:

(b) As shown in Figure 4a, the density of the GCF/graphite composites initially increases and then decreases as the GCF
content increases. The authors should provide a detailed explanation for this phenomenon. 
(c) Can the upcycling process of CFRECs be scaled up, for example, to the kilogram scale?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports a solid-flames upcycling technique for converting carbon fiber (CF) wastes into graphene-grafted
CFs (GCFs) and graphene powders. Although the methodology is well-structured and addresses a sustainability challenge,
the innovation level may fall short of Nature Communications standard due to incremental advancements over existing
thermal/chemical recycling methods. The detailed issues and comments attached as follows: 
1. The as-reported solid-flames technique resembles existing self-propagating high-temperature synthesis (SHS) methods
for carbon materials. A comparison with SHS-based recycling approaches (e.g., flash upcycling of glass fiber composites) is
missing. Clarify how this work uniquely addresses CF waste-specific challenges. 
2. While MD simulations propose a grafting mechanism via CF surface defects, experimental validation at the atomic scale
(e.g., STEM-EELS mapping of C–C bonds at grafting sites) is absent. Without direct evidence, the proposed mechanism
remains speculative. 
3. Graphene powders are only morphologically characterized. Functional properties (electrical conductivity, thermal stability,
defect density) are unmeasured, weakening claims of their "high value" compared to commercial graphene. 
4. GCFs are compared to virgin CFs in graphite composites but not to other advanced reinforcements (e.g., CNTs, plasma-
modified CFs). Without benchmarking against state-of-the-art materials, the claimed "outstanding reinforcement" lacks
context. 
5. GCFs are compared to virgin CFs but not to advanced alternatives (e.g., plasma-modified CFs, carbon nanotubes).
Without benchmarking against cutting-edge materials, the "outstanding reinforcement" claim lacks context. 
(Remarks on code availability)

conductive loss versus polarization loss?

are also possible substances for synthesizing graphene powders in solid-flames. Here, the 
Mg/CaCO3 system is selected for upcycling CF wastes due to low costs of reactants and high 
quality of the as-prepared graphene powders (see ref Mater. Des., 2016, 92, 462 and 
Supplementary Figs. 5-8).

Based on the self-propagating high-temperature synthesis (SHS) of graphene powders 
from low-cost Mg and CaCO3 powders reported in our previous study9, we design a 
methodology to upcycle CF wastes to two value-added products: GCFs and graphene 
powders in solid-flames. Here, solid-flames (the phenomenological description of the as-

solid products even if intermediate products may be gaseous or liquid10.

GCFs (as the yellow arrows point at) due to the difficulties of compressing GCFs under 
sintering. This also indicates that more voids will exist in the GCF/G composite with the 
increased GCF content that cause the decreased density, explaining why the flexural strength 
of the GCF/graphite composite reduces with increased GCF content.

Comment: This manuscript reports a solid-flames upcycling technique for converting carbon 
fiber (CF) wastes into graphene-grafted CFs (GCFs) and graphene powders. Although the 
methodology is well-structured and addresses a sustainability challenge, the innovation level

Response: Thanks for your insightful comment. Our solid-flames upcycling technique 
outperforms existing thermal/chemical recycling methods by some advantages as below. 
In this paper, our solid-flames upcycling technique for CF wastes is based on the self-
propagating combustion of low-cost Mg and CaCO3 powders in a graphite crucible to rapidly 
upcycle the epoxy-containing CF wastes to graphene-grafted CFs and graphene powders. 
Thermal recycling methods for CF wastes rely on pyrolyzing the epoxy resins to molecular 
forms (gas or liquid) in a low (or no) oxygen atmosphere under 400~1000 °C for several hours, 
then removing the residual chars on CF surface by oxidization (Compos. Part B, 2021, 215, 
108768).  
Chemical recycling methods for CF wastes are divided to two categories: destruction of the 
epoxy resins by oxidizing reagents (such as hydrogen peroxide or concentrated nitric acid), 
and catalytic decomposition of the epoxy resins by noble metal catalysts (ruthenium, etc.), 
both aiming to release CFs from the epoxy matrix (Nature, 2023, 617, 730). Moreover, heating 
(60~500 °C) is typically required for chemical recycling methods (Compos. Part B, 2021, 215, 
108768). 
Compared to existing thermal recycling methods, our solid-flames upcycling technique is 
advantageous in these aspects: 
(1) Our technique yields two value-added products: graphene-grafted CFs and graphene 
powders, while thermal recycling methods cause damage to the recycled CFs and generate 
low value-added pyrolytic products. Our graphene-grafted CFs outperform recycled CFs by 
the well-distributed graphene flakes on CF surface that could enhance interfacial bonding in 
composites for effective reinforcement. Our graphene powders are promising candidates for 
battery electrodes, conductive fillers and electromagnetic shielding, while the pyrolytic 
products from thermal recycling methods are used as low value-added fuels (Nat. Energy, 
2025, 10, 226) 
(2) Our technique only lasts for a few seconds during self-propagating combustion (with the 
velocity of combustion wave up to 25 cm s-1, see Mater. Des., 2016, 92, 462) during self-

propagating combustion, while thermal recycling methods generally last for several hours 
during pyrolysis and oxidation. 
(3) Our technique is energy-efficient as it requires no external energy for the exothermic self-

energy from solid-flames could be further harvested for heating supply and subsequent 
energy conversion, while thermal recycling methods are energy-extensive due to lengthy 
pyrolysis and oxidation steps for sustaining high temperatures (400~1000 °C). 
Compared to existing chemical recycling methods, our solid-flames upcycling technique is 
advantageous in these aspects: 
(1) Our technique yields two value-added products: graphene-grafted CFs and graphene 
powders, while chemical recycling methods only yield recycled CFs and soluble polymer

noble metal catalysts (ruthenium, etc.) or oxidizing reagents (such as hydrogen peroxide or 
concentrated nitric acid) that result in toxic waste solvents, hindering the scalability of 
chemical recycling methods. 
In summary, the advantages of our solid-flames upcycling technique, including simplicity, low 
cost, rapid processing, low energy consumption, scalability and commercial attractiveness, 
make our solid-flames upcycling technique outperform the existing thermal/chemical 
recycling methods. We hope these comparisons could promote our innovation level to merit 
the publishing standard for Nature Communications.

3.1 In situ tensile TEM testing 
We greatly appreciate the reviewer's insightful suggestions on employing in situ tensile TEM 
testing to directly observe the load transfer mechanisms at the GCF -graphite interface. This 
would undoubtedly provide a fascinating view of the interfacial dynamics. To this end, w e 
have given this proposal serious consideration and conducted a thorough evaluation o f its 
technical feasibility. Based on our analysis, however, we would like to respectfully outline the 
significant methodological challenges involved: 
(1) To clarify the relation between  interfacial sp3 hybridization and load transfer by in situ 
tensile TEM, it requires employing both STEM-EELS for spectra acquisition and HRTEM for 
observing the sp3C atomic behaviour at the same time. However, this approach presents a 
fundamental technical constraint , that is,  the critical challenge in the mutually exclusive 
optical configurations required for high -resolution imaging and high -resolution electron 
energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) governed by the camera constant ( R = Lλ). Achieving 
atomic-scale spatial resolution requires a large beam convergence angle, while acquiring fine 
C K-edge EELS spectra demands near -parallel beam with a small collection angle to 
minimize energy aberration. Therefore, this testing faces an undesirable trade-off: prioritizing 
temporal resolution for in situ  high-quality imaging by sacrificing the spectral precision 
essential for quantifying sp 3C, while prioritizing precision in carbon chemical states reduces 
the experiment to a series of observations under static positions with the loss of important in

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 49 -->

situ information for mechanistic analysis of sp3C-load transfer relations. 
Moreover, the grafting interface observed in HRTEM image displays no lattice fringes, 
demonstrating that  the grafting interface is amorphous. T o our best knowledge, the most 
advanced TEM technique to directly observe amorphous materials is through a series  of tilt 
imaging in combination with sophisticated algorithm analysis ( Nature, 2021, 592, 60 -64). 
Therefore, it is challenging to simultaneously acquire multiple tilt imaging and dynamic tensile 
observation in the same electron scope to recognize atomic information. 
(2) We also considered that the two potential load transfer mechanisms mentioned by the 
reviewer (dislocation slip and fibre debonding) might not be the most relevant or observable 
phenomena in our specific system through in situ tensile TEM. On one hand, dislocation slip 
is a primary deformation mechanism in metallic materials. In the graphite matrix, tensile 
stress is more likely to cause interlayer separation of graphene sheets before any discernible 
dislocation activity could be initiated and observed. On the other hand, fib re debonding, a 
common failure mechanism in composites, is primarily driven by interfacial shear stres s 
rather than direct tensile stress. 
Given these profound technical and mechanistic constraints, we concluded that performing 
a high-quality in situ tensile TEM test to atomically observe the load transfer behaviour is not 
currently feasible.
