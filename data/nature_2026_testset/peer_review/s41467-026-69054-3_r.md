Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The core objective of this research is to achieve highly efficient oxygen transfer from electrocatalytic water to the inert
benzylic C(sp³)-H bond. The study proposes a strategy utilizing Ni-activated tungsten-oxygen covalent bond (Ni-WOC) to
regulate the interfacial water structure, addressing the challenges of severe OER competition and low Faradaic efficiency
prevalent in conventional systems. Additionally, broader applicability, technoeconomic relevance, and quantitative
characterization of the catalyst structure evolution of this research require further elaboration. The authors must address the
following questions: 
1
、
 The DFT calculation only provides the adsorption energy of water on the W site (-0.921 eV), but fails to quantify the
electronic state changes in the W atom induced by Ni doping, such as the Bader charge transfer on the W atom or the shift of
the d-band center. Consequently, it is impossible to explicitly clarify how the electron transfer regulates the binding energy
between W and oxygen species (*O, *OH). It is suggested that the authors supplement the analysis with Density of States
plots, Differential Charge Density maps, and quantify the electronic interaction between Ni and W. This would elucidate the
electronic essence of "Ni-activated W-O covalency," preventing the mechanism from remaining at the level of macroscopic
phenomena description. 
2
、
 Is there evidence of catalyst degradation or morphology change post-operation (e.g., from XPS/XRD/SEM/ICP-OES)?
Quantitative data would help. 
3
、
 The manuscript pointed out that Ni doping is a key factor in regulating interfacial water structure, but the influence of
electrolyte ion type has yet to be tested. Can Ni doping consistently regulate interfacial water structure across different anion
environments? It is recommended to supplement ATR-SEIRAS data from various anion systems to validate its universality. 
4
、
 The manuscript only tested benzylic compounds and did not extend the study to non-benzylic inert C(sp³)-H bonds (e.g.,
cyclohexane). It is recommended to expand the substrate scope to non-benzylic inert C(sp³)-H bond compounds and
supplement the corresponding FE and yield data. This is crucial for clarifying whether the Ni-WOC selectivity toward
benzylic sites primarily originates from the electronic effect of the benzene ring. 
5
、
 In the comparative analysis of the catalyst's performance, the number of reference samples selected is insufficient. This
may result in an inadequate scope for comparison, making it difficult to fully support the objectivity and universality of the
research conclusions. It is recommended to appropriately expand the number of comparative references, prioritizing high-
impact and representative research achievements from the past five years within this field for comparison, to enhance the
study's persuasiveness and depth. 
6
、
 The current analysis of catalyst performance lacks key characterization that directly reveals the mechanistic role of the
Ni element in active site formation. It is recommended to supplement the study with in situ Raman spectroscopy
measurements of Ni-WOC and WOC. This will explicitly clarify the regulatory role of Ni introduction on the catalyst's surface
electronic structure, active species formation, and reaction pathway, thereby providing direct spectroscopic evidence. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript reports an oxygen transfer process boosted by operando evolved “Ni-activated tungsten-oxygen covalency”.
This work achieves an impressive Faradaic efficiency up to 56% along with near-unity regioselectivity for the oxygenation of
benzylic C(sp3)–H bonds under the additive-free and water-containing conditions. I appreciate the authors’ comprehensive
investigation into the catalyst structure evolution, water structure regulation, and the entire oxygen transfer path using

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

extensive in-situ and ex-situ spectroscopic techniques. The authors have clarified the whole oxygen transfer process with
several isotope-labeling and parallel in-situ spectroscopic experiments, providing valuable insights for further related works.
Thus, I would like to recommend this interesting work for publication at Nature Communications after addressing the
following concerns. 
1. Though the depressed OER performance is manifested by the increased Faradaic efficiency of the C–H oxygenation
process, a direct quantification of oxygen gas evolution is still necessary to validate this result. 
2. The XPS data in Figure 3d-f are crucial for tracking the catalyst reconstruction. For clarity, it would be beneficial to
explicitly state the atomic ratios (e.g., W6+/W0, Ni2+/Ni0, lattice/adsorbed oxygen) in the main text or a supplementary table
at each time point to quantitatively support the description of the oxygen migration process. 
3. The electrochemical surface area (ECSA) is important for ruling out morphological contributions to the enhanced catalytic
performance. The authors should provide full experimental details of the ECSA measurements, including the electrolyte
composition and potential scan range. 
4. The assignment of the active oxygen species as adsorbed oxygen (*O) is supported by both experimental and theoretical
evidence. This finding aligns with several recent reports (e.g., J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2025, 147, 26254–26266; Nat. Rev. Chem.
2024, 8, 277–293), and citing these works would help strengthen the mechanistic claims. 
5. The absence of deuterated products in D2O isotope-labeling experiments suggests that *OH or *OOH species are not
directly involved in oxygen transfer. While this may hold for alcohol formation, the pathway to acetophenone could involve
dehydration of peroxidic intermediates. Though, as a minor product, the ketone formation pathway is still important for
strengthening the mechanistic discussion. 
6. A brief discussion about the observed diversity in yields or FEs in the substrate scope study is necessary. 
And some minor editorial suggestions: 
1. The inset of Figure 2b is mentioned but not clearly labeled in the caption. Please specify what the inset shows. 
2. Please ensure all abbreviations are defined upon first use (e.g., DRT is defined in the caption of Figure 4 but should also
be defined when first mentioned in the main text). 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors report a Ni-WOC catalyst generated by the in-situ electrochemical oxidation of a Ni–W
₂
C pre-
catalyst, which exhibits high efficiency for the oxidation of benzylic C(sp³)–H bonds into the corresponding alcohols and
ketones. Based on combined experimental and theoretical results, the authors propose that Ni doping significantly weakens
the interfacial hydrogen-bond network of water, facilitates water adsorption and dissociation on W sites adjacent to Ni to form
active *O species, and thus suppresses the OER pathway while promoting C–H bond oxidation of the organic substrate. The
discovery that Ni doping modulates the interfacial water structure and reaction pathway is indeed interesting and potentially
important. However, several key mechanistic aspects remain insufficiently clarified and merit further explanation. 
1. The manuscript does not provide sufficient structural evidence for the Ni-WOC catalyst. How is the coordination
environment of Ni and W confirmed? Is Ni atomically dispersed within the WOC structure? Without clear structural
information, the theoretical models adopted in this work lack credibility, and consequently, the derived mechanistic
conclusions are questionable. Admittedly, obtaining precise structural information for the Ni-WOC catalyst is extremely
challenging, as it is in-situ generated during electrolysis and is amorphous in nature. Nevertheless, at least indirect structural
evidence (e.g., XAS, EXAFS, or HAADF-STEM) is expected to support the proposed Ni–O–W configuration. 
2. The manuscript lacks a clear explanation of the design logic behind the Ni-WOC system. Why was Ni-doped W
₂
C chosen
as the catalyst precursor? What is the rationale for combining Ni and W? Why not directly prepare a Ni-doped metal oxide
catalyst instead of starting from a carbide precursor? A discussion of the design concept and motivation would significantly
strengthen the work. 
3. Does the oxidation state of Ni in Ni-WOC change after catalysis? The manuscript does not present post-reaction or
recycled catalyst characterizations. Could Ni be oxidized to Ni³
⁺
 during operation? Such information is crucial to
understanding the stability and real active state of the catalyst. 
4. The results suggest that the truly active site is the W center adjacent to Ni. Please provide a molecular-level explanation
of how Ni modulates the Ni–O–W interface. Could other transition metals play a similar role? A comparative discussion
would make the mechanistic proposal more convincing. 
5. The proposed oxidation pathway of ethylbenzene is not clearly explained. According to the current DFT results, the
substrate is adsorbed at the W site adjacent to Ni, loses an electron, undergoes benzylic C–H bond homolysis to form *C
₈
H
₉
,
which is then oxidized by *O to *C
₈
H
₉
O, and finally gains a hydrogen to form the alcohol product. 
My question is: Where does this final hydrogen come from? If it originates from solvent water, why were no D
₂
O-labeled
products detected in isotope-labeling experiments? If not from water, what is the hydrogen source? Please provide evidence
or additional discussion. 
6. The oxidation process involves multiple steps, including water oxidation to *O, ethylbenzene oxidation to *C
₈
H
₉
O, and
further oxidation of *C
₈
H
₉
O by *O. Which step is rate-determining? What are the kinetic isotope effect (KIE) values for these
steps? Kinetic analysis is essential to support the proposed mechanism. 
7. The regioselectivity mentioned in the manuscript is not appropriate. The preferential oxidation at the benzylic C–H
position results from its inherently higher reactivity, rather than a catalyst-induced regioselective effect. This term should be
revised or clarified. 
8. The reaction yields both alcohol and ketone products, and the ketone is likely derived from further oxidation of the alcohol.
Since two products are obtained simultaneously rather than one dominant product, the description of a “selective
oxygenation process” is not accurate. 
9. In the conclusion, the statement “Ni heteroatoms disrupt the water structure … facilitating the rapid diffusion of oxygen
species to the W–O covalency sites of Ni-WOC” is unclear. What exactly does “oxygen species” refer to — water molecules,

*O, or other intermediates? This should be explicitly defined. 
Due to the above unresolved and critical issues, I do not recommend publication of the manuscript in its current form.
However, if the authors can adequately address these points with convincing structural, mechanistic, and kinetic evidence, I
would recommend resubmission to Nature Communications after major revision.
