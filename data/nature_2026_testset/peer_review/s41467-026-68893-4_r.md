Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Harris and Gong analyzed high-density electrophysiological recordings from the mouse visual cortex.
They proposed that, during stimulation, a cross-scale oscillatory dynamic emerges, involving nested theta-gamma waves
that are hierarchically organized and run alongside spiking activity. After disentangling the Local Field Potential (LFP) signal
into theta, gamma, and the 1/f components, they first show that there are two distinct theta oscillation components: one that is
offset-related, propagating from layer 2/3 to deeper layers, and another that is onset-related, reflecting feedback signaling.
The offset-related theta has a high amplitude when the animal is disengaged from the task, whereas the onset-related theta
appears only during active tasks. This task-evoked theta exhibits hierarchical organization and produces spatiotemporal
dynamics compatible with previously described traveling waves. Gamma oscillations are organized in packets nested within
theta, structured across both cortical areas and layers. This nested activity correlates with spiking activity during stimulation,
suggesting a flexible, spatiotemporal mechanism for information multiplexing and modulation. 
The manuscript analyzes a high-quality dataset and presents intriguing results and ideas. It provides a detailed description
of theta dynamics across the mouse visual cortex, which aligns well with recent views on theta and gamma oscillation
dynamics. However, it lacks a mechanistic characterization of how these nested oscillations contribute to information
processing beyond their alignment with spiking activity. This limitation weakens the manuscript's central message. The
following suggestions aim to strengthen the manuscript’s impact: 
The primary concern is that, while the authors demonstrate significant LFP-spike coupling at both frequencies of interest, it
remains unclear whether this coupling influences perceptual information processing or transmission. For example, the
authors correctly perform a PPC analysis on neighboring LFP channels relative to the spike cluster of interest. However, the
functional relevance of this coupling remains uncertain. Are spike-field locked clusters functionally meaningful? Can
perceptual information be accurately decoded from spike-LFP coupling? Does this coupling facilitate information transfer
across different cortical areas? To enhance the manuscript’s claims, the authors could quantify a potential causal
relationship between LFP oscillations and spiking activity. This analysis could complement their results in Figure 3F on
hit/miss decoding of theta waves and offer a more detailed characterization of the putative causality underlying their findings.
A second concern is that, while the manuscript focuses on nested theta/gamma oscillations, certain additional analyses feel
somewhat disconnected from the main narrative. For example, the decomposition of the LFP signal into its oscillatory and 1/f
components is highly interesting, yet it is unclear whether the 1/f component plays any functional role beyond its description.
The authors could strengthen their hypothesis by contrasting the functional relevance of theta/gamma nested activity against
1/f activity. If functional differences between these components can be identified, this would further support the manuscript’s
key claims. 
A third concern relates to the origin and dynamics of theta activity. The supplementary video suggests that the traveling
offset-related theta wave may have a subcortical (hippocampal?) origin. If so, some of the observed theta activity may not be
intrinsic to the visual cortex but instead volume-conducted from subcortical regions. The authors should clarify whether this
theta activity truly propagates within the cortex or is merely an effect of volume conduction. Although completely ruling out
volume conduction is difficult, the authors could quantify phase differences across cortical areas to check for zero-phase
relationships, which would support the hypothesis of volume conduction. 
Finally, while the discussion section is well-informed, it could reach a broader audience by providing a more comprehensive
conceptual framework for the manuscript’s findings. For example, the authors correctly cite Lisman’s work on nested
theta/gamma oscillations, but they should elaborate on the current state of this theoretical framework. What recent findings

support or challenge this perspective? Additionally, the discussion should address well-known limitations of cross-frequency
coupling analyses and clarify how this study mitigates these limitations.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper analyzes electrophysiological data from mouse visual cortex during a visual task, with or without engaging
attention. The authors show theta-band traveling waves propagating at different scales between regions and cortical layers.
They also relate the wave's phase to the gamma-band activity and spikes. 
Overall, the paper is methodologically sound, and the results are interesting. I'm not entirely sure about the novelty regarding
the previous literature about gamma-theta coupling, but the specificity may apply to the specific animal model used. 
Below are some points that may need further clarification for a broad audience. 
How does the intra laminar theta relate to between areas theta? More precisely, I don't understand the assumption that is
used to calculate the order parameter, i.e., that the average translaminar theta propagation in one region informs about the
theta propagation between areas. 
In figure 1A, the relationship between packets I and II, and between packets III and IV is unclear. Clearly, they are all
modulated by the slow theta wave, but it's unclear how they are related to each other. Doed packet I (III) cause packet II (IV)
? Is the fact that II and IV are enhanced and inhibited unrelated to the presence of packets I and III? 
The residual power in Figure 2F seems similar across cortical regions (except for VISam). Isn't this the opposite of what one
would expect considering the proposed feedback/top-down role? 
It is unclear how the anatomical hierarchy used to obtain the correlations is defined, and how the results can be interpreted
(figure 2E and 2H) other than showing a (well-known) dynamical cortical gradient. 
From an interpretational point of view, what does it mean that theta propagates from deeper to superficial layers? Shouldn't
one expect the opposite, considering that the (theta activity in the) deeper layer encodes expectations rather than sensory
information? Would this reflect mostly top-down modulation rather than sensory processing? Could the author decode some
stimulus information from the theta activity and its propagation? 
I don't think there is evidence to support the authors' proposal (line 262) that theta provides feedforward processing:
shouldn't one observe a forward pass also at the onset of the stimulus, then? 
From the power spectra, they analyze broadband gamma (30-100 in equation 5). Would the authors imply this is a fairly
large broadband effect (possibly present in high-beta as well?), or would it be possible to localize a narrow band gamma
frequency? 
From the method, it is unclear why the instantaneous angular wave number (i.e. the spatial frequency) is the negative spatial
phase gradient (a mathematical or even an intuitive explanation would very much help). It's also unclear why this value
would quantify the directionality of the wave. 
It is also unclear why the 'periods of negative frequency -defined as the derivative of the phase- were rare'. What does this
imply? That the directionality was mostly consistent across trials/electrodes? Can the author quantify and report how many
trials were masked (as specified in lines 568-569)? 
Overall, it's unclear the relationship between translaminar theta and between regions. The results are extremely similar, as
pointed out by the authors (lines 291 onward). Since the laminar direction is roughly orthogonal to the cortical sheet, one
may wonder how and why the two results relate so strikingly. 
How do the authors interpret the findings that spikes are aligned to the trough and peaks of theta at different cortical layers?
This speaks against the oscillatory voltage modulation (i.e. neurons are more likely to fire at the peak of an oscillation), and
it's also difficult (for me) to interpret if we consider theta as a translaminar traveling wave: depending on the wave's speed,
spikes won't be synchronized by the traveling wave.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Harris and Gong have performed an interesting and novel characterisation of oscillatory activity in primary and higher visual
areas of the mouse cortex by analysing data collected by the Allen Institute. The authors provide novel insights into
integration of visual information along cortical depths and across visual areas. It is proposed that theta and gamma
oscillation carry feedback and feedforward related information at different temporal epochs. Specifically, they identify short
gamma bursts that occur based on feedforward processing, which in turn multiplex with travelling theta waves in a feedback
direction after the onset of the stimulus in a change detection task. 
Overall, the analyses are very interesting and bring new perspectives to the field. However, there are many concerns with
the interpretation of the results in terms of feedback and feedforward signals both across depth and across areas. One
additional aspect that is really interesting, but doesn’t seem to be the focus of the study is the difference between task related
activity and non-task related flashes. While we are enthusiastic regarding the potential of this work, there remain many
concerns to be resolved.

Major Concerns 
1. Theta is defined to be between 3-10Hz. This is a relatively wide range of low frequencies which happen to include both 3-
5Hz rhythms and theta (6-9Hz). These 3-5Hz rhythms, which have been likened to human alpha (see Senzai et al. 2019 and
Nestvogel and McCormick 2022), are a dominant feature of mouse visual cortex and should be considered when performing
these analyses. We suggest the authors reanalyse the data as they have done throughout figures 1-3, but only considering a
narrower theta rhythm (6-10Hz, for instance), which also matches their findings from the Phase-Amplitude Coupling
presented in figures 5 and S5. In addition, it would be most relevant, also based on the results from Figure S5, for the
authors to perform a similar analysis on these lower frequency 3-5Hz rhythms. 
2. The key result of the manuscript is that activity is feedback dominated right after stimulus onset, and feedforward right after
stimulus offset. This result seems counter-intuitive and potentially in conflict with previous studies that characterise this
(Samedo et al, Nat. Comm., 2022 and others). Below are a few thoughts regarding this. 
a. Why counter-intuitive: When a visual stimulus turns on, information regarding it needs to move forward through the
hierarchy, so different parts of the system get the information. Therefore, the finding that information is feedback after
stimulus onset is counter-intuitive. 
b. Is the result above only related to theta waves, and instead spiking activity is feedforward as previously observed (e.g.
Samedo et al, 2022)? It would be useful to understand this as it would increase the potential impact of the study. 
c. If it is all related to the task and different in the task unrelated viewing condition, it would be good to highlight these not just
at the start, but across the various results of the manuscript. 
3. The general assumption across the results is that deep to superficial direction is feedback and vice versa. However, L4 /
L6 are where thalamic inputs are coming into visual areas (particularly VISp and VISl) and feedforward is presumably
seeded from here. How do the results of the manuscript compare with this anatomical connectivity? Perhaps a more general
and true statement would be to call it deep to superficial, rather than calling it feedback in the results. Of course, this can be
discussed as potentially feedback in the discussion part of the manuscript. 
4. Is this a very specific task related feature that is being discovered? 
a. Because it is a change detection task, it would be useful to look at stimulus events, repetition events, and those that the
change occurred and show the results separately. 
b. Is the feedback response after stimulus onset actually related to information from the previous stimulus – given it is a
change detection task? Some of the Allen change detection tasks included omission trials. It would be really interesting to
characterise those trials. Presumably, the feedback signals will persist at the time of expected stimulus, and the feedforward
would be absent. This could be a very interesting and significant result. 
c. Related to the above, it has been suggested (Jordan and Keller, Neuron 2020) that feedback signals are actually arising
in L2/3. Again this is contrary to the terminology loosely used in the manuscript. 
5. Is the offset result particular to the timing of the stimulus? Is it a delayed response to stimulus onset? For example, if a
longer stimulus is tested (presumably there are different stimulus lengths in the dataset), is the feedback dominant period
locked to a fixed time after stimulus onset or locked to stimulus offset? This would be important to check. 
6. For analysing the directionality between areas, the analyses are performed across the same cortical depth between areas.
Such an analysis would be appropriate if the connectivity between areas is ordered in the similar way, where L4 is
communicating to L4, L5 to L5 and so on. However, the actual biological connections are often interlaminar. Is the analysis
then valid? 
a. Why would top down/feedback information be processed in L6? If anything, L2-3 would be more likely to integrate
feedback information from higher areas? 
7. Would be good to identify layers based on CSD. All the inferences are based on deep to superficial being L5 to L2/3 and it
would be important to ensure L4 and L5 are not mislabelled. While the new methods do appear to be more reliable, it would
give confidence to readers more familiar with CSD to have those methods to confirm the identification of layers. 
8. In terms of explaining the results a few descriptive illustrations would really help. The first part of figure 1 was very useful
in explaining the concepts for the reader to follow the first few figures. Similar to this it would be beneficial to have: 
a. a figure describing the inter-area analysis. 
b. a summary figure to explain all the results and their temporal order.

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors have analyzed the publicly available data (Allen Institute) to examine the spatiotemporal
dynamics of LFP across cortical layers and visual cortical areas, while head-fixed mice were presented with flash, gray
screen, and natural images (change-detection tasks). They have investigated the “traveling wave” property of theta (3-10 Hz)
oscillations across cortical layers in different cortical areas, and some properties are correlated with the hierarchy scores of
visual cortical areas. They have also detected gamma (30-100Hz) oscillation packets and how these are coordinated with 3-
10 Hz oscillations in different cortical layers in different visual cortical areas. Overall, these analyses are great additions to
the community's knowledge. 
In the meantime, I have a few concerns related to the interpretation of the data. 
(1) The authors interpret the theta wave as signaling the feedback inputs, citing stronger theta power in deeper layers that
are relevant to the feedback connectivity. However, this analysis is mostly based on the non-task-engaging experiments
(flash screen and grey screen experiments, Fig2 and FigS1), not so much on the task-engaging experiments (natural image,
FigS2). Thus, “feedback” interpretation during non-task-engaging experiments could not be a straightforward interpretation.
Also, although the theta power is stronger in deep layers, time scale hierarchy correlations are stronger in superficial layers
(Fig2H, FigS1H, FigS2H). How would this align with the authors’ interpretation of theta as “feedback”? 
(2) The authors interpret the gamma packet as signaling the feedforward inputs, citing stronger gamma power in the
superficial layers is relevant to the feedforward connectivity. However, these analyses are again mostly based on the non-
task-engaging experiments. Indeed, in the natural image task experiments, the gamma-hierachy score correlations are high
in deep layers. Thus, further discussion on how gamma could correspond to the feedforward processing would be
preferable.

I also have a few minor technical comments/questions.

(4) Related to (3), are they really the translaminar traveling wave? Perhaps just interpreting the LFP fluctuations made by

1. Reviewer #1
R1.1: In this manuscript, Harris and Gong analyzed high-density electrophysiological recordings from the mouse
visual cortex. They proposed that, during stimulation, a cross-scale oscillatory dynamic emerges, involving
nested theta-gamma waves that are hierarchically organized and run alongside spiking activity. After
disentangling the Local Field Potential (LFP) signal into theta, gamma, and the 1/f components, they ﬁrst
show that there are two distinct theta oscillation components: one that is offset-related, propagating from
layer 2/3 to deeper layers, and another that is onset-related, reﬂecting feedback signaling. The offset-related
theta has a high amplitude when the animal is disengaged from the task, whereas the onset-related theta
appears only during active tasks. This task-evoked theta exhibits hierarchical organization and produces
spatiotemporal dynamics compatible with previously described traveling waves. Gamma oscillations are
organized in packets nested within theta, structured across both cortical areas and layers. This nested activity
correlates with spiking activity during stimulation, suggesting a ﬂexible, spatiotemporal mechanism for
information multiplexing and modulation. The manuscript analyzes a high-quality dataset and presents
intriguing results and ideas. It provides a detailed description of theta dynamics across the mouse visual
cortex, which aligns well with recent views on theta and gamma oscillation dynamics. However, it lacks a
mechanistic characterization of how these nested oscillations contribute to information processing beyond
their alignment with spiking activity. This limitation weakens the manuscript’s central message.
A1.1: We appreciate the reviewer’s positive assessment of our ﬁndings and constructive comments regarding the
mechanistic interpretation of our ﬁndings. As suggested by the reviewer, we have now updated our manuscript to
include a more detailed interpretation of the role of θ waves in visual processing, backed by new results showing
task-related modulation of neuronal spiking related to propagating θ. We introduce each of these changes in
detail in the following response points.
R1.2: The following suggestions aim to strengthen the manuscript’s impact: The primary concern is that, while
the authors demonstrate signiﬁcant LFP-spike coupling at both frequencies of interest, it remains unclear
whether this coupling inﬂuences perceptual information processing or transmission. For example, the
authors correctly perform a PPC analysis on neighboring LFP channels relative to the spike cluster of
interest. However, the functional relevance of this coupling remains uncertain. Are spike-ﬁeld locked clusters
functionally meaningful? Can perceptual information be accurately decoded from spike-LFP coupling? Does
this coupling facilitate information transfer across different cortical areas? T o enhance the manuscript’s
claims, the authors could quantify a potential causal relationship between LFP oscillations and spiking
activity. This analysis could complement their results in Figure 3F on hit/miss decoding of theta waves and
offer a more detailed characterization of the putative causality underlying their ﬁndings.
A1.2: As suggested by the reviewer, we have now performed additional analysis to show that the interplay between θ
LFP and neuronal spiking is relevant to task performance. We found that both preferred θ phases and neuronal

but most importantly, the wavenumber provides a single-trial measurement of θ propagation that cannot be
captured by the average CSD. By computing the wavenumber at each point in time and space, for each trial,
then taking the median of this statistic across trials, we are measuring the average propagation of the θ signal.
By contrast, conventional single-trial CSD has a poor signal-to-noise ratio due to the noise-boosting effect of the
second derivative. Extracting meaningful information from the CSD requires averaging across trials, and any
traveling patterns inferred from the average CSD would not necessarily reﬂect single-trial propagation. Thus the
trial-averaged CSD cannot disambiguate true propagations that occur in a single trial from spurious propagations
in the average signal—for example, localized activity that occurs at different time points and layers for different
trials may appear as a propagating component in the average signal.

during the task stimulus in Fig. S8. Compared to the wavenumber description, this conventional CSD analysis
captures layer-speciﬁc patterns of activity, but is not sensitive to the propagation of θ across layers.
