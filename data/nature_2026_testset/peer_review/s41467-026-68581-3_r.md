Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper develops a new “operationally classical” framework for modeling quantum state sets, based on the idea that each
classical state-preparation device can only output commuting states in some basis (with the basis allowed to differ across
devices), while a random variable stochastically chooses which device to use. This extends the standard commutation-
based definition of classicality and allows for classical simulation of many quantum sets that do not commute. The authors
present several main results: (i) Exact thresholds for isotropic noise above which the entire d-dimensional quantum state
space becomes classically simulable, showing that the critical visibility decreases with dimension and scales as 1−log(d)/d
for large d; (ii) Analytical bounds for simulating noisy sets of pure states and sets built from multiple orthonormal bases, as
well as a measure of “simulation complexity” based on the subspace dimension used by each device; (iii) Numerical
methods using convex optimization to find classical models for arbitrary noisy sets, outperforming the analytical bounds in
many examples; (iv) Experimental witnesses—linear inequalities—capable of certifying that no classical model exists; and
(v) Foundational connections showing that the framework implies a form of joint measurability, and that qubit set classicality
is equivalent to certain Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen steering conditions. 
Overall, the work maps the boundary between classical and quantum state-sets in a basis-independent, operational manner,
offers tools for both constructing and ruling out classical simulations, and highlights how the power of classical models
declines with dimension. The paper is well written, and the results are presented with sufficient clarity to follow the
arguments. The work is original, and the proofs are rigorous and technically sound. I really enjoyed reading the paper. 
While I find the topic interesting and worthy of publication in some form, I am not convinced about the general significance of
the results in the limit of large dimensions. As the authors themselves show, increasing the system size makes the vast
majority of states fail to be classically simulable—a conclusion that might be anticipated given that the classically simulable
sets are parametrized by fewer variables than the full Hilbert space, bearing similarities to entanglement vs. separability. At
the same time, current experiments routinely demonstrate coherent control over hundreds of qubits or qudits with large d,
already operating in a regime where, under the paper’s definition, almost all states would not be classically simulable. This
raises the question of whether the main results retain their practical significance in such regimes. The authors remark in the
discussion that “This shows that the advantages of high dimensionality have the potential to become very significant.” My
concrete question, then, is whether and in what way the methods developed here could help to harness this potential.

Reviewer 1 
The manuscript presents technically correct results and introduces a new definition of classicality. 
Nonetheless, we find physical justification for adopting this particular definition missing. While the work 
has merit and could warrant publication, significant conceptual clarifications are needed. Our specific 
comments are: 
We thank the Reviewer for their reading of our manuscript. Below, we reply to their comments point by point.  
 The authors claim that the set S of classical models is convex: if ρx in S and σx in S, then pρx + (1-
p)σx  S for any p  [0, 1]. This indeed follows. However, does the convex hull of a classical set 
necessarily remain classical? If yes, a proof should be provided; if not, a counterexample is needed. 
In the resubmission, we clarify that the convex hull of the classical set is identical to the classical 
set, i.e. the set obtained from {p \rho_x + (1-p) \sigma_y}_{x,y} is classical for any {\rho_x}_x in S 
and {\sigma_y}_y in S. The proof is given in a newly added section of supplementary material. 
 The manuscript does not convincingly argue why the proposed construction should be regarded as 
a definition of classicality. A natural operational interpretation arises in the PAM scenario: λ acts 
as a shared random variable; Alice, given x and λ, prepares a diagonal state labeled by z, sends z 
via a d-level classical system, and Bob reconstructs the state. On average, this yields ρx. This 
reasoning would justify the definition, yet the authors seem not to adopt it.  
For instance, the set 0 , + can be classically prepared by Alice sending one classical bit, yet this set 
is not classical by their definition (or since the states do not commute). Moreover, it never 
generates nonclassical PAM statistics. Thus, their definition diverges from the operational notion of 
classicality. We urge the authors to clarify what operational principle their framework captures. 
Indeed, our definition was inspired by the PAM scenario and we agree with the referee that its 
operational relevance is strengthened by making that connection explicit. In the revision, we 
have added a new subsection (IIB + new figure) in which we discuss the relation of our definition 
to the PAM scenario and we also add a highlight of that in the abstract. We thank him/her for 
this useful suggestion. 
Our definition of classicality for a set of states is to the PAM scenario what separability vs 
entanglement is to the Bell scenario. In other words, in analogy with how entanglement tells us 
whether a bipartite quantum state is classical (separable) or not, our definition tells us whether a 
set of quantum preparations is classical or not. Just like entanglement being necessary for 
nonclassical statistics in the black-box Bell scenario (nonlocality), our definition is necessary for 
nonclassical statistics in the black-box PAM scenario. Neither entanglement nor our definition is 
sufficient for the respective black-box statistics advantage: this is the reason why the states |0> 
and |+> represent a non-classical quantum set but cannot produce nonclassical statistics in the 
black-box PAM scenario. |0> and |+> are in a relational superposition (not classical) but if we

In further analogy, the operational principle behind entanglement is that it cannot be generated 
with local quantum devices, even if they may communicate classically. The operational principle 
behind our definition is that the set of preparations cannot be generated by devices that are 
unable to produce superposition states, even if they may be classically pre- and post-processed. 
The “no-superposition” principle is at the heart of what makes a preparation device classical and 
our definition captures the strongest possible model that respects this limitation.  
Finally, thanks to the Referee’s comments, we also noted a nice feature regarding our example 
of BB84 states (in section IIC). In this example, our classical model is both necessary and 
sufficient for a statistics advantage in the black-box PAM scenario. It implies that there is no 
better protocol than the quantum random access code for revealing the nonclassicality of the 
BB84 set. We have added this comment to the manuscript to further showcase the usefulness of 
our definition. 
 
 Furthermore, their construction of witnesses depends explicitly on Bob’s measurement choice, 
unlike conventional semi-device-independent PAM witnesses such as RAC success probabilities. 
This suggests that the proposed framework constitutes a new paradigm where measurement 
settings are fixed by the witness itself. If so, the authors should clearly articulate the operational 
meaning of nonclassicality in this setting. 
The referee is right that what we do is different from conventional semi-device-independent 
PAM witnesses and the reason is directly connected to the previous point.  
Our definition concerns the classicality of the set of states themselves and not the statistics they 
generate in a black-box PAM scenario. Therefore, we use the PAM scenario but with a fixed 
measurements for Bob to witness the non-classicality of the set of states. Continuing the 
analogy with entanglement, it is well-known that if we have some entangled state we can 
witness it by making specific measurements on its parts. If we had assumed that these 
measurements are unknown, we would have called this a Bell inequality test. Similarly, we 
witness the non-classicality of a set of quantum states by making specific measurements on it. If 
we had assumed that these measurements are unknown, we would have called it a semi-device-
independent PAM test. Thus, if in either of these scenarios we had adopted black-box 
measurements, then we would no longer be testing the classical model 
(separability/entanglement or our definition, respectively) for the quantum state(s) but the 
classical model (local hidden variable, or semi-device-independent PAM respectively) for the 
statistics. While tests of whether semi-device-independent PAM statistics is classical is long-

We thank the Referee for their comment. We aware the literature on Wigner distributions and 
will now carefully clarify why we did not comment on it. 
It is well-known that the term “classicality” is in quantum information used in many different 
ways. For contextuality, it means a non-contextual hidden variable model. For nonlocality, it 
means local hidden variable model. For bipartite states, it means separability. As noted before 
by the referee, in semi-device-independent PAM scenarios it has yet another meaning. The list 
can be continued, etc. However, these different forms of classicality often cannot be compared 
with each other since they concern different aspects of classical vs quantum physics. This is the 
reason we did not comment on Wigner-type nonclassicality: it is another, very different, form of 
nonclassicality (a manifestation of the uncertainty principle in phase-space). In the same way 
that nonclassiaclity in Bell scenarios or semi-device-independent PAM scenarios do not have any

strong relations to Wigner-type nonclassicality, also our definition of classicality of preparation 
devices also does not have any immediately meaningful connection to it. 
For additional clarity, we give a few examples of how our notion of nonclassicality for 
preparation devices cannot meaningfully be compared to Wigner-type nonclassicality. 
Firstly, Wigner-type nonclassicality is possible for a single state (since it exhibits uncertainty in 
position and momentum). This is not possible for our notion since it is defined for sets of states 
(a single-state preparation device is trivially classical in our work). Secondly, as cited by the 
referee, Wigner-type classicality has been linked to efficient simulation of quantum circuits on a 
classical computer. This is because of its correspondence to uncertain relations. That connection 
will therefore not be possible for our notion: what is efficiently simulable on a classical computer 
is a different perspective than what is efficiently preparable without superpositions (our notion). 
Thirdly, it is well-known that stabilizer states are Wigner-type classical (efficiently simulable on a 
classical computer), but they are necessarily non-classical when it comes to simulating a 
preparation device that outputs them (our notion). After all, they are at the heart of the BB84-
type protocols.  
To avoid this pit-fall, we now start the “Discussion” section with clarifying that our notion of 
classicality is distinct and independent from the several other notions of classicality commonly 
used in quantum information. 
 
In conclusion, while the results are technically correct, the manuscript in its current form does not 
provide a sufficiently strong conceptual foundation to warrant publication in Nature 
Communications.

replies and revisions and that they will be willing to reconsider their recommendation.

The classical models are defined for sets of quantum states. In Result 2, we consider a hypothetical 
quantum device that can prepare every single state in d-dimensional quantum theory. In this “full state 
space” limit, we indeed prove that for large d the visibility of classical simulation tends to zero for large 
dimension.  
However, it is essential to appreciate that this result only holds in this conceptual limit where the 
quantum device actually prepares every state allowed in the theory: it shows us the potential that high 
dimension could unlock. However, a real quantum preparation device does of course only prepare 
some specific (finite) set of states, e.g. those used in the BB84 protocol or similar. Classically simulating 
such a device is far easier. For example, simulating all qubit states requires visibility 50% whereas 
simulating the four BB84 states requires 71%. Therefore, it is not the case that more or less any large-
scale device will break classical limitations. The methods developed in our paper can assess whether the 
states generated from specific devices (e.g. BB84) admit a classical model. 
The authors remark in the discussion that “This shows that the advantages of high dimensionality have 
the potential to become very significant.” My concrete question, then, is whether and in what way the 
methods developed here could help to harness this potential. 
As mentioned above, the ‘potential’ comes from Result 2 showing that classical models increasingly fail 
to simulate the full state space of quantum theory for large dimension. To harness this potential, one 
must show that real-life devices, limited to only specific and finitely many states, are good enough to 
benefit from the “would be” advantages of the full state space.  
In our manuscript, sections IIE and IIF are respectively dedicated to finding classical models and 
falsifying classical models for such specific sets of preparations. The methods in section IIF are tailored 
for practical implementation (which we now point out in the revision).  
For example, in section IIF we consider a commonly used set of states in quantum information: those 
that form N=2,3,4,… mutually unbiased bases in dimension d. In eq18 we consider qutrits and prove 
that while simulating the full state space would put the visibility as low as 42%, we can start tapping 
into this power using just a small number of states. With 6 states we can reach 67%. With 9 states we 
reach 57%. With 12 states we reach 47%. This clearly indicates that a reasonable number of well-
selected states can harness the power of high-dimensional quantum states. These results are made 
possible thanks to the methods developed in our manuscript.  
In our revised Discussion section, we highlight the above discussion and how our work paves the way 
for concrete advantages from real-life devices.
