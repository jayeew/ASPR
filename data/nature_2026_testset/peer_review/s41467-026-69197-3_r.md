Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper investigates the structural features of flotillin complex in model systems and in Jurkat cells. These complexes are
associated with the formation of distinct membrane domains that are implicated in numerous membrane functions including
endocytosis, mitochondrial function, and cell signaling. The study uses a suite of techniques including direct
experimentation by negative stain electron microscopy, cryo-EM, and tomography, as well as contrast transfer function
modeling for 3D reconstruction and de novo structural modeling using Coot (partially based on AlphaFold2). 
Based on this combined experimental and analytical approach, the study demonstrates that the structure of the flotillin
complex is formed by the assembly of 22 repeats of alternate flotillin-1 and flotillin-2 proteins, resulting in a dome-like
structure with a wide base (~ 30 Å) and a narrow roof region (~ 17 Å). These structures were observed in glycerol fractions,
on vesicles, and in cells. Given that this major finding was already reported ~ a year ago with significantly higher resolution
data and detailed model building algorithms (Fu and McKinnon, PNAS 2024), it raises major questions about the novelty of
this paper. The authors acknowledge the other study, yet they fall short of distinguishing their own work from these prior
discoveries. Instead, they list a series of critiques (such as the use of model vesicles from GPMVs, the use of Ca ions, etc.)
that hardly affect the extracted structure (which as mentioned above agrees remarkably with the present findings). What is
more, the authors ignore addressing the limitations of their own approach, such as the heavy utilization of detergents which
could significantly compromise their studies of flotillin localization in liquid-ordered vs liquid-disordered membrane domains.
On this point, the paper opens with an extensive discussion on the association of flotillin with raft-like domains only to
indicate that their studies showed partitioning of flotillin to the disordered domains in GPMVs. These results seem to be
questionable since the GPMVS studies had to be conducted at much lower temperatures (4 degrees) to observe phase
separation. This is in stark contrast with other GPMV studies (e.g. Balakrishnan and Kenworthy, JACS 2024) which show
Lo-Ld phase separation at room temperature. Is it possible that the introduction of detergent compromised the membrane
composition and lateral segregation state? In fact, the data reported with PFA/DTT vs. NEM show major differences in the
reported observations (Extended Figure 11). These findings also contradict recent studies reporting enrichment of flotillin in
detergent-resistant membrane fractions typically associated with functional membrane microdomains (FMM) that resemble
Lo raft-like domains (Ukleja et al., Nature Commun. 2024). The paper goes on to propose a functional mechanism based on
the clustering of flotillin complexes observed in a few occurrences. However, there speculation is not founded on tangible
evidence. The clustering of proteins on membrane surfaces is quite common, but unless a clear functional consequence is
demonstrated it is hardly a convincing argument to state that clustering is somehow involved in function. Unfortunately, there
are several such speculative, unsubstantiated statements that are made throughout the manuscript. 
Besides the comments above, the paper is very technique heavy with loose connections between the applied methods,
obtained data, and contextualization of the obtained results relative to previous literature. For example, there are numerous
citations of raft-related studies (not including flotillin) and an absence of more relevant, foundational, flotillin-focused studies.
In addition, it is unclear whether some data are obtained by direct experimental observations or through data modeling. As a
case in point, Extended Figure 2b shows cryo-EM images that marginally represent the extracted structure of the flotillin
complex (same applies to Extended Figure 1). Also, the caption of Extended Figure 4a and 4c states that the transparent
surfaces superimposed with the atomic structures are cyro-EM maps of SPFH1 and SPFH2. How? These structures are
smaller than the reported resolution. This is not helped by the fact that several of the images are missing scale bars (e.g.
Extended Figure 4 and Extended Figure 1c and 1d). These shortcomings compromise the potential impact and clarity of the

manuscript, especially considering the quality of research published in Nature Communications.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript describes the cryoEM structure of the flotillin1-flotillin2 complex in vitro and by cryoET in situ. Although the
actual function of flotillin and related SPFH proteins is still somewhat mysterious, they are important for the generation of
membrane domains and potentially endocytic vesicle structures. This work provides a framework for understanding how
these proteins assemble and for future studies of their precise cellular interactions and functions. Notably the work does
follow from a recent similar structure in PNAS by Fu and MacKinnon. This recent paper is properly cited and discussed in
the current manuscript and I believe does not detract from the conclusions or significance. In addition there is another recent
preprint in Biorxiv describing in situ structures of the 
endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-resident Erlin1/2 complex and the
mitochondrial prohibitin (PHB1/2) complex by Gao and colleagues (https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.04.21.649849). Again, I
don’t feel this detracts from this current study, and in fact highlights that there are a lot of current advancements being made
in the field thanks to modern cryoEM developments. 
I thought the paper was really well written and worthy of publication without major changes. I have some minor queries and
edits suggested below. For context I have expertise in structural biology of membrane associated protein complexes, but I
am not specifically an expert in flotillins or cryoEM data processing.

We appreciate the opportunity to clarify our experimental rationale. Our study demonstrates that flotillin’s 
dome-like structure spatially compartmentalizes membranes, prompting us to investigate whether this 
compartmentalization also involves raft-like domain formation (raft lipid partitioning). 
Historically, flotillins were named for their presence in detergent-resistant membranes (DRMs) (Bickel et al., 
J. Biol. Chem. 1997), which were often incorrectly used as an equivalent term of lipid rafts in some 
literatures. However, DRMs are defined by biochemical fractionation and do not strictly correspond to the 
conceptual definition of lipid raft domains in physiological contexts. Thus, while flotillins have been widely 
used as raft markers, their actual property and association with rafts in native membranes remain an open 
question. 
Using the well-established GPMV method (Sezgin et al., Nat. Protoc. 2012) and comparative lipidomics, we 
observed that flotillin preferentially localizes to disordered domains and does not co-enrich with the most 
abundant species of raft lipids. These findings suggest that the flotillin-mediated membrane 
compartmentalization is likely driven by its intrinsic dome-like structural architecture, rather than the self-

association property of raft lipids. 
Regarding the reviewer’s comment on the "heavy utilization of detergents" in our GPMV experiments, we 
acknowledge the concern and will provide detailed methodological clarifications in our subsequent response. 
 
These results seem to be questionable since the GPMVS studies had to be conducted at much lower 
temperatures (4 degrees) to observe phase separation. This is in stark contrast with other GPMV studies (e.g. 
Balakrishnan and Kenworthy, JACS 2024) which show Lo-Ld phase separation at room temperature.  
We appreciate the reviewer’s concern regarding the temperature conditions in our GPMV experiments. 
However, the methodology we employed is well-established and carefully optimized to minimize artifacts 
while enabling the observation of phase separation. 
As noted in the foundational GPMV protocol (Sezgin et al., Nat. Protoc. 2012), the use of PFA/DTT—
common vesiculants in other studies—introduces significant perturbations, including (i) nonspecific cross-
linking of lipids and proteins by the aldehyde; (ii) cleavage of protein disulfides and thioesters, leading to 
depalmitoylation; (iii) specific coupling of phosphatidylethanolamines to proteins. These effects artificially 
elevate the miscibility transition temperature by ~15°C, enabling phase separation at room 
temperature but at the cost of substantial membrane perturbation (Box 2, Sezgin et al., Nat. Protoc. 
2012). The study cited by the reviewer (Balakrishnan & Kenworthy, JACS 2024) further illustrates this point: 
their use of lipid peroxidation dramatically enhances demixing but also strongly perturbs membrane 
organization, even displacing canonical raft markers (e.g., GPI-anchored proteins and CTxB) into disordered 
domains.

and Flot2-mCherry (c). Phase separation into the Lo and Ld phases was induced by cooling, with F AST DiI 
and F AST DiO dye marking the Ld phase, and CTxB (Cholera Toxin Subunit B (Recombinant), Alexa

By contrast, we opted for a milder approach using N-ethyl maleimide (NEM), which avoids cross-linking 
artifacts but requires lower temperatures (<5°C) to observe phase coexistence (Box 2, Sezgin et al., Nat. 
Protoc. 2012). To improve phase separation under these conditions, we supplemented with 400 µM 
deoxycholic acid (DCA), a detergent concentration rigorously validated in prior work (Zhou et al., J. Biol. 
Chem. 2013; Levental et al., Biophys. J. 2016). Importantly, our conditions preserved the expected 
partitioning of CTxB into Lo domains (Rebuttal Fig. 2), confirming minimal perturbation compared 
to lipid peroxidation-based methods in the study cited by the reviewer (Balakrishnan & Kenworthy,

physiological relevance by avoiding aggressive perturbations that compromise membrane integrity. 
Finally, our observation of flotillin enrichment in Ld domains aligns with earlier findings (Fig. 6B, Sengupta 
et al., Biochim Biophys Acta. 2008), further supporting the validity of our experimental approach.  
 
Is it possible that the introduction of detergent compromised the membrane composition and lateral 
segregation state?  
We acknowledge the reviewer’s valid concern regarding potential detergent-induced perturbations to 
membrane composition and lateral organization. Indeed, any GPMV formation protocol may inherently 
perturb native membrane organization to some extent through vesiculation-induced component 
reorganization, with protein asymmetry generally preserved but lipid asymmetry partially disrupted (Sezgin, 
BBA Biomembranes 2022).  
We further note that GPMVs, while imperfect, remain the most experimentally tractable model for studying 
macroscopic membrane domains. As highlighted by the study cited by the reviewer (Balakrishnan & 
Kenworthy, JACS 2024), mammalian plasma membranes are tuned near a miscibility critical point, making 
physiological nanodomains challenging to resolve in live cells. GPMVs amplify these nanoscopic 
fluctuations into microscopically observable phases below the miscibility transition temperature, providing 
unique insights into underlying principles of membrane organization. 
To minimize potential artifacts, our experimental conditions were carefully optimized. The 400 µM 
deoxycholic acid (DCA) concentration used is well below its critical micelle concentration (CMC ≈ 5 mM) 
and has been previously validated to facilitate phase separation without compromising protein partitioning 
(Zhou et al., J. Biol. Chem. 2013; Levental et al., Biophys. J. 2016). Importantly, as established earlier, our 
conditions preserved the expected Lo domain partitioning of CTxB (Rebuttal Fig. 2), indicating the 
maintenance of raft-like organization.

(Extended Figure 11).  
The observed differences between PFA/DTT and NEM treatments are indeed expected. As described in the 
Extended Figure 11 legend, PFA/DTT induces depalmitoylation of flotillins due to DTT-mediated cleavage

of thioester bonds, causing flotillins (which lack transmembrane domains) to dissociate from the membrane 
and localize to the GPMV lumen. In contrast, NEM preserves flotillin palmitoylation and membrane 
association while minimizing artifacts, making it the preferred method for studying their partitioning in 
GPMVs. 
 
These findings also contradict recent studies reporting enrichment of flotillin in detergent-resistant 
membrane fractions typically associated with functional membrane microdomains (FMM) that resemble Lo 
raft-like domains (Ukleja et al., Nature Commun. 2024).  
Our findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating flotillin partitioning into the Ld phase of 
GPMVs (Sengupta et al., Biochim Biophys Acta. 2008).  
The study cited by the reviewer (Ukleja et al., Nat. Commun. 2024) examines FloA, a bacterial flotillin-like 
protein from Staphylococcus aureus, which differs fundamentally from mammalian flotillins. FloA contains 
an additional N-terminal transmembrane helix, a divergent SPFH domain, and a much shorter coiled-coil 
region, making direct comparisons to mammalian flotillins biologically irrelevant. Thus, the reviewer’s 
reference does not contradict our observations, as these proteins belong to the same superfamily but exhibit 
distinct structural and functional properties. 
 
The paper goes on to propose a functional mechanism based on the clustering of flotillin complexes observed 
in a few occurrences. However, there speculation is not founded on tangible evidence. The clustering of 
proteins on membrane surfaces is quite common, but unless a clear functional consequence is demonstrated 
it is hardly a convincing argument to state that clustering is somehow involved in function. Unfortunately, 
there are several such speculative, unsubstantiated statements that are made throughout the manuscript. 
Our study employs multi-modal imaging approaches to investigate the organization mechanism of flotillin 
membrane microdomains. We demonstrate that individual flotillin complexes exhibit a dome-like structure 
that physically compartmentalizes the membrane, forming discrete microdomains (~30 nm in diameter), as 
revealed by both in vitro and in situ structural analyses. These well-defined microdomains then serve as 
fundamental units that can laterally cluster, creating higher-order membrane microdomain organization, 
which is mechanistically distinct from generic protein clustering. 
Importantly, flotillin microdomains of similar dimensions have been reported elsewhere (John Abousawan et 
al., J Cell Sci. 2023; Gregory M.I. Redpath et al., Nat. Commun. 2019) and shown to serve as platforms for 
recruiting client proteins to regulate processes like endocytosis and signaling. Thus, our proposal of 
functional clustering is grounded in direct structural evidence and prior literatures, rather than pure 
speculation.

methods, obtained data, and contextualization of the obtained results relative to previous literature.

Our study employs a carefully designed, multi-modal approach where each technique logically builds upon 
prior findings to investigate flotillin membrane microdomain organization. The experimental workflow is as 
follows: 
1) We first resolved the high-resolution structure of the flotillin complex using cryo-EM SPA, revealing its 
dome-like architecture, which spatially compartmentalizes membranes into ~30 nm microdomains.
