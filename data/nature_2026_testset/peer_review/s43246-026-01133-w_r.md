electrochemistry with practical commercialization barriers. The "user-focused" perspective is particularly valuable, as it

engineering strategies, and supply-chain considerations creates a vital roadmap for researchers and industry stakeholders
aiming to transition this high-potential technology from lab breakthroughs to real-world impact. This work will undoubtedly
accelerate progress in next-generation battery development!

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
Summary: This review article, entitled “Sulfur Cathodes: From Hype to Real Breakthrough Power,” reports a broad and
comprehensive overview of sulfur-based battery technologies, emphasizing their potential to address global energy storage
demands through sustainable, low-cost, and high-energy-density solutions. Rather than adhering to conventional research-
centered themes such as the shuttle effect or redox kinetics, the review uniquely adopts an end-user perspective, organizing
the discussion around practical performance metrics across various sulfur-based battery chemistries. The major focuses
included self-discharge, rate capability, durability, safety, and cost, which are the critical issues of the high-capacity sulfur
cathode. Accordingly, the manuscript provides a structured examination of the current technological landscape, barriers to
commercialization, and the policy, industrial, and infrastructural support needed for market integration. Overall, the work
provides a valuable reference and roadmap for researchers and stakeholders seeking to bridge fundamental research with
scalable, market-ready sulfur battery technologies. 
General Comment: This review article is timely and informative, and it successfully highlights the critical bottlenecks and
innovation gaps that must be addressed to realize sulfur-based batteries. The following revisions are suggested to further
improve the clarity and impact of the work.

Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents a timely and critical review of sulfur-based batteries (SBBs), distinguishing itself from the plethora
of material-focused reviews by adopting a strong application-oriented perspective. I strongly appreciate the authors' effort to
bridge the gap between academic "hype" and the harsh reality of commercial requirements (lean electrolyte, high areal
loading, and manufacturing scalability). This work has the potential to serve as a reality check and a roadmap for
researchers in the field. However, to further enhance the depth and practical relevance of this review, I recommend the
authors address the following technical queries: 
1. In Section 4.3, the authors highlight "dry electrode coating" as a key enabler for reducing costs and environmental impact.
Could the authors elaborate on how to balance the densification inherent in dry processing with the porosity requirements for
SBBs? Are there specific dry-processing strategies (e.g., pore-forming agents, specific binder fibrillation control) that are
uniquely suited for sulfur cathodes to avoid "choking" the ion transport? 
2. The cost analysis in Section 4.4 is compelling, targeting $65-90/kWh. However, this heavily depends on the price of the
anode. While Lithium carbonate prices fluctuate, the cost of ultra-thin Lithium metal foil (<50 µm) is currently dominated by
processing costs (extrusion/rolling) rather than raw material costs, and it carries a massive premium. How sensitive is the
projected cost model to the processing cost of Li foil? If the industry cannot achieve low-cost production of wide-width, thin Li
foils, does the cost advantage of Sulfur over LFP completely evaporate? A brief sensitivity analysis or commentary on "Li foil
processing cost" vs. "raw material cost" would strengthen this section. 
3. Section 3.8 addresses safety. While SBBs are often marketed as safer than Ni-rich ternary batteries, the combination of a
Li-metal anode and highly volatile ether-based electrolytes (low flash point) presents a unique and severe fire risk. Could the
authors provide a more specific comparison of the thermal runaway mechanism between SBBs and LIBs? Specifically, does
the "shuttle effect" or the low melting point of Sulfur play a role in accelerating or mitigating thermal runaway once it starts? A
deeper look into the safety at the pack level (not just cell level) would be beneficial. 
4. The review rightly emphasizes the need for E/S < 3 µL/mg. Beyond just "electrolyte formulation," are there specific cell
assembly techniques (e.g., vacuum filling protocols, temperature-assisted wetting) or separator modifications discussed in
the industry that specifically address the wetting challenges of thick, porous sulfur cathodes? This is often a "trade secret"
area, but any insight into the process engineering of wetting would be a great addition.

Beyond conventional polysulfides diffusion, what new in-situ/operando characterization data reveal

Authors Response: We sincerely appreciate the time you dedicated to reviewing our work and for 
providing such constructive and encouraging feedback. As suggested by the reviewer, to highlight 
the importance of unforeseen decomposition pathways, we have included a comment o n in -
situ/operando findings that show the shuttle effect is exacerbated by parasitic reactions, not just 
diffusion. Specifically, we have added the following references: 
 Cation-initiated Polymerization: We now cite the work of Liu et al. (Science Advances, 2018), 
utilizing FTIR and NMR to show that cation -initiated ring -opening polymerization of 1,3 -
dioxolane (DOL) produces poly -DOL, consuming the solvent and aggravating polysulfide 
accumulation. 
 Radical Intermediates: We incorporated the findings of Zou an d Lu ( J. Phys. Chem. Lett. , 
2016), utilizing operando UV -vis to identify the S₃ *⁻ radical intermediate, which triggers 
solvent side reactions. 
 Anion Decomposition: We reference AIMD simulations (France -Lanord et al., PRX Energy, 
2022) indicating LiTFSI ani on decomposition into LiF and SO₂ fragments, which 
fundamentally alters the CEI composition.

 LiNO₃ Depletion: We highlighted Ding et al. ( Sci. Rep., 2016) regarding the rapid reduction 
of LiNO₃ into LiₓNᵧO_z species, confirming that its depletion weakens shuttle suppression 
over time. 
Reviewer Comment 2: For emerging sulfur hosts, what specific pore geometry and surface chemistry 
metrics most effectively balance polysulfide adsorption with ionic diffusion at high sulfur loadings 
relevant to commercialization? 
Authors Response: We have revised the section on energy density (page 20 in the document with 
tracking changes) to quantify specific metrics required for high-loading commercial cells: 
 Pore Geometry: We clarify that macropore volume (>50 nm) should be maximized (>70% of 
total volume) to act as ion/electrolyte reservoirs for thick electrodes (Zhao et al., Nat. 
Nanotechnol., 2021). Mesopores (3–10 nm) are identified as the optimal range for reaction 
zones, while micropores (<2 nm) must be optimized, not maximized, to avoid sacrificing 
kinetics (Ye et al., Adv. Mater., 2020). 
 Surface Chemistry: We established a benchmark of 5 –10% atomic nitrogen concentration 
(predominantly pyridinic-N) for effective polar site density. 
 Holistic Design: We emphasize that the "paramount figure of merit" is the integration of 
these features: a macroporous conductive scaffold with engineered meso /micropores and 
high polar site density. 
Reviewer Comment 3: Given the focus on "sulfur-based battery systems," what are the comparative 
kinetics limitations of polysulfide shuttling in Na -S/Mg-S/K-S batteries versus Li -S, and how do 
electrolyte design strategies diverge for these chemistries? 
Authors Response: We have added a comparative analysis of shuttle kinetics across cation 
chemistries (page 22 in the document with tracking changes): 
 Li-S: Dominated by high solubility of long -chain Li₂S ₙ  in ethers; requires solubility 
suppression. 
 Na-S: Weaker shuttle due to lower solubility, but suffers from sluggish solid-solid conversion 
and pore blocking. 
 Mg-S: Limited by the strong solvation of Mg²⁺ causing slow charge transfer; shuttle is 
secondary to fundamental transport barriers. 
 K-S: Proceeds via solid -phase pathways with high overpotentials and "dead -sulfide" 
accumulation (e.g., K₂S₂, K₂S₃) (Gu et al., ACS Energy Lett., 2018). 
Reviewer Comment 4:  For metallic compound catalysts that enhance conversion kinetics, what 
degradation mechanisms (leaching, phase segregation, passivation) emerge during extended cycling 
under high-rate conditions, and how can they be mitigated? 
Authors Response: We have addressed catalyst stability under high-rate conditions (page 22 in the 
document with tracking changes)  by discussing:

 Metal Leaching: The loss of active sites (Co, Fe, Ni) into the electrolyte (Song et al., Carbon 
Energy, 2023). 
 Agglomeration: The tendency of nanosized particles (e.g., FeS₂) to coalesce. We cite Zeng et 
al. regarding the use of hollow carbon shells to mitigate this. 
 Passivation: The formation of insulating Li₂S / Li₂S₂ layers that "poison" the catalyst surface
