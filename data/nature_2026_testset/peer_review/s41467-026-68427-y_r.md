Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Vegetation restoration drives long-term dust mitigation in Eastern Asia 
- What are the noteworthy results? 
The main result of this study is to highlight the respective importance of climate factors and vegetation cover as drivers of
dust emission affecting northeastern China, over the last decades (back to 1982) and according to climate projections (until
2100). There is undoubtedly a considerable amount of thorough work behind the manuscript. However, it also raises
concerns that are detailed below. 
- Will the work be of significance to the field and related fields? How does it compare to the established literature? If the work
is not original, please provide relevant references. 
The motivation of the work should be more clearly explained. As it is, it focuses on an area affected by dust emissions which
is actually quite a small – yet indeed densely populated - area of northeastern China. Besides, the types of impacts (e.g. on
human health?) are not stated clearly enough to support the motivation of the study. 
Additionally, there is a clear similarity with the context of the African Sahel, which is located right South of the Sahara, the
main dust source worldwide. The Sahel is also very populated and dust impacts the human population who lives downwind
Saharan dust plumes. It has clear impacts (e.g. connection with meningitis epidemics) in this region, and many studies
attempted to estimate Saharan and Sahelian dust emissions and their impacts. A parallel with semi-arid Asia could have
been briefly drawn in the introduction or discussion parts of the manuscript. 
- Does the work support the conclusions and claims, or is additional evidence needed? 
According to me, there are some discrepancies between the exposed results and the conclusions the authors draw from
them: 
Wind versus vegetation as drivers of dust emissions: In the abstract (and in Main l. 284-289), the main point of the authors,
as currently stated in the manuscript, is that wind dominates interannual variability of dust mission, but that the key driver of
dust emission over multidecadal scale is vegetation. They are preoccupied that recent large dust events would hide the
long-term effect of land restoration in the public perception. However, I am not sure that the results support this statement so
clearly, as can be read in the manuscript itself e.g. in lines 163-168 and line 204. Also, I do not see where in Extended Data
Fig. 4, can be seen the +29.5% of explanatory power of vegetation after 2000 indicated in line 183, especially as ED Fig.4e
(which seems to compare the baseline experiment with experiment 2) starts after 2000 making it impossible to compare to
the pre-2000 period. If this statement refers actually to Fig. 2f, then the gap between yellow and purple curves in year 2000
should be better explained. 
l.144-147: I am not sure that Fig. 2 shows what is stated here. As the dust emissions are temporally averaged in panels a
(1982-2015) and b (2001-2023), I don’t think that it shows a peak in early 2000s for northern China and a subsequent
decline. Indeed, the sign of the trends highly depends on the chosen time-period, as we can clearly see from panel e.
Besides, there is a large gap between Phase 1 and Phase 2 in Fig. 2f, which should be discussed, especially as the
consistency between the 2 Phases was supposed to be checked in Extended Data Fig.4c. 
Similarly, I am not sure that SI Fig. 7 shows what is stated in lines 185-188. How was the inflection point computed? How
about the inflection point curve by curve? And if computing 2 inflection points over the period instead of 1? What are the
dashed lines? That would also be interesting to see annual rainfall in this figure, as vegetation cover is probably highly
related to it.

Vegetation type: The authors focus their analysis on spring (March to May), as it is the time of the year with maximum dust
emissions in their study area. Extended Data fig. 1 shows that green vegetation in their study area starts growing during this
period (March to May), progressively preventing dust emission. Their analyses consider vegetation indices (NDVI and LAI)
that are dedicated to the remote sensing of green vegetation, and consider semi-arid grasslands and croplands, of which
green up is triggered by the annual rainy season. Yet, in spring, the vegetation cover can also be constituted by the
remaining vegetation of the previous year (i.e. brown vegetation, not green). Thus, I wonder whether brown vegetation
should be included in the analysis? 
I also didn’t understand how the vegetation cover was determined for the future scenarios (2020-2100), as the authors need
to estimate its fractional vegetation cover in order to run their DuEM model to compute dust emissions. This point should be
clarified, especially as the main point (vegetation cover drives the multidecadal trends) of the article relies heavily on these
future scenarios. 
In addition, the analysis focuses on vegetation cover, but the cover type is not discussed. For example, 15% cover (a value
identified as a threshold by the authors, to prevent wind erosion) does not translate into the same soil protection for aligned
cereals (potentially inducing corridor effects) or for a scattered herbaceous cover. 
Overgrazing and land degradation: I don’t think that Extended Data Fig7 shows “overgrazing” as stated in line 197,
overgrazing being a quite complex concept that would need to be defined (e.g. by estimating forage production through
vegetation cover and compare it to the grazing pressure and livestock needs). Same comment on line 300 and line 315; in
addition, references 42-43-44 on which the authors rely for this statement do not mention overgrazing or not even grazing. I
also do not really understand how Extended Data Fig.7i supports this statement, as it shows trend of dust emission scattered
over all grazing pressure differences. Similarly, I think that references 15&16 stress the importance of wind regime in driving
dust emissions, which seems contradictory with your statement in lines 317-318, that yet cite this references. All this should
lead to rephrased in a more nuanced way statements in lines 345-347. 
Besides, the figures are very numerous (33 figures, but altogether about 180 panels – some of which are not referred to in
the manuscript: main Fig. 2e; extended data Fig. 7 a&d; extended data Fig. 10 a&b; SI Fig. 1a&b; also, SI fig. 3 is cited after
SI fig. 7; SI Fig. 8&9 are cited after 14, and SI fig. 10 before 8&9). I wonder whether there is perhaps some redundancy
among the figures, and whether the authors could select the most relevant ones to ease the reading. Most of them are also
small and thus hard to read, even when zoomed. 
- Are there any flaws in the data analysis, interpretation and conclusions? 
The whole analysis relies on the use of the MERRA dataset, which is use as a reference. However, this dataset itself is not
discussed, in particular the possible impact of its composition (adding more datasets through time) on the temporal trends
observed by the authors. 
The division of observations in two overlapping phases (1982-2015 and 2001-2023) should be clarify. Especially as different
trends are attributed to the two phases (l.161, l.169) despite Phase 2 only having 8 years that are not part of Phase 1. If the
two phases show similar data (as suggested by Extended figure 4c) during the years of overlap, the period used for analysis
could identify trends of dust emissions with less or no overlapping years. 
The way the authors determine the borders of what they call “Eastern Sources” is not clear enough. It is shown in Fig. 1b but
the way the spatial correlation is computed should be better explained. 
A word on uncertainties in hourly wind speeds would also be interesting, as simulated dust emissions are highly sensitive to
this input. Also, what is the temporal resolution of wind speed in future scenarios, and how was it obtained? 
Some references were cited a bit surprisingly to me, as I am not sure it supports the sentence in which they are cited. See
above (paragraph on overgrazing); also, ref. 5 line 53 and ref. 11 line 54.

According to me, these concerns need major revisions by the authors.

Major comments: 
1. My main concern deals with the way authors mention “vegetation restoration” all along the manuscript, whereas their
results only show the importance of wind and of vegetation cover on spring dust emissions. As also pointed by Reviewer 3,

induce a decreasing trend in the simulated dust emission. Besides, I am not totally convinced by the new perspective of the
revised manuscript, focusing on the idea that wind drives short-term dust emissions, while vegetation drives their long-term
dynamics. I think that it largely depends on the vegetation type, e.g. annual vegetation cover greatly depends on annual
precipitation, which can exhibit a large interannual variability, and of wind conditions. This can be les striking for perennial
vegetation and for woody cover. As the authors specify that 75% of the emitted dust come from the Alashan Plateau, which is
a semi-desert, it would be interesting to know which type of vegetation (annual, perennial, herbaceous, woody…) grows in
that area. 
These misleading mentions can be found in: 
The title: “Vegetation restoration drives long-term dust mitigation in Eastern Asia”. This needs to be changed as you did not
perform analysis dedicated to the comparison of “restored” versus “unrestored” areas, accounting for the vegetation type of
these areas. The title could be changed for “vegetation cover” but should also be nuanced as the long-term impact of
vegetation cover on dust emission depends on the vegetation type and on its resilience to potentially unfavorable climate

Point-by-point response to comments of Reviewer 1 
[Comments] - What are the noteworthy results? 
The main result of this study is to highlight the respective importance of climate factors and 
vegetation cover as drivers of dust emission affecting northeastern China, over the last decades 
(back to 1982) and according to climate projections (until 2100). There is undoubtedly a 
considerable amount of thorough work behind the manuscript . However, it also raises concerns 
that are detailed below. 
[Responses] We thank the reviewer for acknowledging the scope and effort of our study, as well 
as the relevance of our focus on the respective roles of climate and vegetation in shaping dust 
emissions across historical and CMIP6 projected periods. We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful 
concerns, which we address in detail below. These comments have helped us further clarify the 
analyses and further strengthen the manuscript. 
This study provides the first quantitative assessment of how the relative influence of 
vegetation and climate drivers on dust emissions evolves across timescales—a perspective that we 
believe is critical for reconciling recent dust trends and informing future land -climate policy. 
Specifically, 1) we develop a timescale -explicit analytical framework that integrates physically 
based dust emission modeling with statistical attribution, allowing us to disentangle the effects of 
vegetation and climate drivers over both historical and future periods; 2) using this framework, we 
show that vegetation -driven reductions in dust emissions become in creasingly pronounced over 
longer timescales; and 3) we identify strong spatial heterogeneity in vegetation effectiveness, 
suggesting that restoration efforts should prioritize regions with vegetation cover below 15%. 
 
[Comments] - Will the work be of significance to the field and related fields? How does it 
compare to the established literature? If the work is not original, please provide relevant 
references. 
The motivation of the work should be more clearly explained. As it is, it focuses on an area 
affected by dust emissions which is actually quite a small – yet indeed densely populated - area of 
northeastern China. Besides, the types of impacts (e.g. on human health?) are not stated clearly 
enough to support the motivation of the study. 
Additionally, there is a clear similarity with the context of the African Sahel, which is located 
right South of the Sahara, the main dust source worldwide. The Sahel is also very populated and 
dust impacts the human population who lives downwind Saharan dust plumes. It has clear impacts
