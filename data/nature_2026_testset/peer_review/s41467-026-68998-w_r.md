Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, Wu X et al. have revealed that Naprt deficiency promotes two major processes in colon tumorigenesis: tumor
initiation (DNA damage) and promotion (inflammation). They propose that the increased DNA damage in the Naprt-KO mice
is likely due to a reduced PARP activity associated with lower NAD levels in epithelial cells. Given these results, a
phenotype that Naprt deficiency leads to an increased incidence of colon tumors is convincing. These are novel and
interesting findings. However, there are two major concerns regarding this paper that will require many additional efforts, as
outlined below; 
The first concern relates to the inverse correlation between Naprt expression and prognosis in several cancers (Fig 6J-L). As
noted above, data regarding increased tumor formation in Naprt-KO mice is convincing. However, this does not imply that
tumors with Naprt deficiency exhibit higher malignancy or more therapeutic resistance. After diagnosis, the prognosis of
cancer patients typically worsens with increasing malignancy and/or treatment resistance of tumor cells. Importantly, tumor
cells with DNA repair defects often show higher sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiation therapy, which can extend patient
survival. Thus, even if Naprt-low cancers show worse prognoses compared to Naprt-high cancers, it does not seem
reasonable to attribute this to an impaired DNA repair ability associated with Naprt deficiency. 
Another major issue is that this study overlooks recent important findings on systemic NA metabolism. In brief, NA absorbed
from the intestine is actively converted to nicotinic acid riboside (NAR) NAPRT-dependently, then released into the
circulation (Nomura, Nat Commun ’23. PMID: 38092728). Their paper reports that (1) the blood concentration of NA is much
lower than that of NAR, and (2) Naprt deficiency significantly decreases the blood NAR levels in mice. These findings are
critical to interpret the data presented in this paper. For example, in the culture experiments of this study, the authors added
NA at a concentration of 100 µM, which is likely supraphysiological by a factor of 1000 or more in most organs. Direct fueling
form the dietary NA or NA converted from Nam by gut microbiota may result in higher NA concentrations in the colon
epithelium compared to the circulation. However, there is little direct evidence that NAD synthesis using NA as a substrate is
active in other organs. Instead, it is questionable whether the proposed mechanism in this paper applies beyond the
intestinal epithelium. Given the NA to NAR conversion, many of author’s claim in this paper remain speculative. 
Besides the two major issues mentioned above, the following points require significant attention: 
Figs 1-3: The authors added NA at a final concentration of 100 µM in many experiments in vitro. A theoretical basis for this
concentration setting must be provided. Additionally, It would be better to present results with lower concentrations of NA for
comparison. 
Fig 2: The differences in PARylation and DNA damage between the parental and NAPRT-KO cells are relatively small.
Therefore, add-back experiments should be conducted. Alternatively, experiments with multiple KO clones should be
performed. Additionally, gH2AX levels should be examined by WB for a clearer comparison. 
Fig 3: The NAD levels in various organs of Naprt-KO mice under steady-state conditions (without Nam load) should be
reported. 
Fig 4: The reason for administering additional NA through drinking water, given that NA is already provided in the dietary
chow, should be clarified. Panels C-E are underwhelming, and most of them could be moved to supplementary items. 
Fig 6G: The experiment appears to be very important, but the method section lacks essential details. Were comparisons
made using littermates? Were events (e.g., mortality) monitored up to the time of inspection at 24 months? These are critical
details for interpreting the data. Moreover, the results should be presented separately for male and female mice.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Wu et al. explores how the Preiss-Handler pathway in NAD biosynthesis, mediated by NAPRT, impacts NAD
metabolism, DNA repair, and tumorigenesis in the gut. Key findings highlight the role of NAPRT in sustaining NAD levels
across tissues such as the liver, colon, and pancreas, and the consequences of NAPRT deficiency on NAD synthesis and
tissue vulnerability to NAD-depleting stress. 
In NAPRT KO mice, NAD synthesis is significantly compromised under DNA damage conditions, like those induced by
AOM treatment, leading to inadequate metabolic and DNA repair responses, heightened inflammation, and increased gut
cell damage. This dysfunction predisposes NAPRT KO mice to more severe colitis and tumorigenesis, resulting in a higher
tumor burden, advanced adenomas, and upregulated inflammatory genes, underscoring the critical function of NAPRT-
mediated NAD synthesis in safeguarding gut tissue from inflammation and cancer progression. 
This is a very well written paper, that provides important insights into the role of the Preiss-Handler pathway in NAD
biosynthesis, particularly in the context of NAPRT and its impact on DNA repair and tumorigenesis. However, it lacks
novelty in certain aspects, as highlighted in major concerns, particularly point 5. The pioneering study on the role of NAD+ in
tumorigenesis by Tummala et al. (2014) as well as the controversial work by Lee et al. (2022) should be cited, discussed,
and integrated into the context of the manuscript, supported by experimental evidence. Additionally, several key issues
outlined below need to be addressed before this manuscript is considered suitable for publication.Once these issues are
addressed, this manuscript could make a valuable contribution to the field. 
Major concerns 
1. The connection between NAD+ depletion and DNA damage is well-established, as shown in pivotal studies like
Tummala et al. (2014) and Gomes et al. (2016), whose papers have not been cited but should. However, in their study,
authors should mechanistically provide more insights into how NAPRT KO leads to DNA damage. This could result from
decreased PARP activity, inhibition of Sirtuins, reduced dNTP synthesis, or perturbation in alkylation routes, all pathways
where NAD+ plays a crucial role. To clarify this, the authors should measure the activities of PARP, Sirtuins, and dNTP
synthesis in the NAPRT KO context. These assays would provide insight into the specific mechanisms by which NAD+
depletion leads to genotoxic stress and tumorigenesis in their model. Furthermore, performing rescue experiments, such as
supplementing precursors to restore NAD+ levels or enhancing activity in the affected pathways, would significantly
strengthen the study’s conclusions regarding the role of NAD+ metabolism in DNA damage and cancer progression. Rescue
experiments with dNTPs, or using PARP inhibitors are also requested. 
2. A key concern is that while NAMPT depletion results in cell death, NAPRT KO does not induce such effects, in particular
because the medium is supplemented with NAM. Likewise, NAPRT KO mice develop normally without obvious phenotypes,
whereas NAMPT KO mice are embryonically lethal. The authors conclude that the deamidated NAD biosynthesis pathway
is non-essential for survival, development, or normal cellular function. However, the underlying reason for this disparity
remains unclear. Are NAD+ levels significantly more reduced to be completely abolished in NAMPT KO compared to
NAPRT KO, thereby leading to extensive DNA damage, cell death, and catastrophic outcomes in NAMPT KO but not in
NAPRT KO? Could it be that NAD+ depletion is less severe in NAPRT KO, allowing partial compensation through NAD+
synthesis from tryptophan catabolism? Notably QPRT is expressed in human colons and therefore this pathway might still
be active despite the enzymes are not highly expressed in mice. Resolving these questions is crucial, and the study would
benefit from comparative NAD+ quantification between NAMPT and NAPRT KO models, along with an analysis of
compensatory metabolic pathways. 
3. The authors claim that their scRNA-seq analysis reveals distinct cell-type expression patterns for NAMPT and NAPRT in
the small intestine and colon, with NAMPT being ubiquitously expressed across all cell types, particularly in gut immune
cells, while NAPRT is relatively enriched in the epithelial compartments (Figure 1A and S1B). However, this is not entirely
accurate, as NAPRT also appears to be expressed in immune cells. Thus, a complete NAPRT knockout could indeed affect
NAD+ levels in immune cells, potentially impacting their functional role in reducing tumorigenesis. A conditional knockout
mouse model would have been a more suitable approach to target specific cell types for this study. By restricting the NAPRT
knockout to particular tissues or cell types, the authors could better assess the cell-type-specific effects of NAD+ depletion
on tumorigenesis. Alternatively, the authors could utilize a xenograft or patient-derived xenograft model (that will give some
clinicl relevance to the study), comparing NAPRT knockout with NAMPT knockout, to assess the tumor-inducing potential in

4. In line with the previous comment, the authors should provide a more comprehensive explanation of their results
addressing inflammation, in particular when using the DSS-induced colitis models. Reduction of NAD+ levels in their Naprt
KO model induces DNA damage and this is well-known to be linked to tumour formation, especially in epithelial cells, but
the connection between the reduction of NAD+ levels with inflammation is not clear. Why the reduction of NAD+ levels leads
to more inflammation? Which cells contribute to this enhanced sensitivity in their KO mice? Which are the cellular pathways
important for this process? Perhaps an immune cell characterization in the presence of DSS in NAPRT KO mice should be

do understand that this is not possible at this stage, but authors could explored alternative experiments such as PDX or/and
xenografts studies in immunodeficient mice to circumvent this. 
5. A rescue experiment using nicotinamide riboside (NR) both in vitro (Figure 1) and in vivo to counteract the effects of

and in therapy once tumors are formed to better understand the pro- or anti-tumorigenic effects of NAPRT KO. This
experiment could provide direct evidence for the potential therapeutic role of NR in restoring NAD+ levels and mitigating the
negative effects of NAD+ depletion on DNA repair, inflammation, and tumorigenesis. Including such data would significantly
strengthen the manuscript by demonstrating the feasibility of NR as a potential intervention for restoring NAD+ homeostasis
and reducing cancer progression in the context of NAPRT deficiency. 
6. While this study introduces the first NAPRT KO mouse model to explore the role of the Preiss-Handler pathway in
tumorigenesis, the concept of NAD+ depletion in DNA damage and cancer is not novel. The authors overlook the
foundational work by Tummala et al., published in Cancer Cell in 2014, which first demonstrated the significance of NAD+
depletion in tumorigenesis. Additionally, the study by Lee et al., published in Cancer Discovery in 2022, has been ignored.
In their work, the authors showed that APC-deficient colorectal cancer models were susceptible to TDO2 depletion or
pharmacological inhibition, leading to NAD+ depletion, impaired cancer cell proliferation, and enhanced antitumor immune
responses. These important studies should be cited, discussed, and integrated into the context of the current manuscript
through experimental evidence. One possible explanation to the study of Lee et al. is that since APC deletion drives cell
proliferation, cells might rely on increased NAD+ from de novo synthesis to initiate DNA synthesis and repair pathways.
Without the support of the NAPRT-NAD+ arm, APC-deleted cells may not survive. Therefore, it is crucial to measure NAD+
levels under different conditions in order to determine how the depletion of NAPRT affects cellular NAD+ metabolism,
especially in the context of APC deletion. This would help clarify whether NAPRT-mediated NAD+ synthesis is essential for
the survival and function of APC-deleted cells and further elucidate the role of NAD+ in DNA repair and cell survival under
genotoxic stress. 
7. Figure 2D is difficult to understand, and the data interpretation lacks clarity. The authors claim that STF treatment

observation (fewer colonies with STF treatment) would likely emerge. The precise methodology of this experiment,
particularly the comparisons drawn between groups, needs more explanation.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Xiaoyue and colleagues “NAPRT-mediated deamidated NAD biosynthesis enhances tissue resiliency
and suppresses tumorigenesis,” aims to investigate the tumour suppressing roles of the deamidated arm of NAD
biosynthesis. To achieve that, they constructed NAPRT gene knock-out models and assess their effects using both in vitro
(CRC119 cell lines and colonic organoids) and in vivo (mouse) systems. Here they found that removal of NAPRT gene
altered metabolism such as glycolysis, increased DNA damage, impaired PARP activity, heightened sensitivity to
inflammatory stress, and susceptibility to tumorigenesis in mouse models. Furthermore, reduced NAPRT expression
correlates with poor prognosis in stage 1, not stage 3 and 4, CRC patients. 
The manuscript is well-written, and the analyses are thorough and comprehensive. In my view, the findings presented in this
manuscript are valuable and provide some insights into the mechanisms underlying colorectal cancer (CRC) development.
Additionally, these insight may also extend to understanding precancerous conditions, such as polyps, offering a foundation
for further research in this area.

following NA administration. Blood sampling should preferably be performed from the systemic circulation, rather than the
portal vein, to better reflect whole-body metabolism. In this context, blood collection from the right ventricle is considered the
most appropriate.

expression system, rather than transient transfection.

Good questions. NAD has a half-life from 15 minutes to 15 hours in different cells and tissues due to homeostatic activities of various NAD-consuming enzymes (e.g. PARP, SIRT, CD38, SARM, etc.) 4, 19. Therefore, rapid and efficient biosynthesis is essential to maintain NAD homeostasis and cell survival, especially in cells and tissues with high NAD turnover. Theoretically, with a sufficient supply of the correspondent substrates (NAM, NA, or tryptophan) and adequate expression levels of the corresponding biosynthetic enzymes (NAMPT; NAPRT, NADSYN; IDO, TDO, QPRT, NADSYN), the activity of a single pathway (amidated, deamidated, or de novo, respectively) could be sufficient to maintain levels of NAD necessary for cell viability. However, based on the literature reports and our own observations, we believe there are at least two reasons why global NAMPT KO in mice is lethal, whereas mice with NAPRT KO are apparently normal in unstressed conditions at young age. First, NAMPT is expressed in all cells to recycle NAM released by NAD-consuming enzymes back into NAD. Since its activity is also subject to feedback inhibition by NAD 20, this enzyme is essential in maintenance of a homeostatic level of NAD in all cells. In contrast, the expression pattern of NAPRT (as well as various enzymes of the de novo pathway) is more tissue specific and relatively enriched in nutrition-rich organs. Thus, in NAMPT KO, the deamidated and de novo pathways cannot rescue NAD levels at least in some tissues.
