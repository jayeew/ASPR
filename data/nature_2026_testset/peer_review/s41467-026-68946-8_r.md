Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The integration of sorption-based atmospheric water harvesting (SAWH) with photovoltaic power generation to convert solar
energy into potable fresh water is highly appealing. This manuscript reports an enhanced “composite salt in porous matrix”
(CSPM) approach, in which MOFs are loaded with inorganic hygroscopic salts to improve water uptake under high relative
humidity, coupled with device-level optimization to considerably increase overall water harvesting efficiency. 
However, a more critical challenge lies in achieving efficient, long-term water capture under low relative humidity, especially
in arid regions. Furthermore, similar MOF-based CSPM strategies have already been reported (e.g., LiCl@MIL-101,
LiCl@UiO-66, CaCl
₂
@MOF-808), which limits the novelty of this work. The manuscript also lacks essential material
characterizations, and the description of the device is placed primarily in the Supporting Information, hindering reader
comprehension. The writing of the manuscript also requires improvement, as it is somewhat obscure and difficult to follow. 
In particular,I recommend that the authors address the following issues: 
1. Provide key material characterization data, including structural, morphological, and gas sorption analyses, as well as
schematic diagrams of the material structure and working principles to facilitate reader understanding. 
2. Inprove the background introduction. Traditional inorganic hygroscopic salts undergo a solid–liquid phase transition in
high-humidity environments, enabling faster water uptake and higher capacity than MOFs. In contrast, MOF have the
advantage of faster water sorption kinetics under low-humidity conditions. 
3. Also introduce the MOF-based CSPM strategy in more detail and compare the performance of the reported MOF–
inorganic salt composites with existing examples in the literature. 
4. Line 83: Using the wet impregnation method, where exactly is the LiCl located in the MOF structure—on the external
surface or inside the pores or both? In Lines 103–105, the claim that LiCl is loaded into the internal pores of the MOF based
solely on performance data is unconvincing. Please provide additional supporting characterization. 
5. Line 106 first refers to Fig. 1D before Line 112 refers to Fig. 1(A), which is inconsistent. In addition, “Fig. 1D” should be
corrected as “Fig. 1 (D)”. Revise the figure order and figure citation format accordingly. 
6. In Fig. 2A, important component annotations are missing (e.g., solar-driven absorption bed, condenser); the same issue
exists for Fig. 2B. For instance, where is the “mass transfer plate” mentioned in Line 140? 
7. The resolution and visual quality of most figures are poor. Please improve the figure quality and add the missing
schematic. 
8. Line 121: Fig. 1(D) should be Fig. 1 (C). 
9. The applications of photovoltaic cooling and electronic heat dissipation mentioned in Lines 158–159 and Line 277 are not
relevant to the main focus of this work and hence should be removed. 
10. In the abstract, a 91% improvement in solar-to-water efficiency is claimed, whereas Line 257 states that field tests
achiveved only a 50% efficiency increase. Please clarify this discrepancy. 
11. In Lines 286–288, the optimal LiCl/MOF mass ratio for loading efficiency is reported as 4:1. Does this also yield the best
performance? 
12. A distinctive feature of this study is the outdoor testing conducted in three different cities, each during a different season.
Are these results reproducible? How do they compare with laboratory results? Among the many variables involved, which
one is the key factor? The manuscript is somewhat disorganized; please restructure it to improve clarity, coherence, and
overall flow. 
13. Provide a molecular-level explanation for the observed improvement in water sorption performance.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an advanced strategy for atmospheric water harvesting (AWH) by employing metal-organic
framework (MOF) loaded with lithium chloride, combined with an optimized thermal management design. The proposed
approach shows significant promise for enhancing water harvesting efficiency, providing a valuable reference and
comprehensive analysis for future research in this field. The impressive solar-driven performance observed in diverse real-
world experiments comprehensively validates the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed strategy across a range of
scenarios. I believe this work merits publication in Nature Communications to serve as a valuable reference for researchers
in the field of AWH and adsorption science. 
In my assessment, while the core findings are compelling, certain details of the work could be further elaborated to provide a
more comprehensive presentation. I therefore recommend that this paper be accepted for publication after addressing these
points. 
1. There are many types of MOF materials used to uptake moisture water from the ambient air, why this manuscript suggest
using Ni2Cl2(BTDD)? It is important that the author may discuss the advantages and disadvantages of Ni2Cl2(BTDD) in
comparison with other MOF materials, like MOF 801, 101 and etc.. Then why a composite LiCl/ Ni2Cl2(BTDD) is used? 
2. While the authors have demonstrated adsorbent desorption at lower temperatures in field tests, I note that the material-
level testing presented in Figure 1B employed a desorption condition of 80 °C. To provide a more comprehensive
assessment of the composite MOF material's desorption kinetic performance at reduced temperatures, I suggest that the
authors include data obtained at lower desorption temperatures, such as 70 °C or 65 °C. 
3. To enhance the clarity of the water quality analysis presented in Table 2, I recommend that the authors provide additional
details regarding the ion concentration measurements. Specifically, this should include the instrumentation used for the
testing and the experimental setup employed. 
4. To improve the readability of the figures, I suggest that the authors increase the font size of the labels. The current font
size appears small and may hinder clear identification for readers.

In manuscript chapter Introduction:  
…Among various adsorbents, MOFs have the advantages of a large specific surface 
area and fast water sorption kinetics, and therefore have received wide attention (Adv. 
Mater. 2018, 30, 1704304; Nat. Nanotechnol. 2020, 15, 348). Nevertheless, their water 
harvesting capacity is often limited by the limited pore volume. To the best of our 
knowledge, traditional inorganic hygroscopic salts, represented by LiCl and CaCl 2, 
exhibit exceptionally high water  uptake capacity due to their deliquescence property, 
which enables a solid -to-liquid phase transition under high -humidity conditions (Adv. 
Mater. 2023, 35, 2302038;  Adv. Mater. 2025, e12244;  Device 2025, 3, 100798). 
However, they frequently suffer from is sues like leakage and poor cycling stability

Regarding why a composite LiCl@Ni2Cl2(BTDD) is used:  
Firstly, as mentioned above, the water adsorption performance of Ni2Cl2(BTDD) 
is not excellent. And, to the best of our knowledge, the adsorption capacity can be 
greatly enhanced by impregnating MOF composites with hygroscopic salts. Among 
them, LiCl can largely reduce the desorption temperature due to its low enthalpy of 
vaporization, which is one of the best candidates. Moreover, the porosity of MOFs can 
also mitigate salt leakage and improve the dispersion. Specifically, the completely 
exposed Ni2+ and Cl‒ sites on the pore channels of Ni 2Cl2(BTDD) in this manuscript 
should be very suitable binding sites for stabilize Cl‒ and Li+, respectively, which allows 
for superior stabilization of LiCl, leading to significantly improved performance. 
Indeed, the composite LiCl @Ni2Cl2(BTDD)_30 in this manuscript exhibited the 
highest water uptakes at 30% RH (1.13 g g‒1) and 80% RH (3.50 g g‒1) to date.

better highlight the potential of this technical approach:
