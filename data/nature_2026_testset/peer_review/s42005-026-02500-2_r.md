Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper presents a conceptual and experimental demonstration of temporal antireflection using a continuously time-
modulated transmission line. By implementing a temporal impedance taper through varactor-loaded microstrip lines and
controlling them with differential voltage ramps, the authors demonstrated an approach to achieving broadband impedance
matching without requiring spatial transitions. The theoretical treatment of the temporal reflection coefficient is sound and the
experimental validation is well executed, making this work an important proof-of-concept contribution in the emerging field of
time-varying metamaterials. 
However, the paper falls short in addressing practical considerations, including real-world applications, scalability to higher
frequencies, and system-level challenges such as energy conservation, stability, and noise performance. These gaps limit
its current utility beyond a laboratory demonstration. I recommend the paper to be submitted to more subject specific
journals, with particular attention to broader context and physical system implications. Those journals include IEEE TRANS
on MTT, Journal of Applied Physics etc. 
Specific Comments: 
1. Limited Practical Application Discussion: The paper demonstrates a well-controlled experiment at ~80 MHz using
varactors, but does not articulate how such a system could be scaled to GHz, THz, or optical domains, where fast, tunable
switching is substantially more difficult. A discussion of potential use cases, candidate materials, or technology platforms for
high-frequency implementation would significantly enhance impact. 
2. Proof-of-Concept Without Real-World Integration: While the temporal taper transmission line (TTTL) is novel, the
manuscript does not explore how this could be integrated into practical systems (e.g., reconfigurable front-ends, real-time
adaptive matching networks). The lack of demonstrated or proposed applications beyond the academic context weakens the
motivation for this work. 
3. Energy Conservation Considerations Not Addressed: Time-varying systems inherently allow energy exchange between
the wave and the modulating medium. While there is no evidence of unphysical behavior in this work, a brief discussion of
modified energy conservation laws (e.g., Poynting’s theorem in time-varying media) and the role of the modulation source
would provide physical grounding, especially given the observed frequency shift. 
4. Stability Under Time Modulation: The temporal taper used here is monotonic and passive, so the system appears stable.
However, in more general settings, especially periodic or high-Q structures, parametric or Floquet instabilities can arise.
Acknowledging the operating regime in which the method remains stable would clarify boundaries for safe implementation. 
5. Noise Performance Overlooked: Temporal modulation can introduce or mix noise through multiple pathways: driver noise
coupling into varactors, modulation-induced spectral folding, or impedance jitter. These effects are not analyzed in the
paper, yet could limit SNR or dynamic range in practical implementations. Some discussion or quantification of the
experimental noise floor and mitigation strategies would improve the completeness of the work.

6. No Comparison to Alternative Impedance Matching Methods: The manuscript would benefit from a concise comparison
between the proposed temporal matching method and existing spatial tapers or non-Foster matching techniques, to better
contextualize the novelty and performance benefits. 
7. The derivation of the temporal reflection coefficient is mathematically rigorous and aligns with prior work. However, the
assumptions behind the derivation (e.g., nondispersive media, negligible memory effects, time-varying ε(t) only) should be
clearly stated for completeness.

Reviewer #1 – Comments 3: 
“What do the Authors means by “differential time modu lation”, rather than simply “time 
modulation”?” 
Response from authors: 
We thank the reviewer for raising this question. The potential confusion likely stems from our term 
“differential time modulation” , which may have  inadvertently suggested a distinct form of time 
modulation. In fact, as the reviewer correctly pointed out, i t is entirely accurate to describe our 
TTTL as simply undergoing “time modulation”. Here the specific term “differential” refers strictly 
to the physical implementation of this modulation, rather than to the nature of the time variation 
itself. We elaborate below on the motivation and necessity of this differential implementation. 
As the reviewer is likely aware, almost all previous realization s of varactor-based time-varying 
platforms in the microwave regime (including transmission lines, metasurfaces, waveguides, etc.) 
have employed a single varactor in each unit cell. The drawback of such a configuration is that it 
inevitably couples the modulation signal into the signal propagation path (e.g., the microstrip trace 
line in our TTTL) . Moreover, to maintain a reasonable modulation strength of the varactor, the 
applied modulation voltages are typically much larger than the transmitting signal itself (otherwise 
the signal which would also add to the varactor can severely perturb the varactor’s operating point). 
Consequently, the weak transmitting signal becomes obscured by the much stronger modulation, 
making it almost impossible to isolate and exclusively observe the space-time behavior along the 
time-varying transmission line.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

5 
 
To overcome this limitation, we introduced in practice a pair of opposite polarized varactors for 
each unit cell, as shown in Fig. R1 (Fig. 4 in the manuscript), and such an arrangement serves two 
essential functions: 
1. The large , but out -of-phase modulation voltages  applied to  each varactor pair  (see the blue 
descending ramps in Fig. R1a and blue lines in Fig. R1b)  cancel out along the trace line, creating 
a relatively “quiet” environment (see the red solid and green dashed lines in Fig. R1b) for a direct 
on-line signal detection. 
2. Despite the opposite polarity, the two varactors experience an identical time-varying capacitance 
profile, enabled by the two 32 -way in -phase power divider in Fig. R1a,  thus emulating a 
homogeneous time-varying medium in the long-wavelength limit. 
 
Fig. R1. Fig. 4 in the manuscript. 
 
Such a varactor-pair configuration is the so -called “differential” modulation scheme , which is 
actually a common technique in microwave engineering. As the reviewer will appreciate, this is 
purely an implementation strategy,  and does not alter the fundamental physics of “time 
modulation”. 
Therefore, given the importance of the differential modulation deployment s, we have  used 
“differential modulation” directly in the revised manuscript , and removed the original term  
“differential time modulation” to avoid any ambiguity.

signal near the end of temporal taper, followed by gradually decaying oscillations. These 
phenomena are known as overshoot and ringing, respectively. In fact, b oth effects are typical  
artifacts introduced by voltage probes during measurement . In our setup, t he high-impedance 
passive oscillator probe causes signal reflection upon contact with the circuit. Additionally, its

inherent capacitance and parasitic inductance form a resonator network, collectively leading to the 
observed distortion in the detected waveform.  
To illustrate these effects and their origin, we conducted the following experiment and simulation. 
Fig. R3(a) displays the overshoot and ringing observed when the probe directly measures a square 
signal generated by an AWG with ideal rising/falling edges. Fig. R3(b) presents the probe model 
(the left panel), constructed based on the oscillator user guide, along with ADS simulation results 
(middle and right panels). Apparently, while the simulation shows a clean voltage at the varactor 
node (middle), the voltage waveform at a node within the probe model exhibits clear overshoot 
and ringing (right). A similar effect is reported in a recent temporal boundary experiment using 
optical modulation, where the monitor pin of the laser pulser also captured such artifacts [T. R. 
Jones et al., Time-reflection of microwaves by a fast  optically-controlled time -boundary, Nat. 
Commmun., 15, 6786, 2024, see Supplementary Fig. 4]. Importantly, this is solely a measurement 
artifact, and it does not affect the actual varactor operation (the actual modulation voltage is much 
“cleaner”) or the validity of our experimental observations.

“The descending ramp signal (τ~9.2ns) applied to the varactors was empirically optimized in the

Hou et al. have experimentally realized a 1D metamaterial waveguide which can achieve broadband wave 
antireflection via continuous temporal tapering, realized suitably synchronized voltage-controlled 
varactors. By using the temporal taper-based antireflection coating, the Authors demonstrate 
reconfigurable (i.e. agile) spatial impedance matching for various terminal loads. I find the experimental 
work very interesting and timely. However, the novelty claims on the theory are inappropriate, since the 
idea has been known in the literature for several years, as detailed below. After providing fair context to 
the existing literature, and reframing the manuscript by toning down the claims accordingly, I 
believe that the manuscript could be worthy of publication on Communications Physics.. 
 
1. The Authors are inappropriately referencing Ref [39]. The results in there account for any 
arbitrary taper, including e.g. Klopfenstein, triangular and exponential, which are all present in 
Fig. S2 of the supplementary materials of Ref [39]. The distinction attempted by the Authors: 
“Note Eq. (2) is a complete form for an arbitrary temporal taper with phase spectrum also 
included, in contrast to a treatment that mainly focuses on finite response time effects in temporal 
switching in a prior study [39]” makes no sense to me, the Authors probably did not see all of the 
results in Ref [39]. The Authors must abandon the claim of novelty of the temporal tapering 
theory, and properly cite Ref [39] in the introduction (not simply mention in the derivations) to 
provide a fair context and attribute proper credits to previous works, and pitch this paper as an 
experimental realization of the concept proposed in Ref [39]. Ultimately, I believe that the present 
experimental validation of the theory proposed in Ref [39] makes this paper worthy of publication 
anyway. 
2. Given that the concept and theory were already developed, the title is inappropriate, should read 
“Experimental realization of ...”. E.g. “Experimental realization of a full-band wave 
antireflection based on temporal taper metamaterials”. Any other claims of novelty of the 
concept should be dropped (e.g. the abstract should not claim that the concept has been 
“proposed”, but just “realized”). Also later, the false claim is made, that “All current proposed 
temporal antireflection schemes are based on ideal step-like time-varying media with abrupt 
temporal boundaries [34-37]” is incorrect. As testified by Ref [39], and also by [Silbiger, O., & 
Hadad, Y . (2023). Physical Review Applied, 19(1), 014047.], not all proposals were for step-like 
filters. 
3. What do the Authors means by “differential time modulation”, rather than simply “time 
modulation”? 
4. Figs. 2(e-f) are a bit confusing. I understand that the Authors only have experimental data for the 
tapered case, and not for the abrupt one, and it is ok to compare the results to the simulated step 
case, but then I believe it would be appropriate to plot the simulated time-reflection for the 
tapered case alongside the measured one, and perhaps having two different x-scales in panels (e) 
and (f). 
5. However, I also don’t understand why the measured time-reflection data in panels (e) and (f) 
don’t agree at low frequencies as I would have expected they should. The peak is clearly 
different. Both of them are referred to in the caption as frequency spectrum of the time-reflected 
waves but for some obscure reason they are given a different y label. 
6. The measured modulation voltage in Fig. 2a falls below the final value. Could the Authors 
explain why this is the case? 
7. It is important to keep in mind that given an arbitrarily slow taper, it is always possible to achieve 
arbitrarily broadband anti-reflection. The constraint is the limited time-window within which the 
taper acts, which one generally wants to minimize. To this end different designs, e.g. exponential
