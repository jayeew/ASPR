Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a smart pillbox–based electronic medication monitoring intervention
(DIRECT-PrEP) to improve oral PrEP adherence among men who have sex with men in China, comparing it with standard
care. Using trial data over six months, the analysis assessed costs (from health system, multipayer, and societal
perspectives) and effectiveness (QALYs and PrEP-adherence days). Results showed that the intervention produced modest
health gains (0.02 QALYs; 32.7 more adherence days) at incremental costs of about $400, yielding ICERs well below the
willingness-to-pay threshold of three times China’s GDP per capita, particularly among socially vulnerable groups,
supporting its potential as a cost-effective and equity-enhancing strategy in LMIC settings. 
I have some comments below. 
1. In Table 1, the definitions of the health system, multipayer, and societal perspectives are not entirely clear. Based on the
cost breakdown, the health system perspective ($1826.77) is limited to direct medical expenditures—drug costs, testing, and
intervention-related expenses (smart pillbox, labor, follow-up support). The multipayer perspective adds only patient-incurred
food and transportation costs ($19.23), which implicitly assumes that patients receive PrEP services free of charge beyond
these access-related expenses. The societal perspective goes further, incorporating not only time and productivity losses
($20.27) and a small learning cost for using the pillbox ($2.88), but also broader indirect system-level costs such as venue
expenditures ($76.24) and cohort maintenance costs ($8.26). Together, these additions raise the cost to approximately
$1950.76 but not exactly same (calculated as $1826.77 + $19.23 + $20.27 + $2.88 + $76.24 + $8.26 = $1953.65). It would be
helpful to note what specific costs were included for each perspective in the Supplementary Note 3 beyond just the
definitions. 
2. The authors used Seemingly Unrelated Regression (SUR) as their primary analytic method, allowing adjustment for
individual baseline covariates and accounting for correlation between costs and outcomes within the same patient. This
regression-based ICER estimation produced incremental cost and effect estimates, and bootstrapping was applied to
generate uncertainty distributions. However, authors report the system cost as a point estimate (e.g., software development
costs divided by total participants); it is difficult to distinguish which costs are fixed versus variable across different
population sizes. This matters because digital health interventions often have high fixed costs that diminish with scale,
making cost-effectiveness highly sensitive to the size of the target population. Without clarity on cost structure, the
generalizability of findings to other settings is limited. Accordingly, the analysis only accounts the uncertainty based on
patients costs but not system side cost. 
Authors may address this as a limitation or consider additional sensitivity analyses
that vary assumptions on system-side costs, in addition to patient-level variation, would better capture the potential
economies of scale inherent in digital health delivery. 
3. As the authors note, the observed adherence benefit was modest—0.02 QALYs and an increase in PrEP-adherence days
(PADs: 221.47 vs 188.75). This QALY gain was derived through an adherence-based utility mapping, where adherence
≥95% was assigned a utility of 0.95–1.00, 80–94% mapped to 0.90–0.95, and <80% mapped to 0.81–0.90. Using linear
interpolation, adherence was then linked to HIV incidence reduction, with the framework assuming complete protection at
full adherence and applying a baseline incidence of 5.6 per 100 person-years (rounded to 0.06). While this approach offers a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

structured way to translate adherence into health outcomes, the justification for the chosen utility values is unclear—
particularly the assumption that all adherence <80% falls within the relatively high 0.81–0.90 utility range. This may risk
overestimating health benefits in low-adherence groups and warrants greater transparency or sensitivity analyses to test
alternative mappings. 
4. The sensitivity analyses primarily examined statistical methods (SUR, OLS, GLM) and cost perspectives (health system,
multipayer, societal), but the differences across perspectives were minimal—multipayer added only patient
food/transportation costs, while societal added modest productivity losses—resulting in only marginal changes in ICERs.
More critical sources of uncertainty likely lie in system-level factors: how health systems adapt to implementing digital
interventions in different settings, how cost-effectiveness changes with population scale given fixed costs, and whether the
observed adherence difference (221.47 vs 188.75 PADs) can be sustained over the long term. Highlighting these structural
uncertainties as limitations would strengthen the discussion, as they may have greater implications for generalizability. 
5. The paper claims to incorporate “behavioral economic principles” by contrasting electronic medication monitors’
adherence-mediated prevention benefits with SMS interventions’ focus on risk-behavior modification. However, the trial
design and economic model evaluate only adherence outcomes (PADs, QALYs) and do not measure or compare
downstream behavioral risk changes such as sexual risk compensation or reductions in risky behaviors. As a result, the use
of “behavioral economics” feels more rhetorical than methodological: the study does not empirically test behavioral
constructs but rather frames adherence versus behavior-modification strategies as distinct economic logics.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The research investigates a critical economic analysis related to oral PrEP adherence in the MSM population. Focusing on
equity is of utmost importance, considering that those in lower socioeconomic strata are disproportionately impacted by HIV.
The methodology is generally sound and appropriately applied, however, certain critical areas warrant attention, and the
paper does not meet the rigorous standards for Nature Communications. 
Major comments: 
1. The trial is China-specific, and while often considered a LMIC in public health research, the health system differences
may limit generalizability to regions like Sub-Saharan Africa. And especially since the digitial infrastructure differ
significantly from many LMICs. This limitation should be acknowledged consistently throughout the manuscript, not just in
the discussion. 
2. The use of Seemingly Unrelated Regression (SUR) and bootstrap simulations is unconventional for health economic
modeling. While SUR can account for correlated outcomes, it is not standard for cost-effectiveness analysis, which typically
relies on decision-analytic models. Can the authors justify why SUR was chosen over more established modelling
methods? Also explain its limitations compared to more dynamic or long-term modelling. 
3. The conclusion that "intervention complexity" is positively associated with equitable health coverage is not directly
supported by the literature review presented in the “Comparison with existing Literature” paragraph, especially since the
authors are trying to make this claim by comparing across different settings, time horizons, years. However, this
interpretation oversimplifies the literature and possibly conflates methodological differences with causal relationships. The
authors should either reframe this as a hypothesis or provide empirical justification. 
4. While the study references willingness to pay (WTP) thresholds to interpret cost-effectiveness, it does not discuss their

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

origin, appropriateness, or limitation, especially in the context of equity. WTP thresholds are often arbitrary and often fail to
reflect societal values or budget constraints in LMICs. The authors could strengthen their analysis by discussing alternative
approaches (e.g., opportunity cost-based thresholds, equity-weighted thresholds). 
Minor comments: 
1. The study focuses on MSM in China but does not engage with broader literature on PrEP adherence among MSM
globally. There is little discussion of behavioral, structural, or psychosocial factors that influence adherence, nor of
intersectional vulnerabilities such as stigma, income, or housing. This limits the depth of the equity framing and the
relevance of findings to other MSM populations. 
2. Personnel costs are the main driver of the intervention budget (>76%). This raises questions about scalability and whether
similar staffing models are feasible in other LMICs. 
3. PADs are a useful intermediate metric, but their translation into long-term HIV prevention outcomes could be better
contextualized. The study uses perfect adherence as a binary threshold, yet imperfect adherence still conveys partial
protection and this is not accounted for in the modelling. A more nuanced approach is needed. Perhaps modeling
adherence as a gradient would better reflect real-world effectiveness. 
4. The reported probability range for cost-effectiveness, eg. 2.71% to 98.99%, is extremely broad, limiting its practicality. This
suggests high sensitivity to input assumptions or model structure. The authors should clarify which scenarios drive the lower
bound and consider presenting threshold analyses to guide decision-makers more effectively. 
5. The stratification by SDH is descriptive and limited to cost-effectiveness outcomes. The study does not explore underlying
mechanisms or contextual factors that explain why cost-effectiveness differs by SDH strata—thus falling short of a deeper
equity analysis. 
6. The claim that smart pillboxes "critically extend" existing evidence is not fully substantiated. The comparison of
incremental costs ($19,844 vs. $24,705 for SMS) lacks context—are these figures derived from comparable methodologies,
time horizons, and costing perspectives? 
7. The critique of other digital tools (SMS, apps, hybrid models) is broad and somewhat selective. For example, citing
$1021/user for a U.S. MSM app without adjusting for context or scale may unfairly skew the comparison. 
8. Granted that English might not be the first language for the authors, however, spelling errors could be avoided by using a
spell checker, e.g. hightened=heightened; siginificant=significant; coverd=covered; 
trail-wide=trial-wide; to name a few.
