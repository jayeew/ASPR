Reviewer #1 (Comments for the Author): 
In my first review, I pointed out that “an assessment of the real relevance of this new 
characteristic (as well as the system as a whole) for devising new therapies with respected to 
existing alternatives is beyond my expertise.” Thus, I focused my second review on the claims 
related to the nanozyme reactivity and the originality of its design. Specifically, I had expressed 
two key concerns related to the novelty related to the nanozyme activity of the FTn-Pd system. 
First, whether the reactivity was in fac t due to the Pd-FTn complex, or Pd(II) cations released 
in water were responsible for the activity reported. Second, if any additional rationale was used 
in the design of FTn-Pd’s hydrolytic activity introduced. The revision presented by Hu and co-
workers did addressed my previous concerns partially. 
Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for carefully considering our revised manuscript 
and for focusing on the mechanistic and design aspects of the FTn-Pd system. We fully 
recognize the importance of clarifying these issues. Below, we respond to each of the reviewer’s 
concerns in detail. 
Regarding the first concern, the authors provided consistent evidence that the Pd is bound to 
FTn during the hydrolytic reaction, supporting their claims and conclusion regarding the GrB-
mimicking nanozymatic activity. I would just encourage the authors to include the “data for 
reviewer 1” in the paper (f. ex. Fig 2h). 
Response: We thank you for acknowledging that the new da ta (now included as Fig. 2h ) 
supports the conclusion that Pd remains coordinated to the FTn scaffold during the hydrolytic 
reaction. We have now integrated this dataset into the main manuscript as suggested. 
The second concern was also clarified, but the a rguments presented in the point -by-point 
response revealed that the authors did not apply any specific method to rationally design the 
catalytic sites responsible for the hydrolytic activity. In terms of catalytic activity of the FTn -
Pd nanozyme, the innov ation presented is a clever combination of previously known 
components in the literature. The assessment that the hydrolytic activity is unexpected and 
unique is, in my opinion, not convincing. Further, the current draft of the manuscript presents 
still only vague explanations on the choice of the ferritin-Pd combination. 
Response: We acknowledge that the hydrolytic activity arises from a combination of known 
components, rather than from a de novo design of active sites. While we did not follow a 
structure-based design strategy for catalysis, our focus was on establishing a modular a nd 
biocompatible nanozyme system with in vivo applicability. We have clarified this point in the 
revised Discussion and reframed our description of novelty to emphasize system -level 
integration rather than intrinsic catalytic innovation. 
The remainder of my concerns, which were in line with the ones from other reviewers, were in 
my opinion sufficiently addressed with revisions and the point-by-point response. Particularly, 
I enjoyed the authors arguments and new results related to the structure -dependent cleavage 
selectivity, which given the biochemical tools available nowadays, poses an interesting 
perspective for the nanozyme field. Likewise, the incorporation of a nanozyme in a delivery 
system and the demonstration that the reactivity once established at the molecular level can be 
engineered to be delivered in vivo is indeed an interesting demonstration of the potential of 
nanozymes. While the molecular innovation from the nanozyme reactivity perspective is not

evaluation of our work. In response, we have incorporated additional results and clarifications

1. Even though their initial design was not aimed at generating a GrB-mimicking nanozyme, it 
is reasonable to expand the investigation based on their unexpected dis covery that FTn -Pd 
exhibits caspase-cleaving activity when screening the Ac -IETD-pNA substrate. However, the 
ensuing data sets are insufficient to support FTN -PD as a GrB-mimicking nanozyme. Natural 
GrB is known to cleave pro caspase-3, -7, -8 and Bid. Yet with recombinant protein substrates, 
FTN-BD is shown to cleave only pro caspase-3, but has minimal or no cleavage activity towards 
caspase-7, caspase -8 and Bid, and why the lack of effect on other known substrates is not 
discussed. On the other hand, pro caspase-3 is also cleaved and activated by the initiator 
caspases caspase-8 and caspase -9. Figure 2d using recombinant proteins attempted to prove 
FTN-PD’s GrB-like activity by using a GrB inhibitor, Z -IETD-FMK, which is not specific to 
GrB and known to in hibit caspase-8. Even worse, Figure 5a used tumor cells to show that a 
pan-caspase inhibitor, Z-VAD-FMK, completely blocked FNP-antiHER2 induced cleavage of 
caspase-3 and PARP, strongly suggesting that what FNP possesses is caspase-like activity, not 
GrB-like activity. Unfortunately, in the point -by-point response, the pan -caspase inhibitor, Z-
VAD-FMK, is wrongfully cited as a GrB inhibitor, raising concern about the expertise of the 
authors on granzyme and caspase biology, as well as whether the title of t his work can 
accurately summarize the reported data. 
Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for the thoughtful and professional assessment. In 
our study, FTn-Pd is designated as a “GrB-mimicking nanozyme ” based on three lines of 
evidence: (i) it specifically cleaves the same synthetic substrates as natural GrB; (ii) it directly 
cleaves pro-caspase-3, a well-established GrB substrate; and (iii) its cleavage activity toward 
pro-caspase-3 is substantially blocked by GrB-specific inhibitors (now included in the revised 
Fig. 2d ). We fully agree with the reviewer that natural GrB cleaves multiple substrates, 
including pro-caspase-3, -7, -8, and Bid. In contrast, FTn-Pd displayed selective activity toward 
pro-caspase-3 but did not cleave caspase -7, caspas e-8, or Bid. This restricted substrate 
specificity indicates that FTn-Pd does not replicate the complete enzymatic repertoire of natural 
GrB. In other words, while both FTn-Pd and native GrB catalyze pro-caspase-3 cleavage, their 
catalytic profiles are not  entirely identical. Importantly, FTn-Pd induces tumor cell apoptosis 
primarily through pro -caspase-3 cleavage, representing a functionally tailored property with 
direct therapeutic relevance for tumor treatment. Accordingly, and as clarified in our previo us 
revision, we revised the terminology from “artificial GrB ” to “GrB-mimicking nanozyme, ” 
which we believe more accurately describes its functional profile while acknowledging the 
differences from both natural and artificial enzymes.  We also consider it reasonable that FTn-
Pd cannot fully reproduce the enzymatic spectrum of GrB, given the distinct catalytic active 
sites involved ( active-site analysis and discussion have been added to the revised

As the Reviewer #4 mentioned, we would also like to highlight four unique aspects of this work 
in the context of nanozyme development: i) Nanozyme-specific properties: Unlike conventional 
artificial enzymes, FTn-Pd nanozymes exhibit superior stability across a wide range of pH and 
temperatures, can be readily produced at scale using recombinant FTn, and allow facile 
multifunctionalization through genetic engineering. This offers practical advantages compared 
to natural GrB.  ii) Mechanistic insight: By integrating protein crystallography, molecular 
modeling, and nanoscale design, we established —for the first time —a structurally resolved 
framework to understand the catalytic mechanism of protein -metal hybrid nanozymes. 
Specifically, we identified a binuclear Pd catalytic center coordinated by cysteine, methionine, 
and histidine residues, along with water molecules. This structural insight provides a paradigm 
for decoding biomolecular -nanomaterial interactions at atomic resolution, going beyond the 
material-focused approaches typical of previous nanozyme studies.  iii) Catalytic active site 
composition: Distinct from the active sites of nativ e or artificial GrB  (new supplementary 
Figure 3h), the catalytic center of FTn -Pd comprises two Pd atoms coordinated by residues 
M37, C90, C102, and H105 of the FTn scaffold. We found that b oth the Pd atoms and the 
protein scaffold play essential roles in catalyzing GrB substrates, with the protein framework 
facilitating substrate accessibility and the Pd atoms providing the hydrolytic activity. This 
architecture closely resembles metalloprotease active sites. Accordingly, while FTn-Pd mimics 
GrB by selectively cleaving caspase-3 to induce apoptosis, it is reasonable that its activity does 
not extend to the full substrate spectrum of natural GrB (i.e., pro-caspase-3, -7, -8, and Bid). iv) 
Previous studies have shown that Pd(II) complexes, such as Pd(en)(H 2O2), possess hydrolase 
activity; however, their specific incorporation into the FTn framework in this study confers a 
unique protease -like activity not observed in earlier systems. Notably, compared with the 
catalytic activity of FTn -Pd toward GrB substrates , Pd(en)(H2O2) displays negligible activity. 
Together with structural analyses and the proposed catalytic mechanism, these findings 
demonstrate that both the Pd centers and the protein scaffold are indispensable for enzymatic 
function. 
Regarding inhibitor specificity, we agree with the reviewer that Z -IETD-FMK is not GrB -
specific and can inhibit caspase -8. To strengthen our claim, we have now validated FTn -Pd’s 
activity using two GrB -specific inhibitors —human Serpin B9 recombinant protein 1 and Z -
AAD-CMK2—both of which significantly reduced FTn-Pd-mediated cleavage of recombinant 
caspase-3 (new Figure 2d). This provides more direct evidence of its GrB-mimicking activity. 
Importantly, we emphasize that the in vitro assays with recombinant proteins are designed to 
probe GrB-mimicking activity, whereas the cellular assays demonstrate intracellular delivery 
and caspase-3 cleavage, without attributing specificity to GrB, given the complex interplay with 
initiator caspases such as caspase-8 and caspase-9. 
We sincerely apologize for the misstatement in our original response letter, where Z-V AD-FMK 
was inadvertently described as a GrB inhibitor. As correctly stated in the manuscript, Z -V AD-
FMK is a pan-caspase inhibitor; this error in the response text was unintentional and we regret 
the confusion it caused. 
Finally, we would like to stress that the central goal of this study was to emulate the functional 
property of GrB delivery into tumor cells for inducing apoptosis, not to fully replicate its natural 
substrate spectrum. FTn -Pd reproduces the critical feature of GrB —entry into tumor cells

Response: We thank the reviewer for raising these critical points regarding technical rigor and 
data presentation. All key cell -based assays were performed in at least three independent
