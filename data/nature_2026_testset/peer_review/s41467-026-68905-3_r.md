Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an innovative approach to neuromorphic vision by demonstrating homogeneous integration of
MoS
₂
-based optoelectronic leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons and ferroelectric synapses. The authors successfully
achieved key neuronal features including multispectral optical sensing, capacitor-less membrane potential integration,
threshold-triggered spiking with automatic reset, and intrinsic stochasticity. The demonstration of a co-integrated neuron-
synapse array that unifies volatile optical encoding with non-volatile weight storage on a single material platform represents
a noteworthy contribution to the field. The experimental work is systematic and comprehensive, with the system achieving
recognition accuracies of 91.7% and 93.5% for color recognition and object detection, respectively. The concept of
homogeneous integration addresses important challenges in neuromorphic hardware development. Overall, this work
advances the understanding of 2D material-based neuromorphic devices and merits publication after addressing the
following minor concerns. 
1. The manuscript emphasizes "multispectral optical sensing" as a key feature, but the demonstrated wavelength-dependent
response primarily stems from the intrinsic absorption characteristics of MoS
₂
 rather than a novel wavelength-selective
mechanism. The device exhibits different photoresponse at 450 nm, 520 nm, and 650 nm due to the material's absorption
spectrum (Fig. 2b) but cannot simultaneously distinguish multiple wavelengths. Given that conventional CMOS sensors
(Silicon) with color filter arrays already provide mature, cost-effective RGB detection in the visible spectrum, could the
authors comment on extending this approach to infrared wavelengths where 2D materials offer more distinct advantages?
Have the authors considered replacing MoS
₂
 with infrared-sensitive 2D materials, such as infrared heterostructures (e.g.,
Adv. Sci. 2025, 2503492), or other narrow-bandgap materials, to enable detection in spectral regions where silicon-based
sensors face fundamental limitations? I suggest the author provides a short discussion in this section. 
2. While this work presents an innovative proof-of-concept, there exists a substantial performance gap compared to
commercial neuromorphic hardware that should be openly discussed. The integration time is orders of magnitude slower
than commercial chips with microsecond-scale response, the energy consumption significantly exceeds mature systems
(picojoule-range), the 4×4 array is far from million-neuron scales, and mechanical exfoliation fundamentally limits scalability.
So, the manuscript would benefit from adding one to two paragraphs that provide quantitative comparisons with commercial
neuromorphic hardware and discuss the strategies for future improvement, including approaches to enhance response
speed and reduce energy consumption, scalable fabrication methods beyond mechanical exfoliation (CVD growth, wafer-
scale synthesis), long-term stability considerations, and a realistic development roadmap with near-term (1-3 years) and
medium-term (5-10 years) goals. This discussion would better serve the research community by honestly assessing both the
promise and current limitations of 2D neuromorphic devices. 
3. Figure 3b and 3c are missing scale bars, please add appropriate scale bars to both figures. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author)

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

Wang et al. reported “Homogeneous integration of 2D material-based optoelectronic neurons and ferroelectric synapses for
neuromorphic vision”. The authors demonstrated MoS2 FET based optoelectronic neurons and synapses, integrating them
to realize a MAC operation. The study presents a potentially feasible neuron synapse system for spiking neural networks,
capable of performing object level recognition, which is an essential functionality for in sensor computing applications.
However, the specific advantage and necessity of employing 2D FETs remain unclear, and the correlation between
optoelectronic neuron spikes and the LTP/LTD behavior of synapses is not convincingly established. In particular, it is
difficult to understand how the MoS2 phototransistor based neuron can discriminate both wavelength and optical power
density variations. Therefore, I cannot recommend this manuscript for publication in Nature Communications. Detailed
comments are provided below. 
1. The authors employ MoS2 FETs for both neuron and synapse units. Here, the MoS2 FET in neuron generates a
wavelength- and intensity-dependent photocurrent, and that in synapse exhibits current modulation governed by the
ferroelectric polarization in HZO layer. However, it is unclear why MoS2 layer was specifically chosen for this purpose.
Since most neuronal functions are implemented by the circuit and the synaptic behavior primarily originates from the
ferroelectric properties of the HZO layer, similar behaviors could be achievable with conventional silicon FETs. Therefore,
the authors should clarify the reason for using 2D material based FETs in this study. 
2. In Figure 2b, the absorbance spectrum of MoS2 and the corresponding photocurrent are compared. Typically, a higher
absorbance results in a stronger photocurrent. However, the data show that although the absorbance at 520nm is the lowest,
the photocurrent at 650nm is the weakest. The authors should explain the reason for this difference. 
3. The authors claim that the optoelectronic neuron can distinguish both the wavelength and intensity of light. This assertion
seems somewhat exaggerated. Since both frequency and triggering timing are mainly determined by the photocurrent level
of the MoS2 PT, the authors must demonstrate that the photocurrent levels are sufficiently separated to allow unambiguous
discrimination between different wavelengths and power levels. 
4. In Figure 4, the authors demonstrate a MAC operation. However, the role of rate coding and TTFS coding within this
operation is not evident. Since Figure 5 presents object recognition results based on these two encoding schemes, the MAC
operation should also explicitly reflect how rate and TTFS coding contribute to the computation. 
5. Figure 3 presents the synaptic characteristics under electrical pulse stimulation, but it remains unclear how these weight
updates are linked to neuronal spike activity. In general, synaptic weight modulation in SNNs depends on the relative spike
timing between pre- and post-synaptic neurons (so-called STDP). However, since only the pre-synaptic neuron is
implemented in this work, a detailed discussion on how the weight update (particularly the LTD process) is achieved is
essential. 
6. Supplementary Note 2 provides the power consumption of the neuron and synapse devices individually. While these
numbers are informative, it is difficult to assess the overall energy efficiency at the system level. A system-level power
consumption analysis compared with representative conventional systems would strengthen the claim of energy efficiency in
the proposed architecture. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, the authors present an in-sensor computing framework that achieves the homogeneous integration of
optoelectronic neurons and ferroelectric synapses on a MoS2-based platform. The optoelectronic neurons exhibit full LIF
neuronal dynamics, and the ferroelectric synapses show excellent nonvolatile characteristics. The concept of achieving
optical sensing, spike encoding, weight storage, and computation within a single platform is both novel and technically
significant. However, several descriptions in the manuscript lack sufficient discussion, affecting its clarity. Therefore, I
suggest a minor revision before its publication, based on the following comments. 
1.The realization of optoelectronic LIF neuron dynamics is an interesting work. The manuscript should include a detailed
discussion on how the spike voltage and threshold voltage in the neuron circuit influence the dynamics and performance of
the LIF neuron. In addition, it is necessary for the authors to provide photographs of the optoelectronic neuron measurement
setup. 
2.In Figure 3, the MFMIS structure of the proposed FeFET device shows that the ferroelectric layer and the semiconductor
channel are not vertically stacked. Does this configuration affect the device characteristics? Some discussion on this point
would be appreciated. 
3.The programming voltage in the LTP–LTD tests of the FeFET is variable. The authors need to provide more programming
data and discuss the results. 
4.In the manuscript, the authors demonstrate an optoelectronic SNN using a small-scale neuron–synapse array. While this
scale is suitable as a research prototype at the current stage, evaluating the feasibility of scaling the array to larger
dimensions is important for future technological development. The authors need to discuss the prospects of large-scale
integration of two-dimensional materials, which would provide useful guidance for future research. 
5.In Figure 4h, the accuracy increases in a stepwise rather than a continuous form. The authors should provide an
explanation for this behavior.

Response: 
Thank you for your constructive comments. You are correct  that the optical 
response of our device is primarily governed by the intrinsic absorption characteristics 
of MoS2. In this work, our primary aim was not to develop a novel wavelength-selective 
mechanism, but rather to demonstrate the homogeneous integration and optoelectronic 
synergy between the 2D material -based optoelectronic neuron and the ferroelectric 
synapse. The focus of our research is on achieving optoelectronic neuron dynamics and 
modulating ferroelectric plasticity. That said, we fully agree with you on the importance 
of wavelength selectivity for advanced vision- inspired systems. Given that our device 
platform inherently supports integrated optoelectronic functionality, we plan to further 
explore enhanced spectral discrimination in future work. For example, this could be 
achieved by integrating light -absorbing materials into the existing device channel to 
enable wavelength-sensitive response or broaden the spectral response range.
  
In response to your suggestion regarding the simultaneous recognition of multiple 
wavelengths, we were inspired by conventional silicon- based CMOS imaging and 
spectral detection schemes1,2 and proposed a scalable pixel architecture. As shown in 
Figure R1a, each pixel unit integrates three sub -pixels, each capped with a dedicated 
color filter (red, green, or blue) to selectively respond to different wavelength bands of 
incident light. Under illumination, the optoelectronic neurons corresponding to the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

three subpixels generate spikes with  different frequenc y, achieving parallel 
multispectral responses and neural encoding. 
We validated this architecture’s ability to discriminate RGB colors under fixed 
light intensity through simulations. Specifically, we constructed a feed-forward spiking 
neural network (SNN) with a size of 48×7 and fed the three-channel spike trains into it 
as input. The training dataset includes seven typical color combinations (red, green, 
blue, yellow, cyan, magenta, and white) and four-digit classes (0, 1, 4, and 7), totaling 
28 samples (Figure R1b). The results showed that the system achieved a recognition 
accuracy of 96.43%, as shown in Figure R1c. Building on this, future work could focus 
on researching independent light intensity variation encoding for each color. This would 
enable the system not only to distinguish between different colors but also to encode 
additional information based on variations in light intensity for each individual color.
  
Moreover, as you noted, 2D material systems can achieve optical detection in the 
infrared spectral regions, thereby overcoming the limitations inherent to conventional 
silicon-based materials.  In recent years, 2D material systems such as InSe, black 
phosphorus, narrow -bandgap chalcogenides (e.g., Bi 2Te3-Sb2Te3), and MoTe 2/WSe2 
heterostructures have exhibited excellent photoresponse performance across the visible 
to mid-infrared range
3,4. Compared with silicon-based materials, these narrow-bandgap 
or heterojunction 2D materials offer more efficient light absorption and charge 
separation. Therefore, replacing the MoS
2 channel used in this study with such 
materials could further broaden the spectral coverage and enhance the dimensionality 
of optical sensing and neural encoding, thereby endowing the platform with stronger 
multispectral information processing capabilities.

Thank you for your valuable comments. In this work, we do not use the traditional 
STDP method for synaptic weight updates. Instead, we adopt a training approach based 
on backpropagation combined with surrogate gradients1. The surrogate gradient method 
is an approximate technique that allows backpropagation through non- differentiable 
spiking neurons, enabling end- to-end training. Specifically, the spiking activity of 
neurons is backpropagated to the synaptic layer to adjus t synaptic weights, thereby 
controlling the network’s learning process. In this way, weight updates do not rely on 
postsynaptic spike timing differences (as in traditional STDP), but are based on the 
gradient of the loss function, allowing STDP -like synaptic behavior to be emulated 
without directly depending on spike timing. 
Regarding the implementation of LTP (long- term potentiation) and LTD (long-
term depression), these processes are realized through weight changes induced by 
positive and negative gradients of the loss during backpropagation. When there is a 
discrepancy betw een a neuron’s output and the desired output, the backpropagation 
algorithm adjusts the weights according to the loss gradient and uses surrogate gradients 
to approximate the gradient of the spiking neuron, enabling dynamic synaptic weight 
updates. Specifi cally, LTP corresponds to weight increases induced by positive 
gradients of the loss; LTD corresponds to weight decreases induced by negative 
gradients.
