Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In previous studies, the authors showed that YM101, a TGF-β×PD-L1 bispecific antibody (BsAb), exhibits enhanced anti-
tumor activity when combined with an oral STING agonist. In the present study, they investigated the mechanism underlying
this synergistic effect and found that the enhanced anti-tumor effect is mediated by CXCL16-CXCR6 signaling. The authors
show that STING activation and TGF-β inhibition promote CXCL16 expression in macrophages and dendritic cells,
recruiting and sustaining cytotoxic CXCR6+ T cells. The authors also identified STAT1 as a key transcription factor
regulating CXCL16 expression. Given these results, the authors further developed a trifunctional ADC targeting TGF-β, PD-
L1, and STING. This ADC demonstrated superior tumor control and immune modulation over YM101 alone in preclinical
models. 
Overall, the manuscript is well-written, and the data largely support the conclusions, but several points should be addressed
to strengthen the study. 
Main points: 
1. In addition to the selected pathways in Fig.1D, please provide an unbiased GSEA (or equivalent) comparing responders
versus non-responders, with clear ranking metrics and FDR thresholds. The previous study by these authors have already
shown enhanced effect when combining STING agonist and YM101, but unbiased analysis is needed to justify the rational
in this study. 
2. The data in Fig. 2B suggest NK cell involvement. Please clarify whether NK cells contribute to the enhanced antitumor
effect (e.g., depletion experiments, NK activation markers, or NK-specific functional assays). If NK cells are implicated,
discuss how their role intersects with the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis. 
3. In Fig.2K, the authors showed that combination therapy results in the downregulation of many signaling pathways in
cancer cells. This needs further analysis, or at least discussion, to discern effects on cancer cells versus effects on the tumor
microenvironment. 
4. The authors used a concentration of 6.6 mg/kg for the isotype control, anti-TGFb, and anti-PDL1 (P) antibodies but 9
mg/kg for the YM101 (Y) antibody. Yet, the authors present the treatments of these antibodies as comparable, especially
when highlighting that M+Y had a superior anti-tumor effect and immune activation compared to M+P (Fig. 1H-M; Fig. 4A).
These results could also be explained by the higher dosage of Y compared to P, rather than solely by TGFb blockade. 
5. The ADC data is incomplete without the images of tumor sections stained for cancer cells and T cells. 
6. What advantage does this trifunctional ADC have over the combination of STING agonist and YM101? The preclinical
data suggest that the two treatments are similarly effective. Is there any scenario that the triple-targeting ADC might be
superior? 
Minor issues: 
1. The scale of heat maps need to be defined accurately rather than “High to Low”. 
2. More citations should be included to better acknowledge pervious work by others. 
3. Key details are missing in the methods section, such as the delivery method of MSA-2, and the duration of the in vitro
treatment in Fig.7. 
4. Key details are missing in the method of Y101S ADC treatment including the concentration used in in vivo experiments. 
5. Fig. 7A seems to be partially missing.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Yi at al., reports a strong, comprehensive, and mechanistically deep study. The authors present a
compelling rationale for moving beyond dual TGF-β/PD-L1 blockade by identifying innate immune insufficiency as a key
resistance mechanism. The discovery of the CXCL16-CXCR6 axis as the central mediator of the synergy between a STING
agonist and their bispecific antibody (YM101) is novel and significant. The translational leap to develop and test a first-in-
class triple-targeting ADC (Y101S) is supported by the mechanistic data. The manuscript is well-written, and the
experimental evidence, spanning in vitro models, extensive in vivo work, single-cell and bulk omics, and biochemical
assays, is robust and generally convincing. The development of Y101S is very innovative. Converting a complex three-drug
combination into a single ADC is a sophisticated strategy that addresses practical challenges of combination therapy (e.g.,
pharmacokinetic differences, dosing schedules, off-target toxicity). The demonstrated efficacy and preliminary safety profile
of Y101S in multiple mouse models are highly promising and justify further clinical development. While the overall quality of
the study is outstanding, a few points require clarification or represent potential weaknesses that should be addressed: 
- All data are from murine models and murine cell lines. The relevance of the CXCL16-CXCR6 axis in human cancers and
the functionality of human CXCR6
⁺
 T cells, while supported by some TCGA correlation analysis, is not experimentally
validated. A key question is whether human myeloid cells respond similarly to STING/TGF-β modulation to produce
CXCL16. Including in vitro data using human macrophages/DCs derived from healthy donors or patients (as they did for
some PCR/ELISA) to show the same STAT1-dependent CXCL16 induction upon STING activation and its suppression by
TGF-β would significantly strengthen the human translational message; 
- The drug-antibody ratio for Y101S is not reported. This is a critical quality attribute for any ADC, as it impacts efficacy,
stability, and potential toxicity; 
- While the linker is designed to be cleavable, no data is presented demonstrating that MSA-2 is effectively released
intracellularly in the TME or that the conjugate is stable in circulation. This is important to prove the ADC functions as
designed; 
- A discussion on whether the released MSA-2 can affect neighbouring cells (a bystander effect) would be interesting,
though perhaps beyond the scope of this paper. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a well-executed and thoroughly described study investigating the antitumor effects of triple modulation of the TGFβ,
PD-L1, and STING pathways in various murine tumor models. Through a series of in-depth in vivo and ex vivo experiments
—including selective gene knock-out and adoptive cell transfer—the authors provide compelling evidence that this specific
triple-targeting strategy significantly controls tumor growth and prolongs survival in mice, compared to single or dual-agent
combinations. However, several major concerns weaken the study’s conclusions and, more importantly, limit the
assessment of the clinical translatability of this approach. A central issue is the concept of "resistance" to TGFβ/PD-L1
blockade, which is based on evidence that is not entirely convincing. This is further compounded by the relatively limited
immunological characterization of responding tumors, both locally and systemically. In addition, the study would have been
more original and impactful if the full experimental dataset had employed the novel antibody–drug conjugate (ADC)
YM101S, developed by the authors. Unfortunately, YM101S was used only in the final set of experiments, while earlier parts
of the study relied on combinations of separate agents. 
Major Points: 
• A key concern lies in the rationale of the study, which is based on the experiments shown in Figure 1. In these
experiments, the variable response rate observed in EMT6-bearing mice treated with YM101 + MAS-2 was used to identify
the CXCR6/CXCL16 axis as a primary mechanism to overcome resistance to PD-L1 and TGFβ blockade. However, I remain
not fully convinced that inbred mice bearing the same tumor can adequately model heterogeneous responses to
immunotherapy. These mice lack the natural genetic and environmental variability that characterizes patient populations and
drives differential treatment responses. The assumption that tumors exhibiting less than 20% regression upon treatment can
be considered "resistant" in a clinically relevant sense is not fully justified. More appropriate models for investigating
immunotherapy response and resistance mechanisms, such as outbred mice, humanized models, or patient-derived
xenografts (PDX), should have been used to address this specific aim. Furthermore, the experiment in Figure 1 that
identifies “sensitive” versus “resistant” tumors is based on a small sample size (9 animals total: 6 responders and 3 non-
responders). This limited number likely lacks sufficient statistical power to support the conclusions drawn, thus undermining
the biological significance of the proposed resistance mechanism. 
Given these limitations, the authors should consider reframing the focus of the study. Rather than centering the work around
strategies to overcome resistance to YM101, it would be more appropriate to highlight the efficacy of the triple-targeting
approach and the associated mechanistic insights. Alternatively, if resistance remains the central theme, the authors must
provide stronger evidence that tumors with <20% regression (in a statistically meaningful sample size) truly represent
intrinsically resistant lesions, and explain how such resistance could arise in an inbred model. 
• It is not entirely clear why the CXCR6/CXCL16 axis was initially selected as the key target, considering it is not among the
top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Figures 1E and 1F. Other upregulated pathways, such as Nkg7 and Kird1 (both
NK cell markers) were also prominent in the ‘sensitive’ tumors shown in Figure 2K. Please provide a stronger rationale for

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

prioritizing CXCR6/CXCL16. Similarly, it would be interesting knowing whether other potentially relevant immune subsets
such as NK cells or CD4
⁺
 Th1 cells (which may also express CXCR6) could also contribute to the overall antitumor effect,
particularly in light with the data showing that CXCR6+CD8+ T cells do not apparently mediate the same number of
complete responses when re-injected into CXCR6 ko mice, as depicted in figure 5C. 
• While the therapeutic activity of the YM101 + MAS-2 combination is indeed promising, particularly in comparison to single
agents or other dual combinations, it is notable that the percentage of complete responses never exceeds 50%. Although the
substantial tumor regression is encouraging, one might expect full clearance in such a well-controlled murine setting. How
do the authors interpret these data? Could the persistence of residual tumors be due to immunoediting or alternative escape
mechanisms? 
• Since one of the primary advantages of immunotherapy is the induction of long-term protection via immune memory, it
would be crucial to determine whether treatment with YM101 + MAS-2 induces protection upon tumor re-challenge in mice
that achieved complete regression. Are any such data available? 
• The YM101 + MAS-2 approach appears effective across multiple murine tumor models (EMT6, CT26, 4T1, MC38), which
significantly strengthens the study. However, it would be important to describe the immune contexture and antigenicity of
each model. Are these tumors considered “hot” or “cold”? This distinction has critical implications for clinical translation. If
PD-L1 expression on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) is required to initiate the immune cascade, it would
presumably be more readily achieved in hot tumors. Relatedly, can PD-L1 expressed by tumor cells replicate this effect,
given that tumor cells may also secrete CXCL16? Addressing this point is essential, especially in light of the fact that many
human cancers are PD-L1 negative. Additionally, it would be valuable to determine whether CXCR6/CXCL16 plays a
consistent role in tumor regression across other models beyond EMT6, as briefly mentioned in Figure 8. 
• It would be relevant to demonstrate the added value of the YM101S triple-targeting ADC, beyond improving patient
compliance. Specifically, is it necessary for all three immune-modulating components to be present simultaneously at the
tumor site to achieve therapeutic efficacy? The authors could strengthen their argument by showing that TGFβ and PD-L1
are co-expressed by TAMs in “cold” tumors, and by demonstrating in vivo upregulation of CXCL16 following PD-L1/TGFβ
blockade. Do these effects occur exclusively within the TME, or are other organs affected as well? Furthermore, it would be
important to directly compare the ability of the YM101S construct versus the free triple combination to convert cold tumors
into hot ones, within the same murine model. 
• The impact of CXCL16 neutralization shown in Figure 6, completely abolishing T cell activation, is striking. Has such a
dominant role for this chemokine in T cell activity been described previously? Please discuss in relation to existing literature.
• Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) are typically designed to enhance tumor specificity while limiting off-target toxicity, by
delivering cytotoxic payloads via antibodies targeting tumor-associated antigens. In this context, it would be highly
interesting to discuss the specific target(s) that YM101S is designed to recognize within the tumor microenvironment (e.g.,
PD-L1 on TAMs). However, as PD-L1 is not exclusively tumor-specific, this may raise translational challenges. A discussion
on this aspect, along with how the triple-targeting design may overcome known limitations of STING agonists, such as poor
bioavailability, permeability, or resistance, as highlighted in Shi et al., The Innovation 2025, would be appreciated. 
• Literature indicates a dual role for CXCL16, which can support either anti- or pro-tumor activity depending on the
spatiotemporal context (e.g. Chia et al., Front. Immunol. 2024). A brief discussion of this complexity, ideally supported by
clinical or translational data (e.g., immunotherapy response in breast cancer patients with CXCL16/CXCR6 axis activation),
would help contextualize the findings and strengthen the study's relevance to human disease. 
• In Figure 2K, the authors propose that CD8
⁺
 T cell-mediated cytotoxicity underlies the antitumor effect observed with
YM101 + MAS-2. However, the gene expression data indicate enrichment for general cancer hallmarks rather than specific
cell death–related pathways (e.g., apoptosis, necroptosis). Could the therapeutic effect instead be cytostatic or promote
tumor cell differentiation? Please comment on this alternative possibility. 
Minor Points 
• The timing of tumor microenvironment (TME) analyses, both at the RNA and protein level, should be clearly stated. Were
these performed always 17 days after the initial injection? Were the analyzed tumors selected based on their regressing
phenotype? Precise timing and selection criteria are essential for contextualizing the immunological findings. 
• ADCs are typically developed to deliver cytotoxic agents to tumors via antigen-targeting antibodies. Since YM101S does
not carry a cytotoxic payload but modulates immune signaling, it would be more appropriate to describe it as an
“immunological ADC” or “immunomodulatory ADC.” 
• It should be clearly stated that most experiments were conducted using a bispecific antibody targeting PD-L1 and TGFβ, in
combination with a STING agonist. The triple-targeting construct YM101S was introduced only in the final figure (Figure 8).
Also, the figure legends—starting from Figure 1—should include more detailed descriptions of the treatment regimens. For
instance, what do the labels “P” and “T” refer to in Figure 1? Why is anti-TGFβ labeled “P” in Figure 7, while anti-PD-L1 is
shown as “aP”? 
• Please clarify what the three lanes in the expression profiles of Figure 7B represent. 
• In the figure legends (and where relevant in the main text), clearly indicate whether human or murine macrophages were
used.
