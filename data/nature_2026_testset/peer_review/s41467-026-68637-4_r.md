Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present IFUM, a deep neural network that uses deep learning embedding and residue–pair distance probability
distributions to estimate the Gibbs free energy (ΔG). This joint learning approach substantially improves the accuracy of ΔG
predictions compared to models trained on ΔG alone. The model was trained using data from the ‘Mega-scale’ experiment
and disordered proteins. They demonstrated the performance of IFUM across a range of validation datasets, and also tested
its ability to capture the effects of mutations, including sequence insertions and deletions. Finally, they applied IFUM to two
design challenges. 
What makes this work novel is the use of combined sequence and structure information to make predictions about protein
stability, leveraging the existence of models that can extract information and return it as an embedding. 
While the authors provide an extensive dataset supporting the work, the manuscript lacks clarity in places, particularly with
regard to the role of some of the selected validation datasets and the limitations of the model when applied to proteins of a
larger size, as can be seen in the results shown in the paper in both the validation and application sections. 
I have therefore listed here the major and minor points of concern that should be addressed to improve the quality of the
manuscript. 
Major revisions 
• The Mega-scale experiment by Tsuboyama et al. (2023) effectively comprises 331 base sequences (plus de novo
designed proteins), since the majority of the entries are single or double mutants originating from the same sequence. While
these additional variants provide some extra information, their contribution to training likely plateaus after a certain number of
sequences have been included. It is important to assess how model performance improves when training is conducted using
only the original 331 sequences, compared to smaller fractions of the full dataset (e.g. 10%, 25%, 50%) and the full dataset.
At what point does the accuracy plateau? How much of the information provided is redundant? 
Furthermore, the description of the Mega-scale training and test sets is difficult to interpret as the methods section ('Dataset
splitting') provides limited detail. How exactly was the 8:2 split performed? The text mentions that the split is based on
clustering, presumably with the 331 Tsuboyama wild-type and Disprot sequences as centroids. However, why use such an
elaborate procedure when most of the data could simply be splitted by using the wild-type sequences? 
Finally, no figure or table shows the training and validation results for this dataset; only the final 'Mega-scale test set'
predictions are presented in Figure 2a/b. Please provide more detail about the training procedure and results in the text. 
• There is additional data that can be used to validate the model accuracy, especially from previous work such as from
Maxwell KL and colleagues [1] which provide data in standard conditions similar to your training set. Please consider to add
data to your validation. Also consider the use of simple baseline such the use of sequence length or number of total contacts
in the proteins (which has been show to correlate with absolute stability [2]) to provide a more strong foundation for your
model. 
• It is not entirely clear to me what role the CATH dataset plays in this analysis. The model was trained using small, single-
domain proteins of up to ~80 residues, whereas the CATH dataset includes proteins of up to 800 residues. Furthermore,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

many of these proteins are not expected to undergo a simple two-state folding process, as observed in the training set.
Additionally, the dataset lacks experimental ΔG labels. 
Given these differences, what insights are we expected to gain from this comparison beyond demonstrating that DisProt
proteins tend to cluster around ΔG = 0 (as expected)? The predicted stability distributions have not been validated on
proteins larger than 100 residues. Figure 2E clearly shows that predictions for larger proteins deviate substantially from the
expected values. This suggest that IFUM likely overestimates stability in larger system, suggesting that these values are
unreliable. A stronger rationale for including this dataset should be provided, along with clarification of its intended purpose
in the study. Additionally, the purpose of the comparison with SAP is unclear and requires further explanation. 
• Connected to the previous point regarding the CATH dataset, as mentioned in the 'Limitations of IFUM' section, the model
clearly overestimates large proteins (as shown in Figure 2). This is likely due to the greater complexity of their folding
mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that it is possible to estimate the accuracy limits of a model on larger,
more complex proteins [3]. 
Currently, there is no section of the manuscript that quantifies the ability of the model to work
outside of the training distribution. Please include an analysis that addresses this aspect. Specifically, quantify how model
performance varies with protein size and folding type, and clarify where the model’s predictive accuracy begins to
deteriorate. If the limitation in size is confirmed, please clearly state in text as well, as the applicability of the model will be
significantly reduced. 
• In the application section, the model is evaluated on three proteins in order to test its ability to design new variants.
However, I found a few issues that require clarification. Firstly, Figure A shows negative ΔG values for the IFN wild-type
sequence, implying an unstable protein. However, the reported melting temperature of ~65 °C indicates a relatively stable
system. 
Secondly, the model appears to show a strong correlation with sequence length: smaller systems, such as IFN, produce
systematically lower ΔG estimates, while larger proteins (e.g. UGT) yield higher values. While there is a connection between
length and ΔG, this pattern appears to be related to the possible inaccuracy of the predictions for data types outside the
range for which it was trained. Please investigate these observations and justify them, and discuss the implications of
applying the model to design proteins that differ in size or folding behaviour from those in the training set.

• As there are a limited number of points in Fig. 4A, please use a statistical test (such as bootstrapping) to assign confidence
intervals to the points. Please check that also other SI figures have appropriate statistical validations.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript 
"Protein folding stability estimation with explicit consideration of unfolded states" presents IFUM, a deep
learning model designed to predict the folding free energy (ΔG) of proteins by leveraging sequence information and structure
information of both folded and unfolded states. Unlike previous approaches that rely solely on folded structures, IFUM
introduces a Flory random coil-based representation for unfolded states and predicts both ΔG and an equilibrium ensemble
distogram. The authors demonstrate that this joint modeling leads to improved ΔG prediction accuracy, particularly for
diverse mutational scenarios including insertions and deletions. The model is extensively benchmarked against various

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

competing methods and applied to real-world protein design tasks, including protein stabilization and de novo design
selection. 
The manuscript is well-written, methodologically sound and yields promising results. However, certain aspects require
clarification and additional analysis. These include questions in regards the model's extrapolation behavior, insufficient
methodological detail in parts of the application section, and the need for more comprehensive benchmarking against
physics-based methods. After the major revisions suggested below, this work would be well-suited for publication. 
(1) On Dynamic Range Clamping in Predictions (Figure 2D): The authors report that the predicted ΔG values remain within a
–1 to 5 kcal/mol window, even for proteins with experimental values outside this range. It would be beneficial for the authors
to explicitly comment on whether this behavior is due to the limited dynamic range of the training dataset or bias introduced
by the model itself. Furthermore, does this indicate that the model implicitly learns to compress or "squeeze" the dynamic
range, thus hindering its ability to extrapolate beyond trained boundaries? A discussion of this potential limitation, and
whether alternative loss formulations or additional training data could help address it, would be valuable. 
(2) On “Practical Application 2” – Incomplete Classification Details: The section describing the use of IFUM for discriminating
between soluble and insoluble protein designs lacks crucial details. The authors should specify: 
--What threshold of predicted ΔG or plDDT score was used for classification? 
--Were the thresholds chosen empirically or via cross-validation? 
--Include classification performance metrics, such as a confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, or AUROC, to provide a
more quantitative evaluation. 
(3) Comparison to Physics-Based Methods (e.g., FEP): The authors are encouraged to compare IFUM with physics-based
methods such as free energy perturbation (FEP), which, while computationally expensive, offer rigorous ΔΔG estimates. For
instance, reference could be made to studies (e.g. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2020.05.020) that apply FEP to single-point
mutations, and the same benchmark datasets could be used to provide a head-to-head comparison. This would further
demonstrate the trade-offs between computational cost and accuracy, and reinforce IFUM's value as a scalable alternative. 
(4) More Investigation of the Impact of Folded Structure Quality. While the authors show that IFUM's accuracy improves with
higher-confidence structures (e.g., AF3 models outperform ESMFold), this is only partially explored in one application
scenario. To strengthen this analysis, the authors are advised to: 
--Re-run all current benchmarking datasets using AF3 structures as input to IFUM 
--Consider re-training the model with AF3-predicted structures to evaluate whether it improves the performance if AF3
structures are generally better than ESMFold. 
--Construct a benchmark dataset comprising proteins for which both experimental folding free energies and experimental
structures are available, ensuring <30% sequence identity to training data. This would provide a more stringent assessment
of model performance and eliminate the uncertainty introduced by structure prediction models. 
(5) Model training and more ablation study: The author should discuss to what extent the other two tasks incorporated in the
training (residue pairwise contact map and sequence recovery) contribute to better accuracy/generalization of the free
energy prediction task. While the authors state that the ProtT5 sequence embeddings contribute positively, they do not
present an ablation study isolating the impact of sequence embeddings alone (e.g., removing ProtT5 or replacing it with
other embedding models like ProteinBERT and measuring performance drop). 
(6) Inference Speed and Efficiency: The authors should report the inference time per sequence, including typical runtime and
memory requirements on standard hardware (e.g., A6000 GPU as used for training). A comparison to inference times of
competing methods would help contextualize IFUM's utility in large-scale or time-sensitive applications. 
(Remarks on code availability) 
The authors should provide a more detailed guidance in the Github repository for reproducing the IFUM model.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors introduce IFUM, a new method for predicting the thermodynamic stability of proteins that utilizes both the folded
structure and the unfolded state distance probability distributions. 
Because previous prediction algorithms focused on the
folded structure IFUM presents a new paradigm for including the contribution of the unfolded ensemble to deltaG predictions.
The authors demonstrate that incorporating the unfolded state, via a Flory unfolded state model, improves upon existing
deltaG predictions, in some case quite substantially. 
Accurate prediction of protein stability and the effect of point mutations
and insertions/deletions is important for protein design and engineering in the biotech industry so this new tool is likely to be
of interest to a wide audience. The paper is well-written and the work carefully performed. 
The authors make the tool freely
available as a download to all researchers. 
As someone who is not a machine learning expert my comments are more on
the broader impact of the work to a general protein engineering audience more so than the computational aspects. 
I
recommend publication of the work.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author):  
The manuscript "Protein folding stability estimation with explicit consideration of unfolded 
states" presents IFUM, a deep learning model designed to predict the folding free energy 
(ΔG) of proteins by leveraging sequence information and structure information of both folded 
and unfolded states. Unlike previous approaches that rely solely on folded structures, IFUM 
introduces a Flory random coil-based representation for unfolded states and predicts both 
ΔG and an equilibrium ensemble distogram. The authors demonstrate that this joint 
modeling leads to improved ΔG prediction accuracy, particularly for diverse mutational 
scenarios including insertions and deletions. The model is extensively benchmarked against 
various competing methods and applied to real-world protein design tasks, including protein 
stabilization and de novo design selection. 
 
The manuscript is well-written, methodologically sound and yields promising results. 
However, certain aspects require clarification and additional analysis. These include 
questions in regards the model's extrapolation behavior, insufficient methodological detail in 
parts of the application section, and the need for more comprehensive benchmarking against 
physics-based methods. After the major revisions suggested below, this work would be 
well-suited for publication. 
 
> We largely appreciate recognizing the potential of our work. In summary, we have included 
the extrapolation behavior analysis, clarified details, and added benchmarking results as the 
reviewer suggested. Below, we put responses to the points. 
 
(1) On Dynamic Range Clamping in Predictions (Figure 2D): The authors report that the 
predicted ΔG values remain within a –1 to 5 kcal/mol window, even for proteins with 
experimental values outside this range. It would be beneficial for the authors to explicitly 
comment on whether this behavior is due to the limited dynamic range of the training dataset 
or bias introduced by the model itself. Furthermore, does this indicate that the model 
implicitly learns to compress or "squeeze" the dynamic range, thus hindering its ability to 
extrapolate beyond trained boundaries? A discussion of this potential limitation, and whether 
alternative loss formulations or additional training data could help address it, would be 
valuable. 
 
> First, we revised it to clarify the predicted values actually span beyond the range: 
 
Page 6, line 4: “The figure shows evaluation of the ΔGpred values clamped to the dataset’s
