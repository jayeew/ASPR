REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Review of “Major Baltic Inflows do not have long-lasting consequences for 20th-century hypoxia in the Baltic Sea” by Lev
Naumov and Markus Meier. 
This manuscript addresses an important topic — the role of Major Baltic Inflows (MBIs) in influencing stratification and
hypoxia in the Baltic Sea — and applies a series of model experiments to evaluate both the impact of the largest recorded
MBI in 1951 and the broader role of inflows from the North Sea. The spatial separation of results by subbasin, and the
attempt to link stratification to hypoxic are strengths of the work. 
However, in its current form, the manuscript has several major weaknesses that prevent it from providing sufficiently robust
evidence to support its conclusions. These include limitations in model validation, discrepancies with observational studies,
potential overgeneralization of findings, and methodological constraints. 
Main concerns 
1. Lack of robust model validation 
All conclusions are based on a single numerical model with known biases (e.g., near-bottom salinity, biases in oxygen, no
metrics provided). The manuscript does not provide clear quantitative validation of stratification strength, oxygen
concentrations, or hypoxic volume against in situ observations. Without statistical skill metrics or direct model–observation
comparisons (e.g., long-term station time series), it is difficult to assess the reliability of the results. 
2. Large discrepancies with observational studies 
The reported correlations between stratification and hypoxia (R² = 0.03–0.35) are much weaker than those observed in the
literature (e.g., 0.56–0.79 in Krapf et al., 2022). While the discussion acknowledges possible reasons, this difference is not
critically explored, raising questions about model performance.This together with limited validation makes the results
doubtful. 
3. Overgeneralization of conclusions 
The study concludes that “MBIs themselves cannot cause significant changes in Baltic Sea hypoxia on multidecadal
timescales.” This statement is based largely on one event (1951 MBI) and one model configuration, and does not consider
the cumulative effects of multiple inflows or the variability across different periods. It would be better to start with the more
recent time period with more observational data e.g. inflow of 1993. 
4. Methodological limitations 
o The ~6 km (3nm) horizontal resolution prevents representation of submesoscale dynamics (O~1 km eddies) known to be
important for mixing and oxygen transport in the Baltic. 
o The analysis period focuses heavily on early decades (1940s–1980s) when observational coverage is sparse, reducing
confidence in model–data comparisons.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

o Key physical mechanisms are described qualitatively, without quantitative support (e.g., oxygen budgets, transport
diagnostics). 
5. Scope versus evidence mismatch 
The results show substantial spatial variability in MBI effects between basins (e.g., BB/eGB vs. nGB/wGB), yet the
conclusions are framed for the whole Baltic Sea.This risks overstating the generality of the findings. 
Overall assessment 
The manuscript presents interesting ideas and useful spatially resolved results, but the current evidence is not strong
enough to support the main claims. The lack of rigorous validation, unexplored discrepancies with observational data, and
overextension of conclusions from a single model and single event significantly weakens the study’s robustness. 
For these reasons, I cannot recommend the manuscript for publication in its current form. I would encourage the authors to
build on the strengths of their work by: 
• Expanding observational validation across variables and subbasins, 
• Investigating the causes of low model–observation agreement, 
• Broadening the analysis to later periods with richer observational data, 
• Presenting conclusions that reflect the spatial variability and model uncertainties more cautiously. 
With such substantial revisions and a more measured framing of conclusions, this study could make a valuable contribution
in a future submission

General comments 
The topic of this article is highly relevant and it contributes to a better understanding of the physical environment of the Baltic
Sea and its influence on prevailing conditions. Furthermore, the study provides valuable insights into the extent to which
natural factors, specifically MBIs, affect the state of the Baltic Sea. By employing modelling approach, this study provides
such insight to the Baltic Sea which could not be achieved by other approaches.The results are of interest for Baltic Sea
scientist as well as potentially for stakeholders and policymakers. 
I recommend moderate revisions to the manuscript. Improvements are needed in the overall structure and the clarity of the
text. Sharpening the focus and reorganizing the content would enhance the readability, especially for the readers from the
other disciplines. Additionally, there were some gaps in the description of the methods, model and observational data (see
also specific comments). Providing more detailed descriptions would improve the transparency and reproducibility of the
study. 
The discussion section remains somewhat shallow. A more in-depth reflection how these advanced methods used in this
study could be applied in current Baltic Sea studies and policy making, their potential future applications and their limitations
would significally strengthen the manuscript.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
This is a paper of general interest since analysis the effect of salt water intrusions on the oxygen situation in different parts of
the Baltic proper. By performing computer modelling simulations, the authors investigated the long-term effects of salt water
intrusions in different parts of the Baltic Sea. They investigate the salt water inflow effects on energy needed for vertical
mixing and the consequences for hypoxia seawater volume. They show that major inflows from the Atlantic do not have
long-lasting effects on the oxygen situation in different parts of the Baltic. This is an important conclusion from this study. The
conclusion is that internal eutrophication would be a significant cause for oxygen deficiency. 
I miss an upfront and clear definition of the simulation REF. I think this would be crucial for a general reader like me to more
easily grasp the results of the study. In the results part, there is a sub-ordinary clause, saying that REF is without MBIs (line
124-125). However, I think that the simulation settings should be clearly defined maybe already in the Introduction, since
Material and methods are placed at last in the paper. 
Likewise other abbreviations should be clearly explained already in the beginning of the paper, for example RFEs. This
abbreviation is unluckily similar to REFs, but means the opposite, if I understand correctly. In general, abbreviations should
be avoided. 
I miss a conclusion at the end of the Discussion. 
It is good that sensitivity experiments had been performed, and I hope they are conclusive. I am not fully knowledgeable in
all the models used for the study, but it seems very important to refer to and use setting from real wet-experiments or fields
studies. For example, when estimating the P release from sediments, that is internal eutrophication. To me it seems as the
methods could be better explained and that the conclusions better justified.

We acknowledge all your comments and will address each of them below: 1. We agree that the model validation in the first version of the paper (Supplementary Figures 1 and 2) was not perfect. We indeed provided neither quantitative estimates of model performance nor validation for hypoxic volume or PEA, the main quantities analyzed in the paper. We now address this by providing additional validation plots in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). Supplementary Figure 3 shows the temporal distributions of PEA and hypoxic volume, calculated from the model and observational data, and Supplementary Figure 4 provides the quantitative measure of model performance for the most important variables for this study: surface and bottom temperatures, salinities, and DO concentrations, as well as PEA and hypoxic volume. As a qualitative metric, we present the relationship between the cost function (CF; more information added to the paper), developed by Eilola et al. (2009), and 1 – Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). This metric has already been applied, e.g., in Ruvalcaba Baroni et al. (2024) and Edman et al. (2018). We demonstrate that the model captures PEA variability, albeit with some limitations during the stagnation period at stations BY28 and BY31, and minor underestimation at stations BY5 and BY15. Hypoxic volume has higher uncertainty due to the coarse vertical resolution of the observational data (as shown in Supplementary Figure 3). But the model at least demonstrates good agreement with observations on the trend level. A single parameter worth mentioning regarding model performance is the bottom DO concentrations at BY5. The model clearly overestimates them, most likely due to underestimating stratification strength (as shown in Supplementary Figure 3). However, neither the model nor the observations show notable trends in bottom DO at BY5 since regular observations became available in

the 1960s. This fact makes the station above less important for the paper’s conclusions, which, in turn, makes the demonstrated biases acceptable for the paper.  2. There are differences between this study and the study by Krapf et al. (2022). First of all, we investigated different periods, with this study focusing on earlier times when the major change in hypoxia occurred in the central Baltic Sea. The study by Krapf et al. (2022) examines a period (starting approximately in the 1970s) including a well-known and long stagnation period that significantly influenced stratification in the central Baltic Sea. This might elevate the importance of stratification in Krapf et al. (2022). In addition, undersampling in observational data and its averaging, as well as a different metric for stratification strength (they follow Väli et al., 2023, using salinity differences as a proxy for stratification strength, whereas we use PEA), could be sources of differences in estimates. We added additional discussion of the differences to our paper, along with further validation to demonstrate that our findings are robust.  3. We agree that our conclusions might sound overgeneralized. We made some corrections throughout the text and in the discussion. To address the second part of the reviewer’s comment, we extended all simulations with random meteorological and OBC forcing, using the same forcing-generation strategy, through 2021. This extension further strengthened the hypothesis proposed in the paper, as no significant changes in hypoxic volume were observed, despite generally stronger stratification in random-forcing experiments compared to the reference (less pronounced stagnation period). At the same time, we don’t see any added value in focusing on MBI 1993, given its insignificance for the long-term deoxygenation of the Baltic Sea. The Baltic Sea transition from an oxic to a hypoxic state, which occurred in the 1950s, was demonstrated by Moros et al. (2024). The model reproduces MBI 1951, as shown in Supplementary Figure 5, making it a useful tool for investigating the possible effects of this MBI on long-term hypoxia in the central Baltic Sea basins. We also agree that a single-model approach might entail some uncertainties. We added additional validation to evaluate model performance, especially for PEA, to convince the reader that the present model biases are not critical for this kind of study. In addition, model studies with a study period of similar length demonstrate a comparable development of hypoxia in the central Baltic Sea (e.g., Gustafsson et al., 2012).  As for our conclusion about MBIs, it is not based solely on the NO_MBI1951 experiment, but also on random-forcing experiments, in which meteorological fields and sea-level data at the open boundary were taken from periods without strong inflows. This approach significantly reduces the occurrence of MBIs, which depend on local meteorological forcing (Löptien et al., 2025; Meier and Kauker, 2003), while allowing for other forms of variability that could influence hypoxia development in the central Baltic Sea (e.g., changes in runoff, atmospheric warming, etc.). The same general trend in hypoxic volume in all these experiments, as

shown in Figure 3, is the central premise for our conclusion, not the absence of the single MBI 1951.  4. We agree that our study has some limitations related to the model configuration, and we have added additional discussion on this. However, this is true for all studies involving state-of-the-art models. Running even a central Baltic Sea model simulation with sub-mesoscale eddy-permitting resolution is not feasible for the time period considered in this study. One study involving such a model is Väli et al. (2024), which performed only a 9-year simulation. In addition, temporal averaging masks effects associated with short-lived structures, so the inclusion of sub-mesoscale features will not alter the long-term evolution of hypoxia in the central Baltic Sea.  To demonstrate that the model resolution does not critically affect the results, we added data from an additional model (MOM5 with 1 nm resolution) to all validation figures (Supplementary Figures 1-4). The added MOM5 1 nm setup performs very similarly compared to the actually used MOM6 3 nm setup, despite having 3 times finer horizontal resolution. We acknowledge that 1 nm is still coarser than the typical spatial scale of sub-mesoscale processes, but it is the finest resolution for the Baltic Sea model utilized in climate research. The oxygen budget and qualitative transport diagnostics have already been presented for the identical setup of the MOM5 model in Naumov et al. (2023). Regarding the sparseness of observations in the earlier period, we assume the model performs adequately during this period if it performs well in more recent periods with available observations, as this is the standard validation approach for this type of study.  5.
