Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript “Linked skyrmions in shifted magnetic bilayer “ by S. Ghost et al. presents a comprehensive study of novel
topological magnetic states, termed "linked solitons," in a shifted magnetic bilayer system with orthogonal Dzyaloshinskii-
Moriya interactions (DMI) in each layer. The work combines theoretical modeling, atomistic simulations, homotopy group
analysis, and first-principles calculations to explore the formation and stability of these states. The results are original and
contribute significantly to the field of topological solitons in magnetic systems. The manuscript presents a theoretically rich
and original study of linked skyrmions in a bilayer system with orthogonal DMI. The results are well-supported by
simulations and analysis, and the proposed material realization adds practical relevance. I recommend the manuscript for
publication after addressing the following questions and suggestions: 
1. The idea of achieving orthogonal DMI in each layer via defects with strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC) is innovative.
However, it is unclear whether the inclusion of a single atom (e.g., InAs) provides sufficient DMI strength to stabilize the
proposed textures. Could the authors comment on the minimal DMI strength required for the emergence of linked skyrmions?
Is a regular arrangement of defects necessary to ensure the stability of these structures and the desired DMI type? Are there
alternative material systems or defect configurations that could achieve similar orthogonal DMI effects? 
2. The manuscript highlights the formation of solitons with arbitrarily large topological charges. It would be worthful to
mention the potential advantages or applications of such high-topological-charge states. For instance, could they offer
enhanced stability, unique transport properties, or novel functionalities in spintronic devices? 
3. The phase diagram (Figure 2) currently shows only three phases: checkerboard, spiral, and skyrmion lattice. Could the
authors extend the phase diagram to include regions where linked skyrmions are stable? Are these states metastable across
a broad parameter range, or do they require fine-tuning of B_ext and J_c constants 
4. The introduction of the quantity QD=QT−QB to characterize anti-aligned points is intriguing, but its physical implications
are not fully explored. How does QD influence the dynamical behavior or stability of linked skyrmions? For example, does it
affect their response to external stimuli (e.g., currents or fields)? Is the definition QD=QT−QB universally applicable, or are
there constraints (e.g., layer coupling strength, symmetry)? Could the authors compare QD with the topological charge of
Bloch points in bulk systems, emphasizing similarities and differences? 
4. The term "linked skyrmions" is introduced but not explicitly defined. A brief conceptual definition (e.g., "solitons coupled
via anti-aligned points", comparison with Bloch – point states,…) would help readers

(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript entitled “Linked skyrmions in shifted magnetic bilayer” by Sumit Ghosh et al., the authors report the results
of atomistic simulations in which they stabilize various magnetic phases. The authors claim to have found new magnetic
states: the checkerboard pattern and linked skyrmions. The idea is to use a bilayer with a Zincblende structure that gives rise
to a different DMI in the two layers. Therefore, different spin spiral phases are stabilized in the ground state, so that unique

configurations can arise when the layers are coupled. Furthermore, more as a side note, the authors report that skyrmion
bags and kpi-skyrmions can form and they have carried out DFT calculations to predict a material candidate. 
The simulations are of high quality and the results are correct. However, I have several questions and the discussion in the
manuscript should be revised. Most importantly, I think that the significance is overstated (see especially my points 5-7). I do
not think the manuscript should be published in Communications Physics because the criteria of novelty and impact for
acceptance are not fulfilled. However, once properly revised, the manuscript deserves publication in a journal like Scientific
Reports. 
Here are my detailed comments: 
1) Typically, a skyrmion lattice is the superposition of 3 spin spirals with q vectors oriented at respective angles of 120°.
Here, there are only 2 types of spin spirals. The formation mechanism is different from the typical 3q state discussed for
example in [Okubo et al. PRL 108, 017206 (2012)]. I think it would be beneficial to include a short comparison. 
2) Fig. 2g vs Fig. 3: What determines the outcome of a simulation? As far as I understand the two scenarios are stabilized
under the same parameters. Have you considered different starting seeds? 
3) It took me a long time to understand Fig. 3a because the in-plane orientation of the arrows in Fig. 3a(top) do not agree
with the color code in Fig. 3b. However, the problem was only that the coordinate system is rotated. Please use a
perspective more similar to the bottom panel. E.g. a view from (x,y,z) = (0,-1,1). 
4) Using Eq. 4 for the calculation of the topological charge and discretizing the derivative, what are the actual numerical
values that you get for the textures in Fig.3b(top,middle)? Since the textures live on a lattice a perfect integer is not to be
expected. 
5) Topological defects: What is the significance of QD? You are calculating QB and QT from single layers and write that the
difference corresponds to QD which characterizes a topological defect. However, to me this makes no sense. Treating the
two layers as a ‘combined’ object is artificial. After all, the calculation of QB and QT is based on individual layers. If you treat
the two layers individually, there is no topological defect. A topological defect is for example a Bloch point that gives rise to a
chiral bobber. Here the situation is completely different. 
6) In the same context, I also think that a ‘linked skyrmion’ as in Fig. 3b is not a new magnetic state. This combined treatment
of two layers is only done for antiferromagnetic skyrmion or ferrimagnetic skyrmions because the textures are mutually
reversed. In the latter case, it is basically a skyrmion for the Néel vector. Here, such an analogy does not exist. 
7) I have a similar criticism about the checkerboard phase. To me this is not a new state, it is merely two layers that exhibit
different textures. It is an interesting finding and deserves publication but the significance is not large enough to satisfy the
acceptance criteria of Communications Physics. 
8) Fig. 3: Is the following situation also possible: Both layers exhibit the same configuration but not the typical skyrmion state
but the ‘merged’ state as in Fig. 3b(top)? To me, this would be an actual ‘linked skyrmion’. 
9) It is nice to see DFT calculations that show that this idea could be realized. Can you please show that the structure in Fig.
5 is stable? 
10) I know that this is beyond the scope of this manuscript but as an outlook: What would happen if the interlayer coupling
had the opposite sign? 
11) There are some problems in the list of references like missing page numbers, missing DOIs etc.

Reviewer 2 (Remarks to the Author):
The manuscript “Linked skyrmions in shifted magnetic bilayer “ by S. Ghost et al. presents a com-
prehensive study of novel topological magnetic states, termed ”linked solitons,” in a shifted magnetic
bilayer system with orthogonal Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interactions (DMI) in each layer. The work
combines theoretical modeling, atomistic simulations, homotopy group analysis, and first-principles
calculations to explore the formation and stability of these states. The results are original and con-
tribute significantly to the field of topological solitons in magnetic systems. The manuscript presents
a theoretically rich and original study of linked skyrmions in a bilayer system with orthogonal DMI.
The results are well-supported by simulations and analysis, and the proposed material realization
adds practical relevance. I recommend the manuscript for publication after addressing the following
questions and suggestions:
We would like to thank the reviewer for the recommendation and for the valuable suggestions. In
the following we provide our responses to the queries, along with details of the corresponding changes
made to the main text and supplementary material.
1. The idea of achieving orthogonal DMI in each layer via defects with strong spin-orbit coupling
(SOC) is innovative. However, it is unclear whether the inclusion of a single atom (e.g., InAs) provides
sufficient DMI strength to stabilize the proposed textures. Could the authors comment on the minimal
DMI strength required for the emergence of linked skyrmions? Is a regular arrangement of defects
necessary to ensure the stability of these structures and the desired DMI type? Are there alternative
material systems or defect configurations that could achieve similar orthogonal DMI effects?
Please note that we are not using a single impurity as a source of spin-orbit coupling, rather
we are using a lattice implemented via a periodic boundary condition. (see Fig.5 for the periodic
configuration and supplementary Fig.S2 for the unit cell). Our first principle calculations with InAs
lattice as source of DMI show that the interface between the InAs and Ni layer can produce sufficient
DMI to stabilise the linked skyrmions with suitable applied magnetic field where the typical dimension
of the linked skyrmion analogous to Fig.3b in main text is 130nm ×45nm. We also have mentioned
this in section “V. Material realisation” of the main text.
The minimal DMI required to stabilise the linked skyrmions depends on both the interlayer ex-
change and the applied magnetic field. We have added new results in supplementary information “S4.
Stability of the linked skyrmion” to show that.
Our approach is quite generic in nature and is not restricted to any particular material. Similar
behaviour has been observed with InAs-Fe interface. For simplicity we choose a square lattice here
and focus on materials with compatible geometry. Similar studies can be done with other geometries
as well provided they satisfy the symmetry requirements, which opens possibility for more materials.
2. The manuscript highlights the formation of solitons with arbitrarily large topological charges.
It would be worthful to mention the potential advantages or applications of such high-topological-
charge states. For instance, could they offer enhanced stability, unique transport properties, or novel
functionalities in spintronic devices?

One of the main advantage of the linked skyrmions is their high topological charge density. This can
significantly enhance their transport properties. For example it can enhance the Hall angle which has
a profound impact on the spintronic applications. A detailed analysis of the transport properties for
systems of these dimension is computationally quite demanding and beyond the scope of the present
manuscript. Another potential application of these configurations is in skyrmion based computing
such as reservoir computing. We have added new text in the Conclusions elaborating these aspects.
3. The phase diagram (Figure 2) currently shows only three phases: checkerboard, spiral, and
skyrmion lattice. Could the authors extend the phase diagram to include regions where linked
skyrmions are stable? Are these states metastable across a broad parameter range, or do they re-
quire fine-tuning of Bext and Jc constants.
The linked skyrmions are stable over a wide range of Bext and JC as well as DMI ( D). We have
added a new section in the supplementary material (S4. Stability of the linked skyrmions) to explicitly
show the stability of the linked skyrmions over the wide range of Bext and JC as well as the D values.
4. The introduction of the quantity QD=QT-QB to characterize anti-aligned points is intriguing,
but its physical implications are not fully explored. How does QD influence the dynamical behavior
or stability of linked skyrmions? For example, does it affect their response to external stimuli (e.g.,
currents or fields)? Is the definition QD=QT-QB universally applicable, or are there constraints (e.g.,
layer coupling strength, symmetry)? Could the authors compare QD with the topological charge of
Bloch points in bulk systems, emphasizing similarities and differences?
The anti-aligned points are the heart of the linked skyrmions which stabilise them. In this work
we are more focused on their characterisation and topological nature. Due to their topological nature,
they will have a deep impact on the dynamical properties as well as the response functions of the
linked skyrmions. A detailed analysis of these features, as we explained previously, is computationally
quite demanding and is beyond the scope of the present manuscript.
The definition of QD is universally applicable, and the corresponding derivation can be found it
the Method, “Homotopy-group analysis” section. QD is literally the topological charge of the Bloch
point which here is “squized” between two layers, and therefore shows little resemblance to ordinary
bulk cases.
5. The term ”linked skyrmions” is introduced but not explicitly defined. A brief conceptual
definition (e.g., ”solitons coupled via anti-aligned points”, comparison with Bloch – point states,. . . )
would help readers.
We would like to thank the reviewer for this suggestion. The term ”linked skyrmion” is initially
defined in the abstract. Following the reviewer’s suggestion, we have also explained this in the first
paragraph of the section III. Phase Diagram.

For the calculation of topological charge we used the Berg-L¨ uscher method (Ref.21 of revised
manuscript) which is simplicial equivalent of Kronecker integral. This method returns integer values
for the triangulated lattice, without involving derivatives at all (see Ref.39 of the present manuscript
for details). We have also mentioned that in revised manuscript after Eq.4.
5) Topological defects: What is the significance of QD? You are calculating QB and QT from
single layers and write that the difference corresponds to QD which characterizes a topological defect.
However, to me this makes no sense. Treating the two layers as a ‘combined’ object is artificial. After
all, the calculation of QB and QT is based on individual layers. If you treat the two layers individually,
there is no topological defect. A topological defect is for example a Bloch point that gives rise to a
chiral bobber. Here the situation is completely different.
QD characterises the topological defect that links solitons with different topological charges from
different layers. As one can see from the Eq.3 of the main text, the two layers are not independent,
but they influence each other, and this is of great importance. The QD is the essence of the Bloch
point, which here has a rather unusual appearance. See also the Reply to 4th question of Reviewer 2.
This has been elaborated in details in the Supplmentary material S1. Homotopy-group analysis.
6) In the same context, I also think that a ‘linked skyrmion’ as in Fig. 3b is not a new magnetic
state. This combined treatment of two layers is only done for antiferromagnetic skyrmion or ferri-
magnetic skyrmions because the textures are mutually reversed. In the latter case, it is basically a
skyrmion for the N´ eel vector. Here, such an analogy does not exist.
The linked skyrmions are inherently different from conventional antiferromagnetic or ferrimag-
netic skyrmions where the skyrmions on either layer possess same magnitude of topological charge
(|QT |=|QB|). For linked skyrmions the situation is completely different since the top and bottom layer
possess different topological charges ( |QT | ̸= |QB|) and therefore the configuration from one layer can
not be obtained from the other layer by continuous deformation without going through a topological
transition. The difference of topological charges of the two layers is manifested as a topological de-
fect which gives linked skyrmions a distinct identity compared to the skyrmions reported earlier in
synthetic antiferromagnetic or ferrimagnetic system.
7) I have a similar criticism about the checkerboard phase. To me this is not a new state, it is
merely two layers that exhibit different textures. It is an interesting finding and deserves publication
but the significance is not large enough to satisfy the acceptance criteria of Communications Physics.
The checker board pattern itself is indeed not a new finding of this manuscript. Similar pattern
has been reported before, for example by Hayami, which we also mentioned in the main text (Ref.33 in
present manuscript). What is new in this manuscript is the presence of the anti-aligned pointswithin
the checker board pattern. This has been explicitly mentioned in Sec.III. of the manuscript.
The presence of anti-aligned points in the checker-board phase provides a clear overview of the
transition from skyrmion to linked skyrmion. The novelty of our work is not based on the checker-

board pattern, rather on the anti-aligned points in the checker board pattern which lead to linked
skyrmion. This new class of magnetic solitons stabilised by topological defects have not been explored
previously and we strongly believe that this will satisfy the high standard of Communications Physics.
8) Fig. 3: Is the following situation also possible: Both layers exhibit the same configuration but
not the typical skyrmion state but the ‘merged’ state as in Fig. 3b(top)? To me, this would be an
actual ‘linked skyrmion’.
We would like to thank the reviewer for this question. For our case, this is not possible. The
configuration shown in Fig.3b(top) is not stable in itself. If one starts from an initial state consists of
such ‘merged’ state on both layer, they will converge to a single isolated skyrmion on both layers. The
‘merged’ states can be stabilised only when it is coupled to skyrmions with different topological charge
via a topological point defect. This is, as we describe in response to comment 6 and 7, distinguishes
linked skyrmions from the earlier finding where skyrmions with same topological invariants are coupled
together in a bilayer system.

We would like to thank the reviewer for this suggestion. We have added additional details in the

layers. However, their domain of stability will be different due to the response of the bilayer to the
external magnetic field.
