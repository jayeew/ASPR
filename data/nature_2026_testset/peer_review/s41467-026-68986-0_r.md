Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The work presents a numerical and observational investigation of how urban (building) morphological configurations given
fixed size and fixed urban heating influence the formation of clouds. Numerically, this was conducted using an LES model
with varying configurations of morphology consistent with local climate zones. From observational studies, this was
conducted by estimating correlations between morphological statistics and historical cloud formations. Although the findings
are clear, there are multiple issues that need to be addressed before recommending to the next stage. 
1. The research objectives remain unclear. Is the research about proposing parameterizations for the urban effect on clouds?
Is it about expanding on the work of Vo et al. (2023)? There are two research questions, but in the results and discussions
sections, the relationship between these two research questions are unclear. Specifics are as follows: 
* In the intro, the authors state "the contrasting effect of urban canopy on scalar transport and mechanisms behind it need
further clarified.". From this statement, it seems like the authors wish to address these contrasting effects. However, the
conditions of the numerical model seem to be ideal for the effect of urban canopies to generate the clouds. 
* The numerical findings discuss in terms of LCZs, but the observations mainly discuss on morphological statistics. I think
that in order to bridge the too consistency in the urban level metrics would be needed. 
* The significance of LCZ is unclear and is indirect. Using LCZ in the discussion will present extra effort of the readers to
understand the distinctions of LCZ1 to LCZ8. It will help if the authors summarize their distinctions using bulk quantities such
as empirical / bulk z0s? 
* The research questions should at least mention the scope. For example, the first research question should mention "...
under free convection settings?" because it refers to the numerical investigation. 
* Recent global datasets of the morphological parameters may be more realistic than specificying group of values from LCZ
inventories. 
2. Related to the previous comment, to set this work apart from previous works that have established relationships of
turbulence quantified by standard deviations in vertical velocity and urban roughness, and have already investigated the
effects of cities on clouds, it will be necessary to address the following issues: 
* The authors should present results of using their model to the actual LWP_{urban} and not end it with the modeling of the
sigma_w parametrization. Ending the discussion with sigma_w only makes the claim in the abstract "the study for the first
time establishes a mechanistc link between urban form and cloud enhancement" weak. 
* The authors state "We do not directly compare the simulated cloud amount quantified by LWP ... and many confounding
influence". Despite this statement, I believe to help readers understand the applicability and verifiability of the
parametrization, it is necessary to present how this can be implemented in actual settings. It will still be interesting and
informative to see the similarities/differences in the modeled LWP and the delta_clouds. Without this, the link between two
would be quite weak. 
3. There are statements or settings that seem to be assumed, but should be further elaborated due to their importance to both
parametrizations and the simulated outputs. There are variables that seem to need further parametrization. 
* The work seems to parametrize the sigma_w based on morphological parameters. However, it relies on clear definitions of
delta and sigma_w_0. How are these parameters obtained practically? They are important parameters, but it is unclear how
they can be obtained apart from the usage of LES. Without addresing this, it becomes doubtful whether this work can indeed
be a good stepping stone for parametrizations in climate models.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the interesting work conducted by the authors. The study attempts to reveal the influence of urban morphology
on local cloud enhancement. The content is generally well organized, presenting a series of numerical experiments along
with an analysis of observational evidence. The main finding is that taller buildings intensify urban-breeze circulations, while
denser buildings tend to suppress vertical turbulent transport. While the results appear convincing, the limitations—already
clearly acknowledged by the authors—significantly constrain the practical relevance of the findings. Some key parameters,
such as the threshold wind speed that justifies the assumption of free convective conditions, have not been tested. The study
could be improved if the following comments are addressed. 
1. While time-independent surface fluxes have been adopted in various urban microclimate studies focused on near-surface
heat and moisture transport, their use in this context introduces significant uncertainty. Constant updrafts and intermittent or
time-varying updrafts may behave quite differently in local cloud formation. Time-varying updrafts could lead to more lateral
interactions, potentially diminishing the clarity of morphological effects. This issue merits further discussion. 
2. One of the main findings is that the urban breeze develops at the urban–rural interface. This is expected given that all
simulations assume free convective conditions, where buoyancy is the dominant driver of moisture transport and local cloud
formation. Studying only free convective conditions limits the practical implications of the work. It is likely that local cloud
enhancement depends not only on urban morphology but also on background wind conditions. I suggest the authors
investigate and clarify the maximum background wind speed under which their findings remain valid. 
3. Please explain why the analysis focuses solely on simulated results at t = 1950 s across all cases. It would be helpful to
analyze additional time instants or a longer duration. How long does the local cloud enhancement persist? 
4. The choice of surface heat fluxes—0.05 and 0.056 for rural and urban areas, respectively—requires justification.
Additionally, the unit of these fluxes appears unclear or misleading; please verify and correct this. 
5. Regarding the observational evidence, while the correlation matrix illustrates a relationship between the simulations and
observations, the correspondence between the experimental setup and observed conditions should be better explained. For
example, what were the background wind speeds during the observations? 
6. To what extent do the findings depend on the four-layer atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) structure used for the initial
temperature and water vapor profiles? A justification for the choice of these gradients should be provided, and sensitivity
analyses may be warranted to test their influence. 
7. Text in Figure S4 is not readable. Please improve the legibility of the labels and annotations. 
8. Cloud patterns for other LCZs, in addition to LCZ1 and LCZ8, could be included in the supplementary material to provide
a more complete picture. 
9. In Figure 3c, should we expect the LCZs to appear in a particular order from low to high LWP? Please clarify this point. 
10. It appears that the scaling analysis involving two velocity scales has not been validated against either numerical or
observational data. As a result, the values of these scales remain abstract. This should be addressed. 
11. The subscripts of variables should be consistently formatted using the same font throughout the manuscript.

Referee comment on " Local cloud enhancement in cities depends on urban morphology" by Yuanfeng Cui, Sisi Chen, Lulin
Xue, Domingo Muñoz-Esparza, Jeremy A. Sauer, Leiqiu Hu, John D. Albertson, Qi Li, Nature Communications 
This manuscript presents a case study of the impact of urban morphological types on clouds using urban morphology-
resolving large-eddy simulations (LES). Different local climate zones (LCZs) are considered varying the height and width of
buildings, canyon width and building density. Based on the results obtained, a parameterization on the effect of urban
morphology on vertical velocity is proposed. Besides, observational data from 44 U.S. cities are used to validate the results
observed in the LES simulations. This study shows that the two parameters affecting cloud formation in cities are building
height, which affects the urban-breeze circulations at the rural-urban interface, and building density, which reduces vertical
velocity at the urban core. 
The methodology used to evaluate the impact of urban morphology on cloud enhancement is appropriate as well as the
presentation of the results, although the understanding of some figures could be improved (further details will follow in “Minor
comments” below) and some mistakes in labeling corrected. The values selected for some parameters in the LES
