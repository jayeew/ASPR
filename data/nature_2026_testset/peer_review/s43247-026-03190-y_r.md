Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
Dear authors, 
The research presented in this manuscript is relevant and timely, particularly given the increasing threats posed by land
subsidence and sea-level rise to coastal areas. The paper aims to deliver a pan-European assessment of coastal
vulnerability by integrating vertical land motion data with land cover, SLR projections, and socioeconomic indicators. This is
an important and ambitious effort that can make a valuable contribution to the field. However, I found that a few aspects of
the manuscript remain unclear, so please see below my comments on that regard. I’m sorry for any potential harshness and I
wish the authors the best. 
Abstract: 
I’m unsure if 100m can be considered high-resolution in the context of Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar data, please
check. 
- 
Please add citations to where #4 is being cited since the claim at global scale and the only study being cited is about China. 
First paragraph – Please clarify that other impacts of climate change have also been observed and that subsidence and SLR
are among them. The transition between global scale and general impacts of two specific hazards is too abrupt. 
The acronym SLR is introduced in the first paragraph so I kindly suggest to use that every time sea level rise is mentioned. 
The study #10 is about port cities, not sure that claim on second paragraph can be supported by only that. 
Citation #33 is misplaced. 
Please clarify the extent, covered area, or resolution of a pixel. I’m unsure what 65mi pixels mean. 
Fig 1. - Please include a legend, or something in the caption explaining what the blue and red dashed lines represent in the
subplot figure. 
Fig 1. - Please add the numbers of respective countries presented in the boxplots to the map to facilitate the communication. 
Fig 1. - If you chose to keep the coordinate grid, please show the coordinates. 
Fig 2. - Add labels to national boundaries; Add labels to seas/oceans. 
Fig 2. – The gray boxes are stretched and not properly aligned to the maps. 
Add citations to the sentence “…small islands, many of which are further challenged by resource scarcity.” 
Unless this is how the dataset is really called, I don’t think the 100m land cover dataset should be referred to as “high-
resolution” 
Fig.3 – Please remove the figure title and use the caption for that. 
Fig.3 – Is this land use or land cover? Caption is confusing and I also noticed that in the methodology both terms are used as
synonyms (they are not). 
The shift from Europe-wide statistics to regional case studies (Netherlands, UK, Italy) is too abrupt. 
Fig. 5 - There is one label missing in panel C (above Lincoln). 
Fig. 5 – An overview map is necessary to help readers locate where each subplot is in the bigger picture of each country. 
Please avoid adding new topics or new information to the conclusion and focus on presenting the conclusion of this study.
For example, talking about significant risk from tsunamis is something that could have been mentioned earlier in the text and
does not come from your lessons learned in the study. 
Including a discussion (or at least acknowledging) uncertainties in the projections and the bathtub method would greatly
strengthen the scientific rigor of this study. In the same context, authors assume that current demographic structures (e.g., %
of elderly and children) remain static through 2050, which is unlikely given expected demographic shifts. 
The 100 km inland buffer is reasonable, but somewhat arbitrary for the coastal zone definition. A brief justification would be
great about this. 
Transforming economic values to percentiles within each country may preserve national context, but it masks absolute
disparities across countries. This limits cross-country interpretation of adaptive capacity and should be discussed. 
The manuscript states that 24.767 million people and 8.371 million buildings will be affected. Maybe these figures suggest
an unrealistic level of precision given the data that was used. 
The mention of €3.4 trillion in asset value in the Netherlands lacks detail on how this figure was calculated. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
This paper claims to be the first to couple VLM with social vulnerability in a study of exposure to SLR across all of Europe. 
1. VLM analysis: 
The authors use an analysis of VLM that is mostly consistent with the analysis previously published by Thieblemont et al
(2024) for the same region. 
It is not clear to me how the VLM methods used for the current manuscript differ from Thieblemont et al, except that two
overlapping time periods are used in the current manuscript. The authors cite Thieblemont et al 2024 in their Supplemental
Material only in a very general way, referring to those authors' framing of the VLM source data, and does not clearly compare
this new VLM to the Thieblemont et al results from 2024. However, the VLM assessment used here seems generally

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

consistent with the Thieblemont et al results. My point is that the VLM assessment itself is not novel, but the relationship to
the work described in the current manuscript is not clear. In fact, a figure from the current manuscript (Figure 2) seems very
similar to a figure in Thieblemont et al (Figure 7 in that paper), which was the first to identify the Schiavone region of Italy as
vulnerable to SLR because of VLM. The authors should cite Thieblemont et al (2024) much more clearly in the VLM section
of this new manuscript to identify their own original contributions vs. the contributions of that recent paper. It’s also not clear
why they used two strongly overlapping temporal periods to try to establish trends in VLM. 
2. SLR scenario 
The current manuscript notes that it is using an IPCC scenario for SLR in 2050, but the specific scenario is only mentioned
in the supplemental material (SSP2 4.5). This should also be noted in the main manuscript since it is a key driver of their
estimates of exposure. 
3. Study areas 
The authors state that they defined the coastal zone to be the zone 100 km from the shore, then go on to say that they used a
“bathtub model” for SLR flooding exposure with a DEM. It’s not clear whether they used the same Coastal Flood Plain extent
as Thieblemont et al 2024, but their Figure 6 does appear to show those defined Coastal Flood Plains for their analysis of
income levels. The authors should clarify which spatial extents were used for each analysis of population and asset
exposure, and note whether the extents vary (is it sometimes the inundation-prone zone and at other times only the
subsiding area within the inundation zone?) or whether they consistently used the Coastal Flood Plains defined in
Thieblemont et al 2024. 
Original contributions? 
4. Land cover data and exposure analysis 
The paper considers land cover using a highly simplified 5 category typology, and Fig 3 describes the distribution of
"subsiding pixels" for each. The figure has some confusing labels ("land use cover", and the word "less") but is otherwise
useful and interesting in spite of the simplicity of the land cover categories. Thieblemont et al 2024 also looked at land cover
in relation to coastal subsidence, and came to similar conclusions about wetland subsidence rates being high. That older
paper also used finer categories for the built environment (airports, rail lines, etc) which is more helpful than the current
manuscript’s use of “artificial areas” as a land cover category that lumps all aspects of the built environment into one
category. 
5. Exposure of buildings and other assets 
Population and assets were characterized previously on a global scale that included the European region. Jongman et al
2012 reviewed global population exposure, including income levels, as well as asset exposure using GDP. Thieblemont et
al 2024 used specific public assets (transportation infrastructure) for European coastal floodplains, which is arguably more
useful than reporting the value of private assets since transportation systems are typically publicly-owned, intended to be
permanent, and impact many aspects of a regional economy. 
This manuscript used a global database of building footprints, and somehow integrated housing prices from cost of living
data- but the method for the use of the data is unclear. As an economic analysis, this is unusual in my experience – other
papers have used estimates of GDP, for example, which considers economic output in addition to private property values.
Property values are usually used when a cost-benefit ratio is being calculated for adaptation, which is not the case here.
While unsophisticated, the number of buildings and total value of housing could be used to represent asset values in the
exposure zones. But the authors should provide more discussion of the details of their method in the supplemental materials
in order for readers to determine the validity of their methods. 
6. Population exposure and equity 
Identifying the inequalities of exposure for populations is described by the authors as the primary contribution of the paper.
They use a quartile approach to standardize income metrics as their measure of the relative level of private resources across
regions and countries. While income is only an indirect measure of resources (it does not consider wealth in the form of
property, or health status, or education level) it is not an unreasonable beginning for a discussion of vulnerability. The map
they produce in Figure 6 is novel, to my knowledge. 
They used census data to identify proportions of populations 65 years of age and over, or under 14 years of age, and then
assumed that these proportions would hold true in 2050. However this is unlikely to be the case; demographic trends should
have been considered instead of static proportions. 
Finally, the authors used the presence of Roma settlements as an indicator of vulnerability, by focusing on the
presence/absence of a group that has been described as especially vulnerable in regional analyses and policy. 
7. Geographic scale used for comparisons 
A series of country-scale assessments is used to characterize differences in exposure by area, population size, number of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

buildings, and other assets. While normally country-scale comparisons can obscure important spatial patterns, the
differences among these four are useful in pointing out the significance of SLR exposure for urban areas (with higher
population, numbers of buildings, and other infrastructure assets). 
Throughout, there is some lack of clarity about what the unit of analysis is for each variable. Were the demographic,
economic and asset analyses all applied within the areas of the defined inundation zone that are subsiding? What is the
threshold that defines “subsiding” land for this spatial analysis, is it -1 mm/year or simply <0? 
Overall comments 
This manuscript has a strong focus on the VLM analysis, and yet that is not novel. The authors want the reader to focus on
their analysis of the exposure of people, buildings and land cover types, and especially on the exposure of people with lower
incomes / different costs of living or property values / minority Roma settlements. While this socioeconomic exposure
analysis may be new for subsiding areas of coastal flood risk, it’s not clear that the methods used here are based on a
knowledge of methods developed recently by experts in this type of socioeconomic analysis. The primary citation used for
related work in the planning/socioeconomic literature seems to be from 2012 (Jongman et al). It’s also not clear that the
variables chosen to represent inequality are important to policy in the European Union. Since equity is a normative variable
that has been defined in different ways over different historical periods, it would be helpful for the authors to identify metrics
for inequality that are currently being used in EU policymaking. Adaptation planning will use some of the same
socioeconomic characterizations as any other kind of planning. 
I would suggest significant revisions to address (1) the relationship of the methods and results of this study to the methods
and results of Thieblemont et al 2024; (2) a brief review of current methods for assessing social inequality, with an emphasis
on methods relevant to EU policymaking and a revised analysis of socioeconomic vulnerability based on both; and (3)
clarification of the spatial units used for each analysis.
