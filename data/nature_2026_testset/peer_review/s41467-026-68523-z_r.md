Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study presents an interesting result of 3D printed optical glasses. Through functionalized by QDs, the 3D printed glass
with full spectrum luminescence across UV–VIS–IR were realized through doping nanoporous glass with desired metal ion
precursors. This work addresses critical challenge in the field of 3D printed glass, namely the conflict between high-
temperature sintering process required in 3D printed glass and the combination of QDs without degeneration. The 3D
printed functionalized glasses were constructed relying on the spatial and chemical dual nano-confined growth of diverse
QDs under low-temperature, which is innovative and effective. Furthermore, this low-temperature combined strategy for 3D
printed glass is universal, which is applicable for various semiconductors QDs. These results would open the door of 3D
printed glass for complex, spectrally-engineered applications. I believe this work is a valuable contribution to the field of 3D
printed glass, and recommend its publication in Nature Communications after minor revisions to address the points below. 
1. The quantitative atomic ratios of silicon, oxygen, and any potential contaminants (such as carbon) should be provided to
demonstrate the amorphous silica has a 1:2 stoichiometric ratio and no contaminants. In addition, it is recommended that
Raman spectroscopy characterization should be presented to further confirm the glass composition. 
2. The size of the QDs is controlled by tunning the pore size of the nanoporous glass, which is adjusted via the aluminum
lactate concentration. However, the manuscript lacks direct characterization of the different pore size. The authors should
provide pore size distribution data for the corresponding samples. 
3. In the analysis of the relationship between precursor concentration and reaction time (Figure 2h), the authors draw a
conclusion based on a comparison between a theoretical model and experimental data. However, only four experimental
points are presented. The authors should provide more data points to demonstrate the statistical reliability of their claims. 
4. Can the concentration of the QDs be quantitatively controlled? The manuscript briefly discusses a relationship between
QD concentration and the precursor doping level. More quantitative analysis of the final QD concentration and its
controllability should be provided. 
5. To better highlight the method’s versatility, please consider including the organic-inorganic hybrid perovskite QDs in
Figure 1d. 
6. In Figure 1a, the capitalization of initial letters in the labels should be made consistent. In Figure 2, the y-axis for “QD
concentration” is presented without specific units or values. In Figure 3, the labels appear to be misaligned with their
corresponding diffraction peaks. Please carefully check and correct to ensure the accuracy of the figures. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reportes the 3D printing of functional glass materials: a low-temperature post-functionalization strategy for
nanoporous glass (NG). This strategy addresses the critical contradiction of "high-temperature sintering-induced quantum
dot (QD) degradation" that arises during the integration of QDs with 3D-printed glass. Leveraging the intrinsic properties of
nanoporous glass, the authors propose an approach to realize the in-situ growth of diverse QDs through spatial and
chemical dual nano-confinement effects. Furthermore, the manuscript employs a comprehensive set of characterization
techniques to fully validate the underlying mechanism and provides compelling demonstrations of potential applications. I
recommend this work for publication in Nature communications after the authors address the following issues.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

1. The authors did not provide any refractive index data for nanoporous glass, metal-ion doped NG (M-NG), and 3D-printed
QDs-functionalized glass. Since refractive index is critical for the optical behaviors (e.g., light coupling, waveguiding) vital to
the proposed optical applications, these data should be given. 
2. When describing the PL stability of PQD (perovskite QD) glass, the manuscript refers to "ambient condition" (Fig. 3i) but
does not define its specific parameters-especially relative humidity (RH), a key factor which influences the stability of
perovskite. What is the RH level of the "ambient condition"? In addition, can the PQD glass maintain its PL intensity and
structural integrity under high-humidity environments (e.g., 80% RH or 95% RH)? 
3. In the photocatalytic CO2 reduction experiments (Fig. 4), the authors attribute enhanced performance of complex 3D
structures (e.g., channel-like microarray) to "enhanced photon scattering and surface area." However, they do not distinguish
whether the improved activity stems from the porous nature of the glass matrix (e.g., mass transfer of CO2/O2 through pores)
or solely from the increased surface area of the 3D architecture. 
4. The manuscript claims "voxel-level control over the QD bandgap" but does not specify the voxel size of the 3D-printed
structures (e.g., lateral resolution × layer thickness) or how QD uniformity is verified across individual voxels. What is the
voxel dimension achieved via DLP printing? 
5. For the in-situ QD growth process, the authors mentioned that M-NG is "saturated in QDs precursor solution" but did not
report the influence of precursor concentration on the QD growth and the light emitting performance. Will the increase of
precursor concentration lead to excessive QD aggregation, and if so, what is the optimal concentration window to maintain
monodisperse QDs? 
6. There is a spelling error in Line 102 of Page 3 in the manuscript, which should be corrected to Zn(NO3)2.

1. The authors did not provide any refractive index data for nanoporous glass, metal-

and discussion have been added in the revised manuscript (Page 4, Line 128-129) and 
revised SI.

5. For the in-situ QD growth process, the authors mentioned that M-NG is "saturated in 
QDs precursor solution" but did not report the influence of precursor concentration on 
the QD growth and the light emitting performance. Will the increase of precursor 
concentration lead to excessive QD aggregation, and if so, what is the optimal 
concentration window to maintain monodisperse QDs? 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. To investigate the 
influence of precursor concentration on the QD growth, we have performed TEM of 
the 3D printed QD functionalized glass synthesized by precursor solution with different 
concentrations (5, 10, and 15 mg/mL) . As shown in Figs. R1 0a-c, the QDs show  
excellent monodispersity without observable aggregation . The corresponding size 
distribution (Fig. R10d) shows the QD sizes remain consistent across these different 
concentrations. The monodispersity of QDs is attributed to the uniform dispersion of 
Pb precursors and the spatial nanoconfinement effect provided by the M-NG matrix. 
The former ensures a homogeneous supply of Pb precursors, and the latter, where the 
pore size physically defines the QD dimens ions, effectively prevents aggregation and 
renders QD growth independent of precursor concentration variations. 
Additionally, we conducted corresponding PL measurements to demonstrate the 
light emitting performance of QD functionalized glass  with different concentration 
precursor solutions. The PL spectra in Fig. R11 show that there is almost no difference 
in the emission across the three precursor concentrations , indicating that precursor 
concentration does not have a noticeable effect on the light emitting performance of the 
QDs. Even at the high precursor concentration of 15 mg/mL, the emission peak remains 
at 490 nm, indicating the monodispersed quantum-confined QDs rather than aggregated

ones (for CsPbBr3 bulk material, the emission peak is around 534 nm), in agreement 
with the TEM results. We observed the highest PLQY of 82% at a CsBr concentration 
of 5 mg/mL, as shown in Fig. R11b., indicating this is the optimal concentration.
