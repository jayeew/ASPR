Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled, “PD-L1 expressing CD127+ ILC1s inhibit PD-1+ γδ T cells in the mesenteric adipose tissue to
alleviate murine peritonitis” reports the role of crosstalk between mesenteric adipose tissue (MAT)-CD127+ILC1s and γδ T
cells through the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in controlling CLP-induced acute peritonitis by suppressing excessive inflammation.
In this article, the authors showed that ILC1 expressing high levels of IL-7Ra (CD127) are increased during acute peritonitis,
and they suppress the TNFa+ gamma delta T cell function via direct contact with the cells via PD-L1 and PD-1 interaction.
The authors showed that those ILC1s originated from the spleen, and were recruited to mesenteric adipose tissue via
CXCR3 – CXCL10 axis. The authors performed various robust experiments and tried to discover the systemic and cellular
mechanism for regulating inflammatory gamma delta T cells via ILC1s. While most of the conclusions are well supported by
convincing data, additional experiments and appropriate control should be performed and included to further strengthen the
manuscript. 
Major comments: 
1. The authors utilized CLP as an inducer of peritonitis. This model naturally progresses to sepsis, leading to mortality in the
mice. Depending on the severity of CLP, it appears to influence the degree of temperature drop and the time required for
recovery. The authors specifically focus on peritonitis, as mentioned in the title of the paper. According to the Methods
section, the authors performed a 0.5 cm cecal exposure, which corresponds to a moderate CLP induction. However, the
more significant temperature changes seem to occur at later time points, such as 8 and 12 hours, rather than at 6 hours.
Therefore, further explanation is needed as to why the authors chose to focus on the 6-hour time point. 
2. Moreover, the differences in survival observed in the later stages of the study are ultimately related to the effects of sepsis.
If the authors aim to focus on peritonitis, they should demonstrate changes in phenotypes other than temperature to justify
their discussion of the immune response to peritonitis. 
3. The authors claimed that mesenteric adipose tissue (MAT) ILC1 is associated with acute peritonitis (line 81-83). As a
reason for the claim, the authors showed the increased cell number in ILC1, while the number of other ILCs or NK cells did
not increase. However, the absolute cell number of ILC1 is lower than NK cells or ILC2. Furthermore, the authors did not
show the number of peritoneal myeloid cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils, which are abundant in peritoneal cavity
and participate in inflammatory pathways such as acute peritonitis. Understanding the relative abundance of ILCs among
other immune cells could provide important context for their role in the immune response. The authors need to sufficiently
explain why they focus on ILCs over the many other types of infiltrating immune cells. There are some models to specifically
deplete ILCs while sustaining T cell functionality (Rafei-Shamsabadi, D, A. Lack of Type 2 Innate Lymphoid Cells Promotes
a Type I-Driven Enhanced Immune Response in Contact Hypersensitivity. J Invest Dermatol (2018)). If the authors have
performed such a specific ILC depletion, then the importance of ILCs will be highlighted. 
4. In Figure 2D and Figure 2E, the authors claimed cluster 1 cells are a mixed population of NK cells and ILC1. However, in
the violin plot, cluster1 expressed the highest level of T-bet (Tbx21) and IFN-gamma (Ifng). Therefore, rather than a mere
mixture of cells, the cluster is a specific cluster with specific gene expression. Therefore, the authors at least show the
feature genes to see the signatures of each clusters, not merely annotating them as mixture of cells. 
5. In both the Figure 2E and Extended Data Figure 2A legends, it is clear that the same dataset was used. The CITE-seq
results refer to the ILC1/NK population. However, why is there a discrepancy between the expression patterns of CD127 and
IL7r? These are aliases for the same gene. It is necessary to verify whether the authors have confused the results obtained
from different datasets. 
6. In Figure 2F, the authors gated CD127- ILC1 and NK cells together to assess IFN-γ expression. However, based on

Figure E and previous studies, there are likely differences in the IFN-γ expression potential between these two cell types.
For more accurate analysis, it would be better to first distinguish ILC1 and NK cells based on EOMES expression. Following
this, the ILC1 population could be further subdivided according to CD127 expression levels for a more precise assessment
of IFN-γ production in each group. 
7. I wonder why there is no analysis of CD127+ ILC1 in the sham group in Figure 2F. It would be interesting to know if
CD127+ ILC1 show not only numerical differences in the CLP group but also changes in functional characteristics such as
IFN-γ expression, or if CD127+ ILC1 inherently produce low levels of IFN-γ, regardless of CLP. Additionally, investigating
the differences in cell characteristics between these groups could be further explored by performing a more detailed analysis
of the single cell analysis data from Figure 2D, separating the Sham and CLP groups. 
8. In figure 2C, the authors state that after 6 hours of CLP, number of total ILC1 increase 2-fold (500 to 1000). In figure 3F and
3J, after parabiosis and spleen transplant experiments, the percent of ILC1 counterpart that the authors claim to derive from
the spleen out of whole ILC1 increase from 4 to 10 percent for parabiosis experiments and 1 to 3 percent for spleen
transplant experiments. Which means that the spleen derived ILC1 cannot explain the 2-fold increase in total ILC1 in MAT
after CLP. Therefore, while it is reasonable to assert that some ILC1 migrate from the spleen to the MAT, authors need to
conduct additional experiment for explaining the 2-fold increase in figure 2C. Moreover, since spleen-derived ILC1s
constitute such a small population of the whole ILC1 population in the MAT in both sham and CLP condition, it is necessary
to prove that these cells are a critical contributor to the phenotypes observed. 
9. If ILC1 are derived from spleen, authors should verify increased ILC1 circulation in blood with markers including CD127,
CXCR3 and Ly6C. 
10. FACS data in figure 2B shows that CD127+ ILC1 constitute up to 60% of the whole ILC1 population in MAT 6h after
CLP. However, cluster 0 in the UMAP plot in figure 2D, which the authors claim to be the MAT-CD127+ ILC1s does not
seem to occupy 60% of the whole ILC1/NK group, and indeed it seems to be such a small population in the UMAP plot.
Authors should clarify the discrepancy between FACS and CITE-seq data. 
11. While Ly6C, Cxcr3, and PDL1 are emphasized as key molecules in the manuscript, the authors do not present
transcriptomic data for these markers. Providing RNA-level expression data for these molecules would not only complement
the current findings but also offer a more comprehensive understanding of their biological significance. Furthermore, since
the earlier figures consistently distinguish between CD127+ ILC1 and CD127- ILC1 populations, it would be more
scientifically rigorous to compare the expression levels of these molecules within this established framework. This approach
would strengthen the clarity and validity of the authors' conclusions. 
12. The authors demonstrated the role of the CXCR10-CXCR3 axis in ILC1 migration. While CXCL10 protein expression
was assessed in the MAT, and CXCR3 expression on ILC1s was measured in the spleen, it has not been evaluated in the
MAT. Therefore, it is important to confirm the expression of CXCR3 on ILC1s within the MAT. 
13. In lines 202-204, you said CD127+ ILC1s “drive TNF-α production” in γδ T cells through the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway. This
sentence contradicts your data and previous words in this paper. 
14. In the Extended data Figure 5 experiment, is there any data on how many adoptively transferred CD45.2 MAT-ILC1s
were in the host MAT at CLP 6h? 
15. In the Extended data Figure 5 experiment, you used the bone marrow transfer to make an ILC1-depleted mouse and
transferred ILC1 to see the effect of the existence of ILC1. Why did you transfer ILC1 from MAT of the CLP-induced mice?
Your previous study shows that the splenic ILC1s are migrated to MAT when CLP is induced. Transferring splenic ILC1s
from healthy mice is better to show your hypothesis. Or transplant a PLZF-/- spleen or PLZF+/+ spleen from healthy mice to
PLZF-/- bone marrow transplanted mice to see the exact mechanism of the splenic ILC1s. 
16. In Figure 6, TNF-α+ γδ T cells represent only a small subset of the total cells. It raises the question of whether γδ T cells
are the primary producers of TNF-α in response to CLP when ILC1s are absent. Also, to determine if TNF-α-producing γδ T
cells regulated by ILC1s are the main contributors to preventing excessive inflammatory responses, additional experiments
such as γδ T cell depletion, blocking, or TNF inhibitors treatment would be necessary. These experiments would help clarify
whether γδ T cells regulated by ILC1s play a central role in controlling inflammation in this context. 
Minor comments: 
1. In the supplementary figure 2A, the authors wrote the protein level on the violin plot, while the violin plots show mRNA
expression value. 
2. Given the overall flow of the paper, ILC1 is the most critical population. Why are there no marker genes distinguishing the
clusters in Figures 2D and 2E? 
3. In line 130, authors referenced reports on the role of IL-7 in downregulating CD127 expression, and states that such
results are consistent with their findings. Is the any reason why authors chose the reference on T cells? 
4. Figure 3M and 3N should include data after CLP. 
5. Authors should show proportion difference between sham and CLP 6h condition of the UMAP cluster in figure 2D. 
6. It would be helpful to label "Cont" and "a-PD-1 Ab" in Figures 5F and 5G respectively. 
7. It would be better to consistently use either "CD127+ ILC1" and "CD127- ILC1" with spaces or "CD127+ILC1" and
"CD127-ILC1" without spaces throughout the manuscript, as the current version inconsistently mixes both formats. 
8. In Extended Data Figure 4, there is no gating strategy provided for CD4, CD8, and γδ T cells. When presenting the PD-1
levels, it would be more appropriate to display these marker genes on the y-axis. Alternatively, it would be helpful to include
the pre-gating strategy for these populations. 
9. Extended data Figure 4. C and D are not mentioned. It's better to explain the data in the main text or remove it. 
10. At line 202, it's better to unify the terminology: Extended Fig. 4B to Extended data Fig. 4B. 
11. In line 218, the nomenclature “PLZF-BM chimera mice” appeared, but you transplanted bone marrow from both PLZF-/-
and PLZF+/+ mice. It's better to clarify what the PLZF-BM mice are. 
12. In the Extended data Figure 5A, the scheme said that MAT-ILC1 or PBS were injected into both the PLZF-/- chimera and
the PLZF+/+ chimera. Is it true that you used the four-mouse condition?: PLZF-/- chimera + MAT-ILC1, PLZF-/- chimera +
PBS, PLZF+/+ chimera + MAT-ILC1, and PLZF+/+ chimera + PBS. I'm very confused with the experimental scheme even

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript examines the role of CD127+ ILC1s in the mesenteric adipose tissue in a low mortality model of cecal
ligation and puncture 
Major comments: 
1) Globally, the manuscript often conflates association with mechanistic causation. There are a number of times when the
authors find the two phenomena are associated, but this does not mean that one causes the other. 
2) The authors examine a few parameters in figure 1 at 6 hours and 24 hours and find that differences are greater at 6 hours
than 24 hours and conclude that “the strongest inflammatory condition occurs at around 6 hours.” This conclusion cannot be
drawn. All they can conclude is that inflammation is stronger at 6 hours than 24 hours as inflammation might be greater at 1
or 3 or 8 or 12 hours than 6 hours and the authors did not examine for this. The same is true when the authors state that
bacterial invasion was maximally detected at 6 hours after CLP when they only examined 6 and 24 hours. 
3) The results state that IL-b is decreased 24 hours after CLP in figure 1D. This is not consistent with what is in the figure. 
4) Some of the n’s in figures are quite low. For instance, figure 1E appears to be n=3. It is difficult to conclude anything
meaningful from such a low sample size. 
5) Quantification should be shown for figure 1G. 
6) ILC-1 number are not different between 6 and 24 hours and are not different between sham and 24 hours. It may or may
not be that the differences seen between sham and 6 hours are meaningful based upon this. 
7) The conclusion on line 131 that the results “suggest that CD127 expression ILC1s was altered by the reduction of IL-7 in
the MAT during acute inflammation, leading to an increase in CD127+ ILC1s at CLP 6h” is not supported by the data
presented. As per point 1, correlation is not the same as causation. 
8) The parabiosis experiments are poorly described and difficult to understand. 
9) A decrease in splenic Ly6C+CD127- cells could potentially indicate migration but it could also indicate that these cells die
– a possibility that is not considered in the manuscript. 
10) The anti-CXCR3 experiments do not look at MAT. Therefore it is difficult to see how conclusions can be drawn regarding
the MAT. 
11) The data in figure 4D and E is also associative rather than proving causation. 
12) Similarly, the data in Fig. 5F and G are also associative. While one certainly cannot refuse the authors statement in the
results that this indicates a “possibility” of a mechanistic relationship, that is more speculative for the discussion. 
13) The ILC-1 deficient mice appear to be globally deficient as opposed to specifically in the MAT. 
14) While the authors acknowledge that figures 6C and D only show a “potential interaction” between ILC1 and gamma/delta
T cells via the PD-PDL1 pathway in the MAT, there is also an association as opposed to a clear link. 
15) Were mice given pain medicine? If so, this needs to be stated. If not, the reason for withholding them needs to be clearly
stated. 
16) Were data tested for a normal distribution? This is required prior to using a t test – otherwise a non-parametric test
should be used. If so, this should be included in the statistics section. Otherwise, this needs to be checked and the statistics
redone (some of the groups are too small to have a normal distribution). 
Minor comments: 
1) The first line of the introduction states that “peritonitis is a critical illness.” Peritonitis simply means inflammation of the
peritoneum. Peritonitis could be associated with critical illness but more often than not is not as most patients with acute
abdomens do not end up in the ICU. 
2) What was the sex of mice used? This is not stated. 
3) 10-25 weeks is a large span of mouse age ranging from young to middle aged. Why was such a large span of ages used
and were mice age matched in each experiment? 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the present manuscript Nagata et al investigated the role of ILC1s during peritonitis, by using a model of cecal ligation and
puncture (CLP). The authors show that upon inflammation the numbers of ILC1s transiently increase in the mesenteric
adipose tissue (MAT), alongside their expression of CD127. These cells possess a unique phenotype producing less IFNg
than their CD127- counterparts and expressing high levels of PD-L1, suggestive of a regulatory role. Indeed the authors
show that genetic ablation of ILC1s induces increased inflammation, as evidenced by more TNF production by gd T cells
and decreased body temperature, ultimately decreasing survival of the animals. Interestingly, the authors suggest that these
"regulatory" ILC1s migrate from the spleen to the MAT in a CXCR3-dependent manner. The findings presented here are
novel and part of them are supported by very elegant approaches. In my opinion, however, this manuscript would benefit
from additional experiments to strengthen the authors' claims, as delineated below. 
Major points: 
- Even though Nagata et al used very interesting approaches to pinpoint a role for migration in the ILC1 response during
CLP (such as spleen transplants and parabiosis), I think some key points need to be addressed. First, in Figure 1h, we

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

observe that the numbers of ILC1 double at 6h post CLP, however, in both the parabiosis and transplantation experiments,
the frequency of ILC1s coming from circulation is less than 10% (9% and 5% on average, respectively). Have the authors
checked for local proliferation? Also, could these ILC1s coming from other tissues such as the liver and gut? Upon CXCR3
blockade the authors observe an enrichment in ILC1s in the Spleen (suggesting that the cells are trapped there); but have
the authors checked to see if CXCR3 blockade prevents the increase in ILC1s in the MAT? Finally, I understand the difficulty
of underlying the spleen transplant experiment, but the n for that experiment seems quite low (so much so, that the difference
in CD45.1+ ILC1s is not even significant) and I cannot find anywhere if/how many times that experiment has been repeated.
As this supports a big conclusion of the paper, the authors should repeat this experiment to increase the n; 
- Still regarding the migration of ILC1s to the MAT, have the authors checked if blockade of CXCR3 induces an increase in
cytokine production by gd T cells? What about the inflammatory response and survival of these mice? 
- Another important conclusion of the paper is that ILC1 suppression of gd-derived TNF restricts inflammation during
peritonitis. Whereas by the CITE-Seq data it looks only gd T cells produce TNF (Extended Data Fig 4) the intracellular
cytokine levels of TNF in gds seem very low (Fig 5g) and even the total protein levels in the MAT is the lowest when
compared to all the other inflammatory markers (Fig 1e). This raises the question if suppression of gd-derived TNF mediates
the regulatory role of ILC1s. The authors should formally address the role of TNF in the survival and regulation of body
temperature of CLP mice, for instance by blocking TNF in the Ncr1Cre X Tbx21Flox mice; 
- Is the acquisition of CD127 (and the associated regulatory phenotype, i.e. low IFNg production and PD-L1 expression)
dependent only on the low IL-7 levels? If so, blocking IL-7 in naïve mice, or other inflammatory conditions that decrease IL-7
levels would also lead to the appearance of these regulatory ILC1s. Finally, do the authors have a hypothesis for how low
levels of IL-7 might suppress the IFNg and increase PD-L1 expression? 
Minor points: 
- Bone marrow chimeras have a severe deficiency in the population of IL-17-producing gd T cells (Haas, Immunity, 2012) -
which are the subset known to reside in adipose tissue. When working with populations that are not radio-resistant, a way to
overcome this problem is by injecting neonatal thymocytes along adult bone marrow (Papotto, EMBO Reports, 2017). Have
the authors checked that the gd population in the MAT is comparable to that from non-bone marrow chimeras? I.e., are they
still able to produce IL-17 and TNF? 
- How do the authors explain that PD-L1 from ILC1s induce PD1 expression in gd T cells? Could the first be secreting
another cytokine/factor that increases PD1 expression instead? 
- Do splenic CD127+ ILC1s also produce low levels of IFNg and express PD-L1? 
- What happens to ILC1s to reach normal numbers at 24h post CLP? Do they die or do they migrate somewhere else? Have
the authors performed annexing staining or checked for CD45.1+ cells in other organs from the parabiotic/transplanted mice,
for instance? 
- In the final point of their discussion the authors suggest that giving recombinant IL-7 could be beneficial to patients with
sepsis. However, wouldn't this go against what the data shows? Reduced IL-7 induces "regulatory" ILC1s that curb
inflammation during sepsis? 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study by Nagata et al highlights the migration of ILC1s from the spleen to MAT and their interaction modulating γδ T
cells via PD-1/PD-L1 during acute peritonitis. The findings contribute to further understanding of migratory behaviours in
ILCs, particularly ILC1’s, and their interaction with other immune cells namely gdT cells. Here, they identified the PD-1/PD-
L1 ligand-interaction as the mechanism for ILC1’s controlling gdT cell-mediated secretion of TNF and subsequent
inflammation. 
The mouse model Ncr/Tbx21 is compelling in demonstrating the role of ILC1 in early timepoints in this peritonitis model and
represent a useful mouse model for the field. Parabiosis and splenic surgery are methodologically convincing to evidence of
ILC1’s migration, further adding to the field’s understanding of circulating vs tissue resident ILCs. 
While this study adds to immune cell function within AT, this reviewer suggests the following to better contextualise and
support the study’s conclusions. 
In determining the mechanistic crosstalk and what CD127+ PD-L1+ ILC1’s are acting on, the authors primarily pursued
PD1+ gdT cells and their increased TNF production. Could other cells play a (bigger) role: There is a risk of over-
simplification on immune cell crosstalk here as the authors only showed bulk CD4 and CD8 T cells not expressing PD1. Is
there more data or discussion from gdT cell deficient models? More adequate analysis that other immune or T/innate-like T
cell populations are not directly affected by migratory ILC1’s or express PD1 will be more convincing, using analysis on the
Ncr/Tbx21 mice, or at the very least discussed. 
Could TNF be a or one of many bystander effects of gdT cell activation? There is a risk of disproportionate focus on TNF
from gdT cells driving this acute pathology, as they constitute a relatively minor population of ILC1’s/lymphocytes (eg <0.4%
in Fig 5). Is this cytokine from gdT more critical and specific to MAT immune responses during CLP? What’s the relative
contribution of total TNF increase by gdT cells against other cells? 
On the other hand, the critical role of TNF seems to be
downplayed in significance from the start (in Fig 1) prior to pursuing the TNF-producing gdT cells. Are there further evidence
or discussion on functional contribution of TNF in TNF deficient or neutralisation? Furthermore, data on longitudinal tracking
of gdT cells and whether their TNF/other cytokine production significantly change towards resolution or progression across
timeline post CLP could be informative. 
Importantly, details on number of mice and experiments are lacking from Fig 3 onwards across manuscript and figure
legends thus it is difficult to ascertain the data reproducibility and rigor.

Point-by-point responses to the reviewers’ comments  We thank the reviewers for their careful reading of the manuscript and their encouraging comments and suggestions for improvements.
