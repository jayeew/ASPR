Reply to Reviewer #1: 
 
Deciphering the mechanism causing volcanic hazard is impossible without determining 
the inner structure of magmatic system beneath volcanoes. The present work is another 
cornerstone of the authors’ continuing effort to decode the inner structure of magmatic 
bodies beneath past and active volcanoes. Their innovative approach is capable of 
extract the slightest ~10% contrast between the seismic velocity of two types of surface 
waves. And from this contrast, which is called radial anisotropy, the authors could  
estimate how a magmatic system spanning 10s km in depth range could consist of 
stacked layers of varying melt porosity. This time, the authors focused on a relatively 
small and less tectonically active volcano, the Valles. Results show that the magmatic 
system beneath Valles shares the same “sill -complex” or “composite” structure seen 
under Yellowstone, Long Valley, and Toba.  
These ﬁndings advance fundamental questions in volcanology: how magma reservoirs 
form, remain molten over geologic timescales, and respond to external perturbations 
under varying tectonic and thermal regimes. While the manuscript is compelling, the 
physical controls governing the “organization” of the composite reservoir could be 
articulated more coherently. Please see my major comments below. I hope the 
suggestion to link hydrothermal activity and recharge rate could be constructive.  
To sum up, I recommend prompt publication after minor revisions. 
 
Thank you for your constructive suggestions and comments. 
 
Major comments  
Any melt in the crystal-rich layer?  
The current VTI modelling considers three values of Vs for the crystal-rich layer. These 
values can be Vs of completely solidiﬁed rhyolites if the bulk composition and 
temperature are allowed to vary. However, another view is that Vs of crystal-rich layer 
is also reduced relative to a solidiﬁed rhyolite. Even a ~1% melt in the crystal-rich layer 
has signiﬁcant impact on the viscosity and overall connectivity of melt. If the crystal -
rich layer is also somewhat soft, then mechanical mixing of the interlayered  structure 
may be possible. We can further this line of thought through some simple ﬂuid 
dynamics instability analysis. But here, including the possibility of melt presence in 
both types of layers is a minimum.

Answers: We agree that the presence of melt in the crystal-rich layer can influence 
its rheological properties, including viscosity and melt connectivity. However, as 
supported by the modeling results in Fig. 4c, small melt fraction (e.g., ~5%) leads 
to relatively small reduction in Vs, on the order of 0.4 km s -1. Our VTI modeling 
framework is fundamentally sensitive to Vs, and we have already accounted for a 
range of possible Vs values in the crystal -rich layer to reflect such variability. 
Therefore, even if the crystal -rich contains a small amount of melt, its effect is 
encompassed within the Vs variations we considered. Consideration of the 
possibility that some melt resides in the relatively high Vs layers is why we refer 
to them as relatively crystal-rich, rather than strictly ‘melt-free’. 
 
 
Preserving a magma chamber, thermal perspective 
Regardless of the ﬁne structure, a supra -solidus temperature is a necessity for 
preserving the magmatic system for ~ myr. There are a tractable set of factors in the 
thermal evolution. To maintain a steady state temperature, surface heat loss should 
balance advect heat due to recharge from below and latent heat within (Liu and Lee, 
2020, 10.1029/2020gc009220). The most interesting part is that 
observations/estimations of these factors at different volcanoes are readily available 
thus allowing us a systemati c comparison. The Yellowstone has a combination of 
relatively high recharge and fast heat loss through strong hydrothermal system. Does 
Valles have a combination of relatively low recharge and slow heat loss? Let us see 
these volcanoes in a space of recharge vs. surface heat ﬂux.  
Answers: It is likely that Valles has a combination of relatively low recharge and 
slow heat loss compared to Yellowstone. Heat flow is relatively well constrained 
but the recharge rate is not well known. Borehole drilling indicates that the highest 
heat flux within the caldera, ~0.4 W m-2, is localized in its southwestern side (Sass 
& Morgan, 1988), which is significantly lower than the mean heat flux of 2.3 W m-
2 in Yellowstone (Fournier, 1989). The differences in thermal conditions are 
acknowledged in th e manuscript by plotting representative geotherms in Fig . 1. 
Contemporary recharge is small and uncertain because surface deformation is 
near or below the limit of detection with available geodetic data (Maier et al., 2025). 
At longer time scales, thermochronological evidence suggests that a recent thermal 
event with an age of <40 ka at the Fenton Hill site just west of the caldera (Harrison

heat flux is well constrained by observations. It would be an excellent target for a 
thermal modeling study to constrain the viable range of time-dependent recharge 
rates but that is well beyond the practical scope of this 3 -D anisotropic seismic 
imaging study.

Reply to Reviewer #1: 
 
Deciphering the mechanism causing volcanic hazard is impossible without determining 
the inner structure of magmatic system beneath volcanoes. The present work is another 
cornerstone of the authors’ continuing effort to decode the inner structure of magmatic 
bodies beneath past and active volcanoes. Their innovative approach is capable of 
extract the slightest ~10% contrast between the seismic velocity of two types of surface 
waves. And from this contrast, which is called radial anisotropy, the authors could  
estimate how a magmatic system spanning 10s km in depth range could consist of 
stacked layers of varying melt porosity. This time, the authors focused on a relatively 
small and less tectonically active volcano, the Valles. Results show that the magmatic 
system beneath Valles shares the same “sill -complex” or “composite” structure seen 
under Yellowstone, Long Valley, and Toba.  
These ﬁndings advance fundamental questions in volcanology: how magma reservoirs 
form, remain molten over geologic timescales, and respond to external perturbations 
under varying tectonic and thermal regimes. While the manuscript is compelling, the 
physical controls governing the “organization” of the composite reservoir could be 
articulated more coherently. Please see my major comments below. I hope the 
suggestion to link hydrothermal activity and recharge rate could be constructive.  
To sum up, I recommend prompt publication after minor revisions. 
 
Thank you for your constructive suggestions and comments. 
 
Major comments  
Any melt in the crystal-rich layer?  
The current VTI modelling considers three values of Vs for the crystal-rich layer. These 
values can be Vs of completely solidiﬁed rhyolites if the bulk composition and 
temperature are allowed to vary. However, another view is that Vs of crystal-rich layer 
is also reduced relative to a solidiﬁed rhyolite. Even a ~1% melt in the crystal-rich layer 
has signiﬁcant impact on the viscosity and overall connectivity of melt. If the crystal -
rich layer is also somewhat soft, then mechanical mixing of the interlayered  structure 
may be possible. We can further this line of thought through some simple ﬂuid 
dynamics instability analysis. But here, including the possibility of melt presence in 
both types of layers is a minimum.

Answers: We agree that the presence of melt in the crystal-rich layer can influence 
its rheological properties, including viscosity and melt connectivity. However, as 
supported by the modeling results in Fig. 4c, small melt fraction (e.g., ~5%) leads 
to relatively small reduction in Vs, on the order of 0.4 km s -1. Our VTI modeling 
framework is fundamentally sensitive to Vs, and we have already accounted for a 
range of possible Vs values in the crystal -rich layer to reflect such variability. 
Therefore, even if the crystal -rich contains a small amount of melt, its effect is 
encompassed within the Vs variations we considered. Consideration of the 
possibility that some melt resides in the relatively high Vs layers is why we refer 
to them as relatively crystal-rich, rather than strictly ‘melt-free’. 
 
 
Preserving a magma chamber, thermal perspective 
Regardless of the ﬁne structure, a supra -solidus temperature is a necessity for 
preserving the magmatic system for ~ myr. There are a tractable set of factors in the 
thermal evolution. To maintain a steady state temperature, surface heat loss should 
balance advect heat due to recharge from below and latent heat within (Liu and Lee, 
2020, 10.1029/2020gc009220). The most interesting part is that 
observations/estimations of these factors at different volcanoes are readily available 
thus allowing us a systemati c comparison. The Yellowstone has a combination of 
relatively high recharge and fast heat loss through strong hydrothermal system. Does 
Valles have a combination of relatively low recharge and slow heat loss? Let us see 
these volcanoes in a space of recharge vs. surface heat ﬂux.  
Answers: It is likely that Valles has a combination of relatively low recharge and 
slow heat loss compared to Yellowstone. Heat flow is relatively well constrained 
but the recharge rate is not well known. Borehole drilling indicates that the highest 
heat flux within the caldera, ~0.4 W m-2, is localized in its southwestern side (Sass 
& Morgan, 1988), which is significantly lower than the mean heat flux of 2.3 W m-
2 in Yellowstone (Fournier, 1989). The differences in thermal conditions are 
acknowledged in th e manuscript by plotting representative geotherms in Fig . 1. 
Contemporary recharge is small and uncertain because surface deformation is 
near or below the limit of detection with available geodetic data (Maier et al., 2025). 
At longer time scales, thermochronological evidence suggests that a recent thermal 
event with an age of <40 ka at the Fenton Hill site just west of the caldera (Harrison

heat flux is well constrained by observations. It would be an excellent target for a 
thermal modeling study to constrain the viable range of time-dependent recharge

imaging study.
