(Remarks to the Author) 
The electrocatalytic construction of C–N bonds for converting inorganic small molecules into high-value chemicals remains
a significant research challenge. While this work designs a Se-InOx catalyst that achieves highly selective conversion of
nitrate and CO2 to urea, the high selectivity is attributed to a "hierarchical click-site" mechanism, which is conceptually
inappropriate as it bears no legitimate connection to click chemistry principles. Theoretical calculations indicate that CO2
and nitrate reduction occur at the same catalytic sites. However, the manuscript fails to resolve why a site inherently
incapable of adsorbing CO2 becomes favorable for CO2 binding after *NO2 formation. Additionally, the catalyst's structure
remains ambiguous, as all schematic diagrams fail to demonstrate a standard Se-InOx configuration convincingly. The cost
analysis lacks practical significance due to the use of non-industrial current densities; CO2 reduction requires hundreds of
mA cm

2 current densities to approach feasibility. Therefore, the manuscript does not meet Nature Communications'
publication standards in its current form. Major Revision or Resubmission is required before further consideration. Specific
concerns are as follows: 
Regarding concept and mechanism: 
1. The core of click chemistry involves efficient, modular reactions that stitch together small molecular units. This work
describes sequential catalysis occurring at a single catalytic site, which does not align with the concept of click chemistry,
making its appropriation unjustified. 
2. C–N coupling constitutes a more challenging step in the reaction pathway; however, experimental and theoretical
calculations are lacking to explain how *NO2 subsequently couples with CO2 after its formation, whether the actual energy
barrier for this step is genuinely lower than that of multisite catalysts, how CO2 becomes activated, and how the C–N
covalent bond forms. 
3. Given that *NO2 should be preserved to enhance its coupling ability with CO2, theoretical calculations should supplement
the energy barriers for *NO
₂

4. Theoretical calculations lack comparisons with other reaction pathways. A single reaction path does not explain why the
catalyst favors urea formation. 
Regarding the catalyst structure: 
The authors appear to have conducted extensive characterization but fail to clearly describe the surface structure of the
catalyst. Based on the synthesis method, Se likely substitutes oxygen on the InOx surface, and the introduced Se alters the
electronic structure of In, thereby influencing reactant binding affinity. Consequently, precise control over the amount of Se
introduced is critical. What is the quantity of Se introduced? How many Se atoms are adjacent to an In atom? How would the
activity change if the amount of introduced Se is altered? Furthermore, introducing Se via calcination can readily disrupt the
original metal-oxygen bonds, potentially forming other structures like oxygen vacancies, which might be more favorable for
reactant binding. Using EPR to claim the presence of vacancies is fundamentally inappropriate. Please supplement with
more precise structural characterizations, such as XAFS, etc. All structural schematics in the manuscript do not represent the
true structure of Se-InOx; not even an oxygen atom is depicted. XRD indicates that InOx should be In2O3, so why is it
uniformly written as InOx?

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The proposed manuscript evaluates the performance of a novel Se-InOx catalyst system for urea electrosynthesis from
wastewater streams. The techno-economic analysis (TEA) presented in this manuscript is well-executed and represents a
significant contribution to the field of sustainable urea production. 
The authors model an electrochemical urea synthesis process using Se–InOx catalysts and demonstrate economic
feasibility under future low-cost electricity scenarios. Notably, the study estimates a urea production cost of $1.01/kg at
$0.03/kWh electricity, which is in line with current market conditions, and a minimum cost of $0.16/kg when powered by
electricity priced at $0.01/kWh and with valorization of by-products. These electricity prices are optimistic but realistic in a
long-term renewable energy future. 
The economic analysis is detailed and transparent, incorporating well-reasoned capital and operational cost assumptions
grounded in U.S. DOE H2A data and established chemical engineering practice. The financial analysis includes payback
periods and net present value projections, showing that profitability is achievable at current densities above 50 mA/cm², with
a payback period as short as six years at 100 mA/cm². The univariate sensitivity analysis is also useful, identifying cell
voltage, electricity price, Faradaic efficiency, and equipment and labor costs as primary cost drivers. 
However, the sensitivity plot in Figure 6 would benefit from formatting improvements—specifically, the inclusion of baseline
parameter values alongside y-axis labels and the addition of units ($/kg Urea) to the output axis. The TEA methodology itself
is sound, with appropriate treatment of depreciation, electricity and labor costs, and process-scale assumptions. 
While the economic assessment is compelling, the environmental claims would be substantially strengthened by the
inclusion of a life cycle assessment (LCA), particularly to quantify net-negative carbon emissions and to benchmark against
existing urea production pathways. Overall, the TEA is a strong and reproducible component of the work and supports the
manuscript’s conclusions regarding the feasibility and scalability of electrochemical urea synthesis.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript has improved substantially. The TEA revisions are sound and the LCA is now generally consistent.
However, one key issue remains regarding the fate of the CO2 used in the urea synthesis step. The LCA currently treats this
CO2 as if it is permanently sequestered, but that is only valid if the carbon remains stored over a long timeframe. In
conventional fertilizer use, urea hydrolyzes in the field and the CO2 is re-released to the atmosphere. Therefore, if the
primary product is fertilizer urea, the CO2 must be counted as an emission at end use. This point is critical not only for
accurate LCA accounting, but also for any claims related to carbon crediting: carbon markets require demonstration that
captured CO2 is not subsequently re-emitted. If the CO2 is later released, the system cannot be credited as a net carbon
removal pathway, and the LCA should reflect this by including the CO2 release during the product use phase. 
If instead a portion of the urea carbon is embodied in non-fertilizer products (e.g., resins), the manuscript should report: (1)
what fraction of production goes to such products, (2) the expected carbon storage lifetime, and (3) end-of-life fate. This
would allow distinguishing between temporary storage and permanent sequestration. A simple carbon fate mass balance
diagram (CO2 input → product → use → end-of-life) would clarify the assumptions. Without explicitly accounting for the
ultimate CO2 release, the current LCA may overstate the system’s net climate benefit. If the authors choose to conduct a
gate-to-grave analysis that includes CO2 credits, they should also consider N2O emissions. 
In summary, the authors could maintain the LCA as a gate-to-gate analysis if they remove the CO2 credit claim and clarify
the limitations of this approach. However, a gate-to-grave analysis will be closer to industry standards and require careful
consideration of the product use.

Response: We sincerely thank you for this important and insightful comment on our 
use of the term “click chemistry” to describe our catalyst design principle. We fully 
acknowledge that classical click chemistry, as defined in the context of molecular 
synthesis, emphasizes key characteristics such as modularity, orthogonality, high 
selectivity and yield under mild conditions, and minimal formation of byproducts.  
Inspired by the precise and efficient linkage of small molecular units enabled by click 
chemistry, we proposed a hierarchical click -site strategy for urea electrosynthesis, 
wherein two reactants, NO 3- and CO 2, undergo sequential cascade coupling. In our 
manuscript, the term “click-site” is not used to denote a specific classical click reaction, 
but rather to reflect the essential conceptual features of click chemistry —namely, 
modularity, orthogonality, high selectivity under mild conditions, and suppressed 
byproduct formation. Specifically, in our manuscript, Se-modulated In sites serve as the 
first click-site, enabling the selective adsorption of *NO3- while effectively suppressing 
the competitive adsorption of CO2, thereby facilitating its hydrogenation to *NO2. This 
step reflects the modular and selective nature of click reactions under electrochemical

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

3 
 
conditions. Subsequently, the In site, together with the anchored *NO2, acts as a second 
click site to guide targeted coupling with CO 2. This spatial and temporal control 
minimizes cross -adsorption, misaligned interactions, and undesired side reactions , 
again consistent with the design principles of classical click chemistry. Thus, inspired 
by the foundational features of click chemistry, we demonstrated how the Se –InOx 
catalyst enables two successive modular “click -like” steps: (i) preferential adsorption 
of NO3- and stabilization of *NO2 while excluding CO2, and (ii) site-specific coupling 
of CO2 with the surface-bound *NO2 intermediate. This forms the basis of our proposed 
click-site concept as applied to electrocatalysis. The sequential mechanism is supported 
by both in-situ characterizations and DFT calculations, which confirm that the catalyst 
surface first stabilizes *NO2 and repels CO2, followed by CO2 coupling to the anchored 
*NO2 species. Moreover, because this process involves two spatially and temporally 
ordered click-like events, we define it as a sequential-chain coupling at hierarchical 
click-sites. This concept echoes the modularity and efficiency of classical click 
chemistry, while adapting it to the solid-state catalytic interface. Importantly, the term 
“click” in this context is intended as an analogy, used to convey the orthogonal and 
modular nature of catalytic site interactions on the surface, rather than to suggest the 
involvement of a specific, well -defined click reaction.  In summary, the hierarchical 
click-site strategy enhances urea selectivity and suppresses competing pathways, 
reflecting the fundamental philosophy of click chemistry—namely, efficient, selective, 
and high -fidelity bond formation under mild and modular conditions. We thank the 
reviewer once again for the opportunity to clarify our terminology. We believe this 
revised e xplanation more accurately communicates the rationale behind our design 
strategy, while remaining respectful of the established definition of click chemistry.

Response: We sincerely thank you for your constructive and in -depth comment 
regarding the mechanism of the C –N coupling step.  We fully agree that C –N bond 
formation is the most kinetically challenging step in the reaction pathway.  To address 
your concerns, we performed comprehensive DFT calculations, including CI -NEB 
transition-state searches and COHP analyses, to clarify how *NO2 couples with CO2 on 
InOx, S–InOx and Se–InOx. Substitution of lattice oxygen with S or Se rendered CO 2 
adsorption thermodynamically unfavorable, with the adsorption energy shifting from –
0.26 eV (InOx) to +0.17 eV (S–InOx) and +0.27 eV (Se–InOx), respectively (Fig. 2f). 
Integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (ICOHP) analyses revealed decreased 
In–OCO2 bonding strength on Se –InOx surfaces, confirming the suppression of CO 2 
adsorption (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 5-7). In contrast, NO 3- adsorption 
progressively strengthened from InOx to S–InOx and Se–InOx, as indicated by increased 
covalency in In–ONO3- interactions observed via ICOHP analysis, projected density of 
states (PDOS), and differential charge density mapping  (Fig. 2h, 2i  and 
Supplementary Fig. 8-10), suggesting that *NO 2 remains anchored while CO 2 tends 
to diffuse. 
By comparing In –O bond ICOHP values, we found that CO 2 adsorption values of 
0.82, 0.60, and 0.51 for InOx, S–InOx and Se–InOx, respectively, all lower than the 
corresponding values for NO 2 adsorption (1.19, 1.29, and 1.31;  Supplementary Fig. 
73), suggesting that CO2 can migrate before reacting. CI-NEB calculations validate this 
hypothesis, revealing activation barriers for CO 2 diffusion and subsequent coupling 
with *NO 2 of 0.87 eV (InO x), 0.66 eV (S –InOx), and 0.48 eV (Se –InOx) 
(Supplementary Fig. 74). Notably, these values are significantly lower than those 
reported for many state -of-the-art multisite catalysts ( Table R1 ), highlighting the 
superior intrinsic C–N coupling kinetics enabled by our single-site In-based system. We 
further examined the bonding characterist ics of the *CO 2NO2 coupling intermediate. 
The C–N bond lengths decrease progressively from 1.81 Å (InO x) to 1.77 Å (S–InOx) 
and 1.61 Å (Se –InOx) ( Supplementary Fig. 75a), while the corresponding ICOHP

5. Supplementary Fig. 40 indicates that both Se–InOx and S–InOx catalysts exhibit the 
highest Faradaic efficiency for urea synthesis at –0.4 V vs. RHE. A more detailed 
discussion is required to explain why this specific potential is optimal and the 
underlying mechanism responsible for this observation. 
Response: We sincerely appreciate your insightful question. As is well established, the 
applied potential U sets the electrochemical potential of electrode and thereby tunes 
both the free energy and the kinetics of elementary (proton -)electron-transfer steps. 
Within the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) framework, the reaction free 
energy for an n -electron step follows ΔG(U)=ΔG(0) - neU; thus, increasing cathodic 
bias lowers the thermodynamic barrier of reduction steps. Kinetically, Butler –
Volmer/PCET formalisms indicate that overpotential accelerates charge transfer and 
shifts the steady -state coverages of adsorbed intermediates. However, driving the 
potential more negative also (i) promotes by-product pathways (e.g., HER and over -
hydrogenation along NO 3RR) and (ii) perturbs interfacial transport/speciation. 
Therefore, the activity and selectivity often exhibit a volcano -type dependence on 
potential: at low cathodic bias, the target pathway is “under -driven,” whereas at high 
bias, parasitic reactions and mass-transport limitations become dominant. 
In our system, urea forms via a sequential “click” pathway: NO 3- is first reduced at 
In sites to surface-bound *NO2 (first click), after which *NO 2 couples with interfacial 
CO2 to form the key *CO 2NO2 intermediate (second click) that subsequently 
hydrogenates to urea. The applied potential influences this process through two coupled 
effects: (i) Matching intermediate coverage with driving force.  As the potential is 
stepped from –0.2 V to –0.4 V (vs. RHE), the increased cathodic bias both elevates the 
steady-state coverage of *NO 2 generated from NO3- (first “click”) and provides 
sufficient driving force for the rate -determining coupling step (second “click”). 
Because CO2 adsorption on Se–InOx and S–InOx is intentionally weak by design, the 
coupling step depends on the capture of interfacial CO 2 by surface *NO 2. At an 
appropriate potential, the rates of *NO2 formation and consumption become balanced, 
enabling highly selective *CO2NO2 coupling while minimizing competing pathways.

95 
 
In-situ ATR -SEIRAS measurements support this mechanistic: on Se –InOx, the 
*CO2NO2 vibrational band intensifies markedly between –0.2 V and –0.4 V (vs. RHE), 
whereas *NH x signals remain negligible ( Fig. 5i ). This optimal match between 
intermediate coverage and driving force maximizes the urea turnover pathway at around 
–0.4 V (vs. RHE), resulting in peak urea FE, urea yield rate and Jurea (Supplementary 
Fig. 54). S–InOx follows the same mechanistic sequence, leading to an identical optimal 
operating potential. 
(ii) Suppressing by-reactions while maintaining CO 2 accessibility at moderate 
bias. At potentials less than –0.3 V  (vs. RHE), both *NO 2 formation and the C-N 
coupling are kinetically under-driven, thereby limiting the C–N coupling rate. At more 
negative potentials, over -hydrogenation in the NO 3RR pathway (*NO 2 → NH 3) 
becomes increasingly competitive. Although this transformation is thermodynamically 
unfavorable at open circuit (Fig. 5b), its kinetics accelerate as the chemical potential of 
*H rises with increasing cathodic bias. This balance yields a volcano -shaped 
dependence of urea FE, urea yield rate and Jurea on potential (Supplementary Fig. 54): 
the left -hand slope reflects kinetic limitations (insufficient driving force for RDS 
coupling and *NO2 accumulation), whereas the right-hand slope arises from selectivity 
and mass transport penalties (accelerated NO 3RR → NH3 and HER, along with 
decreased interfacial CO 2 availability). The peak at approximately –0.4 V (vs. RHE) 
corresponds to the point where kinetic driving force, intermediate coverage, and 
interfacial transport are optimally balanced. 
Taken together, these effects fully account for the observed volcano -type potential–
performance relationship, with –0.4 V (vs . RHE) emerging as the clear optimum for 
both Se–InOx and S–InOx catalysts.

Reviewer #4 (Remarks to the author): 
The manuscript has improved substantially. The TEA revisions are sound and the LCA 
is now generally consistent. However, one key issue remains regarding the fate of the 
CO2 used in the urea synthesis step. The LCA currently treats this CO 2 as if it is 
permanently sequestered, but that is only valid if the carbon remains stored over a long 
timeframe. In conventional fertilizer use, urea hydrolyzes in the field and the CO2 is re-
released to the atmosphere. Therefore, if the primary product is fertilizer urea, the CO2 
must be counted as an emission at end use. This point is critical not only for accurate 
LCA accounting, but also for any claims related to carbon crediting: carbon markets 
require demonstration that captured CO 2 is not subsequently re-emitted. If the CO 2 is 
later released, the system cannot be credited as a net carbon removal pathway, and the 
LCA should reflect this by including the CO2 release during the product use phase. 
If instead a portion of the urea carbon is embodied in non -fertilizer products (e.g., 
resins), the manuscript should report: (1) what fraction of production goes to such 
products, (2) the expected carbon storage lifetime, and (3) end-of-life fate. This would 
allow distinguishing between temporary storage and permanent sequestration. A 
simple carbon fate mass balance diagram (CO2 input → product → use → end-of-life) 
would clarify the assumptions. Without explicitly accounting for the ultimate CO 2 
release, the current LCA may overstate the system’s net climate benefit. If the authors 
choose to conduct a gate-to-grave analysis that includes CO2 credits, they should also 
consider N2O emissions. 
In summary, the authors could maintain the LCA  as a gate -to-gate analysis if they 
remove the CO 2 credit claim and clarify the limitations of this approach. However, a 
gate-to-grave analysis will be closer to industry standards and require careful 
consideration of the product use.

In line with your suggestion, we have deleted all statements in the LCA section and 
in the conclusions that explicitly referred to, or could be interpreted as implying, a CO2 
sequestration benefit or a carbon credit arising from the use of captured CO 2 for urea 
production. The revised text now clearly states that the captured CO2 is treated purely 
as a process input within a gate -to-gate framework, spanning from CO2 capture and 
NO3−/CO2 feed preparation up to urea production at the plant boundary. No negative 
emissions factor and no carbon credit are assigned to this CO2 stream in our LCA. 
(2) Clarification of system boundary and treatment of CO₂ fate 
We now explicitly define the LCA system boundary as gate -to-gate, excluding the 
product use phase and end-of-life. We also state that, under conventional fertilizer use, 
urea hydrolyses in the field and the CO2 associated with urea are ultimately re-released 
to the atmosphere. We clarify that this use -phase CO 2 release is not included in our 
gate-to-gate assessment and, consequently, we do not claim that the proposed process 
constitutes a net carbon removal pathway. The revised text emphasizes that our LCA 
results should be interpreted as comparative greenhouse gas intensities per kilogram of 
urea at the plant gate, rather than as evidence of permanent CO 2 sequestration or 
eligibility for carbon market credits. 
(3) Scope limitations regarding non-fertilizer products and N2O emissions 
We agree with you that a rigorous gate -to-grave LCA including CO 2 credits would

(4) Explicit statement of methodological limitations 
We now explicitly recognize several limitations of our LCA approach. First, our 
gate-to-gate boundary does not allow us to claim permanent CO2 sequestration, and the 
system should not be considered a net carbon removal pathway. Second, since a full 
gate-to-grave analysis has not been conducted, which would include N 2O emissions 
and CO 2 release during product use, the results cannot be used to support carbon 
crediting. Instead, the primary purpose of this study is to provide a consistent 
comparison between t he proposed electrochemical pathway and conventional urea 
synthesis, both evaluated under the same system boundary conditions.

“2. Life-cycle assessment 
The Life-cycle assessment (LCA ) was simulated using established process modeling 
tools (GPS-X and CapdetWorks). 10 The functional unit is 1 kg of 99 wt% urea. The 
system boundary is clearly defined as gate-to-gate, encompassing only the process from 
feedstock preparation to urea production, and explicitly excluding the product use and 
end-of-life stages. For the backgr ound inventory, the electricity generation mix and 
natural gas supply chain were identified as key contributors to environmental impacts. 
Emission factors associated with electricity production and natural gas combustion 
were derived from the Greenhouse ga ses, Regulated Emissions, and Energy use in 
Technologies (GREET) model. The method focuses on global warming potential (GWP) 
and fossil energy use. In accordance with the gate-to-gate boundary, no CO2 credit or 
negative emission is assigned to the CO 2 used as a feedstock for urea synthesis. 
Captured CO2 is treated solely as a process input and not as a permanently sequestered
