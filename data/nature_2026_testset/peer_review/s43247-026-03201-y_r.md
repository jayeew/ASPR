REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents a geodynamic treatment of the transition from oblique continental rifting to orthogonal segmented
spreading separated by transform faults. The oblique continental rift determines the initial geometry of the oblique spreading
center that nucleates following continental breakup. Plate divergence continues in the original direction and a series of
events follows. 
Detachment faults first form, presumably due to the slow to ultra-slow initial opening rates that limits
magmatism and favors tectonic extension. 
In the model, the detachment faults have alternating dip polarities along the strike
of the ridge so that they induce asymmetric spreading of the individual detachment segments leading to lateral separation of
the segments. 
The segment-wise asymmetric spreading causes it to become sinuous in geometry. As sinuosity and segment
offsets grow, transform faults eventually form to accommodate the offsets. A strain weakening process is then invoked to
stabilize the transform faults by making the strike-slip motion easier than divergence on the spreading ridges. 
The ridge
segments between these transforms then realign to an orthogonal configuration with respect to the transform faults. 
The
orthogonal ridge configuration minimizes segment lengths compared to the earlier inclined axes and thus also minimizes the
total energy needed for continued spreading. 
A stable and orthogonal ridge-transform system is established with symmetric
spreading. In this model the spacing and offsets of the transforms are controlled by the original breakup obliquity. 
Although this geodynamic model invokes a series of steps that progressively transform an oblique continuous divergent
boundary into a series of orthogonally spreading segments separated by transform faults, several of the processes described
do not appear to occur in nature and cast doubt on the plausibility of the mode. 
In addition, the model ignores other
significant mechanisms for the formation of an orthogonal ridge-transform system that have been proposed in the last few
years. 
A critical factor in the model is the development of alternating detachment fault systems soon after continental breakup.
These are needed in order to create a sinusoidal plate boundary from the original oblique but fairly straight system. 
In active
systems, detachment faults often form at the ends of already offset slow spreading ridges where they lead to core complex
formation. So, this mechanism does not apply here. In ultra-slow spreading settings “flip-flop” detachment faults may form
which reverse in direction episodically and do not lead to large scale offsets of the divergent boundary resulting in
approximately symmetric spreading (e.g., Reston, 2018, EPSL). 
The mechanism proposed by the authors should lead first to
the formation of straight magnetic lineations following continental breakup followed by increasingly sinuous magnetic
lineations and eventually truncated and segmented magnetic lineations when the offset ridge-transform system eventually
develops. 
I am not aware of such an evolution of magnetic lineations or of the divergent boundary itself in actual rift-to-
spreading systems. 
For example, in the Gulf of Aden case that the authors propose as an instance of their model, the

orthogonal ridge segments in the eastern part of the system develop abruptly, whereas the western divergent axis remains
straight and oblique. This lack of transforms in the western part is not a result of enhanced magmatism from the Afar hotspot,
as the rift becomes more tectonic to the west. 
Similarly, at the SWIR transforms developed abruptly west of the Melville FZ
whereas the eastern end lacks transforms and forms a broadly curving continuous oblique axis. In both cases, there is no
indication that the axis became sinuous before the offset orthogonal system became established. 
In fact, the juxtaposition of
these two contrasting styles of active extension indicates that the transition, if it occurs, is abrupt and without the sinuous
stage that the author’s model predicts and requires. 
Other examples of ultra-slow spreading systems, show that they typically
lack transform faults, such as the Mohns and Knipovich Ridges in the Arctic Ocean, which form continuous, broadly curving,
and obliquely spreading systems even after millions of years of spreading. In ultra-slow spreading systems, continuous
divergent axes appear to be in steady state despite significant obliquity and need not evolve into orthogonal and transform-
offset systems as described by the authors. In the Parece Vela basin, the development of transform faults followed an abrupt
change in spreading direction from a previous nearly linear spreading system without transform faults which was already
spreading essentially orthogonally. In the case of the Reykjanes Ridge, a linear axis was originally spreading orthogonally
and then abruptly became segmented but remained orthogonally spreading, following an abrupt change in opening
direction. 
It then progressively reassembled its original linear axis, and began spreading obliquely as it became linear again,
but the mechanism is entirely different from what is proposed by the authors (see Thorhallsson et al., 2024, JGR). 
In the case
of the equatorial Atlantic and the Australian-Antarctic ridge the authors do not document the development of a sinuous axis.
What is apparent in these cases is that an orthogonal and offset spreading system developed abruptly from earlier oblique
margins. 
Thus, the geodynamic model proposed by the authors is not supported by observations. 
Completely different explanations for the origin of transform faults have been proposed in recent years that the authors
ignore. Martinez and Hey (2022, EPSL) and Morishige (2024, G-Cubed) propose that the segmented nature of mantle
melting at mid-ocean ridges leads to a corresponding chemical and rheologic segmentation of the residual mantle. 
In
essence, magmatic ridge segments effectively deplete and dehydrate the mantle beneath segment interiors, but mantle
beneath segment ends remains poorly depleted and hydrous due to much lower vertical advection rates and inefficient melt
extraction there. 
Low degree hydrous melts are thus trapped and concentrated in the mantle beneath segment ends and
eventually solidify there, re-infusing olivine with hydrogen defects. 
Because of the strong effect of water on the viscosity of
olivine, segmented mantle melting leads to a corresponding segmented rheology of the residual mantle column beneath
spreading centers that extends onto the ridge flanks. Therefore, segmented mantle melting leads to the formation of
systematic bands of strong and weak residual mantle extending outward from the segment interiors and segment ends,
respectively. 
Martinez and Hey (2022, EPSL) use this basic principle to explain the evolution of transform faults across the
full range of spreading rates. 
Ultra-slow spreading centers do not exhibit steady-state segmented mantle melting, but rather
have irregular melting patterns and therefore do not develop a systematic rheological banding. 
Therefore, ultra-slow ridges
tend not to form offset segments and transform faults; they instead characteristically form continuous curvilinear divergent
boundaries. As spreading rates increase to slow, spaced buoyant upwelling cells develop and form a magmatically
segmented spreading axis, regardless of whether the axis is offset or linear. Thus, a segmented mantle rheology develops
with a spacing depending on the upwelling instability spacing even if the axis is long, linear, and continuous. 
If the axis
happens to be oblique when the upwelling instabilities form, it naturally breaks up into transform-offset segments with a
spacing determined by the instability spacing. 
As spreading rates increase to fast, the pattern of mantle upwelling also
changes from discrete three-dimensional cells of buoyant upwelling to a two-dimensional plate-driven pattern. 
The mantle
rheological pattern correspondingly changes and transform faults form at the wider spacing of the magmatic segments.
Finally, at ultra-high spreading rates, melting and melt extraction become more uniform and efficient across the entire
segment, even at segment ends, and transform faults again disappear as the mantle rheological pattern becomes
correspondingly uniform. 
This simple model incorporates the well-established nature of segmented mantle melting at mid-
ocean ridges and its variation with spreading rate to posit a corresponding segmented mantle rheology which explains the
origin of transform faults and the tectonically segmented pattern of oceanic plate tectonics. The authors ignore in their
manuscript these important effects of mantle melting on rheology and tectonics. 
In summary, the authors present a geodynamic model for the evolution of orthogonally spreading oceanic ridge segments
offset by transform faults from an oblique continental rift which appears to fit the end-member stages. However, important
processes such as the formation soon after breakup of the increasingly sinuously curved ridge, are unsupported by
observations. Yet this stage is essential as a continuing linear ridge will not create offsets that, in this model, evolve into
transform faults. The supposed examples of their model also do not demonstrate this evolution, but only fit beginning and
end stages. Further, the authors do not comment on more recent models that explain the origin of transform faults more
naturally as a result of the segmented nature of mantle melting which results in a segmented rheological structure in the
residual mantle. A segmented residual mantle rheology will naturally develop into an orthogonal ridge-transform system if
the larger scale plate boundary is oblique. Because of these important deficiencies I cannot recommend publications.

I would like to preface this review by noting that I was not one of the original reviewers of this manuscript. However, I have
carefully read the authors’ responses to the previous round of review and will therefore comment on both the current version
of the manuscript and the revisions that were made following the initial submission. 
The authors employ 3D thermomechanical modeling to investigate the consequences of oblique continental breakup and
subsequent oceanic spreading, with a particular focus on the development of ridge-transform systems. These numerical
experiments provide new insights into the evolution of ridge-transform geometries and the links between continental rifting

and ridge segmentation. The topic is of clear relevance to the broader geoscientific community. Overall, I find that the

Comment 1. This manuscript reminds me a previous work done by Ammann et al. (2018), which involved some of the same
co-authors and used a similar numerical approach to investigate the impact of oblique continental rifting on the formation of
long transform faults. While the current study advances this line of inquiry by modeling the transition from a single long
oblique rift to multiple offset ridge segments, this progression is not clearly contextualized within the manuscript. A more
explicit comparison with the Ammann et al. (2018) study would help situate the current work and clarify the novel
contributions being made. I recommend that the authors briefly summarize the key findings of the earlier work and highlight
the specific advances and new insights provided by the present study. 
Comment 2. I find the discussion on the controlling factor of the transform fault geometry is not quite easy to follow and
statistics could be better presented. For example, lines 192-197 tried to indicate that ridge offsets decrease with decreasing
obliquities by comparing ridge offsets in the SE Indian Ocean, Equatorial Atlantic Ocean, and the Gulf of Aden. It was
suggested that average offsets in the Equatorial Atlantic (82 km) is smaller than in the SE Indian Ocean (157 km). However,

offsets. Although it is the case for the Equatorial Atlantic and the Gulf of Aden - this appears inconsistent with global statistics
(e.g., Ren et al., 2022), which show that long transform faults are more commonly observed at slower spreading ridges. I
would appreciate a more detailed explanation of how the authors reconcile these apparently conflicting observations. As it
stands, the natural evidence presented does not seem robust enough to unequivocally support the modeling results, and a
more thorough discussion of these discrepancies would strengthen the manuscript. 
I have no additional minor comments for the manuscript.

REVIEWERS' COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The revised manuscript has additional explanation and discussion of various issues, but the main results remain
unchanged. A critical element in the geodynamic model is the role of detachment faults in the development of ridge
sinuosity. In the model, detachment faults form spontaneously within the linear but obliquely spreading divergent boundary
and are necessary to begin to offset the linear axis into a sinuous shape that eventually forms a transform fault when the
sinuosity reaches a certain point. 
The authors justify this by referring to other geodynamic results (Gerya, 2010) to support
their contention that this stage exists but describe no convincing examples from nature. It should be noted that in the
geodynamical results figure shown in the rebuttal (Gerya, 2010), a rather unrealistic development occurs wherein the
spreading centers develop extreme asymmetric spreading so that one set remains near the eastern continental margin
whereas the other set is near the western continental margin. This geometry is not formed in nature. Furthermore, what
causes a transition to symmetric spreading is also not clear. In ultra-slow spreading systems, the plate boundary can and
does continue to spread obliquely in a steady-state manner without forming transform faults. 
There are no distinct short-
wavelength magmatic and amagmatic segments as the authors contend. 
For example, in the case of the Mohns and
Knipovich Ridges, the magmatic segments indicated in the rebuttal figure are not actual ridge segments but rather are axial
volcanic ridges (AVRs) that commonly develop within the plate boundary zones of slow-spreading ridges (where active
tectonic deformation and volcanic emplacements occur). AVRs form along fissure and fault systems within the plate
boundary zone and are always nearly perpendicular to the opening direction even when the overall plate boundary zone is
oblique. 
The Reykjanes Ridge axis, for example is also dominated by overlapping AVRs oriented perpendicular to the
opening direction and virtually no tectonic segments despite the 30° obliquity of the overall plate boundary (Searle et al.,
1998, EPSL). However, individual AVRs do not represent distinct spreading segments as proposed by the authors. Thus,
there is a distinct contrast in the tectonic accommodation of stresses within the plate boundary zone (forming fissures, faults
and AVRs almost always perpendicular to the opening stresses) whereas the plate boundary zone itself (where the
lithosphere flanks thicken and become essentially rigid) may be quite oblique to the opening direction. The edges of the
plate boundary zone may thus impart a new set of faults parallel to the plate boundary zone and oblique to the opening
direction. There is also a misconception about what is happening in the Parece Vela Basin (PVB). 
It is true that this is a
good example of fracture zone formation because, unlike many other examples, both basement tectonic fabric and magnetic
lineations are observed. At about anomaly 6A a change in opening direction occurred and transform faults abruptly formed
as shown by the bathymetric fabric. 
However, the change in opening direction gradually continued for some time causing a
fanning or reorientation of subsequent abyssal hill fabric. 
Thus, this is not an example of sinuosity as in the author’s model
where a constant spreading direction is maintained. 
The fact that the fracture zones themselves gently curve indicates the
gradual continuing change in opening direction after the initial abrupt change that nucleated the transform faults. With regard
to the effects of mantle melting and magmatic segmentation, I continue to think that this is the major cause for the formation
of transform faults. 
The author’s model focuses on effects within the thin near-axis thermal lithosphere, which although may
be important, are a small fraction of the thickness of the residual lithosphere following melting. 
This residual lithosphere is
over 60 km thick (from the dry solidus depth to the base of the crust) and forms abruptly beneath the ridge axis. 
The
extraction of water from this entire thickness of lithosphere increases its viscosity by over two orders of magnitude (Hirth and
Kohlstedt, 1996, EPSL). 
Furthermore, near segment ends, inefficient melt extraction and infusion of low degree melts from
the damp mantle melting interval maintain, if not increase, the mantle water content in these areas. Thus, a profound
rheological segmentation is formed as soon as segmented mantle melting develops, which seems a more direct cause of
tectonic segmentation. 
The geodynamic processes that the authors describe may then be superimposed on the rheological
segmentation produced by mantle melting adding to tectonic segmentation but are not its proximal cause. 
In summary, the authors have provided additional detail on methods and case examples. 
However, direct clear observations
of the critical transitional stages between oblique linear spreading and orthogonal segmented spreading separated by
transform faults are still lacking. This provides opportunities for additional observational studies of these stages, particularly
where both magnetic isochrons and basement fabric can be observed. Although I continue to think that predominant
rheologic effects of mantle melting are not fully appreciated in this study, they are at least now mentioned. I therefore think
that the study can be published as one step in the continuing better understanding of the formation of transform faults and
evolution of oblique spreading systems.

This manuscript presents a geodynamic treatment of the transition from oblique 
continental rifting to orthogonal segmented spreading separated by transform faults. 
The oblique continental rift determines the initial geometry of the oblique spreading

Comment: Completely different explanations for the origin of transform faults have 
been proposed in recent years that the authors ignore. Martinez and Hey (2022, EPSL) 
and Morishige (2024, G-Cubed) propose that the segmented nature of mantle melting 
at mid-ocean ridges leads to a corresponding chemical and rheologic segmentation of 
the residual mantle. In essence, magmatic ridge segments effectively deplete and 
dehydrate the mantle beneath segment interiors, but mantle beneath segment ends 
remains poorly depleted an d hydrous due to much lower vertical advection rates and 
inefficient melt extraction there. Low degree hydrous melts are thus trapped and 
concentrated in the mantle beneath segment ends and eventually solidify there, re -
infusing olivine with hydrogen defec ts. Because of the strong effect of water on the 
viscosity of olivine, segmented mantle melting leads to a corresponding segmented 
rheology of the residual mantle column beneath spreading centers that extends onto the 
ridge flanks. Therefore, segmented mantle melting leads to the formation of systematic 
bands of strong and weak residual mantle extending outward from the segment interiors 
and segment ends, respectively. Martinez and Hey (2022, EPSL) use this basic principle 
to explain the evolution of transf orm faults across the full range of spreading rates. 
Ultra-slow spreading centers do not exhibit steady-state segmented mantle melting, but 
rather have irregular melting patterns and therefore do not develop a systematic 
rheological banding. Therefore, ultra-slow ridges tend not to form offset segments and 
transform faults; they instead characteristically form continuous curvilinear divergent 
boundaries. As spreading rates increase to slow, spaced buoyant upwelling cells

axis is offset or linear. Thus, a segmented mantle rheology develops with a spacing 
depending on the upwelling instability spacing even if the axis is long, linear, and 
continuous. If the axis happens to be o blique when the upwelling instabilities form, it 
naturally breaks up into transform -offset segments with a spacing determined by the 
instability spacing. As spreading rates increase to fast, the pattern of mantle upwelling 
also changes from discrete three -dimensional cells of buoyant upwelling to a two -
dimensional plate -driven pattern. The mantle rheological pattern correspondingly 
changes and transform faults form at the wider spacing of the magmatic segments.

and efficient across the entire segment, even at segment ends, and transform faults again
