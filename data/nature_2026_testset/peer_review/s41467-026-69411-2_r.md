Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Li, et al. report the characterization of SMT-738, an inhibitor of LolCDE with activity against carbapenem-resistant
Enterobacteriaceae. Using cryo-EM, the authors characterize the interaction of SMT-738 with LolCDE, which suggests two
main mechanisms through which SMT-738 may inhibit its target: 1) by sterically occluding the binding site for nascent
lipoproteins, and 2) through a proposed conformational change leading to dissociation of one the of ATPase subunits (LolD).
The discovery and characterization of new inhibitors of outer membrane biogenesis is an area of considerable interest in the
field, including recent work on molecules target the LPS export system and BAM. Inhibits for Lol are an exciting new
direction worthy of exploration. So, overall, I think this was an interesting manuscript with a lot of potential, but I don't think all
of the key findings are well supported by the data. In particular, the evidence that SMT-738 binding induces LolD
dissociation is relatively weak. There are also some aspects of the structure that suggest that more work is needed to
complete the refinement of the model in a rigorous way, including an error in the modeling of the key ligand, SMT-738. Some
more minor issues should be addressed to increase clarity and rigor. 
MAJOR COMMENTS: 
The model has an unusually low number of residues in the preferred regions of the Ramachandran plot (only 89% overall,
while >95% preferred is more typical of a high quality model. Does the model need more work? What is going on here? 
The ligand appears to have an incorrect chiral center at carbon "CAI". This is a little concerning, because a major point of
this manuscript is the structure of the ligand bound to the complex. The density for the ligand is especially good and the map
clearly indicates that the modeled configuration is incorrect (in addition to being chemically implausible). 
Proposed conformational changes in LolCDE upon SMT-738 binding and dissociation of one LolD subunit: Strictly
speaking, it is difficult to determine from the presented data if SMT-738 binding cause the observed change in the LolCDE
structure, or alternatively, if the absence of a LolD subunit causes changes in LolCE unrelated to drug binding. For example,
if the complex were falling apart during purification, loss of LolD might be incidental. It is also possible that the particles
lacking LolD are less heterogeneous that the complete complex, and therefore the highest resolution class arises from the
subset of particles lacking one LolD subunit. To support these sorts of claims, one would need to show that drug binding
causes dissociate of LolD relative to the absence of drug. I don't see that sort of data presented here. The closest I see is in
Fig 4e, but ATPase activity could be changed through loss of LolD or through other mechanisms such as drug simply
blocking conformational changes in the transporter. 
Is the timescale of the MD simulations sufficient to analyze conformational changes and subunit dissociation? I usually think
of these kind of motions as being on the millisecond timescale, not nanosecond. It would be good if one of the reviewers of
this manuscript has expertise in this area to evaluate the conclusions related to MD and protein dynamics.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Li and co-workers expands the characterization of SMT-738, a small molecule inhibitor of the Lol system.
The authors build on previous data published by another group (Breidenstein et al., 2024) showing that SMT-738 kills MDR
E. coli and K. pneumoniae strains without affecting species outside the Enterobacterales order thus sparing bacterial
species commonly found in the gut microbiome. Interestingly, this previous work identified the Lol system as the possible
target of SMT-738 as all selected mutants resistant to the compound carried mutations in lolC or lolE genes. In the present
work the authors report the cryo-EM structure of the LolCDE-SMT-738 complex and, by using a combination of mutagenesis,
biochemical assays and molecular dynamics simulations, dissect the mechanism of action of the inhibitor showing that it
blocks the LolCDE complex in a dead-lock non-functional conformation which lacks a LolD subunit. Finally, based on
sequence analyses of resistant and sensitive strains, mutagenesis and MD simulations the authors offer insights into the
mechanism of SMT-738 selectivity. 
The work is well organized and well written. The structural data are solid and support the conclusions. 
Few specific comments on the manuscript are reported below. 
1.Release of lipoproteins from spheroplasts 
The authors assess BamE lipoprotein release in the absence of LolA. However, in the absence of LolA, release of
lipoproteins from spheroplasts is very inefficient as shown by the paper by Yokota et al., (mentioned by the authors). Since
this assay is crucial to show the mode of action of the SMT-738 inhibitor in cells expressing wild type and mutant Lol
complexes I suggest that the authors perform the assay in the presence of LolA (to increase the efficiency). 
Moreover, the
efficiency of BamE release should be measured by assessing the level of BamE both in the spheroplasts and in the
supernatant in all conditions tested. 
2.The loss of one LolD subunit from the LolCDE complex following binding to SMT-738 inhibitor is surprising. Since loss of
LolD seems the results of conformational changes occurring at the LolC coupling helix, does LolD subunit loss require ATP
hydrolysis? 
3.In the model presented in Extended Fig. 8 it seems that SMT-738 binds LolCDE complex after ATP binding. Does SMT-
738 compete with lipoprotein binding in the central cavity, or does it bind after the substrate has been released to LolA
blocking the LolCDE complex in the dead-lock conformation? Please comment. 
4.The authors identified LolE residue D264 as key for STM-738 selectivity: can changes at this single residue explain lack of
activity against many members of intestinal microbiota?

(Remarks to the Author) 
This study reports a cryo-EM structure of LolCDE bound to SMT-738 and identifies a binding pocket at the periplasmic ends
of LolC/E TM1–TM2. The authors present resistance-conferring substitutions in LolC (E255R, L256R) and LolE (I59D,
M261R, D264A) that line the pocket and elevate MIC values. They further show that SMT-738 inhibits BamE release in a
spheroplast assay and reduces LolCDE ATPase activity, proposing an allosteric mechanism in which SMT-738 blocks
lipoprotein engagement and destabilises one NBD (LolD) from LolC, thereby generating a catalytically inactive complex.
Finally, they suggest that residue LolE D264 underlies the observed taxonomic selectivity and microbiome-sparing effect. 
The structural analysis is rigorous, and the data are of high technical quality. However, several mechanistic conclusions
would benefit from stronger experimental support, and a few points (outlined below) should be addressed before the work
can be considered for publication. 
Major points 
1. The map/model shows one LolD subunit, with loss of density for the other. Alignments indicate large shifts in the LolC
coupling helix that could clash with a second LolD, but cryo-EM classification artefacts or flexibility may also account for this.
Please provide orthogonal stoichiometry evidence with and without SMT-738. For example, SEC-MALS or mass photometry
on purified complexes, quantitative native PAGE or blue-native gels using LolD-specific antibodies, or native mass
spectrometry to confirm physical loss rather than classification bias. 
Structural comparisons indicate that SMT-738 might physically block the triacylated N-terminus of a connected lipoprotein.
However, the functional assay currently monitors BamE release from spheroplasts without added LolA, a system that can
exhibit spontaneous release of several lipoproteins. To confirm that SMT-738 blocks transfer, please include a reconstituted
LolCDE→LolA transfer assay (in proteoliposomes) ± SMT-738, or at minimum a competition experiment quantifying binding
or transfer of a labelled lipoprotein to LolA in the presence of the compound. 
3. The proposed link between LolE D264 and microbiome sparing is intriguing but remains correlative. Please consider: (i)
swapping LolE between a susceptible Enterobacteriaceae strain and a resistant commensal (or reconstructing the
commensal-type residue at position 264 in E. coli) and testing MIC; (ii) measuring direct binding by SPR, biolayer
interferometry, or ITC for wild-type versus D264 variants to determine Kd. These experiments would convert a sequence
correlation into a causal selectivity determinant. 
4. Given the clinical interest in SMT-738, on-target validation would substantially strengthen the paper. Demonstrate direct
binding to purified LolCDE using SPR, ITC, or thermal-shift assays. 
5. Because lolamicin is also described as microbiome-sparing, it would be valuable to test cross-resistance between SMT-
738 and lolamicin (and vice versa) in the constructed mutants, particularly those involving LolE D264, to assess whether the
two molecules share an overlapping site or distinct allosteric mechanisms. 
Minor points 
In the figures, label key side chains and interactions (hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, hydrophobic contacts) and include a 2D
ligand–protein interaction diagram for SMT-738. 
In the abstract, replace “totally novel mechanism” with “novel mechanism” and specify the functional consequence of losing
one LolD (e.g., “rendering the complex catalytically incompetent”). 
In the results, add a zoomed-in density panel (map at two contour levels) for SMT-738 and surrounding residues
(E195/E255/L256/A259; I59/M261/D264/F367). 
In the results, rephrase “total novel action mechanism” and avoid inferring microbiome sparing solely from sequence
analysis, present this as a testable hypothesis pending cross-species validation.
