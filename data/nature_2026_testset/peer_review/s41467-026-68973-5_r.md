Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study provides robust evidence, through a comprehensive research design encompassing randomized controlled trial
(RCT) data analysis, animal experiments, and transcriptome profiling, that urinary Clusterin may function as a
pharmacodynamic response biomarker for the endothelin-1 receptor antagonist atrasentan. The authors have conducted
thorough and methodical investigations to substantiate their findings. While the work is compelling, several suggestions are
offered for the authors’ consideration to further strengthen and refine this research. 
1.While the authors have conducted extensive research, the complexity of the content may hinder reader comprehension.
The flowchart (Figure 1) in its current form is overly simplified and could be enhanced by incorporating additional details,
such as: 
Clear definitions of study populations, subgroups, and animal groupings 
Study design schematics 
Key analytical methods and their objectives 
Logical connections between different phases of the study 
2.The authors assert that uACR is unsuitable as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for individual therapeutic response to
atrasentan. However, the manuscript does not provide direct comparative evidence demonstrating that CLU is more rapid
and accurate than uACR. Clarification on this point would strengthen the argument. 
3.The rationale for selecting P < 0.01 (urinary proteome screening) and q < 0.1 (animal studies) as significance thresholds—
rather than P < 0.05 or a multiple-testing-corrected threshold (e.g., FDR)—requires further explanation. Were these choices
based on prior literature or statistical power considerations? 
4.The methodology mentions using standardized differences to assess group matching, but these results are not presented.
Including these data would strengthen the validity of the propensity score matching and enhance transparency. 
5.A mediation analysis (e.g., atrasentan → CLU → renal endpoints/uACR) could provide stronger evidence for CLU’s role
as a drug-response biomarker and its potential mechanistic link to improved outcomes. 
6.If CLU is proposed as a predictive biomarker, its utility in forecasting therapeutic efficacy should be explored. Where data
permit, predictive modeling (e.g., machine learning or regression-based approaches) could assess CLU’s importance in
efficacy prediction. 
7.To further validate CLU’s role, causal inference methods (e.g., Mendelian randomization, co-localization analysis) could
help establish whether CLU has a causal effect on drug response or renal function, beyond mere association. 
8.Is there significant heterogeneity in CLU levels within responder or non-responder subgroups? 
9.The described study appears to align more closely with a nested case-control design rather than a nested cohort study
(Page 5, Line 123). Please clarify or correct this terminology. 
10.Several figures lack sufficient resolution or clarity. Enhancing graphical quality (e.g., higher resolution, improved labeling,
consistent formatting) would aid interpretation.

For animal studies we addressed multiple testing using false discovery rate (FDR) using p.adjust in 
R, with the “BH” (Benjamini -Hochberg) method at 10% of the set of significant result . We have now 
clarified the use of this method inthe Methods section (page 28).  
 
For the urinary proteome exploratory study, w hile we agree with the reviewer on using FDR 
adjustments for controlling for multiple testing, due to the well-known challenge of limited sample size 
of the discovery group and the large number of proteins measured
6, we opted to identify candidate 
genes based on nominal p-value thresholds, with the intention of maximizing sensitivity. To increase 
stringency with this approach we used p <0.01 rather than conventional p <0.05 to cut down the false 
positive hits prior to the subsequent multiple validation steps.  Candidate genes passing the nominal 
p value were then subsequently subjected to both qualitative (literature and pathway-based) and

5. A mediation analysis (e.g., atrasentan → CLU → renal endpoints/uACR) could provide stronger 
evidence for CLUs role as a drug-response biomarker and its potential mechanistic link to improved 
outcomes. 
We thank the reviewer for the thoughtful suggestion. A mediation analysis is specifically used to 
determine whether a particular variable (in this case, CLU) lies on the causal pathway between an 
intervention (atrasentan) and an outcome (composite endpoint). A mediator, by definition, is a factor 
that not only changes in response to the drug but also directly accounts for some or all of the drug’s 
effect on the clinical outcome. Demonstrating true mediation, especially in human studies, requires 
robust data to establish temporal and causal relationships among the drug, mediator, and outcome. 
 
It is important to note the distinction between a mediator and a response biomarker. While a mediator 
lies on the causal pathway and mechanistically links the treatment to the outcome, a response 
biomarker indicates that the drug is engaging its target or pathway, and correlates with clinical 
outcomes, without necessarily being involved in the causal mechanism. Only a subset of 
pharmacodynamic response biomarkers have been shown to mediate clinical benefit; more

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 13 -->

commonly, changes in response biomarkers reflect target engagement or concurrent biological 
changes rather than direct mediation of clinical effects. 
 
In our study, the primary aim of the human data presented was to evaluate the association between 
uCLU and kidney outcomes, thereby supporting the use of uCLU as a non- invasive biomarker that 
reflects kidney disease progression, association with Endothelian 1 pathway activation and response 
to atrasentan. We have also now added supplementary Figure S5 that shows this relationship holds 
in glomerular diseases based on the NEPTUNE study data. These findings support the use of uCLU 
as a non-invasive biomarker with a rationale for   monitor ing uCLU levels to assess  individualized 
response to endothelin receptor antagonists. As highlighted in our response to reviewer 1’s first 
comment, we currently lack  the necessary datato establish a direct causal link between CLU and 
ET1/ERA signaling and outcomes in humans.  Thus, we do not claim a direct causal (mediation) 
relationship between CLU and the observed renal benefits of atrasentan. Rather, we present evidence 
that uCLU is associated with kidney outcomes and responds to treatment, supporting its utility as a 
pharmacodynamic biomarker and providing a rationale for monitoring uCLU in future studies and 
clinical practice. This study sets the stage for these investigations.
