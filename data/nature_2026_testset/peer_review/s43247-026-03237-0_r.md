Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
In the manuscript by Zhou et al., the authors detail a study where four tree species, two ECM-associated and two AM-
associated, were selected to study the effects of soil microplastics on root traits, root chemistry, and soil chemistry. I found
this study interesting and certainly timely given the current concerns over microplastics in the environment. I think the root
bag method was an appropriate way to answer the questions posed by this study and I found the results interesting overall.
However, I do have a concern about the lack of information on free-living saprotrophic fungi in the soil that needs to be
addressed prior to publication. I also feel that this study could be strengthened by the inclusion of mycorrhizal colonization

data. 
Major comments: 
1. Given that this study used fresh forest soil to fill the root bags, both mycorrhizal fungi and free-living saprotrophic fungi
would be present in the rhizosphere. Throughout the manuscript, the authors refer to the ability of ECM fungi to mobilize
nutrients from organic matter (lines 78-80, for example). While true and certainly their ability to excrete extracellular enzymes
is a difference between ECM and AM fungi, there are also free-living saprotrophic fungi present in rhizosphere soil that
decompose organic matter. Thus, I suggest that conclusions like those on lines 200-214 and 263-267 should be rephrased
to include the saprotrophic activities and hyphal production of free-living decomposers. The way it is written now, it seems as
though the ECM fungi are the main decomposers and I would be cautious of this given that whole forest soils were used in
the root bags. There are studies that have shown that ECM fungi can accelerate or decelerate overall decomposition in soils,
including decomposition by free-living fungi (see Choreno-Parr and Treseder 2024; 
https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.19748 and
Mayer et al. 2023; doi: 10.1111/nph.18930). I further suggest incorporating these studies into the discussion. I also wonder
what ECM symbiotic nitrification is (line 201). Unless I am mistaken, fungi do not perform nitrification; nitrifying bacteria do.
Please clarify. 
2. Throughout the manuscript, there is a lot of emphasis on the ability of ECM fungi to mobilize organic nutrients. They also
are plant symbionts. I found myself confused by statements like those on lines 267-271. Why would trees allocate more
carbon to their ECM symbionts because of their decomposition ability? I would think that the trees would allocate carbon to
increase mycorrhizal colonization under stress independent of the ability of the fungi to decompose organic matter. As
symbionts, they can aide the plant in nutrient acquisition independently of decomposition. 
Similarly, in the introduction on
lines 78-82, there is an emphasis on the mobilization activities of ECM fungi with no mention to their symbiosis with plants.
Because they are plant mutualists, they could overcome nutrient limitations not only because they can mobilize organic
nutrients, but also because they are associated with the plant roots and transfer N and P to the plant in exchange for carbon.
I suggest adding this information to these sections. 
3. Was mycorrhizal colonization measured on any roots of this study? I think it could strengthen the study if this were done.
Particularly the conclusions like those in lines 267-274 and 283-292 regarding differential carbon allocation to symbionts
between ECM and AM trees are difficult to make when colonization was not measured. If there are colonization data, please
provide them. 
Minor comments: 
Line 71 – I suggest softening this statement. Is it that AM fungi cannot reduce microplastic absorption by plants or just that
this has not be documented as of yet. 
Line 96 – I think the authors mean two mycorrhizal association types here, not tree species, as four species were included in
the study. 
Line 132 – This wording is confusing to me, particularly the use of the word “determinant.” Could this be rephrased to
indicate that these soil factors influenced or affected root traits? 
Figure 6 – What do the two shapes represent in panel a? Please list in a legend. In panel b, the PCA suggests a tree
species affect perhaps for the root traits. It seems like the four groups that separate in ordination space could be the four
different tree species. Is this the case? If so, I suggest adding some results and discussion to this. I would expect root traits
would vary between tree species and there should be at least some discussion of this. 
Lines 14-195 – I would be cautious about referring to nutrient availability, as the data here cannot support conclusions about
how ECM fungi change nutrient availability. I think the authors may mean that soils of ECM trees had higher amounts of
available N and lower amounts of available P with microplastic addition. 
Line 201 – This is mentioned above, but I want to point out again that I am not aware of any evidence that ECM fungi can
perform nitrification. If this is the case, please provide a reference. 
Line 241 – I suggest rephrasing this a bit. There were some differences in how microplastics affected root nutrient content
between ECM and AM trees, so it is not correct to say this was consistent. 
Line 360 – What were the sizes of the trees? Were they all mature adults? The diameter at breast height or equivalent for the
focal trees would be good to have here. 
Line 367 – How many locations in the forest were used to collect soil? Please indicate. 
Line 374 – Please indicate approximately how many weeks the root bags were in the field. 
Line 390 – How was the volume of soil calculated for the TD measurement? With soil cores, the volume can be calculated
based on the core. Since these were bags, please indicate how soil volume was calculated. 
Lines 394 and 395 – Please clarify what unit root length is. Earlier in the manuscript, it is listed that the WinRhizo proveds
average root length. 
Lines 413 and 424 – Please indicate whether these measurements were in rhizosphere soil or bulk soil.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This study investigates how the addition of polystyrene microplastics alters rhizosphere soil properties and fine-root traits in
ectomycorrhizal (ECM) and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) tree species in a mixed temperate forest. The authors report
mycorrhizal-specific differences in nutrient availability (N and P), fine root morphological traits, and root stoichiometry,
underlining distinct adaptation strategies. These findings are contextualized within broader concerns about microplastic
pollution in terrestrial ecosystems. 
The claims made in this study are novel and will be of broad interest to researchers studying plant–soil interactions, soil
pollution, and forest ecology. The exploration of mycorrhizal-specific responses to microplastic contamination is especially
innovative and fills a notable gap in current microplastic impact studies, which often ignore plant symbiotic strategies. 
Strengths: 
The experimental design used root bags in a mixed forest and comparing co-occurring ECM and AM species, which adds
ecological realism while maintaining controlled sampling. The authors employ a multi-dimensional approach, integrating
root morphology, stoichiometry, and soil nutrient measurements. The use of multivariate statistics (RDA, PCA) and trait-
environment relationships improves interpretability of complex responses. 
Limitations and concerns: 
1. The use of 1% w/w polystyrene microplastic is relatively high and does not represent ambient levels in temperate forests
(typically in the ng-mg/kg range). While high concentrations are useful for mechanistic insight, the rationale for choosing this
dose should be clearly stated. Without such justification, conclusions about long-term ecosystem impacts and species
dynamics risk being overstated. 
2. While the AM vs. ECM comparison is central to the paper, it is unclear whether the observed responses are species-
specific or truly strategy-specific. The inclusion of only two species per mycorrhizal type may confound the interpretation.
Ideally, inclusion of additional species or statistical modelling that partitions variance due to species vs. mycorrhizal type
(e.g., mixed-effects models) would help disentangle these effects. 
3. Manuscript would benefit from quantitative assessments of mycorrhizal colonization (e.g., microscopy, staining, or
molecular markers) to confirm the functional status of the symbioses under microplastic exposure. 
4. The divergent responses of rhizosphere N and P are intriguing, but the underlying mechanisms remain speculative. Were
enzyme activities, microbial biomass, or P fractions (labile vs. occluded) measured? Including such measurements would
significantly strengthen the causal framework linking microplastic presence to soil nutrient dynamics. 
5. The study mentions microbial processes (e.g., nitrification, mineralization) but does not directly analyse microbial
communities. Including microbial profiling would help clarify how microplastics influence soil microbial dynamics and
nutrient cycling. 
6. The study does not measure the extent to which microplastics accumulate on or within roots. Quantifying this
accumulation could provide insights into the physical interactions between microplastics and root systems. 
7. Though the study focuses on soil and root traits, it does not assess how these changes affect overall tree growth, health,
or productivity. Including such metrics would strengthen the ecological relevance of the findings. 
8. Statistical analyses: The use of two-way ANOVA, PCA, Mantel tests, and RDA is appropriate for exploring treatment
effects and trait-environment relationships. However, Mantel tests do not establish causality or mediation. Given the claim
that root trait shifts were mediated by specific soil factors, the authors should consider conducting structural equation
modelling (SEM) or variation partitioning to support this assertion statistically. 
9. The methods are generally well described. However, for full reproducibility, consider providing raw or processed data and
code for statistical analyses in a public repository.

REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
I appreciate the authors' attention to my comments. They have made changes to their manuscript based on my comments
and almost all are reflected in the revised manuscript. I do want to point out that the line numbers that the authors referred to
in the rebuttal did not match up with the revised manuscript, which made it a little challenging to conduct this review. This
happens with track changes documents where the line numbers can change. Thus, if there are changes to the manuscript
that I missed, it may be due to this discrepancy. 
I have two comments based on the authors' rebuttal: 
1. The authors state in their rebuttal: "In the revised manuscript, we have revised the part according to your suggestions:
trees would allocate carbon to increase mycorrhizal colonization under stress independent of the ability of the fungi to
decompose organic matter. Please see lines 338-345." I do not see this statement in the revised manuscript. 
2. The authors added to the Introduction "ECM fungi, as obligate mutualists of plants, establish extensive hyphal networks
that closely associate with plant roots, enabling the direct transfer of mobilized nitrogen and phosphorus to their host plants.
In exchange, the host plants supply photosynthetically derived carbon to support the growth and metabolic requirements of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

the fungi." This is incorrect - ECM fungi can be free-living as well as symbiotic. AM funi are obligate mutualists. Th rest of
what is stated here about nutrient and carbon transfer is true of both ECM and AM fungi and, as such, should be moved
earlier in this paragraph when mycorrhizal fungi are introduced. 
I also have a few comments on the revised manuscript: 
In the Abstract, the authors write "soil rhizosphere," but I would change this to just "rhizosphere." 
In the Introduction, the following sentences I think need some grammatical changes: "The metabolic costs of fine roots (fine
root respiration) are major factors that prevents plants from efficiently exploring the soil and absorbing nutrients under
unfavorable conditions 20. Thus, increasing the stele diameter of roots (which have a lower metabolic rate) and/or the
reducing the cortical thickness of roots..." I would change "prevents" to "prevent" and "the reducing" to "reducing." 
In the Introduction, the authors state "The altered habitat caused by the addition of polyester microplastic led to the
colonization of the roots by AM fungi, which conferred positive effects on plant growth by increasing nutrient availability." I
suggest changing "nutrient availability" to "nutrient uptake." The way the sentence is written, it sounds like AM fungi were
increasing nutrient availability in the soil, but the reference cited here largely measured plant performance, not nutrient
availability. 
In the Results section, there is a sentence stating that "microplastic addition significantly increased TC, TN, AN, NO3-, N,
NH4+, N and SWC of ECM association type..." But, NH4+ is shown as "ns" in Figure 1. In this paragraph, I would also add a
sentence about the N increases in the AM association type. 
The authors should check the paragraph that is added to the Results about the partial least squares path models shown in
Figure 6. The paragraph indicates positive effects on root anatomical traits, but the figure shows blue arrows, which I thought
were negative associations. Please check this entire paragraph and ensure it is consistent with the figure. 
The authors should check over the first paragraph of the discussion a bit, particularly the statement about microplastics
amplifying "ECM symbiotic." I first think the authors mean "symbiosis" here, but also what does it mean to amplify a
mycorrhizal symbiosis? Also, what is "riming soil microbes for organic nitrogen decomposition"? This is a confusing
statement; do the authors mean "priming"? 
In the discussion, when the authors discuss the results of the AM association type (paragraph 2), there is a statement that
"The increase of phosphatase activity under exposure to microplastics (Figure 1o) promotes the decomposition and
transformation of organophosphorus compounds, leading to a significant increase in soil inorganic phosphorus." I think this
statement needs a clarification that AM fungi do not produce phosphatase enzymes. So, this increase seen here is due to
the activities of soil microbes or plant production and release into the soil. 
In the "Impacts of microplastics on root traits of ECM and AM association types" section of the Discussion, the first sentence
uses the word "obvious." I suggested the authors change the word consistent here, but obvious is also not the word to use
here. I suggest removing this word and just stating "In this study, we observed the responses of root chemical traits to
microplactic addition..." Later in this section, there is a sentence "Our findings indicate that microplastics have a detrimental
effect on root nutrient contents, although more available nitrogen and phosphorus were found in the rhizosphere soil of ECM
and AM association types, respectively." This is really confusing and I think the authors mean that microplastics reduce root
nutrients despite increases in certain nutrients in the surrounding rhizosphere soil. 
In the Conclusion section, the authors write that "while AM association type tended to produce root systems with higher
specific root length, tip density, epidermal thickness and vascular bundle diameter, but branching intensity, cortical thickness
and root tissue density." I think the authors are trying to say that branching intensity, cortical thickness, and root tissue
density were reduced. 
In the Data analysis section of the Materials and Methods, I am confused by the paired-sample t-tests and why they were
used in addition to the mixed effect models. I also think there needs to be more information on what exactly was done within
the car package as a follow-up to the mixed effects models. For example, how did the authors obtain the chi-square and R-
squared values that are reported in Tables 1 and 2?

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This study investigates how the addition of polystyrene microplastics alters 
rhizosphere soil properties and fine-root traits in ectomycorrhizal (ECM) and 
arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) tree species in a mixed temperate forest. The authors 
report mycorrhizal-specific differences in nutrient availability (N and P), fine root 
morphological traits, and root stoichiometry, underlining distinct adaptation strategies.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 20 -->

10 
 
These findings are contextualized within broader concerns about microplastic 
pollution in terrestrial ecosystems. 
The claims made in this study are novel and will be of broad interest to researchers 
studying plant–soil interactions, soil pollution, and forest ecology. The exploration of 
mycorrhizal-specific responses to microplastic contamination is especially innovative 
and fills a notable gap in current microplastic impact studies, which often ignore plant 
symbiotic strategies. 
Strengths: 
The experimental design used root bags in a mixed forest and comparing co-occurring 
ECM and AM species, which adds ecological realism while maintaining controlled 
sampling. The authors employ a multi-dimensional approach, integrating root 
morphology, stoichiometry, and soil nutrient measurements. The use of multivariate 
statistics (RDA, PCA) and trait-environment relationships improves interpretability of 
complex responses. 
Limitations and concerns: 
1. The use of 1% w/w polystyrene microplastic is relatively high and does not 
represent ambient levels in temperate forests (typically in the ng-mg/kg range). While 
high concentrations are useful for mechanistic insight, the rationale for choosing this 
dose should be clearly stated. Without such justification, conclusions about long-term 
ecosystem impacts and species dynamics risk being overstated.

We express our gratitude for the constructive feedback provided on our revision and

response, we have incorporated the R² values for the endogenous variables within our 
PLS-path models. Given that our study utilizes PLS-path models, it is important to

Goodness of Fit (GoF) index, which is widely recognized as a more appropriate and

We acknowledge the suggestion to include confidence intervals (CIs); however, due 
to the limited sample size derived from our field study, we opted for PLS-path models

small samples, the resampling techniques inherent to PLS-path models, such as

bootstrapping, tend to yield confidence intervals that are unstable and excessively 
broad. Consequently, our interpretation of the results prioritizes the directionality and

microplastics (MP) on soil properties and fine-root traits. Following a comprehensive
