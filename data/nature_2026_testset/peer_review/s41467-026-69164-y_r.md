The manuscript aims to analyze the roles of natural and anthropogenic climate in driving shifts in global heat stress using
human comfort indicators and to examine the disparities in heat stress across countries at varying levels of socioeconomic
development. 
Overall, the study is very well written and interesting; however, areas lacking detail must be addressed. It is silent on some
methodological aspects/procedures. Some sentences also lack appropriate references. Also, other aspects need to be
enhanced, such as discussing the results. While promising in its approach, the manuscript suffers from some shortcomings. 
Although the study aims to have a global dimension, the results mainly focus on Asia, Africa, and Australia. Although some
regions of North and South America and Europe present high heat stress (FIGURE 1 and 2), they are not mentioned in the
results and discussion sections. In this context, it would be nice to see results presented, for instance, in Fig1 b-c, and Fig2
b-c in the regional context of each continent/region. 
The author used the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) to analyze heat stress, but there is no discussion or
comparison with other studies that also used UTCI for the same purpose. We recommend the authors to consider citing the
following papers: 
https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/joc.7051 
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00704-023-04668-x 
rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/joc.7774 
The three scenarios used (historical climate change, natural climate change, and anthropogenic climate change) must be
explained. 
A major concern is about the heat stress threshold. Here, heat stress days are defined as those with a daily maximum UTCI
greater than 26°C. However, most of the tropical and subtropical regions of the world have mean UTCI values that are more
significant than this. For instance, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00704-023-04668-x adapted heat stress
categories according to the regional climate of South America. Without this consideration, most regions are naturally over
heat stress.

Thank you for providing the manuscript. The article presents an analysis of how heat stress trends are experienced
unequally by countries at different levels of socioeconomic development. Additionally, it distinguishes the contributions of
natural and anthropogenic warming, which adds insight to the discussion. 
However, the validity of the results heavily depends on how the contributions to heat stress are decomposed into natural and
anthropogenic warming. Unfortunately, the authors do not clearly explain the methodology behind this decomposition. To
ensure that readers can fully assess the results, I hope the authors can clarify the following questions. 
First, the manuscript mentions that 9 climate models were selected to generate historical and natural-forcing historical
simulations. Could the authors specify which models were used? Additionally, how many ensemble members from each
model were included in the analysis? 
Second, the authors state that they used the “Delta method.” I assume this refers to "quantile delta mapping" as proposed by
Cannon et al. (2015). If so, what type of distributions were applied? Did the authors construct temperature and humidity
distributions at each grid cell before applying quantile delta mapping, or did they aggregate global temperature and humidity
distributions before applying the method? 
After applying quantile delta mapping, the authors would obtain temperature and humidity distributions (as well as time
series) for the synthesized natural-forcing historical period. However, how were the corresponding UTCI values for this
scenario derived? Did the authors simply plug in temperature and humidity values into the UTCI calculator? If so, how were
wind speed and radiative temperature handled? Or, was there a different method used to compute UTCI for the natural-
forcing scenario? This distinction is important because the manuscript states that UTCI for the all-forcing scenario was
directly obtain from the ERA5 dataset, but the approach for the natural-forcing scenario seems to be different. 
After applying the quantile delta mapping, the authors would obtain temperature/humidity distributions (and time series) for
the synthesized natural-forcing historical period. But how do the authors get the synthesized UTCI values for this natural-
forcing scenario? Do they simply plug in the temperature/humidity? What about the wind sped and radiating temperature? Or
do the authors use different method to calculate the UTCI for the natural-forcing scenario? I asked this question because the
authors used the computed UTCI from EAR5 for the all-forcing scenario, but seemed to use different method to obtain the
UTCI for the natural-forcing scenario. 
While some of these questions may seem straightforward, a full disclosure of the methodology is essential to ensure the
robustness of the results. Given the significance of the findings, I encourage the authors to provide a more detailed
explanation of their approach.

Thank you for the revisions — we appreciate the improved effort to present results in a broader regional context. However,

or 'historically marginalized racial groups', which more accurately reflect Brazil's social and structural realities.

Review 
The authors investigated the connection between climate change and thermal 
comfort in economically disadvantaged areas, successfully addressing a critical 
challenge in distinguishing the anthropogenic and natural contributions to climate 
change. 
This is an engaging and timely article that explores topics of significant 
importance, particularly the interplay between anthropogenic and natural factors 
contributing to climate change, with a focus on low- and middle-income countries. 
The exploration of thermal comfort as a related factor is particularly noteworthy, 
as it remains underexplored, especially in more economically deprived regions. 
Additionally, the use of open -source data is commendable and aligns with best 
practices for transparency and reproducibility in research. 
However, there are several areas requiring improvement, particularly in terms of 
methodological clarity and the discussion of related literature, which I wil l detail 
below. While the research demonstrates potential for publication, I do not 
recommend it in its current form due to these substantive concerns.
