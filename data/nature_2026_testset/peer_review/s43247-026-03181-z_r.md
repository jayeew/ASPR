REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript describes a modeling study where Chl concentration and primary production in the Arctic are modeled using
a Phlow relationship with a focus on ice-covered regions. While the general field of Arctic marine primary production is of
great interest and still has a lot of unknowns I don't see that this manuscript contributes much in its current form. It seems like
all results are as expected without any surprises. I also wonder how well the model performs, especially in regions with
significant ice coverage. My specific comments/concerns are as follows: 
1. I would like to see a more comprehensive validation of the model. There have to be more than 3 vertical profiles of Chl
and PP in the Arctic region. How does the model compare to satellite-derived Chl? It would probably be necessary to iterate
the model further than 2004 for such a comparison. 
2. How does the model handle regions with significant ice coverage? The text says "In ice-covered regions, the low-light
environment leads to an increase in chlorophyll content, which results in high under-ice chlorophyll". I assume that is meant
to be the C:Chl ratio? But what happens to total biomass? I'm surprised that the production and biomass are very high in
their regions, especially if the ice edge ecosystems are considered, but there isn't much discussion on how well the model
describes this. 
3. How different are the results from a standard Geider-type C:Chl photacclimation model? Should the models be modified
when used under ice? 
4. It is estimated that 54% of total primary production occurs in waters with more than 10% ice coverage. This number is not
very interesting without looking at what happens when these regions become ice-free. What are the trends? What happened
with ice conditions of the 2020s? It's also not clear if the number presented is Net Primary Production or Gross Primary
Production. This is critical when the numbers are compared to satellite-derived PP estimates, which tend to be closer to
GPP. Several satellite-derive PP models use dynamically set C:Chl ratios (For example the Café or The Platt-
Sathyendranath model).

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Summary 
Yoshio et al., used an ecosystem model to predict the vertical distribution of chlorophyll and particulate organic carbon
throughout the Arctic based on horizontal and vertical distributions of essential light and nutrients. Consistent with
observations, chlorophyll concentrations were highest at deep subsurface chlorophyll maxima or under sea ice. Particulate
organic carbon and net primary productivity did not follow the same trends, leading to a decoupling between distributions of
chlorophyll and productivity, and significant variability in carbon-chlorophyll ratios. This finding has significant implications
for Arctic productivity estimates since many satellite-based productivity algorithms rely on assumed ratios of phytoplankton
carbon-chlorophyll. Further, satellites do not measure under sea-ice, where Yoshio and colleagues estimate over 50% of
Arctic productivity occurs. The key findings of this study identify critical areas of consideration for improved estimates of
Arctic primary productivity. As such, the work within is valuable to the community, however a number of concerns, listed
below, should be addressed before publication. 
Major edits 
1. The discussion section is quite limited and could be strengthened by additional comments on: 
a. Model limitations beyond implicit melt pond albedo effects. (i.e. single general algae in the ecosystem model and no
inclusion of ice algae) 
b. Coherence or disagreement between the model SCM, Chl, POC, and NPP estimates and observations (satellite or ship
based). Rather than comparing ranges, the authors should more directly compare the data across years and sea ice
provinces, which can aid in identification of model biases. 
c. A primary finding of this study is that close to half of Arctic productivity can be attributed to ice algae. Over the course of the
study period, sea ice extent has continuously diminished. The authors should include their findings on the effects of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

diminishing sea ice on interannual variability in productivity over the 7 year study period, and likely outcomes of continued
sea ice retreat with climate change. 
2. The primary point the authors make throughout the manuscript is that vertical distributions of primary productivity deviate
from vertical distributions of Chl. In turn, Chl variability is dictated by photo-acclimation. Therefore, the conclusion that
photoacclimation determines Arctic productivity as the title suggests, seems somewhat misguided since photoacclimation
would likely be a bad predictor of NPP. A more accurate conclusion is that photoacclimation is driving decoupling between
NPP and Chl, which can lead to errors in chl-based NPP algorithms. Some reframing is needed throughout the manuscript
and title. 
3. The manuscript lacks statistics to support the authors claims. Overall, further data analysis is required to support the
arguments throughout the paper. 
Minor Comments 
L18) Since the SCM is mentioned in the title and throughout the abstract, I think something could be mentioned here about
the challenging nature of measuring productivity in subsurface water as well as in ice covered waters. 
L23) Is productivity more strongly correlated with C-biomass than Chl? If so, say that. It doesn’t necessarily follow for me that
a high degree of [chl] variability indicates that productivity is not well ‘aligned’ with chl. 
L32) Why the focus on 2007? Perhaps a more general statement about the decrease in ice thickness over the past few
decades is more appropriate here since the model was only run till 2004. 
L58-69) Paragraph can use some restructuring. While I agree it is critical to mention that Chl:POC is affected by nutrient
availability, this detail and the statement about chl variability observed in incubation experiments are a bit awkwardly
sandwiched in the middle of the paragraph all about photoacclimation. Flow can be improved by starting with more general
details about the drivers of arctic [Chl] variability, then narrowing in on photoacclimation, and then the hypothesis about
higher chl in low-light SCM and under ice environments. Within this paragraph, it is also probably worth noting that
phytoplankton concentrate at the SCM and under ice to access greater nutrient concentrations than are available in the open
surface ocean, which likely also contributes to heightened intracellular chl concentrations. 
L83/Fig 1) How were different sea ice provinces defined? How were the three representative sites chosen? Why not present
the mean properties of the different provinces instead of individual sites? 
L85-90) This feels repetitive with the figure caption. 
L111) This statement could be strengthened with some statistical results comparing the relationship between NPP vs Chl
and NPP vs POC across the entire study region and within individual sea ice provinces. 
L117-119) This comparison of vertically integrated productivity between region C and A and B belongs in the following
section alongside other results regarding vertically integrated productivity values (L142). Authors should also report their
NPP estimates by sea ice province and for the study region as a whole, as well as some discussion of the interannual
variability during the 7 year study period. 
L123) Model validation can be improved by directly comparing the modelled SCM depth against the observed SCM, rather
than simply comparing ranges. Are there biases in the model? Do those biases vary by sea ice province? Similarly
comparing the model Chl and NPP against observations would be illustrative. 
L145) This statement would be strengthened by comparing average SCM depth profiles for each sea ice province, rather
than comparing single grid points that could be outliers within their province. 
L145-148) This is a very interesting and significant result! 
L154-156) Given the large degree of difference between the simulated NPP and satellite based NPP results, I think the word
‘comparable’ should be exchanged for ‘significantly less than’ or other similar phrasing. 
L291) Authors note their ecosystem model does not include ice algae, yet a significant focus of the paper is on ice algae
productivity. What degree of uncertainty in the results is due to applying the general algae model for ice algae
environments? Authors should justify their choice to not include ice algae. 
Fig 1) Definition of sea provinces should be included here. What parameters were used to distinguish provinces? Are
provinces defined by ice cover or geographic location? For example, is the ice-free Barents Sea part of the same province
as ice-free Baffin Bay? I think it would be more appropriate to report the data in terms of the mean +/- standard deviation for
each province. 
Fig 2) I would also like to a see a similar figure with observations on the x-axis and model simulation on the y-axis, for SCM,
Chl, and NPP estimates 
Fig 3) For subpanel b, this doesn’t appear to be a depth integrated value of Chl, what depth is being represented here?
Same question for subpanel c and d, all of these parameters, will vary considerably with depth, as is a focal point of this

Fig 4) How about a line plot with four lines: Total Arctic productivity, productivity in ice-free water, productivity in marginal
zones, and productivity in ice covered waters, plotted over the 7 years the simulation was run? With error bars! 
Fig 2 extended data) For surface depths? Any comments on why the model is underestimating summer nitrate in the Barents
sea in summer and overestimating in winter? Similar question for the Chukchi Sea.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Overall, I appreciate the additional information and especially the additional figures provided in the latest round of edits.
However, I still have some major uncertainties regarding the performance of the model underpinning the manuscript. 
The authors have simulated SCM depths, Chl profiles, and NPP for different Arctic provinces. As noted in my first round of
revisions, the authors claim throughout the manuscript their model successfully matches observations, but do not provide
any quantitative measures to support this claim. I see other reviewers also pointed out the need for more model validation.
While I appreciate the new inclusion of Extended Data Table 1, which provides observed and modelled SCM depths, I was
really hoping to see some statistical measures of model performance. Simply stating that the model reasonably simulates
seasonal changes (L189-200) does not suffice as model validation. 
In attempting to ascertain the model’s performance on my own, I fit a line of best fit to the modeled versus observed SCM
values provided in the Extended Data Table 1. This line (Model SCM = 0.24*Observed SCM + 26.6) displayed significant
deviation from the 1:1 line expected for ideal model performance. It also had a low R2 of 0.11. 
In addition to SCM, the model also simulates Chla and NPP. Yet, model performance statistics are not provided for these
parameters either. However, based on Extended Data Figure 7, overestimate Chla concentrations by up to a factor of 10 in
the Greenland Sea in May and June while simultaneously overestimating ice cover in other Arctic provinces. By contrast, the
model appears to underestimate Chl in July-Sep in nearly all provinces, particularly the Russian Seas. 
While the model does appear to recreate general expected trends based on photoacclimation and resource allocation
theories, more work is required at this point to inspire confidence in the absolute magnitudes of its results. 
Minor comments: 
Note, there is a typo in Extended Data Table 1, Baffin Bay is mistyped as ‘Buffin’. This typo appears again in the caption of
Extended Data Fig. 5

>3. How different are the results from a standard Geider-type C:Chl photacclimation model? 
>Should the models be modified when used under ice? 
Geider's original equations (Geider, 1998) are simplified when applied to 3D ecosystem 
models, and the method of simplification differs among models. Geider’s theory considers three 
prognostic variables, phytoplankton nitrogen, carbon, and chlorophyll, but most of the 3D 
ecosystem models employ only two prognostic variables, assuming a constant phytoplankton 
carbon to nitrogen ratio. As far as we know, there is no study using simplified Geider’s 
equations that successfully simulates Arctic SCM and shows a significant role of variation in 
chl:C in reproducing SCM. We compared simulated Arctic SCM depth between our model and 
two representative AR6 models using simplified Geider’s equations. We found that the SCM 
depth of over 90 m, which occurs occasionally in these two models using simplified Geider’s 
equations, is inconsistent with the observations. Our study shows that if the SCM depth is not 
adequately reproduced, it cannot be guaranteed that chl:C is correctly simulated. As described in 
the reply to major edit 2, chl:C is crucial for calculating the growth rate and resultant primary 
production, especially in low-light conditions such as under ice.
