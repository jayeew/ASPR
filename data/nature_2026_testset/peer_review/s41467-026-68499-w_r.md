Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a work in which they demonstrate that silk fibroin (SF) is suitable to form high-density layers on
nanoparticle (NP) surfaces. As SF is water-soluble, this “coating” of NPs happens in an aqueous dispersion, which is
advantageous. Various types of NPs are used, most of all polystyrene spheres and silica NPs. For the characterization of the
modified NPs a zoo of experimental tools is applied and gives very reliable information. 
It is claimed that the given approach will allow to produce bio-nano interfaces for various applications. 
In the conclusions it is stated that an optimal SF concentration of 0.2 w/v% was found for the discussed applications. Lazy
readers, just reading abstract and conclusions could conclude that this is true for any NPs, however, the optimum SF
concentration should depend on the size, shape and surface characteristics, as it is always the case. 
Except for this given optimum SF concentration (maybe my personal pessimistic view of possible ignorant readers), this is
an excellent manuscript and deserves to be published in a leading journal. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
T. Kim and colleagues’ study, “Enabling Water-based, High-Density Nanoparticles Assembly by using Silk Fibroin as an
Adsorbate,” investigates a water-based fabrication strategy for producing high-density nanoparticle (NP) layers by exploiting
silk fibroin (SF) as a natural amphiphilic adsorbate that improves wetting and packing on hydrophobic substrates. The
authors support their conclusions with an impressive array of physico-chemical and electrical measurements—TEM/SEM,
AFM-IR, DLS and ζ-potential, AFM force–distance spectroscopy, XPS, ellipsometry, correlative Raman-SEM, and device-
level electrical tests—and the experimental descriptions are clear and detailed. 
This work is significant for bio-integrated interfaces, solution-processed electronics, and nanomaterial assembly, and it will
appeal to the Nature Communications readership interested in water-based nanofabrication and biotic–abiotic interface
technologies. 
I recommend consideration for publication after the authors address the following comments. 
1. In the “Concentration-dependent adsorption of SF onto NPs” section, it is not clear how the nanoparticle monolayer in Fig.
1d was prepared. The Methods state only that NP dispersions were drop-cast and dried overnight on lacey-carbon TEM
grids. Under such conditions, nanoparticles typically self-assemble vertically into 3-D opal-like multilayers. Please clarify
whether the monolayer was obtained through surface treatment, dilution control, or shortened drying, or whether the image
shows the top surface of a thicker stack. 
2. In Fig. 1f, the hydrodynamic radius increases with SF concentration up to about 0.4 w/v % but slightly decreases at higher
concentrations, while the ζ-potential changes little. A short discussion of this non-monotonic trend would help readers;
possible reasons include screening of electrostatic repulsion, partial compaction of SF chains, or apparent boundary shifts
caused by free SF molecules. 
3. In Fig. 1g, the AFM force-spectroscopy results compare pristine and SF-adsorbed NPs. It would improve clarity to include
adhesion-force data as a function of SF concentration (e.g., 0.02 %, 0.2 %, 2 %) to illustrate the transition among
“insufficient,” “optimal,” and “excessive” adsorption regimes. Supplementary Fig. 9 shows only pristine and 2 %, so

4. In the “Wetting improvement and high-density NP coatings enabled by optimal SF adsorption” section, the authors
convincingly demonstrate dense NP coating on hydrophobic substrates (PDMS, PTFE). For applications in bio-integrated or
flexible devices, please discuss whether the SF-bound NP layer remains mechanically and electrically stable under

5. In Fig. 3b and 3c, the NP layer at higher SF concentration (2 wt %) appears slightly thicker. A brief discussion of film-

influence interfacial adhesion would provide deeper mechanical insight. 
7. In the “Preserved functionality of NP-based electronics at optimal SF adsorption” section, the authors demonstrate several
devices using SF-adsorbed NP layers. It would be valuable to discuss mechanical and electrical stability during substrate
bending, especially for the hybrid Ag NW/ITO electrode on PDMS (Fig. 4d). Since Ag NWs bridge the inorganic ITO and soft
PDMS, commenting on how this network maintains conductivity and adhesion under strain would emphasize the practical
importance of the approach. 
8. In Fig. 4, the notations SF020, SF002, and SF film appear in several graphs. These abbreviations are defined only in
Supplementary Table 1. They should be clearly defined in the main text (e.g., SF020 = 0.2 wt % SF, SF002 = 0.02 wt % SF)
so readers need not consult the Supplementary Information. Because dielectric thickness strongly affects capacitor
performance, please also provide approximate film thicknesses for these conditions. 
9. The Supplementary Information presents LSPR spectra of Au NPs and Au NRs on PDMS, but the optical structure of
these plasmonic samples is not described—e.g., film thickness, particle density, or whether a monolayer or thicker layer was
used. Since plasmonic response depends on particle arrangement, a concise description of the coating morphology and
thickness would make the optical data more meaningful. 
Overall, this manuscript provides a thorough and convincing study of silk-fibroin-mediated nanoparticle assembly using fully
water-based processes. The data are comprehensive and of high quality, and the work establishes SF as an enabling
material for environmentally friendly nanofabrication. Addressing the points above—particularly clarification of sample
preparation, film-thickness and concentration dependencies, and mechanical stability of flexible devices—would further
strengthen the paper and broaden its impact. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript describes the use of silk fibroin to assist with the formation of dense nanoparticle layers. The authors show
that silk fibroin can be non-specifically adsorbed onto the surface of nanoparticles, and that spincoating a nanoparticle/silk
fibroin solution onto silicon yields a dense nanoparticle layer. The authors pinpoint a specific concentration range of silk
fibroin that yields best results in terms of conductivity and device performance (shown by a model nanoparticle-based
capacitor). The experiments are comprehensive, leveraging a variety of techniques for molecular-scale characterization (e.g.
of adhesion force, morphology, surface charge, device performance metrics, etc). The data analysis is very competent, and I
commend the authors for presenting a very clear manuscript. 
One of my major critique concerns the novelty and significance of the work. The adsorption of silk fibroin to surfaces has
been previously reported by Kaplan and colleagues as far back as 2005 (https://doi.org/10.1021/la051862m,
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jconrel.2007.06.006, https://doi.org/10.1002/adfm.200901774 - this last one even reports coating
nanoplatelets) and by Zha and colleagues (https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlepdf/2018/bm/c8bm01186a,
https://doi.org/10.1021/acsbiomaterials.3c01042, https://doi.org/10.1021/acsami.4c07528). The prior literature shows many
relevant aspects, such as the effect of pH, presence of ions, secondary structure change upon adsorption, and adhesion
(also measured by AFM force spectroscopy). However, there is insufficient discussion in the manuscript of the submitted
work in context of these prior works. 
The ability of silk fibroin to adsorb to surfaces, and that absorption amount can be "tuned" by changing silk fibroin
concentration, is unsurprising, as this is typical macromolecular adsorption behavior. I think the novelty of this manuscript is
the identification of a silk fibroin concentration that can give just enough "stick" to the nanoparticles to assist with forming a
dense, conductive layer. However, I would like a better discussion in the manuscript about how silk fibroin compares to other
(potentially cheaper) molecules that can modify wettability, surface tension, and adhesion (such as surfactants, block
copolymers, amphiphilic, non-amphiphilic polypeptides, oligo/polysaccharides, etc). Is there something special about the
silk fibroin sequence that allows to behave this way? How does the use of silk fibroin stack up against other methods of
improving nanoparticle wettability? This discuss is rather vague in the manuscript. 
The claim that these devices can perform well in a biological setting is perhaps a bit of a stretch. Silk fibroin will swell

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

(increasing the distance between nanoparticles) and be degraded over time if it is in contact with biological fluids. The
electronic properties will certainly change over time. I don't see any mention of using an alcohol or annealing post-treatment
of the films, which means that the silk fibroin will likely dissolve in an aqueous environment. 
I also have a few technical questions. Can the AFM IR data be deconvoluted to get information about secondary structure
composition? The signal at 1650 cm-1 is a random coil/alpha helix signal, but I would expect there to be more beta-sheets in
silk fibroin, particularly if this material is expected to not dissolve in aqueous environments (see
https://doi.org/10.1021/acsami.4c07528 for similar AFM-IR analysis) 
Some parameters critical to macromolecular adsorption are not included in the manuscript, such as molecular weight (and
distribution) of the silk fibroin, solution pH (whether it was buffered or not), and ion content. Without this information,
reproducing the results (e.g. using a different silk fibroin source/batch, in a different lab, etc) will likely be very difficult. 
The coating process is a bit confusing to me. Was non-adsorbed silk fibroin washed away (for example, but spinning down
the nanoparticles then resuspending in clean water) before spin coating? There will obviously be excess silk fibroin
between particles in the case of higher silk concentration if there's no wash step. Incorporating a wash step may mean that
the coating process is less sensitive to the concentration of the adsorption solution. Furthermore, if the non-adsorbed silk
fibroin is still present in the spin coating step, the surface tension may be different with different concentrations, which may
affect the outcomes of the spin coating. 
The adhesion measurements in AFM were performed using a particle on the AFM tip and a flat hydrophobic substrate. Is this
an acceptable model for the suspended nanoparticle system, where both surfaces have substantial curvature? I think this
model needs to be justified by at least showing that silk fibroin coating thicknesses and secondary structures are similar on
the flat surface vs the curved nanoparticle surface.

Comment 5: 
In Fig. 3b and 3c, the NP layer at higher SF concentration (2 wt  %) appears slightly thicker. A 
brief discussion of film -thickness dependence on SF concentration at identical spin -coating 
speeds—supported by SEM or ellipsometry data —would clarify whether this difference arises 
from excess SF matrix or modified wetting. 
 
Response: 
We thank the reviewer for the insightful comment.  
To clarify the possible influence of SF concentration on film thickness, we measured the 
thickness of SiO 2 NP layers spin -coated (2000 rpm, 60 s) from aqueous dispersions containing 
different SF concentrations (0.02, 0.2, and 2.0 w/v%) on PDMS-coated glass substrates (~1 cm2). 
The ellipsometric thicknesses at the center region were 242.5 ± 3.7, 244.5 ± 3.7, and 285.6 ± 5.9 
nm, respectively (N = 5). 
Pairwise t-tests reveal no statistical difference in thickness between 0.02 % and 0.2 % SF 
dispersions (p = 0.51), whereas the 2.0 % group exhibits a significantly thicker film (p < 0.0001). 
This increase originates from the accumulation of free (non-adsorbed) SF molecules that remain 
in the dispersion at higher concentrations, forming an additional matrix phase among the NPs. 
Although wetting improvement above 0.2 % SF allows uniform coating on hydrophobic substrates 
(see Supplementary Figs. 14 –15), such wetting enhancement alone cannot account for the 
pronounced thickness increase at 2.0 %. Furthermore, the viscosity and surface tension of SF 
solutions below 2 w/v % are known to be comparable to those of pure water (Macromol. Biosci. 
8, 1006–1018 (2008)), indicating that modified wetting or rheological effects are unlikely to be 
the primary cause. Therefore, the observed thickness increase at 2.0 % SF mainly results from the 
presence of excess SF matrix rather than changes in wetting behavior. 
We revised the main manuscript to clarify the correlation between film thickness and 
excessive SF in NP dispersion, and added a Supplementary Fig. 16 to support this claim.
