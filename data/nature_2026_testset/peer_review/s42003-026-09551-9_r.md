Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This article proposes a deep learning model which leverages pre-trained representation learning and residue-aware
attention mechanism for kcat prediction. It covers the following central aspects: 
Model Architecture – PMAK combines ProT5 (for enzyme sequences) and RXNFP (for reaction representations), processing
them through a CNN + fully connected network to align feature dimensions. A residue-aware attention block then captures
interactions between enzyme and reaction features. Finally, after max pooling the enzyme representation, both embeddings
are concatenated and fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) for kcat prediction. This approach is claimed to outperform
TurNup and CatPred in accuracy. 
Model Performance Evaluation – PMAK reports good performance in predicting enzyme kcat values across diverse
benchmark settings. It outperforms existing models such as CatPred and TurNuP in both warm-start (familiar
enzymes/reactions) and cold-start scenarios (unseen enzymes or reactions), particularly excelling when sequence or
reaction similarity is low. In enzyme cold-start settings, PMAK achieves improved R² and Pearson correlation scores
compared to prior models, showcasing strong generalization. Additionally, PMAK’s predictions enhance the accuracy of
proteome allocation simulations in genome-scale metabolic models, validating its downstream utility. 
Novelty – The key novelty of PMAK lies in its residue-aware attention mechanism, which allows the model to focus on
functionally important residues in the enzyme sequence based on the given reaction. This offers not only improved
prediction accuracy but also biological interpretability, as high-attention residues tend to correspond to known active or
binding sites. Moreover, PMAK is one of the first kcat prediction models to utilize full reaction SMILES (substrates and
products) via RXNFP, enabling richer reaction-contextual understanding compared to models that only consider reactants.
This combination of interpretability, contextual awareness, and high performance represents a significant advance over prior
approaches. 
Main Contribution of the Article 
Residue-Aware Attention Mechanism: 
The core innovation lies in a learnable attention block that identifies which amino acid residues are most relevant for
predicting catalytic activity. This approach bridges sequence-based learning with residue-level functional insights, enabling
more interpretable predictions compared to black-box models. 
Performance Gains in Cold-Start Settings: 
PMAK consistently outperforms baseline models such as TurNuP and CatPred, particularly in low-similarity and
generalization-heavy regimes. 
Application to Metabolic Modeling: 
The predicted kcat values are shown to improve proteome allocation simulations in enzyme-constrained genome-scale
models (ecGEMs), demonstrating downstream utility beyond regression accuracy. 
Critical Insights & Future Directions: 
Future improvements could involve integrating dynamic structural information (e.g., from molecular dynamics), enhancing
the attention mechanism to model condition-dependent behavior, and expanding the framework to predict other kinetic
parameters like Km and Ki. These extensions would make PMAK a more versatile for enzyme engineering and systems
biology. 
How Does the Work Differ from Existing Tools? 
Unlike previous reviews that may have focused on specific aspects of enzyme function or narrow applications of machine
learning, this review:

Reaction Input Scope: 
PMAK uses full biochemical reactions, including both substrates and products, represented as SMILES strings. Existing
models (e.g., CatPred) use only substrates or reactants, ignoring product context, which can be critical for modeling
enzymatic specificity and reversibility. 
Residue-Aware Attention: 
PMAK introduces a residue-aware attention mechanism that dynamically weighs each amino acid in the enzyme sequence
according to the reaction context. Existing models typically pool entire sequence embeddings (e.g., via mean/max pooling),
lacking this residue-level interpretability and functional insight. 
Does the Work Merit Publication? 
Yes. 
The article offers a timely and thorough review of a rapidly evolving research area that is of high relevance to both
computational biology and industrial biotechnology. 
Are There Any Major Concerns About the Work / Analysis? 
Systematic Ablation Study: 
In section 2.6, the authors assert that components such as the residue-aware attention block significantly enhance the
performance of PMAK, the paper lacks a systematic ablation study to substantiate these claims. The value of using RXNFP
for full reaction representation is not compared against simpler chemical encodings or substrate-only inputs, as used in
baseline models like CatPred. The inclusion of the ProT5 embeddings increases model complexity, but their individual
contributions remain untested. Without a comprehensive ablation study, it is difficult to assess which parts of the architecture
are truly responsible for the performance gains. I strongly recommend the authors include ablations that disable or replace
key components (attention, reaction encoder, and embedding types) and report performance metrics to validate the
necessity and impact of each module. 
Residue Differentiation Claim: 
In section 2.7, the authors suggest that PMAK can effectively highlight key catalytic residues using its residue-aware
attention mechanism (lines 265-266), this claim is not fully supported by quantitative evidence. The current evaluation is
largely based on visual comparisons between high-attention residues and known active or binding sites from UniProt and
EasIFA. To robustly support the claim, it would be helpful to include objective metrics like precision, recall, or ROC-AUC to
assess how well the model distinguishes active site residues from non-essential ones. Additionally, the study does not
compare PMAK’s interpretability with simpler techniques, such as mean pooling or gradient-based attribution. Without these
comparisons, it is hard to tell whether the attention mechanism provides genuinely novel biological insights or if it is just
reflecting patterns already learned by the pretrained embeddings. 
What Is Your Recommendation? 
Accept with revisions. 
The manuscript is well-written and makes a novel contribution to enzyme kinetics modeling. However, to enhance its impact,
reproducibility, and interpretability, the authors should include systematic ablation study as well as enhance the discussion
on the sufficient validation for residue level interpretability 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study introduces PMAK, a novel deep learning framework for predicting enzyme catalytic turnover numbers (kcat) by
integrating pretrained representations and a residue-aware attention mechanism. The model leverages ProtT5 for extracting
enzyme sequence embeddings and RXNFP for reaction SMILES embeddings, respectively, and adopts an attention module
to capture the complex interplay between enzymes and their catalyzed reactions. Evaluations across warm-start and cold-
start scenarios demonstrate that PMAK consistently outperforms existing methods. Additionally, attention score analyses for
2 enzymes show that the model could focus on biochemically relevant residues, providing certain mechanistic insights for
predictions. However, several issues should be addressed before publication. 
Major: 
1. The authors should clarify the reason for re-collecting a new dataset and justify its relatively small size. The dataset used
in this study contains only 4,271 data points, which is significantly smaller than previous works such as CatPred (23,197
kcat values), which also claimed to contain reaction annotations for the database kcat entries. 
2. The authors should elaborate on the data curation process in more detail, including inclusion/exclusion criteria, data
filtering methods, and quality control. For example, how to decide whether a reaction is “non-natural”? Additionally, a
histogram or distribution plot of kcat values should be provided to illustrate the numerical range and balance of the dataset. 
3. To further support the claim that incorporating full reaction information improves prediction accuracy, the authors might
consider conducting an ablation study where reaction-level representations are replaced with substrate-only inputs. This
could demonstrate whether reaction-level information truly provides additional predictive power. 
4. In the benchmark experiment in Figure 3, why are the test sample sizes of different models inconsistent? Model
benchmarking should be conducted on an identical test set to ensure fair and meaningful comparison. 
5. In Figure 5, the authors attempted to use 2 cases to prove the model’s perception of protein functional sites. Specifically,
they found that attention scores for binding/active sites are higher than for general sites. However, this should be further
investigated using all test data with known binding/active site annotations, instead of just 2 cases. 
6. Given that CataPro (Nat Commun 16, 2736 (2025)) is a recently published and competitive approach for kcat prediction,
including it as a baseline could further strengthen the performance comparison and highlight the contributions of PMAK.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript "PMAK: Enhancing kcat Prediction through Residue-Aware Attention Mechanism and Pre-Trained
Representations" presents a novel method for predicting turnover numbers kcat of wildtype enzymes. The method's main
novelty lies in its combination of enzyme information at the amino acid level with chemical reaction information via a
specifically designed attention mechanism. This method outperforms the current state of the art. Many of my comments are
only minor, relating to missing information or clarifications. 
MAJOR CONCERNS: 
1. The current state-of-the-art kcat prediction model is probably EITLEM (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.checat.2024.101094).
However, this model was not used for comparison, nor was it mentioned or discussed. 
2. To compare the models in Figure 3, the mean absolute error (MAE) is used as a metric. Everywhere else, R² and/or MSE
are used as the evaluation metrics. Why did the authors choose a different metric here? In particular, since the metrics in this
figure are based on different test sets, R² would have provided a clearer picture, since it normalises for variance in a dataset,
which the MAE does not do. 
3. Detecting key residues: When validating the ability of PMAK to detect key residues, only two enzymes were investigated
(Figure 5). Given the small sample size, the good performance could be due to chance. The authors mentioned that they
predicted key residues for their dataset using the EasIFA prediction method. However, I could not find any results comparing
the predicted key residues with the importance scores obtained using PMAK. A comparison on the whole dataset would give
better picture how good PMAK is at detecting key residues. 
MINOR CONCERNS 
4. The authors state that the DLKcat model leads to 'effective predictions of kcat values across multiple organisms'.
However, a study cited by the authors later on [12] showed that DLKcat cannot predict the effect of mutations and is no better
than a naïve similarity approach for wild-type enzymes. Therefore, this statement overestimates the predictive capabilities of
DLKcat. 
5. ESM-2 has not been tested when different enzyme-encoding methods were being compared. However, ESM-2 models
are currently among the best performing and the most widely used protein language models. 
6. In lines 173–180, a better R² value for CatPred than for TurNuP is stated, but a worse MSE. However, R² = 1 - MSE/VAR,
so this cannot be the case when comparing the two metrics on the same test set. 
7. The authors write, 'The only model that attempts to incorporate reaction information, TurNuP'. Why 'attempt'? Reaction
information is indeed integrated into the TurNuP model. 
8. Many recent kcat prediction studies also aim to predict the turnover numbers of mutated enzymes. This study does not
discuss whether the model is applicable to mutants or wild type only. However, this information is important for potential
users.

2.6 Systematic Ablation Study 
To rigorously quantify the contribution of each component in PMAK, we conducted a 
comprehensive ablation study spanning the residue -aware attention block, reaction

(bilinear attention), and NeuralAttention_kcat (conventional neural attention) (for details 
on the computation process, see Methods). As reported in Tabl e 2 and Table 3, 
eliminating or replacing the residue-aware attention consistently degraded performance. 
These results support that explicitly modeling residue -level contributions and jointly

2.7 PMAK more effectively differentiates key residues from ordinary residues 
To further demonstrate that our residue -aware attention mechanism not only 
enhances the model’s ability to predict 𝑘𝑐𝑎𝑡 values, but also provides interpretability, we 
conducted two case studies using test sets from both cold-start experimental scenarios. 
It is well established that active or binding sites in proteins are crucial for the interaction 
between proteins and small molecules. Therefore, a model with strong 𝑘𝑐𝑎𝑡 prediction 
capabilities should focus more on residues associated with these active or binding regions. 
In this study, we selected two representative enzymes, AdoMetDC (UniProt ID:Q9WZC3) 
and HTPA synthase (UniProt ID: Q6GH13), both of which possess both binding sites and 
active sites, with a moderate number of functional sites, making them suitable for analysis. 
By inputting their amino acid sequences into our trained model, we obtained the 
attention matrix generated by the attention module for each enzyme and averaged it 
across embedding dimension to compute individual residue attention scores. Using their
