Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Liu et al. estimated that dust aerosols from the Middle East regions act as a dominant external driver of the Indian Ocean
Dipole. In particular, they show that summertime dust over the 1980-2020 period accounts for ~36% of interannual IOD
variance, surpassing the El Niño-Southern Oscillation as the main driver during boreal autumn, using satellite observations
and reanalysis. In addition, they perform CSEM v1.2 with the Parallel Ocean Program (POP2) and Community Atmosphere
Model simulations (CAMS 4.3) to show that reduced summertime Middle East dust enhances warming in the western Indian
Ocean during boreal autumn and induces easterly winds that shoal the eastern thermocline. This tilts of the thermocline,
deepening it in the west and shoaling it in the east, and reinforces the SST gradient through the Bjerknes and wind–
evaporation–sea surface temperature (WES) feedback, amplifying a positive IOD pattern, and vice versa. 
Overall, the central findings are scientifically interesting and clearly relevant to Indian Ocean climate variability, particularly
the assessment that summertime Middle East dust explains a large fraction of IOD interannual variance, potentially
surpassing ENSO’s influence during boreal autumn. However, characterizing Middle Eastern dust aerosols as the dominant
external driver of IOD variability is currently overstated. A more explicit differentiation between the impacts of Middle Eastern
dust and those of Central and East Asian dust emissions is required, as both may modulate the IOD. Furthermore, additional
forcings and modes of variability, including anthropogenic aerosols, greenhouse gases, the Madden–Julian Oscillation
(MJO), and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), can also affect IOD variability during boreal autumn. These contributions
should be carefully separated and quantified before making the strong claim that Middle East dust is the dominant external
driver of the IOD. 
Dust variability can both drive and respond to IOD variability. However, this bidirectional relationship does not yet appear to
be well constrained in the authors’ analysis of dust–IOD interactions, partly because of substantial uncertainty in the
representation of dust emissions in the global climate model CESM v1.2 (Kok et al., 2022; main text reference 18). Given this
known bias, the authors should more clearly justify their choice of CESM v1.2 with CAMS 4.3 for this study and explain how
this underestimate might affect their conclusions regarding dust–IOD interactions. 
Prior work has linked anthropogenic aerosols and greenhouse gases to Indian Ocean warming and IOD-like patterns, and
has also shown that Middle East and West Asian dust influences the South Asian monsoon and Arabian Sea SST (see
Banerjee and Kumar, 2016; Jin et al., 2014; Zheng and Hoteit, 2025). Also, It has been shown that Indian summer monsoon
rainfall responds positively to Middle East dust (Jin and Yang, 2014). These findings from previous studies should be
discussed explicitly in the main manuscript.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript investigates the role of mineral dust aerosols from the Middle East in modulating interannual variability of
the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD). Using a combination of satellite observations, reanalysis datasets, and climate model
experiments, the authors demonstrate that variations in summer dust emissions between 1980 and 2020 explain
approximately 36% of the observed interannual IOD variance—exceeding the influence of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation
(ENSO) during boreal autumn. Overall, the paper identifies a previously underappreciated dust–IOD teleconnection,
emphasizing the importance of aerosol forcing in seasonal prediction and long-term climate projections. Overall, the work is
novel, and supported by comprehensive analysis. However, the manuscript is not always easy to follow, as several figure
captions and axis labels lack clarity and may cause confusion for readers. I recommend publication after revision to address
the points detailed below. 
Major comments: 
1. The paper explains how dust emission reduction over Middle East causes a positive anomaly in solar radiation reaching
to the western Indian ocean surface. However, it does not clearly discuss how this enhanced radiative energy translates into
a positive SST anomaly, considering the ocean’s large heat capacity. Please expand on this physical mechanism. Is the ~2-
month lag used in the analysis related to the ocean mixed-layer adjustment timescale? 
2. Please explain why there is a positive net LW flux at surface over the western Indian ocean in the case of dust deduction?
Moreover, for both SW and LW, why the flux anomaly over eastern Indian ocean is always opposite to the western part? 
Specific comments: 
3. Line 88, ‘reversing the regression coefficients to represent dust reduction’ is very confusing, I figured out its meaning when
I looked at the figure caption of Fig. S3, it means you take the negative values of the regression coefficient. Please modify
the sentence, and also clarify it in the caption of Fig.1. 
4. Fig S3, you mentioned ‘regression coefficient’ and ‘correlation coefficient’ in the figure caption, are they the same? Please
clarify what you used and keep them consistent. 
5. In Fig1 (e), please clarify that the grey dashed line is the mixed layer depth anomaly, right? It is not just mixed layer depth.
Also, please clarify the grey dashed line is corresponding to the y-axis on the right. Otherwise, it’s too confusing. 
6. Line 93, ‘0.5K per standard deviation of dust variability’, please clarify the meaning of this term. Authors use different dust
proxies, e.g., dust AOD, column mass density, and dust frequency, what are their standard deviation of dust variability,
respectively. 
7. Line 336, ‘suppress eastern upwelling’, should that be suppress eastern downwelling?

1. Dust variability can both drive and respond to IOD variability. However, this
bidirectional relationship does not yet appear to be well constrained in the authors’
analysis of dust–IOD interactions, partly because of substantial uncertainty in the
representation of dust emissions in the global climate model CESM v1.2 (Kok et al.,
2022; main text reference 18). Given this known bias, the authors should more clearly
justify their choice of CESM v1.2 with CAMS 4.3 for this study and explain how this
underestimate might affect their conclusions regarding dust–IOD interactions.
Reply: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment regarding the bidirectional
nature of dust-IOD interactions and the known uncertainties in modeled dust
emissions. We agree that this complexity requires careful consideration.
The core reason for our model choice is to isolate the climatic impact of dust without
the complication of dust emission response to climate. Therefore, we required a model
framework that allows dust aerosol concentrations to be prescribed, creating a
one-way forcing. Moreover, the prescribed dust field used in our study has been
validated by previous studies (Buchard et al., 2017; Che et al., 2022).
(1) Choice of CAM version: We selected the Community Atmosphere Model version
4.3 (CAM4.3) because it supports prescribed dust mixing ratios. This capability is
essential for our experimental design. In contrast, later versions (CAM5 and CAM6)
feature more complex aerosol-climate interactions where dust is computed online
based on evolving meteorological and surface conditions, making it difficult to
prescribe fixed values of dust loading.
(2) Choice of CESM version: Consequently, we used CESM v1.2, which is
compatible with CAM4.3. The newer CESM v2 is exclusively coupled with CAM6,
which employs the online dust scheme and the bidirectional dust-climate coupling we
sought to avoid.
The additional explanation has been incorporated into the supplementary information
of our revised manuscript for clarity. We also cited it here for convenience.
The atmospheric component of CESM, the Community Atmosphere Model version 4.3

Reply: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. We have removed
ENSO-related variability from the SST fields by linear regression (Fig. 1 and
Extended Figs 1–5 in the revised manuscript) prior to computing the IOD index, in
order to isolate the influence of ENSO on IOD variability.
Regarding the Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO), which is a dominant mode of
intra-seasonal (30–100 days) variability, we note that its behavior is primarily
modulated by the phase of the IOD rather than directly influencing the IOD itself
(Benedict et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2022). To minimize interference from
intra-seasonal climate signals, we employ a seasonal mean, which smooths out these
higher-frequency variations.
Concerning the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), we acknowledge its potential role

18. Line 267, the response of shortwave fluxes is mainly over the land. How does the

Reply: Thank you for raising this question. The signal propagates from the land to the
Tropical Indian Ocean (TIO) primarily through an atmospheric teleconnection. The
mechanism is a three-step process:
(1) Local Radiative Forcing & Atmospheric Adjustment: Reduced dust over the
Arabian Peninsula/northern Arabian Sea increases surface SW absorption, creating a
strong vertical heating gradient (surface warming, relative upper-level cooling;
Extended Data Figs. 11, 13a). This modifies local stability and circulation.
(2) Atmospheric Teleconnection & Downstream Cloud Response: The adjusted
circulation and zonal cell (Fig. 2d) act as a teleconnection bridge, transporting the
perturbation's influence to the western TIO. This alters the vertical thermodynamic
profile, driving a characteristic cloud reorganization there: decreased low-level and
increased high-level cloud cover (Fig. 3c, d). In the atmosphere-only (CAM) run, this
teleconnected response is limited by fixed SSTs, explaining the weaker direct
maritime signal.
(3) Amplification via Ocean-Atmosphere Coupling: This step explains the stronger
oceanic signal in the coupled model. The teleconnected cloud changes (Step 2) now
interact with the ocean. Reduced low clouds enhance surface SW, warming the SST.
This warming then triggers powerful positive feedbacks: the cloud–SST feedback
further suppressing low clouds (Lin et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Wyant et al., 1997),
the Bjerknes feedbacks and wind-evaporation-SST (WES) feedbacks. These coupled
feedbacks dramatically amplify and extend the initial atmospheric perturbation.

teleconnection. The coupling with the ocean is essential to transform this initial
adjustment into the stronger, basin-scale ocean-atmosphere feedbacks that effectively

influences the TIO.
