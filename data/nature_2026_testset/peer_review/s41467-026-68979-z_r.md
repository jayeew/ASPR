Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript „Protein C-Terminal Variations Broadly Impact Proteostasis” by Ching-Yu Chu et al investigates the
question, how often aberrant C-termini are linked to protein degradation and in which proteins mechanisms like disease-
associated nonstop mutations, alternative splicing, translational readthrough lead to protein stabilization. Oncoproteins and
tumor suppressor proteins have been identified in which disease-relevant nonstop mutations lead to changes in protein
stability. Like in other publications, the authors found that hydrophobic C-termini are targeted for degradation and the authors
have identified further E3s that are involved in this process. The manuscript uncovers some interesting connections between
variations in C-terminal composition of proteins and their stability. Thus, the manuscript is interesting enough to be
published, yet several points have to be addressed before publication. 
Major points: 
1. Page 3, line 13: I did not read anything about believes in the cited literature, I assume the authors wanted to write “were
shown to” or something like that. 
2. Page 3 line 13-17: The sentence However, whether Bag6-RNF126… is somehow misleading, as in the literature citation
“Mechanisms of readthrough mitigation reveal principles of GCN1-mediated translational quality control” by Müller et al
another complex (Bag6-RNF115) was shown to be involved in degradation of a protein with a hydrophobic C-terminus.
Furthermore, it was shown in budding yeast that the degradation of C-degrons with hydrophobic amino acids depends on
Doa10 (MARCH6 in humans) or other E3 (e.g. Orphan quality control by an SCF ubiquitin ligase directed to pervasive C-
degrons by Kong et al; Conserved degronome features governing quality control associated proteolysis by Mashahreh et al;
Degradation Signals for Ubiquitin-Proteasome Dependent Cytosolic Protein Quality Control (CytoQC) in Yeast by Maurer et
al), so it is not surprising that several E3s are involved in targeting degrons with hydrophobic amino acids in the C-terminus. 
Also, it has been shown by Hasenjäger et al (C-terminal sequence stability profiling in Saccharomyces cerevisiae reveals
protective protein quality control pathways) that C-terminal extension of proteins by translational stop codon read through
can generate stabilized or destabilized proteins, but that destabilization occurs more frequently. 
3. Page 3 line 17-18: The literature that was cited here for this sentence contains more than just a few examples.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Chu et al. expand our understanding on how C-terminal deviations impact protein stability. Using Global
Protein Stability (GPS) assay, the authors monitor protein half-lives by comparing the stability of GFP fused wild-type C-
termini to aberrant readthrough variants. This approach allows them to assess the physiologically relevant effect of
readthroughs on protein stability and, by extension, cellular abundance. While it was known that erroneous C-termini can
destabilize proteins, this study reveals that some disease associated nonstop mutations are more stable than their wild-type
counterparts. Beyond nonstop mutations, the authors also investigate the effects of alternative splicing, programed stop
codon readthrough, and commonly used C-terminal tags on protein stability. Although it remains unclear how many
erroneous C-termini containing proteins are recognized by the cell as abnormal and degraded, the authors successfully
identify two E3 ubiquitin ligases, RNF139 and MARCH6, that selectively recognize certain hydrophobic C-termini and
promote their degradation. Overall speaking, this reviewer finds the manuscript compelling, significant to the field and
related fields, and believes it is suitable for publication in Nature Communications once the Major and Minor points are
addressed. 
Major point: 
This reviewer notes a discrepancy in the length of the analyzed regions described in Figure 6 A, specifically, the use of the
last 50 residues for canonical proteins, the last 30 residues for RT proteins, and the last 20 residues for frameshift proteins.
As information clarifying the rationale regarding this analysis is missing in the materials and methods section, this reviewer
strongly encourages the authors to clarify and rectify this inconsistency. 
Minor points: 
1. Page 6 lines 11-14. Based on the data presented in the manuscript, this reviewer is convinced that some nonstop
mutations and translational readthroughs may enhance protein abundance due to a decrease in their degradation. However,
that would not be equivalent to “enhancing protein expression”. 
2. Page 7 lines 7-8. This reviewer would like the authors to clarify, in either the main text or in the materials and methods
section, how they determined that the viral transduction was single-copy. 
3. Page 9 lines 11-14. While there are many implications to the findings reported, this reviewer is not convinced that the data
presented in the manuscript is sufficient to directly support the claim that “nonstop mutations can substantially affect cellular
protein function”. 
4. Figure 3 B and C. This reviewer would find it easier to follow the figure if the panels in B were in the same order as the
sequences in C or vice versa. 
5. Figure 3 B. 
This reviewer had a difficult time distinguishing between the pink trace and the red trace (which is under the
blue trace) in AP1S2 panel. 
6. Figure 5. It is not clear to this reviewer what the percentages next to PC1 and PC2 represent. 
7. Figure S3 C legend. Typo “… in Figure 3C.” should be corrected to “… in Figure 3 D and E.” 
8. The RT sequences and the CDS sequences are misaligned in multiple figures. 
9. As the identity of the amino acid that replaces the stop codon is not important to the half-lives of the proteins, this reviewer
suggests the authors to simplify their figures. For example, in Figure 2 D “ZNF438 *>L” could just contain “ZNF438”. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary: 
This manuscript, "Protein C-Terminal Variations Broadly Impact Proteostasis," examines how changes in protein C-termini
—arising from nonstop mutations, alternative splicing, and translational readthrough—affect protein stability. The authors
use GPS-based screening, in combination with CRISPR knockouts and bioinformatic analyses, to explore how these C-
terminal extensions influence protein degradation. They report that while many extensions have little effect, some can lead
to stabilization or destabilization, and they identify sequence features—such as hydrophobicity and the presence of proline
or cysteine—associated with these outcomes. The study also attempts to link these effects to specific ubiquitin ligases. 
Overall assessment: 
This is a good study, based primarily on an established screening platform. However, many of the key observations—such
as the destabilizing role of hydrophobic residues, the involvement of CRL-type ubiquitin ligases, and the broader principle
that C-terminal sequences can encode degrons—have been reported previously. The inclusion of isoforms generated by

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

splicing and translational readthrough and the random peptide screen add some breadth, but the conceptual insights are
modest and largely descriptive. 
The GPS assay is inherently limited by its artificial nature: it relies on overexpression and fusion to a stabilizing protein, and
does not account for endogenous factors such as localization, complex formation, folding, or post-translational modifications.
Moreover, the study lacks biochemical validation or independent experimental confirmation. As a result, the conclusions
remain correlative and do not reach the level of mechanistic or physiological insight expected for publication in Nature
Communications. The manuscript may be more appropriate for a specialized journal. 
Major Comments: 
1. Limited conceptual novelty: Many central findings—such as the destabilizing role of hydrophobic residues at C-termini,
the function of E3 ubiquitin ligases, and the idea that C-terminal extensions can encode degrons—have been described in
previous literature. The current study largely confirms and modestly extends these observations, without revealing new and
solid mechanistic principles. 
2. Use of a single screening approach and lack of validation: The conclusions are derived almost entirely from the GPS
screen, which involves artificial overexpression and fusion to a stabilizing protein. This setup does not reflect physiological
conditions. No orthogonal validation (e.g., pulse-chase assays, degradation rescue, or western blotting) is presented. Thus,
the results remain observational and descriptive. 
3. Internal inconsistencies and unaddressed contradictions: The authors highlight hydrophobicity and the presence of
tryptophan or cysteine as correlates of protein stability. However, when attempting to verify these associations using the
authors' own data (as presented in Supplementary Table 1), clear inconsistencies emerge. For example, in cases where the
aberrant isoform contains more hydrophobic residues than the WT, the expected destabilization is observed (mean ΔPSI = –
1.84). In contrast, when hydrophobicity is higher in the WT and reduced in the aberrant isoform, the stabilization effect is
minor (mean ΔPSI ≈ 0.41)—well below the authors' own threshold for biological relevance (|ΔPSI| ≥ 1). Similar
inconsistencies are noted regarding the presence of cysteine and tryptophan, which are suggested to correlate with stability
but do not consistently show expected effects. These asymmetries are neither acknowledged nor discussed in the
manuscript and may suggest that other, more complex features of aberrant extensions influence stability beyond the
highlighted amino acid content. 
4. Overlooking a key (negative) finding: One of the most striking observations is that the majority of C-terminal extensions do
not significantly affect protein stability. This negative result is not discussed. Additionally, while the authors distinguish
between isoforms derived from different mechanisms (readthrough, splicing, nonstop mutations)—a potentially novel
classification—this distinction is treated descriptively. The manuscript does not explore whether these mechanisms differ in
their degron content, ligase dependencies, or stability profiles. 
5. Insufficient methodological detail and lack of validation in CRISPR screens: While the manuscript is relatively easy to
read, it lacks sufficient technical detail: definitions of degron features, hydrophobicity scoring, and amino acid enrichment
metrics are vague; PSI thresholds are used without statistical justification; bioinformatic identification of E3 ligases is
underexplained; figure legends lack clarity; and no validation is provided for the CRISPR knockouts. 
6. PCA results potentially biased by unequal sequence lengths: In Figure 6A, the PCA is based on sequences of differing
lengths (50 residues for WT, 30 for readthrough, and 20 for frameshifted isoforms). This difference could dominate the
observed clustering. Moreover, the PCA analysis is visually presented but not quantitatively supported. Figure S4 is
inconsistent with Figure 6 regarding the included sequences. 
7. Unclear analyses in Figure 7: Panels 7C and 7E–F are not adequately described in the text or methods section. Figure
7C lacks any actual information regarding what is the presented data or how the analysis was done. Panels 7E–F appear to
contradict earlier interpretations and reinforce the descriptive—and sometimes inconclusive—nature of the findings. The
rationale for these analyses is not clearly articulated.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the authors’ detailed responses to my comments and the additional data provided. However, after carefully
evaluating the revised manuscript and rebuttal, I must emphasize that my original concerns remain largely unresolved. 
Conceptual novelty: The central claims—that nonstop mutations can stabilize or destabilize proteins and that residue
clustering or enrichment patterns contribute to these effects—remain incremental extensions of prior work. As noted in my
initial review, the destabilizing role of hydrophobic residues, the involvement of CRL-type ligases, and the existence of C-
terminal degrons have all been well documented. The new analyses (e.g., cysteine/tryptophan enrichment, residue
clustering) add breadth but not fundamentally new mechanistic concepts. 
Assay limitations: The GPS system is still the backbone of the study. While the authors added cycloheximide-chase assays
and selected full-length constructs, the overwhelming majority of data remain based on GFP–peptide fusions in HEK293T
cells. These artificial assays cannot fully capture endogenous factors such as folding, localization, and complex formation,
and therefore the conclusions remain correlative. 
Mechanistic depth: Despite the authors explanations attempts in the rebuttable, in my view the manuscript still does not
convincingly resolve the inconsistencies in its own dataset, where proposed rules (hydrophobicity, Cys/Trp enrichment) are
not consistently predictive. While the authors acknowledge complexity, but the result is still primarily descriptive. 
Neutral majority: Despite the authors’ response, it remains clear that a large proportion of extensions have no significant
effect. This important negative result is not sufficiently discussed, and the emphasis continues to be on a minority of
destabilizing or stabilizing cases, potentially overstating generality. 
Comparison to prior work: In contrast, Ghosh et al. (Nat. Commun. 2024) validated nonstop mutations in endogenous
models (e.g., CRISPR-edited PTEN), provided mechanistic links to proteasomal degradation, and explored evolutionary
constraints on C-terminal amino acid usage. That study established mechanistic insight, whereas the present manuscript
remains largely a descriptive catalog of correlations. 
In summary, while the study is carefully executed and provides a valuable dataset, the rebuttal does not fully address the
issues of novelty, reliance mostly on artificial assays, and limited mechanistic depth. I therefore maintain my original
concerns.
