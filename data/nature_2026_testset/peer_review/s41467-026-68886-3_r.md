Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors compare the measurement error of typical T1 structural MRI scans to that of a cluster of
repeated short T1 scans known as cluster scanning. 
The authors assert that cluster scanning significantly reduces
measurement error by densely repeating rapid structural scans. Change in brain structures was assessed in 38 individuals
(6 young, 18 cognitively unimpaired older adults, 9 MCI and AD, and 5 FTLD) at three time points with two sessions on
nearby days at each time point. At each time point, participants received one standard T1 MRI scan and eight rapid scans.
The test and retest scans were analyzed to compare measurement error between standard and rapid scans. Individual
trajectories during the one year long study were compared to normative trajectories derived from the longitudinal UK biobank
sample. The authors concluded that the use of multiple rapid scans reduced the measurement error to an extent that would
enable longitudinal MRI trajectories to be assessed at an individual level. 
This well-designed study demonstrates that cluster scanning can significantly reduce measurement error in small structures
such as the hippocampus allowing brain aging trajectories to be mapped at an individual level over shorter longitudinal time
frames.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The revised manuscript presents a novel approach to measuring individual brain aging trajectories over relatively short
intervals (one year) using cluster scanning. Specifically, repeated rapid MRI acquisitions at each timepoint are leveraged to
reduce within-individual measurement error. The study includes 38 participants across several groups (young adults, older
adults, MCI-AD, and FTLD) and examines psychometric properties, group trends, and idiosyncratic individual trajectories.
Overall, the method is highly innovative and holds promise as a new avenue for detecting individual differences in aging
trajectories, addressing important limitations of cross-sectional estimates and conventional longitudinal assessments. I
believe this study — along with the ongoing work of this group — represents a meaningful and valuable contribution to the
field. Below, I outline my main concerns and suggestions (in no particular order): 
1. Measurement error: Have the authors examined the degree of measurement error before repositioning, within the same
session after repositioning, and in a session conducted after several days? The former comparisons are critical to
understanding the practical limits of this technique – after all, the number of visits individuals have throughout a year
represents a soft-limit - , while the latter is important to rule out possible dependencies between test-retest atrophy estimates.
2. Focus on hippocampus: The authors understandably focus on the hippocampus, where they demonstrate substantial
benefits of cluster scanning. However, the benefits appear more modest for other measures (e.g., AD signature regions,
WMH, ventricles). Could the authors speculate on why the gains are smaller for these structures? Should cluster scanning
be prioritized only for specific regions? Should other cluster scanning designs be used when focusing on other structures? 
3. Choice of ICC: Why did the authors choose ICC(3,1) rather than ICC(2,1)? Given that absolute agreement is often of
interest in clinical and aging studies, some justification for this choice would be helpful. 
4. Idiosyncratic trajectories with traditional scans: The authors highlight idiosyncratic individual trajectories and link them to
specific maintenance or decline profiles. Could the authors also show (perhaps in the Supplementary Information) whether
these idiosyncratic patterns are detectable using atrophy estimates derived from traditional T1-weighted scans alone? Since
a key point of this study is the superiority of cluster scanning, this comparison would be highly informative. 
5. Predictive value of variability /Clinical validation: While the individual case trajectories illustrate the validity and promise of
the technique, one might argue that in these extreme cases, traditional longitudinal scanning/neuropsychological
assessments might already capture such changes. Could the authors explore whether variability in hippocampal atrophy (or
other measures) is predictive of subsequent outcomes in the remaining participants — for example, further atrophy or
cognitive decline? Demonstrating prospective predictive value, would be particularly compelling and showcase the unique
clinical utility of the approach. In a way, one wants to show that relatively minor changes have some validity rather than
reflecting uninteresting events or time-constrained changes. 
6. Estimates for older adults without impairment: In the Results section (p. 3–4), could the authors provide explicit estimates
of change for the cognitively unimpaired older adult group? Unless I missed this, it appears to be the only group for which
detailed change estimates are not reported.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This article presents a novel brain imaging approach, termed cluster scanning, which enhances sensitivity to short-term
brain changes by reducing measurement error. This technical advancement holds significant potential, with wide-ranging
implications in the field of brain ageing and beyond. The manuscript is engagingly written, with a clear and precise
presentation of results, supported by well-chosen examples. Overall, the rationale behind the methods and the findings
presented by the authors are well-argued, making the manuscript accessible to read. However, there are several aspects
that could be improved, as detailed below: 
1. The overall aesthetics and clarity of the figures are excellent. However, I encourage the authors to reconsider the red–
green colour combination used in Figures 3A and 4, as this may present challenges for readers with colour vision
deficiencies. 
2. While the technical advantages of cluster scanning in the context of ageing research are evident, I am less convinced of
the relevance of the individual case reports included in the Results section titled “Unexpected idiosyncratic trajectories of
brain aging.” Although these cases are acknowledged to be atypical, similar findings have already been reported at the
group level within established frameworks such as cognitive reserve, brain resilience, or brain maintenance. It is therefore
unclear why the authors consider these individual cases to be of particular value to the readership of Nature
Communications. 
3. The authors point out the potential benefits of cluster scanning for people who are cognitively unimpaired. However, it
would be helpful if they could explain more clearly how they see this experimental MRI protocol being used in practice. Do
they think its main value lies in clinical settings for looking at individual cases, or is it more suited to research studies
involving large groups?

Thank you for giving us the opportunity to clarify. You are correct to point out group-
level studies that have found evidence of cognitive reserve, brain resilience, and 
maintenance. In fact, our current work was, in part, inspired by this work. The 
limitations of these traditional group studies are that while they imply the existence of 
these exceptional cases, they cannot detect these unexpected trajectories of brain 
aging in individuals over short time intervals. We have now added supplemental 
figure S10  (included below for your reference) to highlight the clear advantage of 
cluster scanning and standard approaches for identifying deviant aging trajectories

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 14 -->

in individuals. As this figure demonstrates, standard approaches find it challenging to 
confidently identify typical and atypical trajectories in individuals over short intervals 
because the confidence intervals in most individuals are large and span from a 
highly accelerated decline on one end to a rapidly growing brain on the other. 
Furthermore, in Figures 5-8, we visualized the independent measurements from the 
test (red) and retest (blue) sessions to show the robustness and repeatability of 
these unexpected results within individuals.  
 
More broadly, our individual case reports are meant to illustrate this potential and 
provide concrete cases of unexpected trajectories even in a relatively small sample. 
They are meant as thought-raising case studies and a way of showing what is now 
possible. We highlight these implications in the following two paragraphs of our 
discussion: 
 
“These promising foundations inspired us to explore approaches to measure 
brain change in individuals over short intervals. The advent of rapid scanning 
techniques that allow for dense, repeat sampling provided the key innovation to 
achieve precision measurement (Mair et al., 2020; Hanford et al., 2021; Elliott et 
al., 2023; 2024). The present findings, which used these techniques to estimate 
brain aging, are consistent with general expectations from group studies but also 
highlight idiosyncratic brain aging trajectories, including atypical patterns in 
unimpaired individuals, that would have been attributed to measurement error in 
prior work. 
Specifically, we observed unimpaired individuals with rapid global decline (Figure 
6), asymmetric decline (Figure 7), and notable preservation (Figure 8). These

findings raise the possibility that standard longitudinal structural imaging may 
have underestimated the true degree of individual differences in brain aging 
trajectories that are present in the population and can be harnessed for 
predicting cognitive decline and maintenance (Di Biase et al., 2023). We 
demonstrate that it is possible to detect unexpected brain aging trajectories in 
individuals in a year. Future research with larger cohorts will be needed to 
describe the prevalence of these atypical brain aging trajectories, their stability 
over time, and their consequences for cognition. For example, there is ongoing 
research into the nature of brain maintenance and its role in cognitive aging and 
resilience to cognitive impairment (Cook et al., 2017; Katsumi et al., 2022; 
Kremen et al., 2022; Nilsson & Lövdén, 2018; Nyberg et al., 2012; Stern et al., 
2020). Cluster scanning is a promising approach to accelerate this research.”

 Thank you for providing us with an opportunity to elaborate on these future 
implications of our research. We’ve added a new paragraph to our discussion to explain 
further. 
“Cluster scanning offers a flexible tool with implications for future research. Cluster 
scanning can immediately reduce participant burden, accelerate longitudinal studies, 
and boost sensitivity to individual differences to a deeper understanding of the true 
heterogeneity and nature of brain development and aging. Over the long term,
