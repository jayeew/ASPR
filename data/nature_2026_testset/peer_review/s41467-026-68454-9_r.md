Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript “Af-CUT&Tag: A Sensitive and Antibody-Free Chromatin Profiling Method Using Genetically Encoded Tag
and High-Affinity Binder-Tn5”. Xindong et al. developed Af-CUT&Tag to profile chromatin-associated proteins by modifying
CUT&Tag. Instead of using antibodies to tether Tn5 transposase to a protein of interest, Af-CUT&Tag employs CRISPR-
mediated knock-in of peptide tags (e.g., HiBiT, ALFA-tag) at the protein terminus. Tn5, fused to binders or nanobodies, binds
these tags, enabling targeted chromatin profiling. Single-cell Af-CUT&Tag was also developed to extend this approach. The
authors applied Af-CUT&Tag to profile RNA Polymerase, CTCF, YAP1, and TAZ1 in both cell lines and tissues. In pre- and
post-partial hepatectomy, they observed dynamic YAP/TAZ1 binding changes at genes associated with regeneration and
fatty acid metabolism. Notably, they identified a strong increase in YAP/TAZ1 binding at Mir122, correlating with its
upregulation after hepatectomy, and demonstrated that Mir122 plays a crucial role in liver regeneration. This antibody-free
Af-CUT&Tag method has potential applications where antibodies are unavailable for CUT&Tag. However, technical
concerns must be addressed to confirm that it profiles protein binding rather than chromatin accessibility. That is, in the
abstract, the authors claim that their method provides “chromatin accessibility analysis”. However, examination of their
results suggests that what they are profiling is not directed specifically towards the intended target, but rather to open
chromatin regions genome-wide, an artifact. Additionally, more appropriate experiments and data analysis are needed to
ensure that the conclusions are well-supported and not misleading. 
Major Concerns: 
(1) The key strength of Af-CUT&Tag should be profiling proteins of interest on chromatin, not mapping chromatin
accessibility. Therefore, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis to confirm that this method effectively profiles
proteins on chromatin, specifically: 1) whether LgBiT-Tn5 or NbALFA-Tn5 binding to POI-HiBiT or POI-ALFA is sufficiently
stable to tether Tn5 to POI-bound regions under Af-CUT&Tag buffer conditions, and 2) whether Af-CUT&Tag signals
represent specific binding rather than accessibility signals. 
PMID: 31036827 showed that although CTCF CUT&Tag primarily maps to CTCF motifs, but un-tethered Protein A-Tn5 can
also contribute to CTCF coverage at accessible DNA unless suppressed. By analyzing the CTCF Af-CUT&Tag and ATAC-
seq data from this study on GEO, I found them to be very similar (attachment). This raises a major concern about whether Af-
CUT&Tag signals primarily reflect chromatin accessibility rather than specific protein binding, as well as the reliability of the
CUT&Tag data generated in this study. If what the authors are measuring is actually open chromatin, then there is no
distinction between where CTCF and other TFs are binding as opposed to where they are not present but are reported to be
present because of artifactual binding to open chromatin. 
To address concerns regarding chromatin accessibility, the authors should assess whether CTCF Af-CUT&Tag and single-
cell CTCF Af-CUT&Tag signals are primarily enriched at CTCF motifs to validate their method. Additionally, a genome-wide
quantitative analysis comparing CTCF, YAP1, and TAZ1 Af-CUT&Tag profiles in tagged and untagged cells, as well as with
ATAC-seq, would provide further clarity.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Wang et al. introduce Af-CUT&Tag, an antibody-free method for profiling chromatin-associated proteins
using genetically encoded peptide tags. The authors tag several chromatin-associated factors (RNA Pol II, CTCF, YAP, and
TAZ) with small peptide tags (ALFA-tag and HiBiT) and use nanobody-Tn5 fusion proteins to selectively target the tagged
proteins. This approach is validated in both cultured cells and mouse liver tissue, demonstrating its applicability across
different biological systems. The study is technically rigorous, and the results are clearly presented. 
However, I have several critical points: 
1. While Af-CUT&Tag is convincingly shown to work, it represents an incremental improvement over existing technologies. It
does not enable fundamentally new types of experiments beyond what is already achievable with CUT&Tag, nano-
CUT&Tag, or ChIP-seq. The main benefit is circumvention of antibody limitations, which—although useful—is not
transformative for most applications. 
2. A major drawback of the method is its reliance on genome editing to insert peptide tags. This restricts its use to systems
amenable to genetic modification, which excludes many primary tissues, patient-derived samples, or developmental stages.
Furthermore, the method is not compatible with detecting post-translational modifications (e.g., histone marks), which is a
major use case for antibody-based methods. 
3. The second part of the manuscript applies Af-CUT&Tag to identify YAP/TAZ binding sites in mouse liver. While the
application is interesting, the transition from method development to biological discovery feels abrupt. The manuscript would
benefit from a clearer narrative thread connecting the two parts, possibly by framing the biological question earlier and
showing how the method uniquely enables this analysis. 
Overall, this is a technically sound and interesting study describing a novel approach that is likely to be of value to the
chromatin profiling field. However, given the incremental nature of the advance and the limitations discussed above, I would
recommend that the manuscript be considered for publication in a more specialized journal. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present Af-CUT&Tag, a novel variation of existing CUT&Tag technologies. CUT&Tag is a transposase-based
method designed to precisely map protein-DNA interactions or chromatin modifications in cells. Unlike traditional
approaches such as CUT&Tag and Nano-CUT&Tag, which rely on primary antibodies to recognize the DNA-binding target
protein or chromatin modification, Af-CUT&Tag eliminates the need for primary antibodies by utilizing modified cell lines or
transgenic mice expressing the DNA-binding target proteins fused to small peptide tags. These tags are then specifically
recognized by high-affinity binders fused to Tn5, which in turn introduces DNA sequences that can be used for NGS-based
identification of the respective target sites. 
While Af-CUT&Tag may in fact represent a potentially valuable addition to the CUT&Tag toolbox — particularly in cases
requiring high spatial resolution or where suitable antibodies are unavailable — the reviewer finds the data presentation and
– at least in part – the data interpretation highly problematic. At least in the current state, the reviewer believes that the
manuscript is not suited for the general scientific readership of Nature Communications.

General Concerns: 
1. Although the authors compare Af-CUT&Tag with existing technologies such as CUT&Tag and Nano-CUT&Tag, they fail

2. Abbreviations are not consistently explained throughout the manuscript. 
3. The manuscript includes an overwhelming 85 (!) display items, distributed across six main figures and eight extended

captions. 
4. The figure legends generally lack sufficient details required for readers to understand the experiments and interpret the
results. 
5. The authors aim to demonstrate two independent proof-of-concept adaptor systems: HiBiT/LgBiT and ALFA-tag/NbALFA.
While they successfully expressed and purified LgBiT-Tn5 in soluble form using a standard E. coli expression system, they
were unable to establish suitable conditions for NbALFA-Tn5 expression. As a result, as stated in the text, the final NbALFA-
Tn5 preparation exhibited low solubility and suffered from poor yield and purity. It would be important to show data on the

Instead, the method is portrayed as a quick and simple alternative, which is at least misleading.

specifically addressed – especially since the authors highlight the narrower distribution in Fig. 2B/C as a major advantage.
Have alternative explanations, such as poor antibody quality, been considered? 
10. Figure clarity: Several figures fail to convey what they are intended to show. For example: 
• Fig. 1G: Tagmented DNA is barely visible (possibly due to the loading dye used) 
• Suppl. Fig. 2C: Tagmented DNA is not visible at all. 
• Fig. 6G (left): The figure is unclear, and the legend does not clarify what the reader is expected to see. 
11. Some primary data is inconsistent with the reported numerical values. For instance, in Figure 1D, the dissociation curves

expected for nanobody/target interactions in general and particularly in contrast to the well-characterized NbALFA/ALFA-tag
pair. 
12. The Methods section should clearly describe what was done, yet it often reads like a step-by-step lab protocol, rather
than a concise account of the procedures actually performed. 
13. The description of experimental results is often erratic, and in some cases, conclusions are stated before the relevant
data is presented. 
14. Overall, the manuscript is exceedingly difficult to read and understand. In addition to the fact that the phrasing and
language are often not scientifically clear and concise, this is a result of a combination of surprising discrepancies: 
a. Too many unreflected details but too little concise information 
b. Too many display items with minimal impact (e.g. large panels with sequencin results) while important data is missing
(e.g. purity of Tn5 fusions)

Discussion: 
While the discussion focuses on the advantages of Af-CUT&Tag — such as independence from antibody specificity — its
limitations should also be acknowledged. For instance, Af-CUT&Tag cannot distinguish between different posttranslational

Depending on the scientific question, this might be a rather significant drawback. A more balanced discussion would
strengthen the manuscript. 
The section from line 467 onward, focusing on AI-generated binders, seems completely out of place: 
First, AI-designed high-affinity binders are not yet widely available nor are they generally validated. The text implies
otherwise, which is misleading. Second, the authors aim to promote Af-CUT&Tag as a method that bypasses target-specific
binders. Why then pivot to advocating AI-designed target-specific binders?

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have provided new experiments and analyses to address most of reviewer comments, strengthening the
manuscript in the process. They optimized their af-CUT&Tag protocol by reducing antibody concentration and incubation
duration and increasing the number of wash steps to reduce accessibility artifacts. They showed that the optimized CTCF af-
CUT&Tag profiles are distinguished from ATAC-seq profiles and that CTCF read count distributions at ATAC-seq–defined
accessible regions are order of magnitude lower than ATAC-seq and detected the CTCF motif. However, there are
remaining concerns that the authors should address: 
1) The authors' response regarding the potential effect of the HiBiT knock-in on chromatin profiles in major concern #2 is
insufficient. A valid performance assessment requires a direct comparison of datasets profiling the same epitope under
identical salt conditions. The data in Figure S3A suggest a profile alteration. The WT RPB1 CUT&Tag showed both TSS
and gene body signals with TSS signals were higher as we expected, however, the RPB1 af-CUT&Tag profile showed
enhanced TSS signals but lost most of the gene body signals. This discrepancy could indicate a genuine knock-in effect or a
salt artifact. Specifically, if Tn5 was incubated at 150mM salt for af-CUT&Tag but 300mM for CUT&Tag, the lower salt would
artificially inflate TSS coverage. 
2) In response to minor concern #12, the authors attribute the reduced YAP1/TAZ signal in pHx-post conditions to a true
biological decrease. However, the af-CUT&Tag signal shows a much milder reduction than the nano-CUT&Tag signal
(Extended Data Fig. 6B). This inconsistency again raises the possibility of either a tagging effect or a technical discrepancy
in salt conditions. To address this uncertainty, the authors should compare the profiles using identical salt conditions and
provide their detailed CUT&Tag protocol in the method section. This will confirm whether the observed difference is a salt
artifact or a genuine effect of the knock-in. Finally, the Fig. 3A browser track and the comparison of CTCF profiles at TSSs
(Fig. 3B, lines 221-224) are not appropriate to support the claim that tagging does not affect DNA binding. As already
suggested, it would be more informative if the Fig. 3B genome-wide comparison can be performed at high-confidence CTCF
binding sites, such as those enriched for the canonical motif. 
3) In response to major concern #2, the authors confirm in their revision that their CUT&Tag and nano-CUT&Tag
experiments profile elongating RNAPII-S2P, while the af-CUT&Tag data represents total RNAPII. It is important to note that
elongating RNAPII-S2P inherently exhibits lower signal at TSSs compared to total RNAPII, as S2P phosphorylation
promotes RNAPII release from the TSS into gene bodies. Therefore, quantifying RNAPII-S2P (CUT&Tag/nano-CUT&Tag)
against total RNAPII (af-CUT&Tag) at the TSS does not support claims of superiority. The authors should remove all
"superior", "outperforms” or similar claims derived from this comparison in the abstract, main text, and elsewhere. 
4) In the abstract, the authors claim that "this method outperforms in promoter enrichment." The basis for this claim is unclear
and should be removed. 
5) In the abstract, the authors describe af-CUT&Tag as "a transformative tool for studying gene regulation in development,
disease, and regeneration." The term "transformative" is an overstatement. However, a major limitation of af-CUT&Tag is its
reliance on genetic engineering to introduce tags. This inherently restricts its application in primary samples, patient tissues,
and other contexts where genetic manipulation is not feasible.
