Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Z. Guguchia and co-workers explore tunable superconductivity in the 3D Kagome lattice compound CeRu2
using muon spin rotation (µSR), uniaxial stress, and hydrostatic pressure. Uniaxial stress applied along the (111) Kagome
plane induces a dome-shaped variation in Tc: initially stable, then enhanced, and finally suppressed, without structural
transitions. Stress also drives a crossover from anisotropic to isotropic s-wave pairing. In contrast, hydrostatic pressure
leaves Tc largely unchanged but alters the superfluid density from exponential to linear temperature dependence,
suggesting pressure-induced nodal superconductivity. These findings reveal CeRu2 as a unique platform for geometry- and
correlation-driven tunability of unconventional superconductivity. The methodology is sound, and the conclusions are
generally well supported by experimental results. I recommend acceptance of the manuscript after minor revisions. 
1. The authors report the growth of CeRu2 single crystals but does not provide sufficient characterization data. To establish
the quality and phase purity of the grown crystals, the authors should include X-ray diffraction (XRD), energy-dispersive X-
ray spectroscopy (EDAX), and Laue diffraction patterns. These are essential to confirm crystallographic orientation,
stoichiometry, and absence of secondary phases, thereby ensuring that the subsequent superconducting and µSR
measurements are based on well-characterized single crystals. 
2. How does the interplay of Ru-derived and Ce-derived flat bands specifically influence the stress-induced modulation of
Tc? 
3. Why does uniaxial stress lead to isotropic s-wave pairing, while hydrostatic pressure induces nodal behavior? 
4. Could the 16% suppression of Tc at high stress be quantitatively linked to shifts in the density of states near the Fermi
level?

(Remarks to the Author)

the publication to Communications Physics article after the authors have accounted for the following points and address
them satisfactorily:

presented in the manuscript only extend to 1.9 GPa. This discrepancy should be clarified. 
2) It should be clarified whether the magnetic or electronic anomaly at T* coincides with a possible coexisting phase as
discussed in Ref. [14].

corrected or calibrated by comparison?

7) Regarding the superconducting gap analysis, is the difference in fitting quality between isotropic s-wave and anisotropic

Our response: Stating just estimates, the bandwidth of the Ru 4d dominated band is of the order of a few eV, while its flat section hybridized with the Ce 4f can be a factor of up to 10 smaller. 3 
Our response: Naively, the notion of an anisotropic perturbation enhancing the isotropic nature of an s-wave superconductor indeed sounds counterintuitive. The key perspective to appreciate such as phenomenon is to reconcile the phenomenology from unconventional s-wave pairing. There, the harmonic composition of the s-wave gap is not dominated by an isotropic constant particle-particle wave function, but a complex superposition of a series of k-dependent harmonic contributions to the superconducting gap. In turn, even a moderate stress perturbation may affect the synopsis of competing electronic ordering propensities such that the resulting superconductor might adopt a harmonic composition of the anomalous propagator (i.e. particle-particle wave function) that can reach higher or lower anisotropy. For CeRu2, a tangible scenario we imagine for the data at hand is that of starting from a decently anisotropic unconventional s-wave state. Upon addition of uniaxial stress, the competing subleasing ordering propensities might be impacted such that the preferred harmonic composition of the extended s-wave gap brings about a reduced difference between the maximum and minimal gap on the deformed Fermi surface, and hence a reduced gap anisotropy. Detailed future studies from functional renormalisation group or random phase approximation might be able to shed further light on this issue. For hydrostatic pressure, starting again from a moderately anisotropic extended s-wave gap, we envision the hydrostatic pressure to trigger the enhancement of anisotropy to a point that the extended s-wave gap reaches accidental nodes, as has been previously observes in many phosphorous-based pnictide superconductors.  As also insinuated by the referee, however, we do agree that the scenario sketched above, while plausible, would certainly need to be substantiated by further future experimental studies and theoretical modelling.   1.5 Reviewer’s comment: Could the 16% suppression of Tc at high stress be quantitatively linked to shifts in the density of states near the Fermi level? Our response: In CeRu2, the situation is complicated by the presence of multiple flat-band features revealed by ARPES measurements. These include the Kondo resonance (4f5/2) at the Fermi level, its spin–orbit sideband (4f7/2) at −0.25 eV, and a Ru-derived flat band originating from destructive interference in the kagome pyrochlore lattice at −0.11 eV. The proximity of these features implies that a significant density of states lies close to the Fermi level. Under suitable in-plane stress, the Ru-derived flat band and its associated DOS peak may be shifted toward the Fermi level, slightly enhancing superconductivity. Beyond a critical stress, however, this band may overshoot the Fermi level, shifting the DOS away and suppressing Tc. Whether the observed ∼16% reduction in Tc is driven primarily by the Ru flat band or by cooperative effects involving both Ce- and Ru-derived states remains an open theoretical question. While stress-induced band broadening is unlikely to be a leading effect-given that the Ru-4d bandwidth is on the order of eV, whereas the stress energy scale is only a few meV-the applied uniaxial stress is comparable to the Ce-4f hybridization scale. As a result, it can modify low-energy electronic correlations and superconducting properties, consistent with our experimental observations. We discuss these effects in the revised manuscript.

Specifically, Figs. 3a and 4a present ambient-pressure data measured using two independent μSR instruments, in agreement with our previous μSR report. In both cases, the superfluid density saturates only below approximately 1.2 K. In contrast, for measurements performed under 0.07 GPa and higher uniaxial stress values, saturation sets in already below about 2.3 K. This pronounced extension of the saturation regime provides direct, model-independent evidence for a more isotropic superconducting gap under pressure. Consequently, the isotropic s-wave model provides an excellent description of the high-pressure data, whereas at ambient pressure an anisotropic s-wave model offers a superior fit. This distinction is further supported quantitatively by a comparison of the χ2 values, which are approximately 15% lower for the anisotropic s-wave model than for the isotropic s-wave model at ambient pressure. Moreover, the ratio between the minimum and maximum superconducting gaps obtained from the ambient-pressure fit is 0.41, in good agreement with values reported by NMR and photoemission studies (0.33 and 0.45, respectively). Importantly, however, the central conclusion—the pressure-induced crossover toward an isotropic nodeless superconducting state—is already clearly visible even without invoking any specific gap model. We have added additional clarification of this point in the revised manuscript.  3.9 Reviewer’s comment: The uniaxial-stress-induced crossover from anisotropic to isotropic s-wave pairing is intriguing. As also insinuated by the referee, however, we do agree that the scenario sketched above, while plausible, would certainly need to be substantiated by further future experimental studies and theoretical modelling.  3.10 Reviewer’s comment: The identification of a transition from nodeless to nodal superconductivity under hydrostatic pressure is primarily based on the linear temperature dependence of the superfluid density at low temperatures. Our response: The conclusion of nodal superconductivity under pressure is based on the observed linear temperature dependence of the superfluid density. Even without invoking any specific gap model, there is a clear qualitative distinction between the low-pressure data-where the superfluid density saturates at low temperatures-and the high-pressure data, which exhibit a continuous increase in the superfluid density down to 300 mK. The absence of any tendency toward saturation at low temperatures rules out a fully gapped two-gap scenario. In addition, the diamagnetic shift shows qualitatively different behavior at 1.9 GPa, most notably the emergence of a paramagnetic upturn below approximately 1.5 K. Similar paramagnetic shifts have been reported in cuprate and iron-based high-temperature superconductors and have been interpreted as signatures of field-induced magnetism or, more generally, as manifestations of unconventional pairing symmetry. In the present case, no magnetic order is detected in CeRu2 within the investigated pressure range. Therefore, the observed paramagnetic shift below 1.5 K, together with the emergence of nodal behavior, is most likely associated with intrinsic modifications of the superconducting state. μSR provides a direct, bulk-sensitive measurement of the superfluid density and, when combined with unique high-pressure techniques, enables us to draw these conclusions and identify nodal pairing under pressure. Other gap-sensitive probes, such as scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), cannot be performed under hydrostatic pressure. At present, μSR therefore offers the only direct experimental access to the superconducting gap structure in the high-pressure state.

Consequently, the isotropic s-wave model provides an excellent description of the high-pressure data, whereas at ambient pressure an anisotropic s-wave model offers a superior fit. This distinction is further supported quantitatively by a comparison of the χ2 values, which are approximately 15% lower for the anisotropic s-wave model than for the isotropic s-wave model at ambient pressure. Moreover, the ratio between the minimum and maximum superconducting gaps obtained from the ambient-pressure fit is 0.41, in good agreement with values reported by NMR and photoemission studies (0.33 and 0.45, respectively).
