Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a well-written paper on stroke risk in relation to heat. The paper includes analyses of large data resources from
China, and makes a link with actions based on risk. 
Essentially 2 different issues are presented: risk estimation with a new model (Stroke Heat Risk Grading Prediction Model vs
China Meteorological Administration policy); and health benefits from interventions based on risk. 
Comments: 
1. risk estimation 
The data used for analyses seem to be representative; and are large in number. So, reliable modeling can be done. The
modeling as such as rather simple and straightforward. Four risk levels are defined (low risk, moderate risk, high risk, and
extremely high risk). 
a) It is unclear what is meant with “grading strategies for the Stroke Heat Risk Grading Prediction Model were validated
based on their sensitivity”. A continuous risk score is categorized it seems; in principle a waste of information; perhaps
justified by practical applicability. But not by "sensitivity". If anything, the link with treatment / intervention decisions should
be leading: when is the risk sufficiently high for action? This is a basis decision-analytic principle, related to cost-
effectiveness. This perspective is lacking it seems. 
b) The Meteorological uses overall data, while the proposed Risk Grading Prediction Model uses information at the
individual level. This may be considered an unfair comparison. The framing might be better as: what does individual level
information, such as age and sex, add to temperature data? 
2. health benefits 
The health benefits are not from an empirical study, but from a modeling exercise it seems? This is a severe limitation; there
is only modeling support come for the conclusion on utility of the new model. 
Thank you for the opportunity to review. Ewout Steyerberg, Netherlands 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study aims to establish the stroke health risk grading prediction model. It is an interesting and ambitious work. However,
I have great concerns about the methodology. 
1. I am concerned about two different methods (time-series and case-crossover) used in 2013-2018, and 2019-2022,
separately. It is better to use the same strategy (one or both two methods used in both periods). 
2. The authors used the existing heatwave warning grading system by CMA as another intervention scenario. But this study
is about the hot temperature, but the CMA warning grading system is specific for defining the heatwave. I am worried about
the comparability. 
3. I am confused about the training and evaluation sets used in this study. In my experience, we used the training data to
establish the predicted model, and then used the evaluation sets to test and update the model. But here, the authors used
only information from the exposure-response association between temperature-mortality from the training sets to the

evaluation sets. There is not testing or updating process for the predicted model. Therefore, it may be better to use the whole
data but not to split it into two parts. 
4. For the “4.5.3 Evaluation of health benefits”, only the attributable number (or attributable fraction) of heat-related deaths
was calculated based on the OR. Here, it should not be interpreted as “predict”, which could easily cause misunderstanding.
5. Exposure-response curves for temperature and mortality are the first step for the stroke health risk grading prediction
model. I would suggest adding these important figures to supplemental materials. 
6. Some key information is unclear. 
1) Line 336, "we conducted a derivative analysis of", the "derivative analysis" is unclear here. 
2) Line 339, “Three distinct risk grading strategies were then employed”. It is unclear how did these three distinct strategies
were decided. And what is the rationale behind? 
3) Line 349, how do you define the “sensitivity” here?

categories; what is the rationale behind?

rationale behind?

Following standard methods established in previous research, heat health risk studies 
are almost all conducted during high-temperature periods (i.e., the summer months), a 
methodological framework long established in this field. Researchers have consistently 
used summer -month data for heat health risk assessments 23-26. This methodological

health risks. Our study collected mortality data, meteorological data, and air pollution

Mortality data  
Mortality data, classified according to the Internation al Classification of Diseases, 
10th Revision (ICD -10), were sourced from the China Cause -of-Death Surveillance 
dataset, maintained by the Chronic Non -Communicable Diseases Prevention and 
Control Center of the China Center for Disease Control and Prevention . To ensure 
data quality, counties with a mortality rate exceeding 5‰ were selected, resulting in
