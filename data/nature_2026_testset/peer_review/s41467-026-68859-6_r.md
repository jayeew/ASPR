Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript of Lai et al. describes an exciting study on the biology of the hyperthermophilic archaeon Calditenius
ramacidaminiphagus. Using a combination of FISH-nanoSIMS, genome and metaproteome analysis, the authors were able
to show that this organism oxidizes (and assimilates) branched chain amino acids. This helped them to establish an
enrichment culture of these archaea, deepening our understanding of their biology. In doing so, the authors addressed the
major problem in microbiology that the vast majority of microorganisms on Earth cannot be cultured, and provided an
excellent example of how this problem can be addressed experimentally. I like this manuscript and see only a few minor
issues that need to be addressed before this paper can be published. 
The main question is that I do not understand why the authors talk about the oxidative Stickland reaction here. The Stickland
reaction involves the conversion of two organic compounds, one oxidised and one reduced. This is not the case here and I
would rather say that Calditenus carries out a partial oxidation of BCAA under aerobic conditions, i.e. the process is similar
to the metabolism of e.g. acetic acid bacteria. In fact, the data could also be interpreted as an example of aerobic oxidation of
BCAA, accomoponied by overflow metabolism. 
l. 382-383: I appreciate the citation of the original literature. However, I am not sure that the 1934 reference is appropriate to
discuss the distribution of the oxidative Stickland reaction in archaea (discovered first in the 1970s). 
The bioinformatic identification of the BCAA and PAA transporters is important for this study. Please indicate the degree of
similarity of the identified transporters to those biochemically characterised.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Lai et al reports on the genome-informed enrichment and metabolic characterization of a novel member of the
lineage previously described as Aigarchaeota, currently classified as Nitrososphaeria_A in the GTDB, also known as
Caldarchaeia. The authors managed to enrich members of the studied lineage from environmental samples of sediments of
a geothermal spring in the US, and maintain stable enrichments of the desired microorganism, named provisionally
Candidatus Calditenuis ramacidaminiphagus, over years. Although not particularly high, these were adequately enriched to
allow investigations into their metabolism through combined FISH-nanoSIMs methods, as well as metagenomics and
metaproteomics. In this way, the authors were able to prove that Candidatus Cal. Ramacidaminiphagus utilises branched
chain amino acids (BCAAs) as a source of carbon and energy, and reconstruct the metabolic pathway of their utilization.
Moreover, they perform evolutionary analyses and ancestral reconstructions to identify the evolution of this type of
metabolism within the lineage. 
The study is well-designed and executed, and the methodology used appropriate. It is a great example of utilizing state of
the art methods to enhance our understanding of lineages that are otherwise doomed to remain poorly characterized due to
absence of pure cultures, and illustrating that a lot can be learned even with enrichments. A major comment concerns the
evolutionary scenario presented. I have some reservations as to whether transporter complexes with multiple genes are
appropriate for this type of analysis, and whether it would be more informative to look at the evolutionary history of other key
enzymes in the pathway as well. Especially since the result shows that the BCAA transporters were acquired at the
ancestral node of the lineage, and are quite widespread among Caldarchaeia, and no donor lineage was identified. So what
do we actually learn from this analysis? The authors could perhaps extend this to other key enzymes in the pathway such as
BCKDH, BkdA and DBT that seem to be important especially for Calditenuis members. A second major comment concerns
the integration of the metaproteomics results. It is great that this analysis was performed, and I’m sure there is a trove of
interesting observations in the dataset, but in my opinion these data are not really exploited or presented fully. It is only
mentioned that the transporters are expressed, which leaves the reader asking a number of questions such as: was there
any quantification calculation performed on the data? which were the top proteins expressed? Were the rest of the proteins
in the proposed pathway expressed? This would be easily integrated in Fig. 4, or all other figures where pathways are
presented. 
I only have minor comments on some of the analysis and interpretation of the results, as well as clarifications that would
improve the reading of the manuscript and answer some of the questions that arise when reading the study. 
Questions on the cultivation methods and the enrichments: 
- Did the authors try any antibiotics in the enrichment process? If so, which ones? 
- What are the remaining contaminants in the enrichments? 
- Since the second most successful enrichment condition in terms of C assimilation was with propionate, it would be great if
you could present a metabolic model on how this compound is assimilated. Are the enzymes for conversion to succinyl-CoA
present?

manuscript because it presents a goo d summary of the experimental support for this metabolism

This is an important comment. Negative results are always difficult to deal with. In our in-situ 
FISH-nanoSIMS experiments, we think it’s likely that the Cal. ramacidaminiphagus cells were (i) 
inactive/slow-growing or (ii) active but disrupted by sampling/incubation and did not recover high 
activity during the 24 -hour incubation period.  These explanations are consistent with the FISH-
nanoSIMS results showing that Cal. ramacidaminiphagus cells incorporated very little, if any, 13C 
or 15N from any of our experiments with freshly collected sediments, as shown in Supplementary 
Figure 2. That experiment included not only 13C-labeled amino acids, but also fatty acids (butyrate, 
propionate, acetate), starch, and bicarbonate, each also amended with 15N-ammonium as a general 
marker for activity . We feel these two possibilities are  the best interpretation s because Cal. 
ramacidaminiphagus cells, if active, should at least assimilate some organic matter present in the 
sediment samples or released by other microorganisms during the 13C labeling period or even just 
15N from amended ammonium . Note that many other cells are labeled with both 13C and 15N 
following addition of amino acids, fatty acids, and bicarbonate in our experiments (Supplementary 
Figure 2). Thus, we don’t think our negative results with amino acid uptake by Cal. 
ramacidaminiphagus in freshly collected sediments can be interpreted as evidence against their 
use of BCAAs in situ. 
We also agree with your statement that amino acid uptake (in general) is not limited to Cal. 
ramacidaminiphagus in GBS sediments, as evidenced by much higher 13C-amino acid uptake by 
non-Cal. ramacidaminiphagus cells (Supplementary Figure 2). Indeed, we recently showed that 
amino acids are rapidly assimilated by cells of Fervidibacter sacchari in fresh GBS sediments 
(https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-53784-3) and our data presented in Figure 5 provide 
evidence that Fervidibacter sacchari prefers or possibly only metabolizes polar amino acids over 
BCAAs. Ideally we could do more FISH-nanoSIMS experiments from hot springs and enrichments 
focused specifically on BCAAs, but you probably understand that funding and workload make this 
challenging. It’s also important to mention that we only learned about this focus on BCAA 
metabolism after many years of study. We just didn’t have the foresight to focus on BCAAs from 
the beginning.
