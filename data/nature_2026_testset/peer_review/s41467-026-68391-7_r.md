Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Reviewer’s report to NCOMMS-25-65087 
This manuscript reports the first synthesis and spectroscopic characterization of the simplest iminophosphane (HPNH, cis
and trans) and its photoisomerization to aminophosphinidene (H
₂
NP). The study combines high-vacuum flash pyrolysis,
cryogenic matrix isolation, isotopic labeling, and high-level quantum chemical calculations. The results are significant
because these are fundamental, long-sought [2H, P, N] species, relevant both to main-group chemistry and astrochemistry.
However, I find it difficult to envision a sufficiently broad scientific role or impact of these species that would meet the
standards of Nature Communications. The novelty lies primarily in the generation and detection of elusive prototype
molecules, but the manuscript does not convincingly demonstrate why these particular species are of major significance
beyond the specialized field of phosphorus–nitrogen chemistry. 
The work is carefully executed and clearly written, but I am not convinced it reaches the level of broad conceptual impact
expected for Nature Communications. The results may be better suited for a specialized high-quality chemistry journal
focused on molecular spectroscopy, main-group chemistry, or astrochemistry. 
Some aspects require clarification and further discussion to strengthen the paper. 
(1) The singlet–triplet gap is reported to be ~0.2 kcal/mol, a very small value. How robust is the experimental assignment of
the singlet state? Could matrix effects influence this ordering ? 
(2) Most experiments are performed in solid N
₂
, with some in CO or Ar matrices. Could matrix stabilization bias the
isomerization pathways ? 
(3) Upon 395 nm irradiation, H
₂
NP is observed, but not H
₂
PN. The authors conclude H
₂
PN is not an intermediate. Can this
be definitively excluded, or might H
₂
PN form transiently but decay too fast to detect? 
(4) The authors assign the singlet ground state of H
₂
NP based on the observation that reaction with O
₂
 occurs only under
photoexcitation, but not upon thermal annealing at 30 K. They argue that this implies a spin-forbidden process for the closed-
shell singlet, and therefore supports singlet character. However, this reasoning is not fully convincing: The reaction observed
under 395 nm irradiation may proceed via excited-state chemistry of H
₂
NP or O
₂
 (e.g., singlet O
₂
 generation or transient spin-
crossing), rather than reflecting the nature of the ground state. Thus, the photoreaction is not necessarily diagnostic of the
electronic ground state. 
(5) The introduction extensively discusses PN and related molecules, but the conclusion only briefly touches on
astrochemistry. Could the authors expand on plausible interstellar formation pathways for HPNH/H
₂
NP, e.g., hydrogenation
of PN on icy grains or photochemistry in dense clouds? How likely are these molecules to survive in astrophysical
environments given their demonstrated high reactivity ? 
(6) The main contribution is spectroscopic identification supported by computation. There is little in terms of unexpected
reactivity, mechanistic novelty, or conceptual paradigm shift. Beyond spectroscopic curiosity, what important role could
HPNH or H
₂
NP play in chemical, materials, or astrochemical systems?

(Remarks to the Author) 
This article reports an excellent work on very unique molecules. The article is very well written. First report of unique
molecule HPNH has been included in this work. Photoisomerization of the species is also impressive. 
The experimental evidence provided for the formation is based on matrix isolation IR and UV-vis. 
Photoisomerization details
are on the cryogenic matrices. The chemical reactivity was reported with reference to reactions with CO and O2. 
Whereas all
these evidences are quite worthy, they are related to transient existence of the species. What is the half-life of the cis and

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript reports on the generation and spectroscopic identification of the simplest iminophosphane (HPNH) and
aminophosphinidene (H
₂
NP), as well as their photochemical interconversion and their reactions with O
₂
 and CO molecules.
The identification of these species, based on their IR spectra and supported by high-level calculations, is convincing. The
work is interesting and, with minor revisions, could be suitable for publication. 
The authors do not explain why the experiments were carried out in an N
₂
 matrix. The more common choices are Ar, Ne, or
para-H
₂
 matrices. N
₂
 matrices interact more strongly with isolated molecules, leading to larger matrix shifts, and due to site
effects, many of the spectral features may split. I may have some ideas about why the authors chose an N
₂
 matrix, but it is
important that they provide their own explanation in the manuscript. 
The computational method employed is also not the most typical one for this kind of study. It would be helpful if the authors
could provide references to earlier works demonstrating the expected accuracy at the chosen level of theory. 
The introduction is somewhat unfocused in its early part, but it rightly emphasizes the astrochemical relevance of the studied
molecules toward the end. Given that the identification of such species is more likely to come from radioastronomy rather
than IR astronomy, it would be valuable to provide highly accurate calculated rotational constants and dipole moments, with
a brief interpretation. 
In my opinion, the calculated UV–vis spectrum shown in Figure 2 should not appear only as an inset, but rather below the
experimental spectrum with matching axis scales. Similarly, displaying the calculated and experimental IR spectra in the
figures may be worthwhile, even if the agreement is already clear from the tables.

General comment:  This manuscript reports the first synthesis and spectroscopic 
characterization of the simplest iminophosphane (HPNH, cis and trans) and its 
photoisomerization to aminophosphinidene (H₂NP). The study combines high -vacuum flash 
pyrolysis, cryogenic matrix isolation, isotopic labeling, and high -level quantum chemical 
calculations. The results are significant because these are fundamental, long-sought [2H, P , N] 
species, relevant both to main-group chemistry and astrochemistry. However, I find it difficult 
to envision a sufficiently broad scientific role or impact of these species that would meet the 
standards of Nature Communications. The novelty lies primarily in the generation and 
detection of elusive prototype molecules, but the manuscript does not convincingly 
demonstrate why these particular species are of major significance beyond the specialized 
field of phosphorus -nitrogen chemistry. The work is carefully executed and clearly written, 
but I am not convinced it reaches the level of broad conceptual impact expected for Nature

Author reply: We greatly appreciate the Reviewer’s positive comments. As mentioned by the 
reviewer and also demonstrated by the references 5 -33 in the manuscript, the prototype 
PN-containing molecules including the diatomic species PN, HPN/HNP, and HPNH/H2NP are 
of broad interest in the fields of molecular spectroscopy, main -group chemistry, and 
astrochemistry. For instance, PN is a key interstellar P -bearing specie that has been also 
detected at the edge of the Galaxy [reference 12: Koelemay, L. A., Gold, K. R. & Ziurys, L. M. 
Phosphorus-bearing molecules PO and PN at the edge of the Galaxy. Nature 623, 292−295 
(2023)]. On the other hand, new methods for generation of PN from different precursors have 
been recently reported as main breakthrough in main-group chemistry [reference 22: Eckhardt, 
A. K. et al. Taming phosphorus mononitride. Nat. Chem. 14, 928−934 (2022) ; reference 23: 
Edin, S. et al.  Unleashing phosphorus mononitride . Nat. Commun . 16, 5596 (2025)]. 
Furthermore, the reactivity of PN has also been very recently disclosed [reference 7: Zhu, C. 
et al. The elusive cyclotriphosphazene molecule and its Dewar benzene -type valence isomer 
(P3N3). Sci. Adv. 6, eaba6934 (2020); reference 24: Qian, W., Wende, R. C., Schreiner, P. R. & 
Mardyukov, A. Selective preparation of phosphorus mononitride (P≡N) from phosphinoazide 
and reversible oxidation to phosphinonitrene. Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 62, e202300761 (2023)]. 
By analogy, it can be expected that the simple derivatives HPNH/H2NP should be of interest 
as well in different fields. However, both species remain elusive due to the lack of effective 
synthetic rou tes, although their properties keep attracting numerous theoretical calculations 
(references 27-33) since the first theoretical study 40 years ago [ Nguyen, M. N., McGinn, M. 
A. & Hegarty, A. F. A theoretical study of the phosphinonitrene (H 2P=N)-iminophosphane 
(HP=NH) rearrangement. J. Am. Chem. Soc. 107, 8029−8033 (1985)]. The first-time synthesis 
and characterization of HPNH/H 2NP in the present study will enlighten further experimental 
studies on the fundamental properties and potential applications of HPNH/H2NP in the future.

Comment 3: Upon 395 nm irradiation, H₂NP is observed, but not H₂PN. The authors 
conclude H₂PN is not an intermediate. Can this be definitively excluded, or might H₂PN form 
transiently but decay too fast to detect? 
Author reply:  Similar to  H2NP, no significant absorption near 395 nm was predicted for 
H2PN, indicating that it would not undergo further photoisomerization once it was generated . 
Moreover, the isomerization barrier from H2PN to HPNH was calculated to be 38.8 kcal mol−1 
(Fig. S8), which is sufficiently high to ensure the kinetic stability of H 2PN in the cold matrix. 
Therefore, H2PN should be spectroscopically detectable by theory. However, all the attempts 
to generate H 2PN from photochemistry of H 2NP by using different light sources were 
unsuccessful. Moreover, further evidence against its formation comes from the CO-trapping 
experiment. The exclusive formation of H2NPCO without H2PNCO as the CO -trapping 
products strongly suggests that H2PN is unlikely involved in the photochemistry  of H 2NP. 
Please note that a previous study shows that similar species  F2PN can be efficiently trapped 
by CO as  F2PNCO (Angew. Chem. Int. Ed.  2009, 48, 4828–4831). In the meantime, the IR 
spectrum for the assumed CO -trapping product H2PNCO has been reported  (Angew. Chem. 
Int. 2025, 64, e202414456). As mentioned by the Reviewer, another possible reason for the 
absence of H2PN is probably due to fast quantum tunneling by hydrogen atom, however, our 
experiments with the deuterated sample H(D)NP show the photoisomerization with HNPD 
without intermediacy of H(D)PN. We further performed calculations on the tunneling reaction 
rates for H2PN→ HPNH with the ring–polymer instanton theory at the B3LYP/aug-cc-pVTZ 
level. The tunneling rates were predicted to be extremely slow (60 K, 10 −18 s−1, Table S6), 
indicating that the absence of H 2PN during the photochemistry of HPNH in the cryogenic 
matrix at 10 K is unlikely to be caused by quantum tunneling . Notably, the tunneling 
controlled isomerization from HPN to HNP has been very recently observed in the matrix at 3 
K (reference 25). 
On the basis of th ese experimental and theoretical observations , it can be safely concluded 
that H2PN is not involved in this reaction.  To make it clear, details about the CO -trapping 
experiments have been described in the manuscript: “Additionally, chemical trapping reaction 
was performed by photolysis of HPNH at 395 nm in solid CO ice at 10 K. The corresponding 
IR difference spectrum (Fig. 3A) shows exclusive formation of H 2NPCO,47 implying first 
formation of H 2NP from HPNH with subsequent reaction by CO. Similar photolytic 
CO-trapping reactions have been observed for singlet p hosphinophosphinidenes60 and triplet

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

phenylphosphinidene.61,62 Absence of the CO -trapping product of the putative intermediate 
H2PN (+ CO → H2PNCO) suggests that it is not involved in the photochemistry of HPNH, 
since similar CO -trapping reaction was observed before for the fluorine derivative F2PN (+ 
CO → F2PNCO).59”. 
 
 
Comment 4: The authors assign the singlet ground state of H₂NP based on the observation 
that reaction with O₂ occurs only under photoexcitation, but not upon thermal annealing at 30 
K. They argue that this implies a spin -forbidden process for the closed -shell singlet, and 
therefore supports singlet character. However, this reasoning is not fully convincing: The 
reaction observed under 395 nm irradiation may proceed via excited -state chemistry of H₂NP 
or O₂ (e.g., singlet O₂ generation or transient spin -crossing), rather than reflecting the nature 
of the ground state. Thus, the photoreaction is not necessarily diagnostic of the electronic 
ground state. 
Author reply: As detailed in our response to Comment 1, the singlet ground state of H 2NP 
was ascertained according to  the excellent agreement between the experimental and 
theoretical IR spectra (Table 2). The photoreaction with O2 was performed to further explore 
the reactivity of H2NP, from which the novel compound H2NPO2 was obtained. Given the 
previously reported spontaneous O 2-reactions with triplet phosphinidenes61 and nitrenes 66-68 
under matrix -isolation conditions, thermal annealing was also performed for the H2NP/O2 
system, however, no reaction was observed. As suggested by the Reviewer, the manuscript 
has been changed by  removal of the discussion linked the O 2 reactivity to the nature of the 
electronic ground state. 
 
 
Comment 5:  The introduction extensively discusses PN and related molecules, but the 
conclusion only briefly touches on astroch emistry. Could the authors expand on plausible 
interstellar formation pathways for HPNH/H₂NP , e.g., hydrogenation of PN on icy grains or 
photochemistry in dense clouds? How likely are these molecules to survive in astrophysical 
environments given their demonstrated high reactivity? 
Author reply: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. The chemical evolution of 
phosphorus-containing species such as PN and PO is a hot topic in astrochemistry (Please see 
the two references below).

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

In recent chemical models of phosphorus astrochemistry, H 2PN, H2NP and HNPH have been 
proposed as candidates that could be derived from successive hydrogenation of phosphorus 
nitride (PN) , which is one of the most abundant phosphorus -containing species in the 
interstellar medium (for examples, see references: 11 -16). Moreover, hydrogenation reactions 
have been known to play a key role in the chemical growth/evolution of interstellar species in 
dense molecular cloud ices  (for an example, see: McClure, M. K. et al. An Ice Age JWST 
inventory of dense molecular cloud ices , Nat. Astron.  2023, 7, 431–443). Additionally, 
gas-phase collisional reactions may offer a complementary pathway, particularly in warmer or 
shocked regions , since HNP and HPN  (key intermediates for the formation of  HPNH and 
H2NP from PN) were proposed in the astrochemical model of phosphorus through  collisional 
reactions P + NH and N + PH  (for an example, see reference 14: Silva, M. X.  et al.  New 
routes for PN destruction and formation in the interstellar medium via neutral -neutral 
gas-phase reactions and an extended database for reactions involving phosphorus. Astron. 
Astrophys. 2025, 696, A170). However, none of the hydrogenated species of PN (including 
HNP/HPN and HPNH/H2NP) has been yet detected in the interstellar medium partly due to 
the lack of experimental data about these species. We think that the facile generation of 
HPNH by high -vacuum flash pyrolysis of di -tert-butylphosphanamine in this work will 
facilitate further studies about its astrochemistry, so as to understand its role in the interstellar 
PN-chemistry. 
Considering the reactivity of these small molecules, their survival is strongly 
environment-dependent. In the shielded interiors of dense molecular clouds, where 
temperatures are low and irradiations are negligible, HPNH/H2NP isomers are expected to be 
relatively stable. The absence of tunneling  reaction and the low th ermal energy under these 
conditions prevent rearrangement or destruction  via further reactions with other interstellar 
species such as CO, which has been proposed as a major component of interstellar ices . In 
contrast, the survival timescale of these isomers might be  shorter in irradiated regions. 
Recently, photochemistry has been proposed as an important driving force for formation of 
complex organic molecules (COMs) in the interstellar medium (for an example, see: Caro, G. 
M. M. et al. Photochemistry of interstellar ice forming complex organic molecules. Nat. Rev. 
Chem. 2025, 9, 2025). To estimate the chemical behavior and distribution of HPNH/H2NP in 
the interstellar medium, further modeling studies are necessary, which is beyond the topic of 
the present study. 
As suggested by the Reviewer, the last part of the Conclusion has  been changed to : 
“Similar to the broad applications of the closely related singlet phosphinophosphinidenes 60 
and phosphinonitrenes, 41 H2NP features as a fundamentally new and highly reactive 
aminophosphinidene that might be used as a building block in synthetic phosphorus chemistry. 
The photoreactions of H2NP with small molecules CO and O 2 to the formation of H2NPCO 
and H 2NPO2 underscore its role as a potentially useful intermediate in constructing novel

N−P−C and N−P−O motifs. Considering the possible involvement of the [2H, N, P] isomers 
in the astrochemical network of the important phosphorus -bearing species PN in the 
interstellar medium (ISM), the facile generation of HPNH in the gas phase will stimulat e 
further studies on its role in interstellar phosphorus chemistry. Particularly, the disclosed

Comment 6: The main contribution is spectroscopic identification supported by computation. 
There is little in terms of unexpected reactivity, mechanistic novelty, or conceptual paradigm 
shift. Beyond spectroscopic curiosity, what important role could HPNH or H₂NP play in 
chemical, materials, or astrochemical systems? 
Author reply: As described in the introducti on, the fundamental importance of HPNH and 
H2NP in main -group chemistry has been demonstrated by the novel bonding/electronic 
properties and reactivity associated with the prototype phosphorus-nitrogen multiple bonds, as 
evidenced by numerous theoretical studies (for examples, see references 27-33), including the 
first theoretical study by Trinquier in 1982 [reference 32: Phosphinonitrene, phosphazene, and 
aminophosphinidene. Structures and stabilities. J. Am. Chem. Soc.  104, 6969−6977 (1982) ] 
and also the very recently predicted singlet ground state nature for H 2NP using the high-level 
CCSDTQ/CBS method by Schaefer III  et al. [reference 46: Group 15 and 16 nitrene -like 
pnictinidenes. Chem. Eur. J. 27, 14461−14471 (2021) ]. Although phosphazenes RP=NR and 
aminophosphinidenes R2N≡P are versatile building blocks in synthetic phosphorus chemistry, 
all the early attempts to prepare the parent [2H, N, P] isomers were unsuccessful due to the 
lack of synthetic methods. Therefore, the first time synthesis of the long sought -after HPNH 
and H2NP contributes to the fundamental knowledge of main-group chemistry. 
Recently, significant advances have been made in the chemistry of the elusive diatomic 
molecule PN thanks to the newly discovered methods for its preparation [for examples, see 
reference 22: Eckhardt, A. K. et al. Taming phosphorus mononitride. Nat. Chem. 14, 928−934 
(2022) and reference 23: Edin , S. et al. Unleashing phosphorus mononitride . Nat. Commun. 
16, 5596 (2025)]. By analogy, the successful preparation and characterization of HPNH and 
its isomer H 2NP will enable further studies on their structures and  reactivity. Given the 
involvement of [2H, N, P] isomers in interstellar phosphorus chemistry, the disclosed 
photochemistry in this work is crucial for building the chemical network for the important 
interstellar species PN. 
To make it clear, the conclusion has been changed to “Similar to the broad applications of the 
closely related singlet phosphinophosphinidenes 60 and phosphinonitrenes,41 H2NP features as 
a fundamentally new and highly reactive aminophosphinidene that might be used as a 
building block  in synthetic phosphorus chemistry . The photoreactions of H2NP with small 
molecules CO and O 2 to the formation of H2NPCO and H 2NPO2 underscore its role as a 
potentially useful intermediate in constructing novel N−P−C and N−P−O motifs. Considering 
the possible  involvement of the [2H, N, P] isomers in the astrochemical network of the 
important phosphorus -bearing species PN in the interstellar medium (ISM), the facile 
generation of HPNH in the gas phase will stimulate further studies on its role in interstellar 
phosphorus chemistry. Particularly, the disclosed photochemistry of the [2H, N, P] isomers 
including the reactions of H 2NP with CO and O 2 could be of interest in understanding the 
phosphorus chemistry in cold  dense molecular clouds , where photochemistry has been 
proposed as an important driving force for the formation of complex organic molecules.69,70”.

The cis and trans isomers of HPNH were generated by pyrolysis of ( t-Bu)2PNH2/matrix
