Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Lim et al. present a comparative mechanistic study of the established lipopeptide drug colistin and two new lipopeptides,
turnercyclamycin A and B. They set out to determine mechanistic differences between these lipopeptides with respect to
outer and inner membrane damage. The authors found clearly distinct behavior of the turnercyclamycins in comparison with
colistin and convincingly argue for distinct mechanisms of action of this new lipopeptide class. While the study is in principle
interesting, it leaves many questions unanswered and does not provide a conclusive model for the mechanism of
turnercyclamycins. Unfortunately, the paper also has several critical issues regarding study design and experimental
execution that hamper the interpretability of the findings. 
Major issues 
1. How were the antibiotic concentrations chosen? It seems that 4xMIC was selected arbitrarily. The authors show killing
curves, but these are not sufficient for guiding concentration and treatment time selection as even just one log unit in CFU
reduction will result in 90% dead cells in every assay. For studying antibiotic mechanisms, it is not appropriate to look at
dead cells, as they only show the final result and do not allow assessment of the complex processes leading to cell death,
i.e., the mode of action. Instead, the authors should have done acute shock assays, using growth experiments conducted
under the same conditions used for their mechanistic experiments (here, log phase cells). Based on those, they should have
selected a concentration (and treatment time) that visibly inhibits bacterial growth without leading to excessive killing and
cell lysis. With lipopeptides, it is even often necessary to examine two concentrations as their window for growth inhibition
without lysis is typically extremely narrow. Similarly, the treatment times, at least for colistin, are far too long to see the effects
leading to inhibition/death, as evidenced by the killing kinetics shown in Figure 2A. At the very least, the authors need to
perform acute shock assays with the chosen concentrations and up to the chosen treatment times. If these reveal that the
majority of the cell population is already dead, the whole study is called into question and experiments would have to be
repeated with suitable concentrations and timeframes to allow interpreting the findings correctly. 
2. The DiSC assay has multiple issues. Most importantly, it lacks essential controls and was not performed as would be
appropriate for Gram-negative bacteria. In general, spectroscopic measurements of the membrane potential do not work well
in Gram-negative organisms, at least not without eliminating the OM barrier first. Obviously, in most cases this is not the
method of choice for OM-permeabilizing lipopeptides. Therefore, the assay should have been performed microscopically.
The authors should read Buttress et al. (https://doi.org/10.1099/mic.0.001227) and perform DiSC microscopy accordingly. In
brief, the uptake of DiSC is limited by the OM, which gives wild artifacts depending on the efficiency and kinetics of OM
permeabilization by the tested drugs. Given the clear differences between colistin and turnercyclamicins in that regard, the
DiSC assay as performed by the authors is inconclusive. 
Additionally, the compounds were added before the baseline was stable. This is not correct. Also, the fact that the baseline
does not stabilize is further evidence of the issues that Gram-negative bacteria pose in the DiSC assay. 
The assay lacks vital controls for interaction between the compounds and the DiSC dye. This is very common, especially
with peptides, and should always be assessed by recording fluorescence in solution with and without compounds. 
The timeline of the assay is also problematic. DiSC is highly toxic upon prolonged exposure and assays should typically not
exceed 30 min for that reason. In E. coli, the OM protects from this toxicity but upon OM disruption, the dye does become
toxic, importantly at concentrations below those used by the authors. It is therefore likely that what the authors observe are
combined effects of the lipopeptides with DiSC toxicity. What the authors should do, is a microscopic assay where they treat
the cells, then withdraw aliquots at different timepoints, stain with DiSC for 10 min, and then examine fluorescence under the
microscope (note that the readout in the microscope is opposite of the spectroscopic readout, see Buttress et al.).

Further, the timeline for colistin makes no sense. It appears that the cells are dying faster than they depolarize, which would
indicate that depolarization is a secondary effect of cell death. This would go against what is know about colistin and is
further evidence that there are issues with the DiSC assay as performed here. 
Further, given the concentration issue pointed out before, it is pivotal to gain a population overview and not just a batch
measurement, another argument for performing the assay microscopically instead of spectroscopically. 
3. The NPN results do not align with the DiSC results, likely due to the issues pointed out above. The turnercyclamycins do
not even permeabilize the OM at the timepoint where they seem to depolarize the IM. This could fit with the authors’
interpretation, but could also be based on artifacts, as outlined above. Properly performing DiSC assays is therefore pivotal
for the interpretation of the mechanism. 
Like the DiSC assay, also the NPN assay lacks peptide interaction controls. Just like DiSC, NPN is prone to interacting with
different types of drugs, especially peptides, making these controls essential. 
Additionally, the timelines of the DiSC and NPN assays do not line up. According to NPN, the turnercyclamycins reach their
maximum OM permeabilization at 3-4 h, while the DiSC was only performed up to 2.5 h. It would be good to include
measurements up to 4 h when repeating DiSC. 
4. The TEM results with ΔlptD are problematic. It is obvious from the untreated controls that the mutant strain has a generally
reduced fitness. Additionally, Figure 4A shows differences in susceptibility compared to the WT. Yet, it does not seem that
the treatment concentrations were adjusted accordingly. This can skew the results in either direction. Again, the authors
should have performed acute shock assays with this strain to determine appropriate concentrations for this assay. 
5. Single transporter mutants do not say much as the compounds could travel through more than one and thus still have an
available route in a single mutant. To make a reliable claim that the compounds are not taken up by porins, systematic
testing of multiple mutant strains should have been done. I am aware that this sounds easier than it is in practice, but at the
very least, the results need to be put into the right perspective by acknowledging this limitation. As for efflux pumps,
however, this can be relatively easily tested by simply using de-energized cells (incubated in glucose-free buffer or treated
with a depolarizing drug like CCCP). 
6. In OMV and lipid supplementation experiments, it seems that the authors have not included controls with only the
antibiotics incubated under the same conditions as the antibiotic-OMV/lipid mixtures. This would be essential to truly
attribute the observed effects to the presence of the different additives. 
7. The conclusions drawn from the lipidomics are very superficial, essentially just descriptions of similarities between groups
without biological insight. Is it really not possible to go deeper into the analysis and provide a more thorough discussion and
testable hypotheses? 
8. The discussion is too superficial and fails to address several interesting points. I would encourage the authors to think a
bit further on their study. For example, a few thoughts that came to me: 
Do turnercyclamycins bind to LPS that is bound to the IM? Maybe they bind to intermediates or they need to form a complex
with IM components that then allows binding to IM-bound LPS, or if not mediating direct binding, indirectly affects how it is
processed and transported. 
How would turnercyclamicins achieve any of the proposed mechanisms, if they neither interact with LPS nor use porins to
enter the periplasm? How would they cross the OM/the outer OM leaflet to interact with the inner OM leaflet or enter the
periplasm? Would that not be an argument that the porin single mutant screen is unreliable? 
If the compounds would bind to the inner leaflet of the OM, would they not be effectively attenuated by pre-incubation with
OMVs? Similarly, if they cross the OM to enter the periplasm, would they not enter OMVs? Would it not make sense to
measure whether they are taken up into OMVs? Similarly, is it possible to make protein-free OM vesicles and compare them
with native OMVs? This would give more conclusive insight into a possible uptake through porins. 
Other comments 
1. The title is not specific enough and suggests a higher degree of generality than the paper provides. It would be good to
specify which lipopeptides were studied. 
2. In the second paragraph of the introduction, it would be good to mention that lpxC can be deleted in Acinetobacter and
that is where the data are derived from. This information comes a little late in the paper and may cause confusion right off the
bat with the non-expert reader. 
3. I would suggest being more careful with phrases stating that turnercyclamycins do not affect the IM, act IM-independently,
etc. If there is initial depolarization, this is an effect, even if transient. Also, there are many other effects the compounds could
have that the assays used in this paper would not capture and it would be an overstatement of the authors’ results to
generalize their findings too much. I would advise to be more precise and only state what was really observed in this study
(transient depolarization, no large-scale IM disruption visible in Sytox assays and TEM). 
4. Similarly, there are effects in the Sytox assay and, while the authors logically explain in the results that this is likely due to
increased permeability after cell death/lysis, in other instances in the paper it sounds as if no effect was observed at all. I
would suggest being more careful and using precise language to avoid misleading the reader. 
5. It would be good to clearly specify early on (abstract, introduction) whether lipidomics was performed on IM, OM, or whole
cells. 
6. Throughout the text, please always specify whether you are talking about the inner or outer membrane to avoid unclarities.
7. In the legend of Figure 1, please specify what the numbers in parentheses mean. 
8. The lines in Figure 2A and F are too thin and colors are virtually invisible. In 1F, I cannot see what is happening at all.
Please use thicker lines. 
9. Figure 4 does not allow reading actual MIC values. It would be very useful to supply these as supplementary tables. 
10. In the section describing TEM, it would be appropriate to acknowledge that not all types of membrane damage can be
seen in TEM (in fact only the most extreme can be seen). 
11. Likewise, it should be noted in the discussion that the IM assays performed here only inform about IM permeabilization.
Any other effects (fluidity changes, phase separation, domain formation, protein delocalization, impairment of membrane-
bound processes like respiration or membrane transport, etc.) remain elusive.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I read the article "Differential membrane lipid disruption by lipopeptide antibiotics" with interest. I found the manuscript well
written and detailed, with no fundamental flaws. The strength of the contribution lies in the combination of whole cell work,
combined with some standard biophysical methods, such as isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). 
The study, however, raises some questions, some of them minor, that I think the authors might want to address before
publication. 
1. The most striking of these is the observation that 
ΔlpxC knockouts lose sensitivity to turnercyclamycins. While this
observation is not new, the authors specifically test for interaction with LPS by ITC – and find none. While it is possible that
the free energy of binding to LPS is dominated by entropy and not enthalpy and thus not detectable by ITC, it is unlikely.
Which leaves us with the observation that in the absence of lpxC, cells lose sensitivity to the agent, while not interacting with
its product, LPS. 
On line 63 in the MS, the authors state (and repeat throughout the MS) that turnercyclamycins require LPS for action. I don’t
think that conclusion is warranted. The authors have shown that the turnercyclamycins require the presence of enzymes in
the biosynthetic pathway to LPS for action, but not LPS, per se. It has been shown that lpxC interacts with a number of other
proteins in the biosynthetic pathways of membrane lipids, including phospholipids
(https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2018.03285). The observation that that turnercyclamycins alter the cell phospholipid
composition may be linked to this point. While a resolution of this question is obviously beyond the current MS, a discussion
is warranted, I believe. 
2. Throughout the article, including in Fig 6, the authors assume that turnercyclamycins act on the OM. However, the
membrane potential is established across the IM. While it is necessary for the lipopeptide to penetrate the OM, it must act on
the IM to depolarize the cell, however transiently (many antimicrobial peptides are known to do this). 
3. This would also account for the clear binding of turnercyclamycins to PG, which is abundant in both the IM and the inner
leaflet of the OM. In this context the attenuation of turnercyclamycin activity in the presence of PG vesicles and OMV is
simply explained by a partitioning of the peptides between exogenous vesicles and the CM, leading to a smaller fraction of
cell-bound peptide. 
4. L. 52: Minor point: it would be clearer to state that turnercyclamycin A is N-terminally amidated by myristic acid (C14:1),
and turnercyclamycin B by palmitoleic acid (C16:1).

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Lim et al. present a comparative mechanistic study of the established lipopeptide drug 
colistin and two new lipopeptides, turnercyclamycin A and B. They set out to determine 
mechanistic differences between these lipopeptides with respect to outer and inner 
membrane damage. The authors found clearly distinct behavior of the 
turnercyclamycins in comparison with colistin and convincingly argue for distinct
