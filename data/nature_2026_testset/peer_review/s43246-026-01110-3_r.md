Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript concerns the dielectric anisotropy of semiconductor surfaces, 
which has a long tradition of addressing the electronic structure of such a 
system with quite straight-forward, easy-to-use optical experiments. 
The interrelation between the internal electronic structure and the measured 
anisotropy (i.e., differences in the reflectance depending on electric-field 
orientation), however, is a very complicated issue. 
In the present manuscript the authors (i) present state-of-the-art first-principles 
results for the calculated anisotropy, and (ii) try to analyze and interprete their 
results in terms of bulk and surface contributions, to arrive at a deeper understanding. 
Task (i), i.e. first-principles calculations (Bethe-Salpeter equation) provides 
quite good data in reasonable (but not perfect) agreement with experimental data. 
I would believe that various issues (k-point convergence, slab thickness, 
and in particular the treatment of optical transition matrix elements) may still 
pose problems. I do not at all hold this against the authors, because in my opinion 
their approach constitutes the best one can do at the moment. However, 
the deviations from the experimental data makes it difficult to clearly identify 
the microscopic origin of the spectral features, since it might be falsified by the 
above-mentioned issues. 
In total, the quality of the calculated results surely warrants publication. 
Task (ii), i.e. the attempted identification of the interrelation between bulk and 
surface properties, does not yield truly conclusive statements, in my opinion. 
The authors argue that in case of Si(100):As most of the anisotropy comes from a subset of 
excitonic states that are localized at the surface. 
However, in the case of Si(100):As-Si-H, this localization at the surface is much 
weaker, while the anisotropy features seem to be quite similar. 
Therefore a conclusive correlation between the two quantities is difficult to identify. 
Also, the manuscript does not discuss why one surface shows so many surface-localized 
excitons while the other does not. 
In total, the claimed relationship remains quite vague - this is probably just the nature 
of optical anisotropy, and the results of the aforementioned remaining tchnical difficulties 
of the calculations, even if they are of such high quality as in this manuscript. 
Therefore, simple predictions for other systems appear nearly impossible 
(this would be the ultimate goal). 
In my impression, the nature of optical anisotropy has always been tricky and unclear, 
and unfortunately the present manuscript does not really contribute a quantum leap 
of better understanding. 
Unfortunately, this would somewhat limit the impact of the paper.

We agree with the reviewer’s comment and have clarified the terminology and definitions. In the revised manuscript, we
explicitly explain our use of the term ’bulk-related’ and define our use of the word ’surface’. The Introduction now states
that ’bulk-related’ is used in quotation marks to remain consistent with existing literature, while emphasizing that the
term can be misleading and does not imply that features originate directly from the bulk. In addition, we define ’surface’
as the topmost reconstructed atomic layer. These clarifications strengthen our original intent, which we did not express
clearly enough. While revising this, we also noticed that, when introducing SIBA, it seemed sensible to us to include
a cautious note to the reader, similar to the term ’bulk-related’. Thus, we added a short additional sentence about
SIBA right after its introduction. In addition, the discussion was expanded to provide a refined interpretation of the
terminology, emphasizing that the term ’bulk-related’ should not be used generically without microscopic justification.
We clarified the terms SIBA, ’bulk-related’, and ’surface’ at the beginning of the Introduction.
Today, RAS features due to transitions between surface states are well understood and have been enormously
useful in surface exploration for decades, as reviewed in detail by Weightmanet al. [1]. However, a topic that
has been observed for many surfaces and has been heavily debated for the last 40 years (e.g., Refs. [2–6]) is the
origin of ’bulk-related’ features in RA spectra, i.e., features that coincide with critical points of the imaginary
part of the bulk dielectric function, also referred to as surface-induced bulk anisotropy (SIBA). Here, SIBA is used
as a conventional label for these features when not explicitly stated otherwise, in line with previous literature
and as will be clear throughout this study. The use of SIBA does not imply that the microscopic origin of these
features has been established unless this is explicitly stated. Irrespective of the investigated surface or specific
reconstruction, e.g, Si(100)-c(4× 2), Si(100)-(2× 1):H and Si(100)-(1× 1):H [7], Si(111):H and Si(110):H [8],
GaAs(110) and GaAs(100)-β2(2× 4) [9], GaAs(100)-c(4× 4) [10], As-rich GaAs(100)-c(2× 8) [11], ZnTe(100) [12]
and InP(100)-c(2× 4) [13] the occurrence of SIBA features has been noted. Their origin is often argued to be
related to "bulk-like electronic states perturbed by the surface" [13]. Specifically, it has been proposed that they
2

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

may be related to strain induced by surface reconstructions, steps or dimerization via the piezo-optic effect [8] or
to surface electric fields via the linear electro-optic effect [14, 15]. Aspneset al. [3] suggested that they are caused
by photon-induced localization of bulk electronic states. In fact, some authors [9] have gone so far as to state:
"after realizing that many RA line shapes are similar to the imaginary part of the bulk dielectric function, or to its
energy derivative, it is a growing belief that surface optical spectra are mostly determined by bulk effects, and
therefore, not very useful as a tool of surface characterization". While this sentiment has softened over the years,
a clear explanation of SIBA features remains elusive. Throughout this work, we use the term ’bulk-related’ to
refer to such features, in order to remain consistent with established literature on this topic. The use of quotation
marks highlights that this terminology can be misleading and does not imply a direct bulk origin. The present
study focuses on how such features emerge on a microscopic level, i.e., whether they emerge directly from surface
states or perturbed/extended bulk states. In this context, we define ’surface’ as the reconstructed topmost atomic
layer, a definition that will be used throughout the manuscript.
We added a statement at the end of the Discussion section reiterating that the term ’bulk-related’
should not be used generically without microscopic justification.
In summary, the present work shows that ’bulk-related’ anisotropies in surface optical spectra do not necessarily
originate from the bulk. In fact, they may arise directly from surface reconstruction or be attributed to what we
