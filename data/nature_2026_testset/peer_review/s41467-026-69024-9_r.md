Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Biomolecular condensates play key role in protein physiological functon(including ribonucleoprotein assembly, ribosome
formation and gene expression) and pathological formation of aggregates. These condensates of proteins, nucleic acids and
intracellular components when formed are extremely dynamic and sensitive to changes in situ. Multiple factors contribute to
the formation of biomolecular condensates, including intracellular protein-RNA interactions and weak, transient multivalent
interactions between intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). The size of the bio-condensates has been suggested to vary
from micro- to nano-sized condensates. 
Here the authors used the precise resolution of single-molecule fluorescence spectroscopy to identify the dynamic switches
in protein interactions that yields biomolecular condensation. The authors took advantage of the abundance of IDRs in the
DNA/RNA binding protein, TDP-43, to study liquid-liquid phase separation at nanoscale resolution. Importantly, the authors
also make use of a chemical chaperone – trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) to induce LLPS. 
Major comments: 
1. The authors made use of only the Atto647N fluorescent labels. There was no fluorescent tag control to determine potential
unique contribution of Atto647N to their condensation experiments. Controls with only Atto647N in solution is needed. In
addition, the authors should provide the effect of TMAO on TDP-43 labelled with fluorescent proteins such as EGFP or
mCherry or small molecule probes like SNAP-tag or HALOtag. 
2. The authors make use of Leishmania extracts to provide a complex environment to study TDP-43 condensation. However,
these extracts contain nucleic acids that might be rich in GU-repeats oligonucleotides, that TDP-43 preferentially binds to,
which also contributes to TDP-43 condensation (Grese et al., EMBO J 2021, PMID: 34787357). Authors state the that use of
Leishmania tarentolae are ‘orthogonal to human proteins and are presumed not to contain specific interactors for TDP-43’
(line 100) – however TDP-43 is a promiscuous protein binding to hundreds of mammalian proteins and >one-third of all
human mRNA transcripts, and the impact of this presumption on the findings is unclear; it seems unlikely that TDP-43 would
bind to no Leishmania tarentolae proteins or RNAs. Authors should discuss this in greater detail. The authors should
therefore speculate on whether the solution in which TDP-43 is dispersed already has contributing factors to its
condensation or not, as the authors observed more condensation events in the LTE extract compared with the use of 2M
TMAO (Fig. 2G) and as the authors also did not observe any differences in condensations events with serial dilutions and
titrations of TDP-43 LCD in LTE extracts compared to differences observed in their αβ
ϒ
 buffer (Fig. S8). 
3. The authors observed condensation of TDP-43 LCD fragment as much lower concentrations (500nM) compared with
previous studies. Could the authors speculate on and provide data on how the use of full length TDP-43 in the study might
impact its condensation propensity by TMAO use. 
4. The authors should re-present the data in Fig. 3A, to reflect the rise in number of peak events with TMAO only, prior to a
decrease in the number with 1,6 hexanediol treatment. As the figure currently is, it is hard to appreciate the reversible fixture
of 1,6 hexanediol treatment on formation of condensates. 
5. The in-depth analysis of the kinetics of onset of condensate formation (in figure 4) can be merged with the data in figure 2.
The authors should consider providing this in-depth outlook on the kinetics prior to introducing data on 1,6 hexanediol. 
6. This study would benefit from full-length TDP-43 condensation analysis, as well as validation in cells and neurons to
clarify relevance of findings – authors should at a minimum expand on this in the Discussion.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Houx et al. present a series of experiments for characterizing species formed in the early stages of protein liquid-liquid
phase separation (LLPS) using single molecule fluorescence techniques. The approach is demonstrated using the low
complexity domain (LCD) of TDP-43 (TDP-43LCD) and also employs TMAO and clarified cell lysate (LTE) to investigate the
effects of crowders on LLPS. Working in a concentration regime not generally accessible to other techniques for
characterization of LLPS, the authors reaffirms that crowders promote LLPs, increasing the size and number of early
condensates. The authors further conclude that although crowders do not significantly accelerate condensation, they do
stabilize the droplets at these early time points. Finally, the evolution of protein droplets is observed over a longer timescale
of 1 h, showing an increase in droplet size over the first ~15 min following initiation of LLPS. The paper is well written and
the data organized and presented logically. However, I do not believe that the conclusions are fully supported by their data,
and I have technical concerns regarding the lack of controls to evaluate the impact of the AttoDye. It is also noted that some
of the experimental details are lacking such as (1) how the protein is labeled and which amino-groups have been labeled
and (2) how LTE is made and how it’s characterize. Is there quality control regarding the composition of LTE? I have serious
reservations regarding this study. I am also not excited about the use of TMAO while I understand this is used for a proof of
concept methodology. I am more interested in in LTE however, this is not well characterized and mostly used in a supportive
role. 
First concern is how we can be certain that we are observing nanocondensates and not just dynamic oligomeric
assemblies? I don’t think that the use of 1,6 hexanediol is definitive. Case in point, larger, brighter assemblies that are
difficult to dissolve are reported with the presence of TMAO. Can the authors confirm that these remaining condensates are
indeed condensates and not protein aggregates? 
It is concerning that the titration and dilution results shown in Figure S8
have values similar to those in Figure 2G and 2H for LTE. This would suggest that the condensates formed in LTE are not
reversible. Are the authors confident they achieve phase separation and not aggregate formation in the LTE conditions? 
The
authors should show additional validation that the condensates formed are not solid. Their reversibility assays appear
inconclusive. 
I do laud the authors for preforming these challenging experiments carefully. The authors work in an unusual concentration
regime where a doubling of the TDP-43LCD concentration from 500 nM to 1000 nM is sufficient to transition the protein from
an apparently fully monomeric to condensed state. Although there is no characterization of the monomeric state, which the
experiment is blind to. There are several unusual methodological choices and concerning observations that the authors do
not address. Firstly, the proportion of dye-labeled protein is very high at 50%, atypical for these experiments. Is there a
reason that the authors did not use lower dye-labeled protein? Although Atto647 is a small fluorophore compared to the
fluorescent proteins employed elsewhere, it may nonetheless significantly perturb the behavior of a highly dynamic
unstructured protein as it is highly hydrophobic molecule, especially near the threshold between solubility and
condensation. Moreover, the authors do not characterize where the fluorophore is labeled (is it at the N-terminus or Lys406
or both?), which also could have an effect. As mentioned above, additional detail is needed about the protein construct used.
The authors reference Choi et al. 2018 for the protein expression and purification; however, it is not made clear if the
construct used is indeed the wild-type TDP-43 LCD, which is presumed to be the case since they are using NHS. The
experimental details for labeling also need to be stated as the reference they cited used Cys mutations. 
The authors report reduced fluorescence of the droplets formed in LTE relative to those formed in the presence TMAO. This
is not explained, but could conceivably be a result of the Atto647 increasing the interaction of TDP-43LCD with other
biomolecules. Though other explanations are certainly possible, the authors do not investigate this further. One of the more
striking unexplained observations is the apparent hysteresis of LLPS in the titration and dilution experiments. In this case,
the ratio of labeled to unlabeled protein changes throughout the experiment from 25% labeled initially to 100% labeled at the
end. Although this hysteresis may indeed be an interesting characteristic of LLPS, at the very least more experiments need
to be conducted to rule out the effect of some artifact such as variable percentage of labeled protein are needed. 
All of this
points to the need for some characterization of the effect of Atto647 on TDP-43LCP LLPS, especially at such a high labeling

level. 
Lastly, I question whether the authors have fairly accounted for protein absorbed onto the surface of their chambers, which
will skew their protein concentration. Moreover, I have concerns about the calibration curve which relies on the large change
seen with the 450 nm particle. Without the 450 nm point, there’s little differentiation between the sizes. Thus, I question the
validity of their size estimation of nanocondensates. 
Overall the study serves as a reasonable proof of concept for the approach; however, no evidence is provided to determine if
the technique proposed will be sensitive in a cell or more complex environment. 
As it stand, it also lacks significant new
insight into LLPS and into the specific behavior of TDP-43LCD. 
With revisions, the work may be suitable for publication in a

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work the authors present methodology for the real-time detection of, and subsequent characterization of fluorescently
labeled protein monomers which coalesce in solution to form ‘nanocondesates.’ This is accomplished via diffraction-limited
fluorescence fluctuation spectroscopy wherein clusters of TDP-43 proteins are differentiated from monomers in solution by
their large fluorescent peaks following treatment with a chemical crowding agent (TMAO), or in a model cell lysate. The
authors report the kinetics of this process, and describe the relative size/properties of the observed condensates through
their ‘fingerprinting’ analysis methodology which utilizes the size and shape of the signal peaks. 
Comments: 
1. An important point that has not been addressed is that the experiment is essentially identical fluorescence correlation
spectroscopy (FCS), only the analysis is different (instead of autocorrelation, individual fluorescence burst events are
analyzed). FCS also enables measurement of diffusion coefficient, size, and concentration of fluorescent species, and is a
well-established and widely used technique. It seems to me like FCS could have been used to measure everything in this
work (or something very similar, for example, residence time instead of line width, etc.). What advantages this analytical
framework offers over FCS? 
2. Fluorescence intensity fluctuations for the monomeric TDP-43 has zero intensity (Figure 1C). Why is this? A similar
confocal microscope should be able to detect single Atto647N. If the laser intensity or other optical settings have been
adjusted such that it is not detected, it must be explained because that is an arbitrary cutoff to the detectable particle size
range. 
3. Along the lines of the point above, the particles being detected are 100-200 nm in size, and are in no reasonable
interpretation “single molecules.” It seems like a stretch to describe the experiments as “single-molecule” if the actual single
molecules (i.e. monomers) are not even detected. “Single-particle” would be technically correct. 
4. Page 6, line 235 – “Intuitively, it makes sense that a larger object would take more time to cross the volume of detection.
However, because of Brownian motion, the relationship between the two parameters is non-trivial.” I don’t understand what
this means because Brownian motion is not complicated at all. For the confocal geometry, the diffusion coefficient D and the
residence time τ_D have the following relation: τ_D=s^2/4D where s is the radial focus dimension. This can be found in any
text on fluorescence spectroscopy. I don’t see why this should not apply to this case--please explain if it does not. 
5. Line 47, 292 – the authors state the biological relevance of condensate formation at sub-saturation concentrations.
However, it is difficult to consider this study as being translational to living systems given both the high concentration of
protein studied, and high concentration of small molecule chaperone required to induce condensation. TDP-43 is present at
~ 500 nM concentration in HeLa cells, however, this corresponds to the monomer control in the study where no
condensation was observed. Further, TMAO is present in healthy individuals in the single-digit micromolar regime--distant
from the molar concentrations used here. It would be appreciated if the authors were to contextualize/rationalize the model
system further in this regard. 
6. Lines 150 – 154 – It is noted that the smaller signals disappear readily as a function of 1,6-hexanediol concentration,
while larger signals persist, or increase. While a temporary (over the measured 10 min interval) increase or persistence is
rationalized by the authors, the authors should include data that shows the evolution of these signals when observed over
longer time periods. This would provide data to resolve the kinetic/thermodynamic uncertainty mentioned in lines 168/169.
Further, if the large signals are robust to longer time intervals or higher 1,6-hexanediol concentrations, then perhaps the
authors are in fact observing protein aggregates of TDP-43. 
7. Figure 4D – Between 8 and 10 mins for the sample with 0 M TMAO there is a dramatic increase in fluorescence intensity.
Is the concentration of protein being studied high enough to spontaneously LLPS in the absence of TMAO, or is there some
other process at play here? I would not expect the zero concentration point to evolve with time.

8. Figure 6C – The values describing the linear fit should be shown. It is strange that the authors have a greater abundance
of experimental data compared to simulated data. The authors should consider simulating additional data points (particularly
larger sizes) to validate the simulation relative to the observed results and demonstrate linearity.

9. There should be a space between a quantity and the unit. This rule is almost universally not followed. 
10. Please insert the figures in the text where they are referred to, as is now the norm.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have addressed some of the previously mentioned concerns. The authors’ responses to questions regarding
experimental details of labeled protein, LTE preparation, and protein adsorption are sufficient. The authors’ explanation of
the labeling methods are sufficient, but they are again encouraged to explicitly state that they are not working with “wild-type”
TDP43 LCD since the referenced publication for their source of protein primarily utilizes cysteine mutants. The authors'
expanded discussion of the reduced fluorescence in LTE is sufficient. 
The authors have added controls for the fluorescent probe and conducted experiments with two additional probes to
determine the influence of labeling. While the authors claim the fluorescent label does not affect the results, the control data
do not fully support that interpretation. I still have concerns regarding the use of the high fraction of dye labeled protein. The
control experiments with tenfold lower protein concentration raise serious questions about the effect of the fluorophore. The
authors clearly show in Figure S5 that the peak width in a sample with tenfold less labeled protein is significantly narrower
but do not address this at all. The results of the fluorescent probe controls have not convinced me that the concentration of
fluorophore used is affecting the results and hence could lead to misinterpretation of the system. 
Another major concern was the reversibility of the condensates and, thus, the assertion that no aggregate formation was
being observed. The authors attempt to address this by conducting experiments with ThT where they observe a small
fraction of ThT-positive assemblies. There is no difference in the peak intensity of the ThT-negative compared to ThT-
positive assemblies, but there is a significant difference in the width of these assemblies. These results convinces me that
those assemblies, although a small fraction, may be skewing the data analysis. This raises the question whether it would be
more appropriate to screen out all species over a certain size threshold.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

The hysteresis in the titration and dilution experiments remains unaddressed in the manuscript. The detectable (labeled)
protein in these experiments is not equivalent. In the case of titration, the probed protein is unchanged, with only the “dark”
unlabeled protein being added to the sample. In contrast, in the dilution experiment fresh labeled protein is being added. So,
in fact what is being probed is a mix of protein in preformed species re-equilibrating to a lower total protein concentration
AND monomeric labeled protein that is re-equilibrating to a higher protein concentration. It is not clear whether this is likely
to be important or if it was in some way controlled for. Indeed, it is not clear whether the kinetics of re-equilibration in the
titration vs. dilution experiments are the same. The authors’ argument that “If the system is reversible, we expect to see
similar values in the two cases as the system reequilibrates. Evidence that the system is dynamic is the fact that we observe
more assemblies upon dilution” is confusing and should be better explained. Additionally, were the data collected or
analyzed again? If so, why? I ask because the peak intensity plot in Figure S12 (formerly S8) is not the same as what was
initially provided, but the other plots remain identical. Most notably, the error is significantly smaller. This questions the linear
fit used to represent the dilution effect for a non-dynamic system. Would this fit cross more points in the old data set and thus
suggest the system is not as dynamic as the authors claim? 
The authors’ reanalysis of the peak width calibration curve is appreciated, but additional simulations are not an appropriate
substitute for additional data in the creation of a robust calibration curve. 
In conclusion, the changes to the manuscript did not fully address my concerns and did not to raise the significance of the
work to merit acceptance to Nature Communications.
