Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper addresses one of the central challenges underlying persistent discrepancies between measurement-based (“top-
down”) methane estimates and government-reported (“bottom-up”) emission-factor inventories. It is well established that
super-emitters are not adequately represented in inventory emission factors, and that limited knowledge of their frequency,
magnitude, and duration impedes efforts to reconcile top-down measurements with inventory-based estimates. 
The present study focuses on facility-level methane emissions in the New Mexico portion of the Delaware Basin, a major
sub-region of the Permian oil and gas basin. Using repeated aerial remote sensing observations, the study seeks to
characterize the frequency and duration of super-emitting infrastructure. While the results contribute to a clearer
understanding of the role of super-emitter events in this region, further clarification and revision are needed before the work
is ready for publication. 
Abstract: 
• The abstract should include a clear definition of “oil and gas methane super-emitters” as applied in this study. 
• It would also be valuable to highlight one key finding that directly addresses the study’s main objective—namely, what is
the characteristic intensity and duration of super-emitters in this portion of the Permian Basin? 
Results: 
• Page 6, lines 164–165: The reported total uncertainty (<10%) appears low. Are additional systematic uncertainties
considered, and if not, should they be? 
• It is unclear whether the analysis assumes that all methane detections originate from oil and gas sources, or whether
source attribution was conducted to exclude other potential contributors (e.g., landfills or concentrated animal feeding

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

operations). The same clarification is needed for the TROPOMI-based estimates on page 7: do these represent all methane
emissions, or specifically oil and gas–related methane? 
• Figure 3 illustrates the challenge of extrapolating from limited observations (single or multiple scans) to annual emission
totals, particularly given the variability associated with the “fat-tail” distribution of super-emitters. Based on the data
presented, can the authors comment on the minimum number of scans required to reliably characterize the contribution of
super-emitters (e.g., lines 222–223)? 
• Page 10, “Comparing time-integrated event emissions to time-averaged totals”: This section could be restructured to more
directly address the central research question—what is the typical intensity and duration of a super-emitter in this region,
based on this campaign? This finding is at the core of the paper, yet it becomes obscured by the extended discussion of
assumptions and methodological alternatives. 
Discussion: 
• Lines 378–380: The finding that super-emitters contribute approximately 50% of total emissions is a significant result and
should be highlighted explicitly in the abstract. 
• The study shows that super-emitters are typically intermittent and of short duration. Are the differences between “shortest”
and “longest” duration assumptions approximately equivalent to the differences expected when comparing scenarios in
which super-emitter duration is explicitly accounted for versus assuming continuous (100% persistent) emissions? It would
strengthen the manuscript if the authors commented on how their findings regarding super-emitter duration could be
incorporated into improved estimates of annual methane emissions, particularly with respect to quantifying the contribution
of super-emitters to total basin-level emissions.

Line 356. These results underscore the importance of proper duration estimation 
for site-level accounting across the complex set of processes that lead to super-
emissions. Assuming a single duration value for all super-emitter events can 
severely bias aggregate accounting of emissions. For example, if we apply a 2.4 
hour duration, the value derived for finite short-lived events, to all detected 
events in this study, the regional aggregate emission estimate would be 0.21 Gg, 
whereas applying a single 72 hour duration to all detected events would result in 
6.4 Gg, not dissimilar to the shortest-duration integrated estimate in Figure 4a. 
Future study could connect durations we derived to operator-level information 
where available, so that more characteristic durations can be applied 
appropriately to specific processes. However, given the continuing uncertainty, 
we do find clear evidence in this study that a small but significant fraction of 
super-emitters last days to weeks, and that short-lived super-emitter events likely
