Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a very interesting and valuable study. The authors established a visceral pain model—neonatal colonic inflammation
(NCI)—using an early-life stress paradigm. Music has long been used to relieve stress and induce pleasant emotions. In
recent years, its regulatory functions—such as promoting sleep and alleviating pain—have gained increasing attention.
However, the precise underlying neural mechanisms remain largely unclear. 
In this study, Yu et al. demonstrated that classical and electronic music, but not rock music, significantly increased the
visceral pain threshold in NCI mice. Through c-Fos staining, viral tracing, and optogenetic and chemogenetic manipulation,
the authors showed that GABAergic neurons in the auditory cortex (ACx) were activated and subsequently inhibited
glutamatergic neurons in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). Visceral pain, such as that seen in irritable bowel syndrome, is
often stress-related. Thus, music may offer a unique advantage in treating visceral pain by simultaneously alleviating both
emotional and sensory components. 
While the findings are exciting, several aspects of the conclusions require further clarification. Below are my major and minor
comments: 
Major Comments 
Involvement of the Insular Cortex (IC): 
The ACC is well-documented in pain processing; however, the authors observed dense projections from the ACx to the
insular cortex (IC), which is also strongly implicated in both somatic and visceral pain processing. Given the IC's well-
established role in interoception and visceral sensation, the authors should justify why they focused on the ACC instead of
the IC. Furthermore, it would be valuable to examine whether the ACx–IC pathway plays a role in music-induced visceral
analgesia. 
Need for Electrophysiological Evidence: 
The authors assessed neural activation in the ACx and ACC using c-Fos staining, a classical but indirect marker of activity.
To enhance the strength of the conclusions, electrophysiological validation—particularly in vivo recordings—should be
provided to directly assess real-time changes in neuronal activity during music exposure. 
Specificity of Auditory Cortex Neuronal Activation: 
The authors reported that Au1 neurons showed minimal responses to tactile stimulation, which is expected. However, in the
NCI model, the number of c-Fos–positive neurons in Au1 significantly increased. This raises the question: are the activated
neurons in the auditory cortex specifically involved in visceral pain processing? The authors should investigate whether
these neurons are also engaged in other pain modalities, such as inflammatory or neuropathic pain. 
Potential Stress-Induced Analgesia: 
As the authors noted, it is unclear whether mice perceive music similarly to humans. For mice, music may act as a form of
sound-induced stress. Given that stress is known to induce analgesia, the authors should examine whether classical and
electronic music exposure induces stress responses in mice, which could confound interpretation of the analgesic effects. 
Minor Comments 
Please replace the images showing co-localization of RV signals with glutamate in Figure 3D to enhance clarity. 
In Figures 1P and 2J, statistical data for the control group should be included for comparison. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript “A Primary Auditory Cortex–Anterior Cingulate Cortex Circuit Underlying Cross-Modal Visceral Pain
Modulation,” Yu et al. demonstrated that music therapy produced analgesia in adult mice with visceral pain. They further
delineated the underlying circuit and microcircuit mechanisms: music stimulation enhanced the activity of GABAergic
neurons in the auditory cortex, which in turn suppressed glutamatergic neurons in both the auditory cortex and the anterior
cingulate cortex. Overall, the study is compelling, featuring significant novelty, robust data, and a clear narrative.
Nevertheless, I have several concerns that, if addressed, would further improve the quality of the work. 
Majors: 
1. The authors emphasize the analgesic effect of sound on visceral pain. However, the study’s real significance, in my view,
is that music therapy produce analgesia in adult mice suffered from early-life stress attack. Indeed, sound stimulation does
not relieve CRD-evoked visceral pain in normally reared mice, and the analgesic effect of music is only observed in
NCI+CRD animals. This nicely explains why music therapy benefits only a subset of patients in the clinic. 
Another intriguing question within this framework is whether the analgesic effect of music therapy against combined early-

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

life and adult stress is restricted to visceral pain. The authors propose that neonatal stress reduces the number and activity of
GABA neurons in the auditory cortex, and that music exposure in adult mice can rescue the activity of GABA neurons. If this
mechanism operates via modulation of GABAergic circuits in the auditory cortex, it may not be exclusive for visceral
nociception. The authors should therefore perform an additional experiment: after early-life NCI, deliver a somatic pain
challenge (e.g., CFA plantar injection) in adult mice and test whether music therapy still produces analgesia. Given that
music engages the auditory cortical GABA network, such an effect is plausible. Besides, the authors named the title of
manuscript as ‘…underlying cross-modal … modulation’. However, the whole work did not really represent the cross-model
modulation, if they test both visceral and somatic pain, then this statement should be more proper. 
I believe that reorganizing the whole paper outlines and results data around this perspective would substantially enhance
the work’s novelty and value and would broaden the therapeutic relevance of their findings.. 
2. In a previous Science paper (2022, 377:198–204), the authors show that inhibition of the glutamatergic auditory cortex -
thalamus (VP and PO) pathway induces analgesia. They also show that music alleviate the activity of these Glu projecting
neurons. This study further demonstrates that music exerts its analgesic effect by directly enhancing the activity of
GABAergic neurons in the auditory cortex, thereby indirectly suppressing glutamatergic neurons. These findings provide an
important mechanistic complement to the Science paper. This point should be explicitly addressed in the Discussion. 
In addition, in the Science study, the authors show that 5 dB, rather than 15 dB, white noise produces analgesia, indicating
that sound intensity is crucial for the analgesic effect. However, the present work does not address sound intensity at all.
Moreover, different music genres differ in efficacy (classical, but not rock, music is analgesic), yet the manuscript offers no
convincing explanation. This issue should be addressed by further experiments. 
Minors: 
1. On page 12, line 306, the authors state, “Since Au1 exhibited minimal involvement in direct encoding of visceral pain, it is
inferred that the role of Au1 in visceral pain modulation likely depends on its projections to other brain regions encoding
visceral pain.” This reasoning is weak. While I agree that probing the downstream projections of Au1 is critical for elucidating
the mechanism underlying the auditory cortex–mediated analgesia, the justification cannot be that Au1 itself is poorly
activated by visceral pain. If neurons in Au1 do not participate in pain processing, their projections are unlikely to do so
either. 
2. In Fig. 2B, the color of the circles indicates different density of EGFP-expressing fibers, but what does the size of the
circles represent? 
3. In the model cartoon, the authors depict two balance scales: in the orange one, the GABA-laden side is shown as heavier
than the glutamate-laden side, implying the E/I imbalance in the Au induced hypersensitivity of nociception. According to the
study’s findings, this should be reversed—the GABA side should actually be lighter.

narrative. Nevertheless, I have several concerns that, if addressed, would further

Majors:  
1. The authors emphasize the analgesic effect of sound on visceral pain. However, the 
study’s real significance, in my view, is that music therapy produce analgesia in adult 
mice suffered from early-life stress attack. Indeed, sound stimulation does not relieve 
CRD-evoked visceral pain in normally reared mice, and the analgesic effect of music 
is only observed in NCI+CRD animals. This nicely explains why music therapy benefits 
only a subset of patients in the clinic.  
Another intriguing question within this framework is whether the analgesic effect of 
music therapy against combined early-life and adult stress is restricted to visceral pain. 
The authors propose that neonatal stress reduces the number and activity of GABA 
neurons in the auditory cortex, and that music exposure in adult mice can rescue the 
activity of GABA neurons. If this mechanism operates via modulation of GABAergic 
circuits in the auditory cortex, it may not be exclusive for visceral nociception. The 
authors should therefore perform an additional experiment: after early-life NCI, deliver 
a somatic pain challenge (e.g., CFA plantar injection) in adult mice and test whether 
music therapy still produces analgesia. Given that music engages the auditory cortical 
GABA network, such an effect is plausible. Besides, the authors named the title of 
manuscript as ‘…underlying cross-modal … modulation’. However, the whole work 
did not really represent the cross-modal modulation, if they test both visceral and 
somatic pain, then this statement should be more proper. 
I believe that reorganizing the whole paper outlines and results data around this 
perspective would substantially enhance the work’s novelty and value and would 
broaden the therapeutic relevance of their findings.

In addition, in the Science study, the authors show that 5 dB, rather than 15 dB, white

Thanks for your comments. Indeed, our findings advance the insights of the Science 
paper by providing an important mechanistic complement that music potentiates the 
activity of Au1 GABAergic neurons, which in turn suppress local glutamatergic output. 
This has been highlighted on page 23 in the revised manuscript. 
In our study, the sound pressure level of music was setup at 50-55 dB, only 10 dB above 
ambient noise level (40-45 dB), consistent with that Science paper. As our focus was to 
dissect the underlying neural circuitry mechanism, we did not explore the influence of 
higher sound pressure level. 
Regarding the differential analgesic outcomes across music genres, we proposed 
acoustic parameters (such as tempo) as an explanation in our initial submission. We 
therefore have conducted additional experiments to assess the effect of slower classical 
music and faster rock music. The Beats per Minute (BPM) of the identical classical 
music piece was digitally slowed to 91, while the rock piece was accelerated to 160 
(Table S1). Interestingly, slower classical music lost its analgesic efficacy, whereas 
faster rock music acquired analgesic effect (Figure S4H&I), suggesting that tempo is a 
critical parameter for music therapy.
