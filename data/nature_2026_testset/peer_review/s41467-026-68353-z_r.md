Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
## Overview of the Manuscript 
The manuscript investigates the effects of dietary carbohydrate-to-fat ratio (CFR) on metabolomic profiles, treating CFR as
either an ordinal or continuous variable to assess monotonic trends. The study employs a crossover design and a post-
weight-loss dietary intervention to evaluate metabolic shifts across different CFR levels. The authors use Partial Least
Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) to classify participants based on metabolomic changes and conduct enrichment
analyses to identify key metabolic pathways associated with dietary CFR. While the study provides valuable insights into
diet-metabolite interactions, several methodological aspects warrant further clarification, particularly regarding CFR
modeling choices, adherence fluctuations, and metabolite selection methods. 
--- 
## **1\. CFR as an Ordinal vs. Continuous Variable** 
It remains unclear whether CFR was modeled as an **ordinal variable (low/medium/high coded as 1, 2, 3) or a continuous
variable (e.g., 1/3, 1, 3)** in the linear models. A similar clarification is needed for the **crossover study** to ensure
consistency in interpretation. The authors should explicitly state their approach and, if applicable, discuss any sensitivity
analyses performed to assess how this choice impacts the findings. 
--- 
## **2\. Treatment Heterogeneity Within Dietary Arms** 
While the study effectively controls macronutrient intake through prescribed dietary regimens, participants had flexibility in
food choices within each macronutrient category. This introduces potential **treatment heterogeneity** that could influence
metabolomic responses via differences in micronutrient intake or bioactive compounds. The authors should acknowledge
this limitation and discuss its potential impact on causal interpretation. 
--- 
## **3\. Missing Data and Its Potential Biological Significance** 
The authors apply missingness thresholds, removing metabolites with **\>30% missing values** and samples with **\>25%
missing values**. While standard, this approach assumes missing data is random. If a metabolite is systematically
undetected in one diet group but present in another, this could reflect a **true biological effect rather than a technical
artifact**. The authors should explore whether missingness patterns correlate with diet and discuss their potential biological
relevance. 
--- 
## **4\. PLS-DA and Biomarker Selection (Figure 5)** 
The study employs PLS-DA to classify participants based on metabolomic profiles, optimizing separation using
Mahalanobis distance. However, PLS-DA does not inherently perform feature selection, raising the following concerns: 
- **How many PLS components were retained?** Reporting a **scree plot or cross-validation curve** would clarify whether
additional components captured meaningful biological variation or noise. 
- **Could feature selection be improved?** Alternative methods such as **sparse PLS-DA (sPLS-DA), LASSO regression, or
Random Forest** could enhance metabolite selection robustness. 
- **Are the identified biomarkers predictive?** Since the authors propose these metabolites as potential **biomarkers of
dietary adherence**, validating their predictive value using **logistic regression with L1 penalty** or another classification
model would strengthen the findings. 
--- 
## **5\. Duration-Dependent Metabolite Changes (Figure 4)** 
The manuscript examines **early (MID-START) vs. entire (END-START) test phase** metabolite changes to assess
metabolic adaptation. However, some metabolites are significant **only at END**, raising key questions: 
- **Could these metabolites represent delayed responses (i.e., metabolic lag effects)?**

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

- **Do these delayed-response metabolites cluster within specific pathways (e.g., lipids vs. amino acids)?** 
A discussion on why certain metabolites take longer to respond would enhance the interpretation of metabolic adaptation
and distinguish between **immediate vs. sustained dietary effects**. 
--- 
## **6\. Adherence Fluctuations and Their Impact on Metabolite Trends** 
The study defines **non-adherence** as a weight change exceeding **2kg from START to END**, but it does not evaluate
adherence fluctuations at **MID**. Given that some metabolites exhibit **non-monotonic trends**, adherence variability may
be influencing the observed results. 
- **Could transient non-adherence at MID affect metabolite trends?** If participants deviated mid-phase but returned to
adherence by END, metabolite responses might not follow a clean trajectory. 
- **Would removing early non-adherers alter results?** If non-adherence fluctuates, dietary CFR effects may be diluted by
transient deviations. 
- **Should adherence be assessed at MID?** The study does not report whether weight deviations >2kg occurred at MID. If
such fluctuations exist, a **MID-specific threshold** (possibly <2kg) could better capture early non-adherence. 
- **Would a weight trajectory plot help?** A figure showing **individual weight changes across START, MID, and END**
could clarify whether deviations were gradual, fluctuating, or abrupt, improving adherence analysis. 
A more nuanced **adherence assessment at MID** could refine the study’s ability to link metabolite trends to dietary CFR
rather than uncontrolled variability. 
--- 
## **Conclusion** 
This manuscript presents a valuable contribution to understanding diet-metabolite interactions, but additional **clarifications
on CFR modeling, treatment heterogeneity, missing data, PLS-DA methodology, and adherence variability** would
strengthen its findings. Addressing these concerns would improve the interpretability and robustness of the conclusions.

We appreciate the reviewer’s suggestion to try to further elucidate the causal relationships between metabolomic changes and changes in other metabolic biomarkers, especially those with established relationships with health outcomes. While such an analysis is in principle possible, it depends on assumptions concerning the biological relationship between the diet intervention-affected metabolites and specific disease-related biomarkers. We also note that in this study, we cannot examine causes of change in BMI, because energy intake was adjusted to maintain a constant amount of weight loss, thereby keeping BMI stable during the diet intervention. Moreover, the sample collection structure in the RCT lacks the temporal resolution to use purely data-driven approaches to establish causal relationships based on the time sequence of molecular changes.  Despite these caveats, we agree that learning the causal relationship between the different diet-induced metabolic adaptations and their health consequences would be of interest. Therefore, based on the reviewer’s comment, we have included mediation analyses in the revised version of the manuscript. Specifically, we evaluated which metabolite changes might mediate the effect of varying dietary fat-to-carbohydrate ratio on cardiometabolic risk biomarkers (specifically, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, non-HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, lipoprotein(a), lipoprotein insulin resistance index, 1,5-anhydroglucitol, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, diastolic blood pressure, and systolic blood pressure). As future work that is outside the scope of this manuscript, we plan to comprehensively investigate the future metabolic health consequences associated with metabolites identified as diet-responsive in this study, and we will perform genome-wide association studies to identify leverage variants associated with the metabolites to use as instrumental variables that could provide stronger evidence for causality via Mendelian randomization. Thus, the present study provides a foundational step for these and other future research efforts. Response: We thank the Reviewer for the thoughtful comments regarding the ±2 kg threshold and the opportunity to clarify and support our chosen approach. First, we would like to emphasize that the ±2 kg threshold was not an arbitrary or post-hoc criterion. Rather, it was pre-specified in the (FS)2 protocol as part of the feeding RCT design. During the test phase, participants' energy intake was periodically adjusted to maintain body weight within ±2 kg of the stabilized post–run-in weight. This range was used to guide dietary adjustments and thus served as a surrogate marker of adherence to the test diets, rather than as a study outcome. For the present manuscript, the ±2 kg threshold was applied in the sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness and consistency of the metabolite-diet associations observed in the main analysis. Since this threshold was used solely as a surrogate marker of adherence in these secondary analyses, the statistical power of the primary analysis (to identify diet-associated metabolites) was not affected by the choice of threshold.  However, to further address concerns regarding the potentially arbitrary nature of the ±2 kg threshold, we conducted additional secondary sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds (±1.5 kg, ±2.5 kg, and ±3 kg), using identical methods to those used in the main and sensitivity (using the 2kg threshold) analyses. The correlation coefficients comparing metabolite changes using each alternative threshold with using the ±2 kg threshold were consistently high (all rho > 0.92). Moreover, all FDR-significant metabolites exhibited consistent trends across all evaluated thresholds. We now present details in updated Methods and Results sections and the newly added Supplementary Table 11. The strong correlations and concordant findings across different thresholds thus support the robustness of our results and the appropriateness of retaining the pre-specified ±2 kg threshold. To address the Reviewer’s suggestion about adherence fluctuations at the MID time point and their potential impact on metabolite patterns, we conducted additional analyses to assess whether incorporating weight change at MID into the adherence definition would have an effect on missclassification rates for identifying the correct diet assignment via analysis of metabolites.  In our manuscript, we considered weight gain >2 kg from START to END as a surrogate marker of lower adherence, based on the observation that participants who maintained weight loss or did not exceed this weight gain threshold were more likely to be correctly classified by the predictive models. Specifically, weight gain >2 kg at END (regardless of weight change at MID) was associated with significantly higher missclassification rates, with the odds of a correct prediction approximately 81% lower in this group compared to those who did not gain >2 kg at the END (OR = 0.189, p = 0.016).
