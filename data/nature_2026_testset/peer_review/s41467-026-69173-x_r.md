Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript "Genomics of rafting crustaceans in tropical oceans: adapting to marine climate change" through ocean
current dispersal simulation, population genomics, molecular evolution and 
22 salinity stress experiments proposes to evaluate the rafting dynamics on a direct-development species distributed across
the Indo-Australian Archipelago. Using climatic and dispersal models, the authors predict that the species will be able to
adapt to changes in salinity levels driven by shifting oceanic patterns. 
This is a well-written and thoroughly presented study, with analyses that are mostly soundly performed. I recommend it for
publication, provided the following points are clarified and addressed: 
Line 394: The manuscript does not specify how linkage disequilibrium was accounted for during the filtering process. This is
a crucial detail, as linkage disequilibrium can significantly impact demographic and selection estimates. Please clarify this
aspect. 
Line 494: The MAF cut-off threshold appears to be very conservative, potentially excluding important rare variants in coding
regions. Could you provide a rationale for this choice and discuss its implications for your results? 
Line 516 and throughout the manuscript: Replace the term "adaptive SNPs" with alternatives such as "SNPs putatively
under selection" or "putative adaptive SNPs". The association results do not necessarily confirm that these SNPs are
adaptive. Additionally, it is unclear how "adaptive SNPs" were defined—did you consider the union of results from LFMM
and RDA, or only the 1,180 SNPs identified as common to both methods? The latter, being more conservative, would likely
be the more robust approach. Please clarify and justify this choice. 
Lastly, the bioproject/biosample SRA numbers should be publicly available. 
(Remarks on code availability) 
The codes for evaluating the ocean currents dynamics are presented, bur not for the DE analyses. The latter is partially
described on methods section. I would suggest that the authors add this one to the figshare repository as well.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Given my limited expertise in the broader scope of this study, my evaluations focus primarily on the application of
Lagrangian particle tracking. 
Overall evaluation: 
This study employs OpenDrift, a widely used particle tracking package, to simulate the ocean current dispersal trajectory
and indicate the possible drift trajectory of T. martensii, which rafts on driftwood. The approach provides valuable insights
into the potential rafting pathways of this species. However, I have one major comment that I believe warrants further
discussion in the manuscript: 
Could the authors comment on the accuracy of using numerical particles without mass or volume to simulate the trajectory of
driftwood, which may have varying sizes and shapes? Are there previous studies or evidence supporting the use of a 2%
wind drift factor for simulating rafting driftwood with numerical particle tracking? A discussion addressing this question would

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript provides a thorough investigation of the genomics of rafting crustaceans, detailing how ocean dispersal
impacts gene flow, what genes are associated with adaptation, how those genes are expressed, and how this might
translate to future maladaptation. The strength of this study is the interdisciplinary and comprehensive set of methods across
multiple fields to understand the genomics of a rafting species, T. martensii. The authors integrate ecological and population
genetics, dispersal simulation, ocean current modeling, and salinity stress experiments. The most robust findings include the
recovery of 5 populations of T. martensii in the Indo-Australian Archipelago, determination of divergence times and effective
population size for this species, and identification of wood-feeding genes required for energy intake during long rafting
journeys. 
While there is evidence supporting the above mentioned conclusions and claims in the manuscript, there are a number of
other claims that could be problematic. In general, this study highlights the importance of plasticity for the species in variable
salinity environments, but the genomic maladaptation framework assumes no plasticity in the prediction. This contradiction
calls into question the validity of the genomic offset analysis. The salinity stress experiment and subsequent plasticity
analyses also use a terrestrial species rather than the marine rafting species, calling into question the applicability of these
results to rafting species. Finally, the dispersal simulation results are unclear. More detail on these issues is provided below.
Lack of framework: The claim in the abstract that this paper provides a framework for studying adaptive shifts and
predictability of adaptation seems a little strong; while analyses are comprehensive, no actual framework is proposed within
the text.

Concerns with GEA analysis: The genotype-environment associations (GEAs) could also benefit from using the more up-to-
date multivariate implementation of LFMM from the LEA3 package, and the authors should also note that recent work has
shown RDA is not the most reliable method for GEA analysis, though much of the field still uses these methods (see
Lotterhos 2023). GEA additionally assumes that each population occupies only a single environment, which is not true for
highly migratory rafting species experiencing large environmental fluctuations. 
Limitations of Genomic offsets analysis: Genomic offset modeling assumes no plasticity, so the fact that significant plasticity
was found under experimental conditions undermines the genomic offset results and should be discussed. Furthermore,
much like GEA, genomic offset assumes that populations occupy and are locally adapted to a single environment. Again, as
a rafting species, T. martensii experience large environmental fluctuations and local adaptation may be limited in this
species. Finally, while forward and reverse genetic offset methods are appropriate for species that can disperse, how
dispersal and genomic offset modeling are integrated using ∆D is unclear from the methods. In general, genomic offset
predictions have only been empirically tested in a few systems (mostly trees), and these tests have yielded results spanning
from inaccurate predictions to relatively accurate predictions. Some empirical validation is needed before using genomic
offsets in this system to predict maladaptation. 
Interpretability of gene flow and dispersal modeling: The dispersal and current modeling analyses are unclear, specifically
what ∆D actually means - while the authors explain that this is a dispersal direction deviation, it is unclear why dispersal
direction would be different from current direction if this species truly lacks autonomous dispersal and instead disperses
passively. Figure 2 shows the quantified model and particle trajectory simulations, but does not clarify how ∆D is calculated -
is it averaged across all modeled cells, or specific to just the final cell? Furthermore, when doing the gene flow model
testing, the authors use K=3 despite recovering K=5 in previous analyses. 
Limitations of Plasticity analysis: The salinity stress experiments use a terrestrial species from a different genus than T.
martensii, which is the focal species of the rest of the manuscript. While the plasticity analyses themselves seem sound, this
calls into question the applicability of these results to marine rafting species, particularly T. martensii. At a minimum the
discussion should address how applicable the salinity stress experiments are to the focal species of the manuscript.
Additionally, there is significant evidence that plasticity can promote acclimation, but theoretical work suggests that it can
also be harmful in the long term by precluding the need for species to genetically adapt to stress (see Ghalambor et. al.
2007). The authors should caveat these results in relevant sections of the discussion. 
Missing details and code: Because the manuscript covers so many topics and fields, detail is sometimes lacking in the main
text as to what some of the terms and results actually mean. For example, the distinction between forward and reverse
genetic offset is unclear in the main text, and this section would benefit from definitions of these terms that are biologically
relevant. This was just one example, but other result and discussion sections would also benefit from additional detail. While
the text is already long, clarifying terminology and what each analysis can actually do is important given that it is unlikely that
any one reader will be an expert in every field covered in the manuscript. 
The written methods section is detailed and thorough. However, the provided code does not include all of the analyses;
scripts for the DEG analysis, DAPC, selective sweep analysis, quantification of ocean current dynamics, gene ontology
enrichment analysis, LFMM, and RDA were not in the zip file - additionally, the demographic analysis and ocean current drift
modeling have no provided scripts, though again, the methods are detailed in the text. Inclusion of code used to conduct
these analyses is necessary to ensure the reproducibility of the work and to ensure that the analysis is sound. 
Overall: Overall, the work integrates concepts across the fields of oceanography and ecological genomics to gain a more
comprehensive understanding of how rafting species disperse, adapt, and respond to shifting climates, particularly ocean
currents and salinity. While some of the conclusions of the work are well supported by the analyses conducted, many others
are not, namely the genetic offsets, the salinity experiments and subsequent plasticity analyses, and the dispersal modeling.
In addition to potential reanalysis of these data and separation of the experimental salinity work from the population genomic
and dispersal/current modeling work, this manuscript would benefit from a more nuanced discussion of the caveats and
limitations of the methods used and the results found, and from more explicit descriptions and definitions of relevant
terminology and analyses in the main body of the text.

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study investigates how the rafting crustacean Talorchestia martensii adapts to climate change in the Indo-Australian
Archipelago. The authors combined ecological modeling and genomics to explore the dispersal patterns and adaptive
mechanisms of rafting species under rapid environmental changes. From 203 samples collected across the region, five
geographically distinct T. martensii populations were identified. Simulations revealed stronger northward migration patterns
to shape the populations compared to random or southward dispersal. Using a quantified model and particle trajectory
simulations, the authors further confirmed asymmetric northward gene flow, particularly during summer monsoons,
consistent with seasonal ocean currents. 
Genomic data from the identified populations revealed that salinity fluctuations acted as a major selective pressure during
rafting dispersal. The authors utilized transcriptome data from salinity stress experiments to identify genes associated with
salinity adaptation, such as GRIK2 and SLC6A13. However, the lack of statistical significance tests and functional validation
raises concerns regarding the robustness of these findings. 
Overall, this is a well-executed study on the adaptation of rafting crustaceans, and the manuscript should be considered for
publication after addressing the following comments: 
The manuscript applies various bioinformatics tools, which are essential for obtaining reliable results. However, listing all
software without explanation in the main text is distracting. I recommend moving the software to the Methods section and
briefly explaining the conceptual rationale behind each analysis in the main text. 
Line 180: 
Could the authors provide a list of GO terms enriched in salinity-associated genes? It is unclear whether these
genes represent false positive associations or are truly enriched in certain biological functions.

Line 186: The duration of salinity stress experiments (24, 48, and 72 hours) seems too short to reflect long-term acclimation.
Given that rafting voyages can last for months, a longer experimental period would better represent natural conditions. 
Lines 190-192: The manuscript highlights a few selected differentially expressed genes (DEGs). However, what is the
general expression pattern for all DEGs? Cherry-picking without functional validation makes it difficult to draw robust
conclusions. 
Line 215: It would be interesting to see how gene expression changes under longer treatments. This could provide insights
into whether plastic responses increase over time. 
Line 218: Are there significance tests for these genes? Including statistical validation would strengthen the interpretation of
results. 
Lines 332-333: This section seems a bit weak to me. The discussion on wood degradation genes seems narrowly focused
on a few candidates. Whole-genome comparisons between rafting and non-rafting species would help identify a broader set
of key genes involved in driftwood digestion. 
The discussion is fairly weak, I would expect to see more discussion on how biotic interactions (e.g., competition) affect
rafting success; comparisons between salinity adaptation mechanisms in T. martensii and other marine species; the
evolutionary history of GRIK2 and its role in other species. 
Lines 348-349: Could the authors quantify the expression dosage of duplicated genes in T. martensii and compare it to
species with fewer gene copies?

methods in marine systems, although a number of claims by the authors need revision before this can be published. First,
offsets do not measure or predict “long-term evolutionary responses to broader environmental variation”, nor do they
measure “genetic variations required for long-term adaptation of future climate change”. Rather, they are a measure of an
environmental distance, weighted by environments that correlate most strongly with genetic structure, and the interpretation
is debated (see papers by Matt Fitzpatrick, papers by Lotterhos, especially Lotterhos 2024 Interpretation issues with
“genomic vulnerability” arise from conceptual issues in local adaptation and maladaptation, see Rellstab et al 2021:
Prospects and limitations of genomic offset in conservation management, see Hoffman et al: Opportunities and challenges in
assessing climate change vulnerability through genomics). In addition, whether the finding of strong outlier loci justifies the
use of genomic offsets is debatable: whether offsets correlate with fitness changes in new environments depends on
patterns of local adaptation in the metapopulation, the fitness landscape, and other biological responses such as plasticity.
In addition, despite many other studies predicting genomic offset to future climates, it is important to note that these analyses
do not account for adaptive evolution, instead assuming an instantaneous environmental change. Thus, genomic offsets are
not necessarily a reflection of

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
The study "Genomics of rafting crustaceans in tropical oceans: adapting to marine 
climate change" uses an interdisciplinary approach combining ecogenomics, 
population genetics, dispersal and oceanography simulations and modeling, and 
salinity stress experiments. The authors have satisfactorily addressed all comments. 
My only concern is that the LD threshold used appears too low, and I would expect a 
justification for this choice in the Methods section. Statistically, an r² ≥ 0.5 is 
considered sufficient to avoid LD, as values above 0.5 occur in less than 14% of the 
allelic frequency space (VanLieren & Rosenberg 2008. Theor. Popul. Biol. 74:130). 
Using a lower threshold can only be justified in specific cases, such as very large 
sampled populations. A brief explanation of this choice should be added to the text. 
That aside, I consider the manuscript acceptable for publication. 
Response: Thank you for your wonderful suggestions. In the Plink software, --ld-
window-r2 controls the r2 threshold for a variant-pair to be included in the report.  The 
default setting is 0.2. Although this value might be considered relatively low, when we 
used a window-sliding based method in 50bp, using an r² threshold of 0.2 helps to 
reduce the impact of physical linkage while preserving enough independent markers 
for the following population structure inference. We also employed an r² threshold of 
0.5 for population structure analysis, and found that the inferred structure (K=5) was 
nearly identical to that derived from the two comparison values. 
We have changed the sentence in the Materials and methods section as “We 
further excluded highly correlated SNPs by performing linkage disequilibrium based 
SNP pruning in PLINK v.1.9.078 with a default pairwise r2 threshold 0.2. This 
threshold reduces LD-induced bias while preserving enough independent genetic 
markers to accurately infer population structure, an approach previously used in 
marine genomic studies79.”
