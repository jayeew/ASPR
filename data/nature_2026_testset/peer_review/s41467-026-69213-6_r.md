Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study introduces an innovative paper-based fluorescent assay (PFA) for blood typing and antibody titer measurement.
Unlike traditional methods, it utilizes freeze-dried bioengineered red blood cells, enabling long-term ambient storage. The
PFA, which delivers results in approximately 8 minutes without complex equipment or subjective interpretation,
demonstrated 100% accuracy in ABO blood typing and high concordance with standard methods in clinical tests, promising

safer blood transfusions in emergency or resource-limited settings. Its design also facilitates standardization and integration
with digital and AI-assisted analysis. 
However, there are come 
comments that could strengthen the paper. 
1. In general, the section related to membrane integrity should be reinforced. Although the results provide strong validation of
the system, this aspect would benefit from being revised or presented in a different manner by the authors. 
• In line 272, the sentence “TEM results for RBCbm@Fluo (Fig. 1a) confirmed the integrity of the cell membrane” is not
entirely accurate. The TEM images provided are not sufficient to support such a statement, and it is recommended that this
phrase be removed. 
• Line 285: Figure 2f shows significant differences between membranes, and in both the bm and bm-Fluo conditions, a well-
defined membrane is not observed as it is in the other samples. Similarly, in the confocal images (Fig. 2g), actin appears
disrupted. 
• Line 331: This statement contradicts earlier claims that the membrane remains intact. 
2. Although the paper reports 100% accuracy for ABO blood typing (which is excellent), the 80.75% concordance with the
reference method for antibody titration is a point that requires further analysis and justification. 
Is this level of concordance acceptable in clinical practice for all antibody titration applications (e.g., in monitoring
pregnancies with Rh isoimmunization, where precision is critical)? What are the implications of the 19.25% discordance?
Are these due to inherent methodological differences, variability in the reference methods, or limitations of the PFA in certain
titer ranges? The discussion should explore the causes of this discordance in greater depth and clarify whether there are
plans to improve this rate, or if the method is considered "good enough" for its intended applications—with a clear rationale.
Comparing this performance with the known intra- and inter-laboratory variability of traditional methods would also
strengthen the argument. 
3. The paper predominantly focuses on the detection of IgM anti-A and anti-B antibodies. Although it mentions the potential
to "disrupt current antibody detection techniques for IgG/IgM," it does not elaborate on the system’s actual capability or future
plans to detect IgG antibodies, which are critical in many clinical contexts (e.g., hemolytic disease of the newborn, delayed
transfusion reactions). 
Is the method equally effective for detecting IgG antibodies? If not, what are the challenges in adapting the system for IgG
detection? Is this approach primarily designed for a specific application where IgM detection is more relevant? 
4. While the practicality of ambient-temperature storage is emphasized, the paper does not sufficiently address the
challenges or considerations involved in the large-scale production of the bioengineered RBCs and the PFA devices. 
Is the bioengineering process for RBCs easily scalable to meet mass demand? Are there significant regulatory or cost-
related considerations associated with the industrial production of these reagents and devices? 
5. The clinical study was conducted with a considerable number of samples; however, it is not specified whether these
samples came from a diverse population or a single geographic region. The prevalence of certain blood groups or antigenic
variants can vary significantly across populations. 
Was the method tested on samples from individuals of different ethnic backgrounds or with less common antigenic variants? 
6. Overall, it is recommended that the manuscript be reviewed carefully to correct grammatical errors, spacing issues, and
other minor formatting inconsistencies.

Response: We thank the reviewer for emphasizing the importance of situating 
our work within the broader development of paper -based blood typing diagnostics. 
Comprehensive overviews of this field can be found in the reviews by Then et al. 
(V ox Sanguinis, 2015, 108(2):186–196)[1] and Ebrahimi Fana et al. (Biomedical 
Microdevices, 2021,  23(3):34)[2], which summarize advances in paper -based 
analytical devices for blood group phenotyping and highlight ongoing efforts toward 
miniaturization and point-of-care implementation. 
Commercially, two principal categories of paper -based diagnostics a re currently 
available. Consumer-oriented cards (e.g., EldonCard) are widely used for self -testing 
and donor screening, providing ABO/Rh results within minutes through visible 
agglutination. Professional rapid -test formats (e.g., GLIF -type devices) utilize  
capillary flow and spreading kinetics to achieve ABO/Rh typing in roughly one 
minute. These platforms are rapid, convenient, and well established, but they remain 
restricted to forward typing, relying on immobilized anti -A/anti-B/anti-D antibodies 
and yielding primarily qualitative results. 
In the academic literature, various research groups have attempted to improve 
analytical accuracy, reproducibility, and usability. Wanida Laiwattanapaisal [3] 
combined wax printing and impregnation techniques to construct a paper -based

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

analytical device capable of simultaneously performing ABO forward/reverse typing 
and Rh determination within ten minutes using whole blood, achieving 85 –96% 
accuracy relative to conventional slide testing. Abolfathi [4] optimized a 
nitrocellulose-based matrix, which required smaller sample volumes and provided 
clearer separation between agglutinated and non-agglutinated cells, thereby enhancing 
readability and reproducibility. Gil Garnier[5] developed a rapid droplet-wicking assay 
based on capillary absorption, detecting ABO groups within ten seconds and Rh D 
within twenty seconds, even with antibody dilutions of 1:2 –1:8; their work also 
showed that lower -density, thicker paper improved sensitivity. Xu [6] introduced a 
swinging microfl uidic cotton -thread device, in which vigorous phosphate -buffered 
washing removed free cells more effectively than passive diffusion, increasing 
contrast and reducing interpretation bias. Luo Yang [7, 8] employed cotton-pulp paper 
combined with the colorimet ric indicator bromocresol green, enabling real -time 
detection of ABO/Rh types within 30s through quantifiable color changes supported 
by a calibration algorithm. Their studies provide valuable mechanistic insights into 
how hydrophilic polymer matrices modu late capillary dynamics and preserve 
antibody bioactivity under ambient storage. However, current efforts remain focused 
on ABO forward typing, with reverse typing still dependent on fresh red blood cell 
reagents, and no fundamental technological innovatio ns have been introduced in this 
regard.

titration using engineered, lyophilized RBCs that preserve antigenicity at ambient
