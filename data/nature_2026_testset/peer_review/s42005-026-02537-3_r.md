Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary 
The article is interesting and addresses a fundamental question in network sociology: How do people integrate
multidimensional identity information (such as ethnicity, gender) when forming social ties? To do so, the authors develop the
MAPS (Multidimensional Aggregation of Preferences in Social ties) , which allows the comparison of different mechanisms
of identity preference aggregation, and apply this framework to real school friendship networks in the United States. The
work stands out for its systematic approach to empirically evaluate how preferences are combined and for offering an
interpretable tool to quantify the relevance of each dimension in the formation of social ties. 
Strengths 
- The motivation is well stated and the relevance of the problem is clear. 
- The use of real data and empirical validation by Bayesian 
selection lend rigor and credibility to the results. 
Concerns/Weaknesses 
1. The article presents a solid and well-contextualized theoretical and methodological framework. However, considering that
the manuscript has been submitted to Communications Physics, it would be valuable to explicitly recognize and engage
with the literature and tradition of sociophysics. For example, the authors could reference the Axelrod 
and related
approaches that have been pivotal in bridging statistical physics and social phenomena. We leave other possible
references: 
Castellano, C., Fortunato, S. & Loreto, V. Statistical physics of social dynamics. Rev. Mod. Phys. 81, 591, DOI:
10.1103/RevModPhys.81.591 (2009). 
Boguña, M., Pastor Satorras, R., Díaz Guilera, A. & Arenas, A. s of social networks based on social distance attachment.
Phys. Rev. E 70, 056122, DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevE.70.056122 (2004). 
2. The MEAN 
uses an equally weighted average for preferences in each dimension (h1, h2,...), but the question arises
whether the results hold if different weights are considered, especially if it is known a priori that one dimension is more
relevant than another (e.g., using the “normalized preference” as a guide). Could the authors comment on whether assigning
non-equal weights could improve the fit with respect to the AND function? We recognize that exploring all possible
combinations may be tedious and beyond the scope of the analysis, but it would be valuable to mention it in the discussion if
so. 
3. Figure 3b shows that the AND function outperforms OR (and MEAN) on average in likelihood, AIC and BIC, but is this
difference statistically significant? Looking at the range of values for each mechanism, it appears that in some datasets OR
or MEAN may equal or exceed AND. It would be useful for the authors to quantify the significance of these differences. 
4. The study focuses on school friendship networks, but it would be important for the authors to explicitly discuss the
limitations of generalization. In other social contexts such as dating platforms, homophily may not be cumulative in all
dimensions and may be heterophilic in gender but homophilic in age and ethnicity. It would be advisable to frame the results

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

within the specificities of the school context and discuss possible limitations to generalization. Alternatively (ideally), results
on a different social context could be presented. 
5. The article mentions that a “Hispanic female graduate” can be considered as a single category, in contrast to considering
three categories with values “hispanic”, "female” and “graduate”. I understand that this is introduced to understand the
concept of multiplicative preference: the more attributes in common, the greater the similarity and, therefore, the greater the
probability of connection. However, as we add more dimensions to the , we also increasingly segment the population and
the h parameters that are introduced into the 
to adjust between categories also increase. Doesn't this imply that, by
considering increasingly specific categories, we are segmenting the population into smaller and smaller groups, which can
lead to overfitting problems? What should be the limit to know that we are not overfitting? What is the criterion for deciding to
what level of segmentation?. 
We would appreciate a more explicit discussion of this point, as it has important implications for
both the interpretation of results and the applicability of the 
in contexts with many identity dimensions. 
Other remarks 
6. On network generation: The text mentions that the choice of pairs is random, but it is not clear what is the stopping
criterion for link aggregation. Are links generated until a number equivalent to that of the real network is reached? It would be
advisable to make this point explicit in section ‘3. ing preference aggregation mechanisms’ . 
7. In Figure 4d, it would be useful to clarify in the legend what the colors represent. It seems to be related to the number of
dimensions considered, but specifying it would improve readability. 
8. We think the discussion would benefit from a brief reflection on the ing approach used here, and how it fits between the
classic tradition of sociophysics —where s are explicitly built and compared to real data— and modern deep learning
approaches, which learn latent spaces and then try to interpret the fitted parameters (the field of explainable AI). While the
sociophysics approach is more deductive, aiming for simplicity, universality and high interpretability with fewer parameters,
deep learning methods are more inductive, often using a much larger number of parameters to maximize fit but at the cost of
interpretability. Highlighting this fundamental difference could help readers appreciate why MAPS, as an interpretable and
empirically testable , offers a valuable alternative to black-box s. 
9. The visualizations of the graphs included in the supplementary material could be improved in terms of interpretability by
adjusting the visual encoding of the nodes. We recommend testing the following: 
a. Assign the color of each node to a continuous scale (for example, a color gradient) that represents the student’s grade
level. This would make it easier to visually identify patterns of homophily across grades. 
b. Associate the shape of the node with the student’s ethnicity (or ethnic appearance). 
c. Additionally, to incorporate the gender dimension, an extra differentiation within shapes could be used: for example,
different types of squares for females and different types of circles for males, ensuring that the visual distinction between
gender and ethnicity is maintained without sacrificing clarity. Or perhaps, considering size or fill variables for gender. 
Language and spelling 
10. We recommend unifying the terminology for “race” (race/ethnicity/ethnic appearance) and for ‘sex’ and “gender”, as they
are sometimes used interchangeably and have different connotations. We would suggest using “ethnicity” or “ethnic
appearance” instead of “race,” given the negative historical baggage and current debate over the term. 
Considering all these, we recommend major revision.

We would only like to make one additional remark, which does not require a further round of revision, but is offered as a
recommendation for nuance. Regarding the response to question 2, we suspect that the MEAN mechanism could also yield
networks with a fairly modular structure, depending on the relative weights assigned to different social dimensions.
Therefore, we are not entirely convinced that modularity should be attributed exclusively to the AND function, especially
considering that s similar to MEAN (such as the one proposed by Boguñá et al. (2004)) are also capable of generating
modular networks. It might be worth briefly mentioning this possibility in the discussion, although this does not affect our
overall positive assessment of the manuscript.

We thank Reviewer #1 for this additional recommendation: 
“We thank the authors for their detailed replies and for the improvements introduced in the 
revised version of the manuscript. We consider that all requests have been adequately 
addressed and all questions clarified. We especially value the inclusion of an additional 
dataset, which strengthens the robustness of the results. Overall, we believe the 
manuscript is ready for publication. 
We would only like to make one additional remark, which does not require a further round 
of revision, but is offered as a recommendation for nuance. Regarding the response to 
question 2, we suspect that the MEAN mechanism could also yield networks with a fairly 
modular structure, depending on the relative weights assigned to different social 
dimensions. Therefore, we are not entirely convinced that modularity should be attributed 
exclusively to the AND function, especially considering that models similar to MEAN (such 
as the one proposed by Boguñá et al. (2004)) are also capable of generating modular 
networks. It might be worth briefly mentioning this possibility in the discussion, although 
this does not affect our overall positive assessment of the manuscript.” 
In response, we have edited our discussion to clarify that while the AND aggregation 
naturally produces highly modular networks, MEAN-based mechanisms can also generate 
modular structure under appropriate parameter choices, and included a reference to the 
suggested related work (Boguñá et al., 2004).  
Here is the edited discussion paragraph: 
“In this work, we have considered the simplest variant of the MEAN aggregation function, 
where all dimensions are weighted equally. Allowing the weights to be free parameters 
could in principle improve its fit by learning the relative importance of dimensions directly 
from the data. A key feature of the AND aggregation that MEAN seems to struggle to 
reproduce is its ability to generate highly modular networks, which are pervasive in real-
world social systems. The AND rule multiplies dimension-wise connection probabilities, so 
a single low probability in any dimension drives the overall link probability down and 
naturally produces sharp modular blocks. By contrast, an arithmetic mean smooths 
extreme (low) values and assigns intermediate probabilities to “half-matches” (nodes that 
match in one dimension but not another), which blurs block-like separation.  
However, suitably chosen weights and/or more extreme preference values could also 
induce modularity: related models with MEAN-like interactions have been shown to 
generate community structure under appropriate parameter settings 
\cite{bogunaModelsSocialNetworks2004}. Learning weights in a weighted MEAN would be

straightforward, but it would add $D-1$ global parameters for $D$ dimensions. Although 
explicit weights would provide a direct measure of salience, the AND function already 
encodes a form of differential relevance: if a dimension’s probabilities are close to 
neutrality (similar across target groups), its influence on the aggregated preference is 
minimal. Overall, the weighted MEAN is a plausible extension, but its increased complexity 
would need to be justified by clear empirical gains, and we leave a systematic exploration 
of this richer parameter space for future work.”
