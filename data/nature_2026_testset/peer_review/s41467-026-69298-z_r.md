Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study employs in-situ tensile scanning transmission electron microscopy (S/TEM) to reveal the stress-driven domain
transformation and plastic deformation behavior of Ag2Te at room temperature. This work provides significant insights into
the anomalous plasticity mechanisms of covalent/ionic-bond semiconductors, particularly by proposing a novel “cation
hopping-lattice cooperative deformation” mechanism that transcends traditional dislocation-based plasticity theories. This
may open broad prospects for developing high-strain-rate flexible semiconductor devices at room temperature. I would
recommend minor revision before it can be further considered. Here are the comments in details: 
1. The manuscript mentions that "As shown in Figure S4a, after fracture of the beam, there is no clear sign of necking, and
the fractured Ag2Te has the same crystalline structure... " Here, the author explicitly states that the FIB-prepared nanoscale
specimens exhibited no discernible necking phenomenon upon fracture. In contrast, when describing tensile tests on bulk
specimens, the author only presents the stress-strain curve without mentioning the post-fracture morphology. It remains
unknown whether necking occurs during fracture of macroscopic samples. If necking does occur at the bulk scale, could it be
inferred that sample size or surface effects are the primary drivers of plastic deformation?

of ions.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work studied the mechanism of room-temperature plasticity in Ag2Te. By in-situ scanning and transmission electron
microscopy investigations, the authors demonstrated that the Ag+ ion hopping and Te-sublattice deformation are the source
of plasticity. I find this conclusion arbitrary and lack of sufficient comparisons with other mechanisms reported in this material
system. Moreover, the manuscript is not well-organized, and the writing could be significantly improved. Therefore, I cannot
recommend this manuscript for publication in Nature Communications. The following comments should be addressed before
they submit the paper to another journal. 
1. At the very beginning of the Abstract, the authors highlighted the importance of silver sulfide and emphasized that the
origin of plasticity in such materials remains poorly understood. However, this work focuses on Ag2Te, not Ag2S. Moreover,
the mechanisms of plasticity in Ag2S or Ag2S-Ag2Te alloys have been well investigated by many studies, such as [Shi et
al., Nat. Mater. 2018, 17, 421-426; Yang et al., Adv. Mater. 2021, 2007681; Wang et al., Adv. Funct. Mater. 2023, 2300189;
Wang et al., Nat. Mater. 2025, doi.org/10.1038/s41563-024-02112-7; Hu et al., The Innovation, 2022, 3, 100341]. The
authors should clarify the differences between the Ag hopping mechanism and those reported in the literature, and explain
why the new mechanism takes over. 
2. In the main text, the authors should (but they did not) provide an overview of previous research progress on the plasticity
of Ag2Te, nor did they include any description related to thermoelectric properties. 
3. The introduction regarding previous studies on plastic semiconductors is overly general. At present, numerous plastic
semiconductors have been reported, and a variety of plasticity mechanisms are proposed. Yet, the authors simply attribute
them to dislocation motion and stacking fault mechanisms. 
4. Are the rotation domains twins of the original domains? It has been demonstrated in this work [Wu et al., Adv. Funct.
Mater. 2025, 35, 2423014] that many deformation twins form along the (001) plane, which inhibits crack initiation. I do not
see clear evidence of Ag hopping. It could be a simple structural deformation and twinning-induced plasticity. 
5. The DFT calculated migration barrier for Ag+ shows minimal variation. More details about the selection of the NEB path
should be provided and compared. 
6. The ZT values reported in this manuscript are not high among plastic semiconductors. Furthermore, the comparison of ZT
in Figure 4d is selective, which does not represent the state-of-the-art of ductile thermoelectrics. The authors should either
stick to the maximum ZT value reported in each work or make the comparison at the same temperature. Alternatively, they
can also compare the temperature-dependent ZT curves. In addition, corresponding references to the data in Figure 4d must
be indicated clearly. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors demonstrate that the hopping of Ag ions could enable plasticity in Ag2Te, and the obtained
Ag2Te has good TE performance. However, the deformation mechanism, including the Ag ions hopping and rotation
domains, is not well clarified. In addition, the tensile results in this work are not consistent, and there are obvious errors in
basic concepts and knowledge, raising concerns about the reliability of this work. Moreover, the reported TE performance is
not outstanding among previous articles. I would not recommend this work for publication at this stage, considering these
scientific issues. 
1. The current evidence for “Ag ions hopping” remains insufficient. Ionic conductivity measurements would offer additional
experimental support. In addition, as the exact migration path of Ag ions is unknown, how to define path 1 & 2? On the other
hand, references 47-50 do not state that monoclinic Ag2Te contains ionic bonding. Please provide more evidence from
experiments or theoretical calculations. 
2. The manuscript attributes plasticity of Ag2Te to the formation of rotation domains. But what are these domains? And how
are they formed? These crucial issues are not clarified. In addition, other defects, including dislocations, twins, and staking
defects, are not studied or discussed. 
3. The high diffusion coefficient of Te atoms (Fig. S7) is doubtful, in contrast to its rigid sublattice characteristic. Taking Ag2S
as an example, the diffusion coefficients of S atoms in both monoclinic structures and superionic conductors are near 0. 
4. The tensile results for bulk Ag
₂
Te in Fig. 1b and Fig. S2b are contradictory: Fig. 1b exhibits a strain-hardening effect,
whereas Fig. S2b shows no strain hardening—instead, the stress even decreases after yielding. Such inconsistent
mechanical responses in the same material should not be caused by sample preparations. 
5. In the background, the statement “high-Te materials are typically brittle55” is incorrect. Both Ag2Te0.7S0.3 and
Ag2Te0.6S0.4 are Te-rich but show especially high ductility. In addition, reference 55 talks about Ag2(Se,S), not Ag2(Te,S). 
6. The author misuses “strain rate” to describe the deformation degree, e.g., “the average tensile strain rate of ~10.6%”.
“Strain rate” defines the time derivative of strain (s
⁻
¹) (PRL 100, 025502, 2008). 
7. The phrase “Ag+ ions” is wrong. “Ag+” already implies ionic nature and charge of silver, and combining “Ag+” with “ions”
creates unnecessary repetition. Use either “Ag+” or “Ag ions”. 
8. There has recently been a work that reveals Ag-ion diffusion is crucial for the plasticity of Ag2Te1–xSx semiconductors
(Nat. Mater. 2025). Is there anything different or common compared with this work? In addition, the plasticity of Ag2Te has
been reported before (Adv. Mater. 2023, 35, 2302969), and the thermoelectric performance of Ag2Te in this work is not
outstanding compared with the previous work either.

Author Reply: We thank you for this exceptional and forward-looking suggestion. We 
agree that refining our discovered mechanism into a universal theoretical model, such 
as a "semi -solid" or "pseudo -rheological" system, represents a fascinating and 
impactful direction for the entire  field of flexible inorganic semiconductors.  In direct 
response to your insight, we have formulated the following generalized physical picture 
based on our findings in Ag2Te: 
1. The material can be conceptualized as a  dynamic composite: a  rigid, covalently-
bonded Te-sublattice framework that defines the crystal structure, permeated by 
a quasi-fluid sub-lattice of mobile Ag ions. 
2. Plasticity is achieved through a stress-activated, cooperative process: macroscopic 
stress induces elastic deformation of the rigid Te framework, which in turn creates 
a driving force and lowers the energy barrier for the Ag ions to hop. The rapid ion 
migration then stabilizes the deformed framework, allowing for massive plastic 
flow without fracture. In other words, the ions "lubricate" the reconfiguration of the 
rigid lattice.

careful consideration, we have decided not to incorporate this broader theoretical 
extrapolation into the main text of the current manuscript. We believe that asserting 
such a universal model in the manuscript would require direct experimental evidence 
from a range of other compounds, which is a significant undertaking that constitutes 
the focus of our ongoing and future research.
