Review of “Dust and Smoke layers over the Atlantic Ocean weaken the underlying low-level cloud-top radiative cooling
through different pathways” by Satyendra Pandey and Adeyemi Adebiyi. 
In this manuscript the authors use satellite retrievals of cloud and aerosol profiles combined with heating rate profiles and a
radiative transfer model to investigate the role of absorbing aerosol over low-altitude marine clouds in the Atlantic Ocean.
The authors perform the same analyses on two domains – one strongly influenced by dust aerosol and another strongly
influenced by smoke aerosol. Although the two species are classed as ‘absorbing’ aerosol species, they differ in the
longwave spectrum. The comparison of the two species is novel, as previous studies have focused on one of these species
at a time (and primarily smoke aerosols). The authors demonstrate that both species impact cloud top heating and cloud
fraction, but via different mechanisms, and of different magnitudes. Dust is found to drive a stronger response to cloud top
cooling (manifesting as an anomalous warming) than from smoke. This is in itself an important and original result and will be
of great interest to the wider community. The authors also investigate the role of aerosol-layer moisture on the cloud top
response. They find that although both dust and smoke layers contain moisture, its presence has contrasting impacts on
cloud-top warming. For dust there is strengthening of the cloud top warming from the direct effect, but for smoke the cloud-top
warming induced by the moisture counteracts the direct effect (cloud top cooling). This is a very interesting result and of
great interest to the wider community. The manuscript is well written throughout and the choice of figures is well suited. The
methods section is appropriately informative, and the authors provide justification for all their choices. This work would be
reproducible given the detail of information provided. The authors also put great effort into considering different properties of
the aerosol and thermodynamic environment that may be influencing their results. This is achieved by categorizing the
aerosol retrievals in order to isolate different properties of the cloud. They then use a radiative transfer model to investigate
additional properties of the cloud, aerosol, and thermodynamic environment that would not be possible from the retrievals
alone. This latter analysis is used to support previous results in the manuscript and also identify key mechanisms that link
the aerosol layer properties to the cloud top response. The authors include uncertainties in their values throughout the
manuscript.

publication following some largely minor comments that are detailed below.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The study investigates the impact of dust and smoke plumes on the cloud top radiative cooling of underlying low-level
clouds over the northeast and southeast Atlantic Ocean. The authors claim that these elevated absorbing aerosol layers
reduce the cloud-top cooling mainly via longwave warming near cloud top, leading to anomalous decreases in cloudiness.
They also find the dust plumes to impact cloud top cooling by about an order of magnitude higher than the smoke plumes.
Furthermore, they highlight the contrasting roles of the humidity accompanying these aerosol plumes: while moisture
enhances the dust induced longwave warming at cloud top, it counteracts the smoke induced cloud top cooling caused by
shortwave absorption above cloud top. The paper highlights the importance of considering both longwave and shortwave
mechanisms in estimates of aerosol semi-direct effects. 
The work is well designed and the methodology employed to investigate the the impacts of dust and smoke aerosol plumes
is extensive and well founded. 
Overall I think the manuscript provides relevant and original results to the community studying
the radiative impacts of elevated absorbing aerosols on underlying low-level clouds, and it can inspire further research in
this topic. 
I recommend this paper for publication after a minor revision. Below there is a list of comments for the authors to consider:

We thank the reviewer for their thorough reading of our manuscript and for providing constructive 
and insightful comments. We are pleased that the reviewer  found the study interesting, well -
written, and relevant for the community. We are grateful for the feedback that helped us refine the 
quality of the presentation of our manuscript. Our responses to the general and specific comments 
are detailed below.  
General comments: 
1. Interesting that smoke/dust layers were more likely to be positioned >2km above the cloud 
layer (Fig S2). Are the authors surprised by this? Is this due to some potential self -lofting 
mechanism or could it be due to the timescale of layer subsidence and extent of analysis 
domains. May be interesting to include a PDF of vertical position relative to the cloud layer 
in the SI (but not required). 
 
Yes, CALIPSO-retrieved profiles show that both dust and smoke layers during the selected 
period are predominantly located above 2 km altitude , on average . However, individual 
aerosol events may vary significantly in height, as shown in Fig. S1  (see the uncertainty 
bars in the smoke/dust top/bottom height).  This mean value and its uncertainties  are 
consistent with previous studies that used CALIPSO along with complementary datasets 
to characterize the vertical distribution of aer osols over the selected domains  1,2. The 
elevated dust laye rs are associated with the Saharan Air Layer (SAL) —a warm, dry air 
mass transported westward by mid -tropospheric easterly flow  3–5. This air mass remains 
largely above the cooler and moist marine boundary layer, capped by a strong temperature 
inversion, which inhibits frequent mixing of dust with the boundary layer. In addition, 
absorbing dust particles further heat s and stabilize s the SAL, helping to maintain its 
elevated vertical structure during trans -Atlantic transport 6. Similarly, transported smoke 
layers often remain elevated due to strong convective injection at their source regions and 
subsequent radiative self -lofting that s tabilizes them above the boundary layer 7,8. In 
addition, whereas both smoke and dust are absorbing aerosols that can have potentially 
self-lofting properties, we have not analyzed this specific mechanism in the present study.  
  
2. Was there any clear dependence on distance from the coast for the dust case? Do the authors 
think the dust size distribution would change to an extent that the LW direct radiative 
effects may differ as a function of distance? 
 
We found that the frequency of profiles showing dust layers above clouds decreases with 
increasing distance from the coast. Consequently, the number of such cases in the eastern 
part of the domain becomes too small for a meaningful statistical comparison w ith the 
western part. Regarding the dust size distribution, it indeed substantially changes during 
long-range transport , and the fraction of the coarse-mode dust particles significantly 
decreases as the dust travels farther from the source regions owing to gravitational settling

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

and size-selective removal processes 9–11. In addition, in a related study, we found that such 
changes in the dust size distribution strongly influence the downwelling longwave radiation 
reaching the cloud top, suggesting that  potential changes in dust size distribution  with 
distance from the coast can affect the underlying low-level clouds 12.  
 
3. What is the net TOA radiation response for each case? 
Thank you for the comments. In the present study, we did not explicitly derive the net top-
of-atmosphere (TOA) radiative responses, as the SBDART simulations were conducted 
using prescribed cloud properties. Although these results are representative of the dust or 
smoke radiative responses, they include multiple other assumptions on clouds and aerosols 
(e.g., effective radius and aerosol size distribution) that may deviate the TOA values from 
actual estimates . A more physically consistent quantification of the TOA radiative 
responses, as a result of cloud responses to dust or smoke  layers, will require numerical 
simulations using regional and global climate models.   
4. Moisture content of the non-aerosol-layer atmosphere. I presume the specific humidity of 
the surrounding atmosphere will play a role in the responses due to the layer moisture. Was 
this taken into consideration? Is it likely to impact the differences observed between the 
two domains? 
 
We agree that the specific humidity of the non-aerosol-layer atmosphere may influence the 
cloud top radiative cooling. In our analysis, w e considered the ambient thermodynamic 
environment surrounding the aerosol layers, including temperature and humidity profiles 
from satellite retrievals. Our sensitivity analysis, by varying background temperature and 
moisture profiles , indicates that whil e variations in background moisture can slightly 
modulate the magnitude of cloud -top heating/cooling, they do not dominate the overall 
relationship observed between aerosol-layer properties and cloud-response.  
 
5. Global scale perspective. What are the implications of these results on other regions? 
 
Our study focuses on the southeast and northeast Atlantic, regions dominated by 
climatically significant marine stratocumulus clouds. These stratocumulus regions play a 
key role in reflecting incoming solar radiation and controlling the planetary energy budget. 
Even small perturbations to these low-level cloud systems can have a far-reaching impact 
on the global climate. Our findings show that absorbing aerosol  layers above clouds can 
modulate cloud-top radiative cooling through distinct mechanisms linked to their ability to 
influence the longwave radiation reaching cloud top. Although our analysis focuses on the 
north and south Atlantic, similar above-cloud aerosol–cloud configurations are frequently 
observed over other regions  13–15. For example, using CALIPSO observations, studies 
indicated that the frequency of aerosol above cloud can exceed 20% regionally (and about 
10% for smoke alone), with nearly half of these cases involving layer separations of less

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

than 1 km, from underlying clouds 13. The prevalence of these  aerosol-cloud scenarios, 
particularly in the tropical and subtropical regions where optically thicker aerosol layers 
above clouds are common, highlights their broader relevance. Thus, t he mechanisms 
identified in our study may also be relevant in other regions where low-level clouds coexist 
with above-cloud aerosols, influencing both shortwave reflection and longwave emission 
from the cloud top. Moreover, any aerosol -induced modulation in low-level clouds can 
alter cloud feedback, which is critical for accurately simulating the present-day climate and 
future climate projections. We included the relevant discussion, which reads  
 
“Although our analysis focuses on the North and South Atlantic Oceans, where absorbing 
aerosols frequently overlie low-level clouds, similar occurrences of above-cloud absorbing 
aerosols are common over other oceanic and continental regions14–17. Over other oceanic 
basins such as the Pacific and the Arabian Sea, elevated smoke and dust layers above low-
level clouds can similarly influence the cloud-top radiative cooling as described above16,17.

The study investigates the impact of dust and smoke plumes on the cloud top radiative cooling of 
underlying low-level clouds over the northeast and southeast Atlantic Ocean. The authors claim
