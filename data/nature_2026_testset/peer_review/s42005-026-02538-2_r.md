Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, the authors propose a method to improve the performance of a quantum-inspired algorithm for combinatorial
optimization called simulated bifurcation (SB). This method is based on a conventional approach for combinatorial
optimization called tabu search. To my knowledge, this is the first trial of combining these two approaches. In more detail,
the proposed method is also similar to the stochastic gradient descent method in machine learning in the sense that we
randomly choose a few local minima from the tabu list at each time step (iteration of update). This may be essential for the
performance of this method. The authors provide numerical evidence for the effect of the random choice. Consequently, the
authors succeeded in finding best known solutions in short times by this method, compared to conventional SB. Thus, the
method is new and effective. So, I recommend publishing this paper in Communications Physics. However, the following
should be reconsidered before acceptance. 
1. We need m-time repetition of standard SB in warming-up phase, where m is the number of local minima in the tabu list.
This means that we need an m times longer time for one trial of this method than the standard SB. But in the present
benchmark, this is not considered. That is, the proposed method is compared with single-run SB. In my opinion, we should
compare the proposed method with m-run SB (or longer-time single-run SB) for fiarness. Also, in Fig. 2, the warming-up
phase may be repeated m times. Then, the results in Table I will be changed, because dSB can find the best known
solutions for G21, 22, 24-26 with low probabilities. 
2. Algorithm 1 lacks the schedule (update) of a(t).

Our reply
Computational fairness is indeed a crucial point. We would like to clarify that our comparison is based on the standard
Time-to-Solution (TTS) metric, which combines empirical success probability and wall-clock time per sample, and that
the experimental protocol ensures a fair assessment between TESB and standard SB.
To estimate success probabilities, we perform independent parallel trials on the GPU. For standard SB, we execute
1,000 trials. For TESB, we ﬁrst run a warming phase with 100 parallel trials to populate the tabu list, followed by a
checking phase with 1,000 parallel trials for evaluation. Thanks to full parallelization, the wall-clock time is dominated
solely by the number of iterations per trial, not by the total number of trials.
For standard SB, each trial consists of a full run of 10,000 iterations.
In contrast, TESB operates in two phases:
(i) a warming-up phase that performsm = 100 runs (each with 1000 iterations) to construct the tabu list;

(ii) a checking phase that reuses this single tabu list across all 1,000 trials, each running for the remaining 9000
iterations.
This design reﬂects a key efﬁciency advantage of TESB: by sharing one carefully constructed tabu list across many
trials, it avoids redundant exploration while actively steering the dynamics away from previously visited suboptimal
regions. In the checking phase, TESB incurs only a modest overhead from computing the tabu term (random sampling
from the list and vector addition), which slightly increases per-iteration time compared to standard SB. Nevertheless,
this small additional cost is more than compensated by the signiﬁcant boost in success probability, leading to a lower
overall TTS for TESB.
Therefore, our comparison is not between “single-run SB” and “m-run TESB,” but rather between two strategies
evaluated under the same statistical protocol (1,000 trials) and realistic timing measurements. We acknowledge that
this distinction was not sufﬁciently clear in the original manuscript. To address this, we have added the following
clariﬁcation in Section “Tabu-Enhanced Simulated Bifurcation” (page 3):
In standard SB, all trials can be executed in parallel on the GPU. Similarly, in Tabu-Enhanced SB (TESB), the warming
up phase runs multiple trials in parallel to construct a shared tabu list, and the subsequent checking phase trials are
also executed in parallel using the same tabu list.
We hope this addition eliminates any ambiguity regarding the computational protocol.

Reviewer 2’s comment 4
Can anything be said about the convergence of such a procedure?
Our reply
The convergence behavior of the base Simulated Bifurcation (SB) algorithm has been analyzed in prior works by
Goto et al. [1, 2], who showed that SB implements a dissipative Hamiltonian system whose total energy decreases
monotonically over time, a key property that underpins its ability to converge to local minima.
Our Tabu-Enhanced SB (TESB) introduces an additional state-dependent penalty term derived from previously visited
solutions, but it does not introduce any new explicit time dependence beyond the scheduled parametera(t). Conse-
quently, the modiﬁed dynamics preserve the dissipative structure of the original system. Therefore, the convergence
behavior of TESB remains qualitatively consistent with that of standard SB: the trajectory is still attracted to local
minima of the modiﬁed landscape, and convergence to a stationary point is expected whena(t) is sufﬁciently large and
slowly varying.
To clarify this point, we have added a brief discussion on convergence in the Overview section (page 4):
Regarding convergence, the original SB dynamics form a dissipative system with monotonically decreasing total energy,
and have been shown to converge to local minima of the Ising energy landscape under suitable conditions on the
scheduling ofa(t) [1, 2, 6]. Our tabu-enhanced formulation introduces a state-dependent penalty term that reshapes
the landscape but does not add explicit time dependence beyond a(t). Consequently, the modiﬁed dynamics retain

For every instance in Table I, the success probability Ps was estimated from exactly 1,000 independent runs under

Speciﬁcally, the tabu term introduces a quadratic penalty proportional to the squared overlap between the current state

inadvertently penalize desirable regions, leading to degraded performance.

landscape topology, basin geometry, or spectral properties, represents an important direction for future work. We now
