Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zhang, Shao, Kong et al. present an interesting study analyzing the role of chandelier cells (ChCs) in the medial prefrontal
cortex (mPFC) in encoding salience information and influencing associative learning. Using Unc5b-CreER mice with
tamoxifen administration, the authors selectively target and manipulate ChCs in the mPFC. Through a combination of a fiber
photometry recording of ChCs’ activity, optogenetics, chemogenetics, and circuit-specific manipulation in this transgenic
mouse, the authors demonstrate the following key findings: 
1) Compared to PV and SST interneurons, ChCs are selectively responsive to stimulus salience. 
2) ChCs were activated by novel stimulus. 
3) ChC responses scale with the intensity of familiar stimuli (volume of water reward). 
4) Abolishing synaptic outputs from AI or PVT neurons disrupts salience detection by ChCs. 
5) Inactivation of AI or PVT synaptic outputs similarly abolishes ChC salience detection. 
6) ChCs acquire salience-coding property for behaviorally relevant stimuli through associative learning. 
7) Bidirectional manipulation of ChCs’ activity leads to improvement and impairment of fear conditioning. 
The manuscript thoroughly examines ChC activity across different contexts—novelty, intensity, and associative learning—
and links these dynamics to behavioral outcomes. The findings position mPFC ChCs as key circuit elements in the salience
processing for detection and utilization of salience information in learning and behavior. In its revised form, this manuscript
could provide meaningful insights into salience processing and the role of ChCs in cognitive functions. 
Overall, this study addresses an important role of the ChCs in salience processing and learning. Despite its strengths, some
of the key assumptions are not sufficiently validated experimentally, and some results lack critical details. These limitations
hinder the clear interpretation of the authors’ main claims. With major revisions to improve clarity and data presentation, this
manuscript could make a valuable contribution to the field of neuroscience, with implications for both basic research and
translational studies in neuropsychiatric disorders. 
Below, I offer detailed comments for the authors to consider. 
Major Concerns: 
- Inconsistent Use and Definition of Salience-Related Terms: 
The authors frequently use terms related to salience (e.g., stimulus salience, salience processing, novelty-based salience
detection, Familiarity-based salience detection, intensity-based salience detection, task-driven salience, acquired salience,
the perceived salience) without clear definitions or distinctions. The lack of clarity undermines the interpretation of ChC
contributions to salience processing. Salience generally refers to the physical distinctiveness of an object or a stimulus and
behavioral relevance, yet the manuscript does not clearly define how distinctness is operationalized. For example, the
definition of novelty is unclear and not consistent. In Fig 2, novelty is based on the first stimulus presentation (description of
familiar responses is missing), whereas in Fig 3, it is determined by experimental stage rather than trial. 
Instead of using one stimulus for each modality, the author should consider adding control stimuli to allow comparisons
between two stimuli (e.g., familiar tone A vs. novel tone B, or familiar odor 
A vs. novel odor B) to strengthen their
conclusions. 
- Comparison between PV and ChC Responses: 
In Fig 1c and EDF3, optic fiber placements appear to precisely target L1 and L2 of the mPFC. Since ChCs are a subset of
PV cells, I expected that responses of PV cells in these layers somewhat resemble those of ChCs. However, they do not for
Tone and Water stimuli. A clear explanation of response differences between PV and ChC is necessary to support the claim

that ChCs play unique role in salience processing. 
- Movement-Related Confounds in ChC Activity: 
In recent ChC literature (Schneider-Mizell et al., 2021; Dudok et al., 2021; Jung et al., 2023), ChCs’ activity is often
correlated with movement speed. Although the authors mentioned that ChCs exhibited a high level of spontaneous activity
in freely moving animals and ChCs were robustly activated by all tested stimuli, they do not address potential confounds
from movement-related activity. It would be valuable to analyze the relationship between movement speed and ChC, PV,
and SST activity to disentangle sensory-driven responses from movement-related inputs. 
- Distinction Between Novelty and Intensity Coding: 
The manuscript presents compelling data showing that novelty drives ChC responses in S1, while stimulus intensity
modulates responses in S3, suggesting evidence of neuronal adaptation of ChC activity. However, several factors may
confound this interpretation: 
1) The authors used only water reward for their conclusion. It is possible that intensity of tone or shock does not reliably drive
scaled responses in S3. To claim that stimulus intensity is a key factor to drive the adaptation in ChCs, additional testes
across modalities (e.g., tones, shocks) that are less influenced by motivational factors would strengthen the conclusions. 
2) Just 2-3 days of water deprivation may differentially affect motivation level for water reward between S1 and S3,
influencing ChC responses. 
- Circuit-Specific Manipulations of AI and PVT outputs: 
Regarding circuit-specific dissection focusing on AI and PVT, background and rationale for focusing on AI and PVT circuits
is not well-articulated. The author should provide more detailed background, rationale on the unique properties and
connectivity of these circuits with ChCs in mPFC to understand their interplay. 
The authors used TetTox to block AI or PVT outputs. However, this approach raises two concerns: 1) The hSyn promotor
targets not only excitatory neurons but potentially other types of neurons. 2) Blocking AI or PVT outputs are not limited to
inputs to ChCs in mPFC. It may block AI or PVT outputs to other brain regions rather than L1-2 mPFC. To support the
author’s claim precisely, evidence of monosynaptic connectivity between excitatory neurons in AI or PVT and ChCs is
needed, along with manipulation of AI or PVT activity limited to these connections. While optogenetic inhibition of AI or PVT
axons in mPFC partially addresses this in Fig 5, as shown in Fig 5, their axonal projections extend beyond L1. Thus,
showing monosynaptic connectivity between excitatory neurons in AI or PVT and ChC and the effect of optogenetic
inhibition of AI or PVT inputs to ChCs (similar to EDF7) would be needed. 
- Trace Fear Conditioning (TFC) Paradigm: I have several comments regarding Trace Fear Conditioning paradigms and
results. 
1) It would be helpful for readers if authors can provide rationale why TFC paradigm was chosen instead of classical fear
conditioning. 
2) Why was the tone present on D0 for fiber photometry recording? It would be informative to compare response to tone
between D0 and D1 in a trial-by-trial manner. 
3) How CS-US, CS only, and US only were presented on D1 is not clear. The authors need to describe clearly how each
condition was conducted. Were all three CS-US, CS only, and US only conditions tested within subjects on D1? If so, how
was the order of conditions determined? 
4) The increasing responses of ChCs to CS in CS-US condition are very compelling to show the involvement of ChCs in
learning. To precisely support the role of ChCs in encoding acquired salience, comparing responses to a second, distinct
CS (e.g., a different tone) would be critical. 
5) Furthermore, similar to Fig 6, manipulating AI or PVT inputs to mPFC during TFC and measuring their effects on ChCs
responses to CS could elucidate the role of AI or PVT circuit in acquired salience. 
- Discussion: While the discussion effectively integrates the findings with existing literature, it tends to overstate the role of
ChCs as central hubs for salience processing. The authors should adopt a more cautious tone, acknowledging the complex
interplay between ChCs, other interneuron types, and mPFC circuits. Moreover, potential compensatory mechanisms in
ChC-deficient conditions and their broader behavioral relevance are not sufficiently addressed. Including more nuanced
perspectives on how ChCs interact with local and long-range circuits would provide a balanced interpretation. Additionally,
the discussion would benefit from highlighting limitations such as the focus on specific brain regions without considering
global network dynamics.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zhang and colleagues use fiber photometry and opto/chemogenetic manipulation strategies to examine the encoding
properties and function of PFC chandelier cells on salience detection and associative fear learning. 
The experiments are
thorough, with the exceptions of not including an appetitive associative learning task and only including male subjects. The
conclusions are interesting but are quite overstated, as the authors did not have single-cell resolution during imaging and
the effects of the presynaptic manipulations on salience detection are questionable at best (other interpretations are just as
fitting). 
While I do have significant concerns, I also think that the findings are novel and will be very useful for the field. I recommend
that the authors significantly dial back their conclusions, better integrate past literature on PVT, AI, and most importantly PFC
single-cell dynamics in the context of salience detection, associative learning, behavioral inhibition (in regards to input
circuitry) and circuits. 
The authors should provide better alternative interpretations including those I mention below
throughout the manuscript. 
Major Concerns: 
1. The authors are using fiber photometry to infer how bulk populations of interneurons respond to various stimuli. They
make the conclusion that ChCs respond to novelty/salience, but there are significant caveat interpretations to this idea. Most
notably, others in the field have shown that PFC excitatory neurons in general can encode many types of distinct stimuli, yet
single-cell resolution imaging demonstrates that such effects are attributed to unique populations of neurons (e.g.,
34184635) Thus, there is divergent encoding by ensembles that overall could seem like a salience signal if visualized
through a bulk signal. The same could be true here. Results could simply be due to distinct ChCs that respond to aversion
vs reward and so on, and that resolution would be completely lost with the current approach. 
This limitation needs to be adequately addressed within the manuscript. First, the authors need to cite relevant literature
discussing the encoding dynamics of prefrontal (including prelimbic, studied here) neurons at a single cell level, which has
been shown to be highly heterogeneous at the level of excitatory neurons. The authors also need to expand discussion of
how this circuitry influences learning, including through its interactions with the AI and PVT at the level of computationally
diverse neuronal ensembles (e.g., PMIDs: 34913438; 25600268; 38747563; 34637750; 34184635; 28225752, 31196673,
31160741; 39415234). 
Second, the authors need to thoroughly discuss the potential that diverse populations of ChCs could have unique encoding
dynamics, and thus it is possible that one ensemble of ChCs is not encoding salience per se, but rather the bulk population
could be an amalgamation of diverse signals that overall is a representation of salience. This would fit with the citations
provided above that are performed at the single-cell level. 
2. There are important differences in the results of Figures 4 and 5 that are not discussed. Most notably, while lesions of AIC
and PVT cells reduces PFC ChC population activation to a novel stimulus, inhibition of AIC and PVT terminals in PFC do
not. This suggests that the lesion effects are likely due to polysynaptic effects, perhaps on salience detection, potentially
involving many structures not under study here. Furthermore, the JAWS results actually suggest that these inputs do NOT
relay information about novelty per se but rather enable PFC ChCs to habituate to novel stimuli (while control animals show
habituation in ChC responsivity, JAWS animals show perseverative activation). 
Similarly, the value scaling experiment
clearly demonstrates that AI and PVT lesions or inhibition amplify the response of PFC neurons to low value reward. This
suggests that these neurons may actually mitigate responsivity to stimuli that have low salience, rather than amplify
responsivity to stimuli that have high salience. This framework is highly consistent with data showing that PVT is critical for
suppressing information under certain conditions, such suppressing behavior when reward is omitted (28426970) or when
reward is paired with behavioral suppressors including stress, fear, and extinction which is highly relevant to the current
manuscript (36369508; 38141605). 
3. Optogenetic inhibition of ChCs for 5-minutes is likely to have many effects on neurophysiology, as has been recently
reported in the literature. Thus, the value and interpretability of data in Ext. Data Fig. 7 is of significant concern. Related to
this, the authors indicate that optogenetic inhibition was given in a “continuous pattern”, which seems misleading. If it was
20s or 5min of pure light, that needs to be clear. Pure light is not really a “pattern”. 
4. If authors wish to conclude that PFC ChCs are critical for associative learning via generalized salience detection, the
functional experiments should be performed not only in the context of Pavlovian fear conditioning, but also for appetitive

conditioning which has been shown to rely on distinct PFC excitatory neuronal ensembles including those that interact with
the PVT. 
5. Use of male mice only is of significant concern.

1. Authors suggest that the TetTox experiment is an inactivation of synaptic output experiment, which is a very poor

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zhang et al. investigate how a specific interneuron subtype, chandelier cells (ChCs), in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)
contribute to detecting stimulus salience and guiding associative learning. The authors employed a combination of
advanced techniques, including cell-type-specific genetic targeting (using Unc5b-CreER mice to selectively label ChCs), in
vivo calcium imaging via two-color fiber photometry, optogenetics, and chemogenetics. Using these tools, they monitored
ChC activity in freely behaving mice while presenting a range of stimuli (auditory tones, sucrose rewards, air puffs, foot
shocks, and others) and manipulated inputs and ChC excitability. Key comparisons were made with other interneuron types
(parvalbumin-positive [PV] and somatostatin-positive [SST] cells) to determine if salience encoding is unique to ChCs. The
study also examined circuit mechanisms by functionally disconnecting upstream regions of the known “salience network” –
specifically the anterior insula (AI) and paraventricular thalamus (PVT) – to see how this affects ChC responses. Finally, the
authors tested the causal role of ChCs in learning by observing their activity during trace fear conditioning and by
bidirectionally manipulating ChC activity during learning. 
The major findings are novel and compelling. First, ChCs in mPFC responded robustly to stimuli across multiple modalities
and valences, firing strongly to both appetitive and aversive cues, which suggests they encode the salience or significance
of stimuli rather than their specific pleasant or unpleasant nature. Second, ChC responses were greatest to novel stimuli and
diminished upon repeated exposures (habituation), indicating that ChCs dynamically track stimulus novelty. In contrast, the
other interneurons (PV and SST cells) did not show such adaptation between first and later presentations, highlighting that
this novelty-driven response pattern is unique to ChCs. Third, ChCs also encoded stimulus intensity: for example, they
differentiated between larger versus smaller reward volumes, especially once the stimulus was familiar, thereby signaling
relative intensity when novelty was no longer a factor. The authors demonstrate that inputs from AI and PVT are essential for
these salience signals – blocking synaptic output from either region greatly reduced ChC responses and abolished their
ability to distinguish novel vs. familiar or high vs. low intensity stimuli. Fourth, in an associative learning paradigm (trace fear
conditioning), ChC activity reflected acquired salience: a tone that was initially mundane (habituated) elicited only weak
ChC activity, but once that tone was paired with a foot shock, ChC responses to the tone increased significantly. Notably,
ChCs maintained strong responses to the shock only when the shock was part of a learned cue-shock association, whereas
if shocks were given randomly (unpaired), ChC responses to those shocks rapidly declined. This indicates that ChC firing
tracks the learned significance of both predictive cues and reinforcement, not just the physical stimulus. Finally, the
manipulation experiments establish a causal link to behavior: optogenetic silencing of ChCs during the critical cue/shock
pairing period markedly impaired fear learning (reduced freezing to the tone), demonstrating that ChC activity is necessary
for normal associative learning of that stimulus. Complementary chemogenetic experiments showed that enhancing ChC
excitability (with a DREADD agonist) dampened ChCs’ calcium responses to stimuli and led to poorer learning (less
freezing), whereas suppressing ChC excitability (making them less active at baseline, which paradoxically led to larger
responses to stimuli) enhanced learning (greater freezing). Importantly, these manipulations did not produce general
performance deficits or hyperactivity, suggesting the effects were specific to learning and salience processing rather than
due to nonspecific behavioral changes. 
In summary, this study presents strong evidence that mPFC chandelier cells serve as a dedicated cortical module for
detecting stimulus salience (through novelty, intensity, and learned relevance) and that their activity can bias learning
outcomes. This represents a significant contribution to our understanding of how prefrontal circuits filter important stimuli and
influence cognitive processes, adding cellular-level insight to the broader concept of the brain’s salience network. 
Major Concerns 
1. Experimental Design and Controls 
While the scope of experiments is impressive, there are some concerns about the experimental design and control
conditions used. A primary issue is the adequacy of control groups for the optogenetic and chemogenetic manipulations.
The authors inhibited ChCs optogenetically (NpHR) during conditioning and observed a learning deficit, but it is not stated
whether control mice (e.g. animals expressing a control fluorophore but no opsin) were also subjected to the same light
delivery. Such a control is important to ensure that the effect on learning is truly due to ChC inhibition and not any
unforeseen consequence of light or fiber implant (e.g. heating, tissue damage, or behavioral distraction). If no “light only”
control was included, the authors should either provide justification or consider this in a revision, as it strengthens the causal

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

claim. Similarly, for chemogenetic modulation, it’s unclear if the study included vehicle injections or control-virus mice to
control for any off-target effects of the DREADD actuator (CNO or equivalent). Given known issues with CNO, it would be
reassuring to know that CNO injection in mice lacking the DREADD did not alter learning or ChC activity. The manuscript
mentions that locomotor activity was unchanged by these manipulations, which is a good control for general effects;
however, controls for the learning context specifically would further ensure interpretational clarity. These additional control
experiments (or clarifications if they were done) would bolster confidence in the conclusions. 
Another aspect of experimental design to consider is the sample size and statistical power. Many of the key experiments
(e.g. fiber photometry comparisons, optogenetic and DREADD groups) used around n ≈ 6–8 mice per group (as indicated in
the figure legends). While these sample sizes are not uncommon in in vivo neuroscience studies, they are relatively modest
and might limit the robustness of detecting effects, especially given between-subject variability. The fact that many
comparisons reached statistical significance suggests the effects are reasonably strong; however, some results could be
underpowered. The authors should clarify how sample sizes were determined (was a power analysis conducted?) and
whether each “n” refers to an individual mouse’s averaged data. Additionally, it would help to report the number of trials or
cells contributing to each mouse’s data point for fiber photometry. If certain groups had any exclusions, that should be
reported. Overall, slightly larger cohorts would increase confidence that the findings generalize, though the consistency
across multiple experiments already suggests the phenomena are reliable. 
Potential confounding variables were generally well-addressed, but a few points merit discussion. One could argue that
factors like slight differences in arousal or attention between the start and end of a session might contribute to the decline in
response, though the authors did control for certain behavioral measures such as licking behavior. It would be useful to
know if the stimuli were presented in pseudorandom order or fixed sequence; randomizing or counterbalancing stimulus
sequences would prevent any systematic order or time-of-session confound. Another possible confound is the specificity of
the fiber photometry signal to ChCs, but the authors used Unc5b-CreERT2 mice and performed
morphological/electrophysiological validation. Lastly, for the fear conditioning paradigm, the use of trace fear conditioning is
appropriate to engage the prefrontal cortex, though clarifying any context counterbalancing or exclusions would be helpful.
The control groups (CS-only or US-only) are an excellent demonstration that freezing specifically requires cue-shock
pairing. Overall, the experimental design is innovative and multi-faceted, but providing a bit more detail on controls and
addressing the above points would strengthen the rigor. 
2. Statistical Analysis and Reporting 
The statistical approaches used in the manuscript raise some consistency and justification concerns. Throughout the figures,
the authors employ a mix of parametric tests (e.g. two-tailed paired Student’s t-tests) and non-parametric tests (e.g. Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests), as well as ANOVAs for multi-factor comparisons, but it is not always clear why a particular test was
chosen. It’s possible that the authors decided on a case-by-case basis depending on normality of the data distribution, but
this rationale needs to be stated explicitly. The manuscript would benefit from a clear statement in the Methods about the
criteria for choosing parametric vs. non-parametric tests. If normality tests were not done, it would be safer to use non-
parametric tests throughout for consistency. Repeated-measures ANOVA (with Sidak’s multiple comparisons) for multi-level
intensity experiments is appropriate if assumptions were satisfied; the authors should confirm that. In terms of reporting, the
figures and legends do present p-values and mention the tests used, which is commendable. However, ensuring every
figure uses a consistent notation for significance (whether asterisks or explicit p-values) would be helpful. For multiple
comparisons, clarifying whether the reported p-values were thresholded with corrections is also important. Finally, the
authors should consider reporting effect sizes. Overall, the analysis approach is sound, but a more uniform application of
statistical tests and thorough reporting of assumptions would improve rigor and reproducibility. 
3. Interpretational Robustness and Data Presentation 
The authors conclude that ChCs are specialized for salience detection and that this drives their influence on learning. The
data largely support this interpretation, but there are some points that should be addressed. One concern is whether ChC
responses could be explained by more general factors such as global arousal or neuromodulatory states. The authors
partially address circuit inputs by examining AI and PVT, which indeed are part of the salience/relevance network. It would
strengthen the interpretation if the authors briefly discuss how neuromodulators or other inputs might be involved. Another
alternative explanation to rule out is that ChC responses might correlate with behavioral reactions to stimuli, but the authors
did well to check for licking and locomotion confounds. Highlighting these controls more in the main text would assure
readers that behavioral confounds were examined. 
Another interpretational point is the causal link to learning. The impairment of fear conditioning with ChC inhibition (and
enhancement with disinhibition) shows that ChCs contribute to associative learning. Yet, we should consider the precise
mechanism. ChC control of pyramidal neuron axon initial segments is powerful but not fully examined here (the authors did
not directly record pyramidal activity). A brief discussion of how reduced or enhanced ChC activity translates to changes in
excitatory circuits would be valuable, especially given the seemingly paradoxical result that silencing ChCs might disinhibit
local circuits. For the DREADD experiments, the authors suggest that increasing ChC excitability leads to overall reduced
stimulus-evoked Ca²
⁺
 signals and impaired learning, presumably because the baseline inhibition is higher. A short
mechanistic discussion tying these results to known ChC function would clarify the significance. Finally, data presentation is
generally good, but a few minor suggestions (e.g. marking the analysis window on photometry plots, providing raw ΔF/F
traces) would improve clarity. This can be done in figures or supplemental materials.

To summarize my review, Zhang et al. have presented a substantive and insightful study that identifies chandelier cells of
the mPFC as key players in salience detection and learning. The experiments are ambitious in scope, combining
observational and interventional approaches to make a strong case that ChCs not only encode salient features of stimuli
(novelty, intensity, predictive value) but also causally contribute to how an animal learns about those stimuli. The
manuscript’s strengths lie in its innovative cell-type-specific approach, the clear demonstration of both necessity (via
optogenetic inhibition) and sufficiency/modulation (via chemogenetic tuning) of ChC activity for learning, and the linkage of
circuit-level mechanisms (input from AI/PVT) to behavior. The data are largely convincing and novel, and they significantly
advance our understanding of cortical interneuron function in cognitive processes. 
That said, there are important revisions needed to ensure the work meets the highest standards of rigor and clarity. The
major concerns outlined regarding controls, statistical consistency, and interpretational clarity should be addressed before
publication. Most of these issues can be dealt with through additional analysis, explanation, or minor experimental
validation, rather than requiring entirely new experimental paradigms. The minor concerns are straightforward improvements
that will enhance readability and transparency. None of the concerns undermine the fundamental conclusions of the paper,
but resolving them is critical.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Zhang, Shao, Kong et al. present an interesting study analyzing the role of chandelier cells (ChCs) in the medial prefrontal 
cortex (mPFC) in encoding salience information and influencing associative learning. Using Unc5b-CreER mice with 
tamoxifen administration, the authors selectively target and manipulate ChCs in the mPFC. Through a combination of a 
fiber photometry recording of ChCs’ activity, optogenetics, chemogenetics, and circuit-specific manipulation in this 
transgenic mouse, the authors demonstrate the following key findings: 
1) Compared to PV and SST interneurons, ChCs are selectively responsive to stimulus salience. 
2) ChCs were activated by novel stimulus. 
3) ChC responses scale with the intensity of familiar stimuli (volume of water reward). 
4) Abolishing synaptic outputs from AI or PVT neurons disrupts salience detection by ChCs. 
5) Inactivation of AI or PVT synaptic outputs similarly abolishes ChC salience detection. 
6) ChCs acquire salience-coding property for behaviorally relevant stimuli through associative learning. 
7) Bidirectional manipulation of ChCs’ activity leads to improvement and impairment of fear conditioning. 
The manuscript thoroughly examines ChC activity across different contexts—novelty, intensity, and associative learning—
and links these dynamics to behavioral outcomes. The findings position mPFC ChCs as key circuit elements in the salience 
processing for detection and utilization of salience information in learning and behavior. In its revised form, this manuscript 
could provide meaningful insights into salience processing and the role of ChCs in cognitive functions. 
Overall, this study addresses an important role of the ChCs in salience processing and learning. Despite its strengths, some 
of the key assumptions are not sufficiently validated experimentally, and some results lack critical details. These limitations 
hinder the clear interpretation of the authors’ main claims. With major revisions to improve clarity and data presentation, 
this manuscript could make a valuable contribution to the field of neuroscience, with implications for both basic research 
and translational studies in neuropsychiatric disorders. 
Below, I offer detailed comments for the authors to consider.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Zhang et al. investigate how a specific interneuron subtype, chandelier cells (ChCs), in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) 
contribute to detecting stimulus salience and guiding associative learning. The authors employed a combination of advanced 
techniques, including cell-type-specific genetic targeting (using Unc5b-CreER mice to selectively label ChCs), in vivo 
calcium imaging via two-color fiber photometry, optogenetics, and chemogenetics. Using these tools, they monitored ChC 
activity in freely behaving mice while presenting a range of stimuli (auditory tones, sucrose rewards, air puffs, foot shocks, 
and others) and manipulated inputs and ChC excitability. Key comparisons were made with other interneuron types 
(parvalbumin-positive [PV] and somatostatin-positive [SST] cells) to determine if salience encoding is unique to ChCs. The 
study also examined circuit mechanisms by functionally disconnecting upstream regions of the known “salience network” – 
specifically the anterior insula (AI) and paraventricular thalamus (PVT) – to see how this affects ChC responses. Finally, 
the authors tested the causal role of ChCs in learning by observing their activity during trace fear conditioning and by 
bidirectionally manipulating ChC activity during learning. 
The major findings are novel and compelling. First, ChCs in mPFC responded robustly to stimuli across multiple modalities 
and valences, firing strongly to both appetitive and aversive cues, which suggests they encode the salience or significance of 
stimuli rather than their specific pleasant or unpleasant nature. Second, ChC responses were greatest to novel stimuli and 
diminished upon repeated exposures (habituation), indicating that ChCs dynamically track stimulus novelty. In contrast, the 
other interneurons (PV and SST cells) did not show such adaptation between first and later presentations, highlighting that 
this novelty-driven response pattern is unique to ChCs. Third, ChCs also encoded stimulus intensity: for example, they 
differentiated between larger versus smaller reward volumes, especially once the stimulus was familiar, thereby signaling 
relative intensity when novelty was no longer a factor. The authors demonstrate that inputs from AI and PVT are essential 
for these salience signals – blocking synaptic output from either region greatly reduced ChC responses and abolished their 
ability to distinguish novel vs. familiar or high vs. low intensity stimuli. Fourth, in an associative learning paradigm (trace 
fear conditioning), ChC activity reflected acquired salience: a tone that was initially mundane (habituated) elicited only 
weak ChC activity, but once that tone was paired with a foot shock, ChC responses to the tone increased significantly. 
Notably, ChCs maintained strong responses to the shock only when the shock was part of a learned cue-shock association, 
whereas if shocks were given randomly (unpaired), ChC responses to those shocks rapidly declined. This indicates that ChC 
firing tracks the learned significance of both predictive cues and reinforcement, not just the physical stimulus. Finally, the 
manipulation experiments establish a causal link to behavior: optogenetic silencing of ChCs during the critical cue/shock 
pairing period markedly impaired fear learning (reduced freezing to the tone), demonstrating that ChC activity is necessary 
for normal associative learning of that stimulus. Complementary chemogenetic experiments showed that enhancing ChC 
excitability (with a DREADD agonist) dampened ChCs’ calcium responses to stimuli and led to poorer learning (less 
freezing), whereas suppressing ChC excitability (making them less active at baseline, which paradoxically led to larger 
responses to stimuli) enhanced learning (greater freezing). Importantly, these manipulations did not produce general 
performance deficits or hyperactivity, suggesting the effects were specific to learning and salience processing rather than 
due to nonspecific behavioral changes. 
In summary, this study presents strong evidence that mPFC chandelier cells serve as a dedicated cortical module for 
detecting stimulus salience (through novelty, intensity, and learned relevance) and that their activity can bias learning 
outcomes. This represents a significant contribution to our understanding of how prefrontal circuits filter important stimuli 
and influence cognitive processes, adding cellular-level insight to the broader concept of the brain’s salience network.
