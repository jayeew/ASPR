Review of “When will the stratospheric water vapour return to pre-Hunga level? ” 
The manuscript presents an elegant analysis of the evolution of the unprecedented stratospheric water vapor (H
₂
O)
enhancement following the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai (HT) eruption in January 2022. While the sulfur injection
associated with this event was relatively small, the eruption led to a record-breaking input of H
₂
O into the stratosphere. The
study focuses on the temporal evolution and residence time of this excess H
₂
O, a key factor determining the duration of its
impact on stratospheric chemistry and radiative balance. 
The authors compare Microwave Limb Sounder (MLS) observations with TOMCAT model simulations to investigate the
evolution and decay of the stratospheric H
₂
O excess. The model results reproduce the observed evolution remarkably well,
especially when considering the uncertainties associated with the derivation of excess H
₂
O from MLS measurements. Their
analysis shows that the decay of the injected H
₂
O was initially dominated by dehydration processes linked to Antarctic polar
stratospheric clouds (PSCs) during 2023, followed by an increasing role of stratosphere–troposphere exchange thereafter.
Importantly, the characteristic decay timescale derived from the observations closely matches that calculated by the model,
providing a very valuable test case for all the global models. 
Overall, the manuscript is clearly written, well-structured, and scientifically sound. The results are both novel and compelling,
providing valuable insights not only into the stratospheric impacts of volcanic eruptions but also into the general
understanding of the residence time of compounds injected into the stratosphere. The work represents a significant
contribution to the field and fits very well within the scope of Communications Earth & Environment. I recommend publication
after minor revisions. I would appreciate the opportunity to review the revised version.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This paper focuses on the decay rate of water vapor following the Hunga eruption. MLS observations of stratospheric water
vapor through ~ May 2025 are compared to the TOMCAT model simulation. Understanding the decay in total stratospheric
water vapor has already been discussed in terms of the Toohey et al. (2025) formalism by Schoeberl et al. (2025). 
The authors clearly show the role of PSC formation in dehydrating the stratosphere in the post-Hunga period, and correctly
show that this is not a major sink of water vapor. This is not surprising. The polar region encompasses a small area, and the
ultra-cold Antarctic vortex temperatures can only process the small amount of stratospheric air present at the vortex formation
since the vortex acts as a barrier to tracer transport. Fig. 1 demonstrates this process using TOMCAT with monthly mean
ERA5 data and the authors note that this is normally about 23 Tg each year, but for 2023 the number was closer to 35 Tg
due to the Hunga H2O enhancement. 
Arctic dehydration, which is more intermittent played almost no role in removing
Hunga water vapor and this is also no surprise. Figures 2 and 3 expand on this point showing the water vapor loss due to
PSC formation and STE along with budgets. It is not surprising that STE loss is greater (Fig. 3d) than PSC loss given the
restriction of PSC loss to the poles. 
I presume that STE loss is counted when the water vapor anomaly is below the tropopause. The authors might want to add
some detail here about the computation.

Discussion starting on line 354 
There is some confusion in this paper about tracer lifetime. 
Toohey et al. (2025) created a formalism that clarifies the
appearance of different time scales and that formalism should be used here: 
Basically, the total stratospheric amount of a
conserved tracer can be divided into two time periods: the lag time and the decay time. 
The sum is the lifetime. The lag time
is the period when no total tracer loss is observed (before May 2023 in Fig. 4a). 
The decay time is when boundary loss (e.g.
STE) occurs and the total concentration decreases as a characteristic e-folding 
rate. Using Figure 4a, the lag time is ~1.25
years and the decay time is 3.36±0.13 years leading to a lifetime of 4.61 years. 
Schoeberl et al. (2025) computed a lifetime of
4.2±0.56 years which not inconsistent. The slightly shorter lifetime in Schoeberl et al. (2025) is due to the shorter decay time
of 2.8 ±0.56 years (the authors have ~3 years). 
These estimates are not inconsistent. 
The authors should note that, the lifetime (lag + decay time) is less sensitive to choice of the boundary between the decay
period and the lag period and is thus a better number. In this paper the authors choose a starting point for the computation of
the decay time. 
If they start earlier (e.g. January 2023) you would get a longer decay time but the lifetime would be about the
same. 
I frankly don’t believe the uncertainty value assigned to the MLS decay rate shown in Fig. 4 – it appears to be way too small
given the fluctuations in the observations and the uncertainty in the MLS measurement itself. The uncertainty is a factor of 5
smaller than that reported by Schoeberl et al. (2025) using the same data and estimating the decay uncertainty using Monte
Carlo methods. 
Figure 4b would be more useful comparing decay rates with a graph showing a generic return concentration time given the
decay rates. I have made a simple graph (below) that illustrates a simpler comparison. I am using the paper’s definition of
recovery time (point where H2O anomaly < 15 ppmv), but I am using the time since the eruption rather than the time where
the decay starts. You can see from this figure that the TOMCAT model does not agree with their estimates of the recovery
time from MLS. However, the Schoeberl et al. (2025) estimate with the larger uncertainty covers TOMCAT. 
(there is a figure here - see PDF)

Referee #1   The manuscript presents an elegant analysis of the evolution of the unprecedented stratospheric water vapor (H₂O) enhancement following the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai (HT) eruption in January 2022. While the sulfur injection associated with this event was relatively small, the eruption led to a record-breaking input of H₂O into the stratosphere. The study focuses on the temporal evolution and residence time of this excess H₂O, a key factor determining the duration of its impact on stratospheric chemistry and radiative balance. The authors compare Microwave Limb Sounder (MLS) observations with TOMCAT model simulations to investigate the evolution and decay of the stratospheric H₂O excess. The model results reproduce the observed evolution remarkably well, especially when considering the uncertainties associated with the derivation of excess H₂O from MLS measurements. Their analysis shows that the decay of the injected H₂O was initially dominated by dehydration processes linked to Antarctic polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) during 2023, followed by an increasing role of stratosphere–troposphere exchange thereafter. Importantly, the characteristic decay timescale derived from the observations closely matches that calculated by the model, providing a very valuable test case for all the global models. Overall, the manuscript is clearly written, well-structured, and scientifically sound. The results are both novel and compelling, providing valuable insights not only into the stratospheric impacts of volcanic eruptions but also into the general understanding of the residence time of compounds injected into the stratosphere. The work represents a significant contribution to the field and fits very well within the scope of Communications Earth & Environment. I recommend publication after minor revisions. I would appreciate the opportunity to review the revised version.  We thank the reviewer for these positive comments.   Minor comments:

The STE loss is diagnosed using the model by comparing two sets of paired simulations: (1) a pair without polar ice sedimentation (Hunga-NoDehydration and Control-NoDehydration) (2) a pair with both STE loss and polar ice sedimentation (Hunga and Control). Discussion starting on line 354  There is some confusion in this paper about tracer lifetime. Toohey et al. (2025) created a formalism that clarifies the appearance of different time scales and that formalism should be used here: Basically, the total stratospheric amount of a conserved tracer can be divided into two time periods: the lag time and the decay time. The sum is the lifetime. The lag time is the period when no total tracer loss is observed (before May 2023 in Fig. 4a). I frankly don’t believe the uncertainty value assigned to the MLS decay rate shown in Fig. 4 – it appears to be way too small given the fluctuations in the observations and the uncertainty in the MLS measurement itself. The uncertainty is a factor of 5 smaller than that reported by Schoeberl et al. (2025) using the same data and estimating the decay uncertainty using Monte Carlo methods.  Using all available MLS data up to the time of writing, the least-square nonlinear fit (curve_fit routine in SciPy) gives a two-standard-deviation uncertainty of ~0.13 years, assuming that the timeseries is an exponential decay with random noise. To verify this estimate, we performed a Monte-Carlo resampling and obtained a posterior distribution with a similar two-sigma spread (~0.12 years), confirming the robustness of the fit-based estimate.  If we follow the same approach used by Schoeberl et al. (2025) to include additional 10% measurement error by around 14 Tg, the Monte-Carlo analysis produces a larger uncertainty of ~0.24 years. The value is still smaller than their reported uncertainty (~0.56 years), likely reflecting differences in the length of the observational record (their study used ~6 months less data) and the treatment of the background interannual variability (they removed the natural trend before performing the fit but we are not clear how it was achieved). We agree, however, that natural variability should also be reflected in the uncertainty. To address this, we have added a new figure (Extended Data Figure 4) showing how the estimated decay time evolves as the record lengthens. The plot shows that it was simply not possible to obtain a robust decay time with no significant decline during the first two years following the eruption. One year after the onset of decay in May 2023, the estimated e-folding time stabilizes but continues to fluctuate modestly due to natural variability (e.g., ~3.6 yrs in May 2024, decreasing to ~3.2 yrs in early 2025, and returning to ~3.36 yrs by July 2025). The mean of the stabilized value is 3.34 years with a two-sigma spread of ~0.26 years. By adding the natural variability into the uncertainty estimate in quadrature, we obtain a total uncertainty of ~0.29 years.  Thus, we adopt an uncertainty of ~0.29 years for MLS, which incorporates both fitting error and natural variability. We have added a new section in Methods (Section 5.4) and a new plot (Extended Data Fig. 4) to describe the full uncertainty procedure. Section 5.3 4 It doesn’t sound like you included the uncertainty in the MLS H2O measurement (~10%) in the error budget. This would increase your estimate a little.  The MLS V5 data quality document gives the precision of a single MLS H2O profile as 5-6% at the levels that contribute significantly to the total mass. However, we have averaged 3500 profiles and 12-15 pressure levels to get daily total mass values; thus, the precision of the total mass estimates is substantially better than the single-profile precision. A 10% random error on a single profile would only be a 0.05% error on the daily mass, which will not increase the uncertainty estimate. A 10% accuracy error (i.e., a systematic bias) does not average down but is unlikely to affect the anomaly mass estimates, as it should influence the pre-Hunga and post-Hunga periods in a similar manner.
