REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This presents presents observations of wind and wave interactions on Lake Ontario using combinations of weather data and
lake floor DAS. The authors argue that observations of low and high frequency microseismic energy can be explained by
different mechanisms, namely wind strength and fetch, and present some novel ideas that help explain spectral glide

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

observed in dominant frequency peaks in DAS data. 
The paper is interesting, but is in need of a "storyline" overhaul to make it more readable and coherent. Moderate revisions
are recommended. 
Language: I would suggest an overhaul of the use of english. Though not strictly incorrect, the authors use a large amount of
"Yet, Especially, As a results, etc" to start short sentences, resulting in a bit of an awkward read (this mostly applies to the
introduction). There are also several statements that could use some polish: 
line 84 "infers" is not the right term here. 
line 111: awkward sentence; consider rewriting. 
There are a variety of tense issues (i.e. switching back and forth between present and past tense). In general, when
describing accomplished work, use the past tense. 
There are many other small examples that are not syntactically wrong, but awkward. 
Specific points: 
238: This claim is pretty broad and tenuous as per Figure 4. There is only a slight relationship between wind variability with
the Maximum PSD here, particularly for the storm event. The moderate events show an instantaneous relationship certainly,
but only for the primary increase in PSD energy in 4a. After that, it does not seem to correlate whatsoever. There is however
a reasonable correlation with water levels, at least for the storm event. It looks like there exists a complex lagged time
relation between wind variability and long term resulting water level that both contribute to microseism excitation. 
Figure 5 is much more convincing, but clearly the effects in the storm events are more complex. 
Figure 6b-c: This is a bit tenuous; to me, the relationship looks like it might be quite complicated, and your data space is
pretty undersampled for regression to be convincing. For both moderate and storm events, the pattern of mismatch is the
same (and it is not linear! I see an under-estimate for DAS at low wave factors, and an overvalue at higher wave factors in a
logistic pattern). Certainly agree with the wind speed and fetch relation to the wave factor. This seems to hold for moderate
winds. 
I find figure 7 particularly interesting. The spectral glide does indeed suggest the presence of a gradual appropriation of
energy budget toward the flow direction through the exploitation of existing wave patterns, at least intuitively. That being, is
the pattern a true conversion from HFM to LFM, or is the transition a fading of HFM as fetch increases? What evidence is
there that the HFM energy is actually converted? 
NOTE: I feel like the idea that pre-existing high frequency chaotic energy can be progressively turned into coherent low
frequency energy should be introduced concretely earlier, otherwise the reader is somewhat unclear as to where the paper
is headed until the discussion. As such, the paper feels “thick” and lacks a good story flow in spite of the very interesting
hypothesis and observations. The primary conclusion of the paper is one of explaining some of the spectrum glides in the
DAS data (Fig 2), but it takes a long time to get there. I spent a lot of time looking at figure 4 for instance, trying to find
relationships between variables, and while the text describes the figure at length, I found little compelling information there. If
the point is to show that the waves generating HFM energy is chaotic, then this should be described more succinctly and
with a more compact figure. 
Overall, this is a neat paper, but needs some clarity in terms of story flow, statement of purpose (in the intro, “lake hazard and
safety” is stated, but the authors stop short of going any further than Figure 6b/c in terms of DAS quantified wave growth, for
instance, whereas in my opinion that’s quite important). In short, this paper will be acceptable but needs to be more
“readable” in terms of a guiding story and theme.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents innovative ideas and significant advances in using DAS technology to study wind wave dynamics.
The approach of combining DAS data with wind models is particularly promising and is likely to attract significant interest
from the environmental science community. Overall, the manuscript seems well written (I am not a native English speaker),
and the figures and graphs are well designed, although some are difficult to fully understand . The discussion of the
correlation between DAS strain and wind velocity is thorough and insightful. Overall, I enjoyed reading the paper. 
-------------------------------- 
Here are some points that could enhance the paper. While these suggestions are not mandatory, implementing the
proposed revisions and additional clarifications could significantly improve the paper, making it a valuable reference for
future research in DAS applications and wind wave dynamics. 
-------------------------------- 
Introduction: 
- Lines 30-50: Please, simplify the discussion on wave origins and focus only on wind waves and eventually on swells.
There are more types of oscillation of the sea surface apart from those already written about (capillary waves, seiches,
cnoidal waves and bore phenomena...), but it doesn't seem relevant to introduce them all. 
- Lines 72-88: The JONSWAP model was developed for deep water. The authors should clarify Lake Ontario's depth
characteristics and emphasize that the JONSWAP model should be used outside the lake's shallow border.
