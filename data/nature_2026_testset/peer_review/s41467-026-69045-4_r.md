Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study introduces a groundbreaking advancement in multispectral camouflage technology through a bioinspired
hierarchical architecture of aluminum-PA66 bilayer films. The innovative design synergizes molecular-level functional group
engineering of PA66 with microscale disordered fibrous structures, effectively resolving the inherent trade-off between
radiative cooling efficiency and multispectral (infrared/laser) stealth performance. Notable achievements include
demonstrated angular insensitivity of optical properties, field-validated thermal regulation with concurrent camouflage
effectiveness, industrial scalability via roll-to-roll electrospinning technology, and environmental adaptability through
parameter customization. The developed functional group screening methodology represents a significant contribution to
polymer-based photonic material design. This work establishes a paradigm-shifting platform that integrates sophisticated
optical engineering with large-scale production capabilities, offering transformative potential for thermal management and
stealth applications in challenging outdoor environments. The following are my comments, with the hope of further improving
the quality of the manuscript: 
1. In the Introduction section, the summary of the current research status of multispectral infrared stealth in the paper is
somewhat insufficient. In fact, there have been many related studies on infrared stealth or radiation cooling fabrics in recent
years. Suggest providing a more comprehensive supplementary introduction to the research status section. 
2. Fig.1b only shows weak angular correlation and cannot indicate that the structure has stealth properties. Does the
invisibility mentioned here refer to the visible light spectrum? Suggest modifying the relevant description. 
3. How was the absorption peak obtained in Supplementary Fig. 2? Suggest supplementing relevant information and direct
measurement data, rather than just providing the band positions of the features. 
4. Supplementary Note 4: The accuracy and feasibility of the Kubelka-Munk approximate theory model need to be
discussed, especially in the calculation of angle correlation. The traditional KM theory is an approximate solution method for
the radiative transfer equation based on a series of assumptions. This method cannot accurately characterize the reflectivity
in different directions. It is recommended to verify this conclusion using more accurate solving methods, such as directly
solving electromagnetic scattering or solving the radiative transfer equation more accurately. 
5. The paper seems to only clearly indicate the thickness of the aluminum foil in Fig.2. Suggest clearly stating the thickness
of the aluminum foil used in the main text. 
6. Line 292: The introduction of a series of environmental durability tests is too brief. Suggest providing specific testing
conditions and testing time, rather than just displaying results before and after. 
7. X-films are prepared by electrospinning and are actually more like porous films composed of filamentous fabrics. Can the
thickness alone fully characterize this film? Additionally, can films of the same thickness prepared multiple times maintain
consistent film properties? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work presents a manufacturable approach to multispectral camouflage through roll-to-roll fabricated aluminum-PA66
bilayer films. The material demonstrates compelling optical performance, balancing infrared stealth capabilities with
radiative cooling functionality. The systematic experimental characterization provides evidence for the film's dual-mode
operation across critical wavelength bands. The proposed technology shows particular idea for applications requiring
adaptive thermal management and visual-infrared concealment.

1. The manuscript presents spectral trends for X-films with varying PA66 thicknesses. Please include a consolidated
summary table reporting for each thickness: (i) average emissivity in MWIR and LWIR bands, (ii) reflectance (or return signal
strength) at 10.6 μm, and (iii) average emissivity in the non-atmospheric windows (5–8 μm and 14–20 μm). A recommended
thickness range balancing camouflage and cooling performance should also be provided. 
2. While the study demonstrates the feasibility of color customization, the side effects of pigment loading on mid-infrared
performance are not quantitatively summarized. 
3. The “plicated aluminum foil” substrate is credited with enhancing laser camouflage via scattering, but no quantitative
surface morphology (e.g., roughness, wrinkle wavelength/amplitude) is provided. 
4. In Fig. 1e caption, symbols “√” and “×” are mentioned but not present in the figure. 
5. The citation for Supplementary Fig. 14 appears to refer to Supplementary Fig. 15. A thorough proofreading is needed. 
6. Mie scattering theory requires strict assumptions regarding wavelength and particle size. Given the fiber diameter (~100
nm) and MIR wavelengths (3–20 μm), the applicability of Mie theory is questionable. The authors should justify its use or
employ a more suitable model to support the claim that scattering is negligible in the MIR regime. 
7. The bio-inspired aspect is underdeveloped and appears disconnected from the main technical content. The term “bio-
inspired” appears in the title, but the Introduction does not adequately motivate this perspective, and the link to insect
cocoons feels tenuous. 
8. The manuscript states that laser camouflage at 10.6 μm results from both PA66 absorption and substrate scattering. More
detailed discussion is expected. 
9. The method of bonding between the PA66 layer and the aluminum substrate is not clearly described. The interfacial
adhesion and long-term stability of the bilayer structure under mechanical stress or thermal cycling should be discussed. 
10. The study acknowledges a trade-off between MIR camouflage and radiative cooling performance depending on PA66
thickness. However, the manuscript does not sufficiently address how this trade-off limits simultaneous optimization. The
research gap regarding materials that truly combine both functions without compromise is not fully resolved by this work. 
11. The claim that “the heat dissipation capacity of X-films was correlated negatively with the thickness of PA66 film” is
misleading. Both experimental and spectral data show that thicker PA66 layers lead to higher emissivity in the non-
atmospheric windows, which should enhance radiative cooling.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments to NCOMMS-25-60184 
This work reports a PA66 electrospinning nanofiber film combined with aluminum foil to realize a camouflage membrane.
The design leverages the infrared optical properties of nylon66, achieving the desired high reflectance in the 3–5 μm and 8–
14 μm atmospheric windows while maintaining strong absorption in other thermal infrared bands, thus offering a low-cost
stealth solution. Moreover, the absorption peak of nylon 66 at 10.6 μm—though relatively weak and of questionable
effectiveness—may impart some capability for laser detection stealth. It should be noted that the core material,
electrospinning nylon nanofibers, has previously been reported in the Nature Communications (doi.org/10.1038/s41467-
025-57366-9) for radiative cooling textiles, with quite similar material properties. This study, however, nicely repurposes

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

those same infrared optical characteristics for stealth applications and investigates them in detail. However, the idea of
camouflaging and radiatively cooling hot objects by emission in the 5-8 and > 13 μm is a well known idea, with several
papers detailing this concept reported in the literature. Therefore, from both a material and conceptual standpoint, the work
appears to have limited novelty. Furthermore, the cooling ability of this design is likely limited because of its emittance
outside the window. On the scalability front, this is a nice advance, but perhaps more suited for a more discipline specific
journal. 
Specific comments on the manuscript are as follows: 
1. Nylon fibers as a longwave transmissive but otherwise thermally emissive material has been previously discussed by
others.1,2 So materially, this is not very new, even though the authors detail the properties of nylon. This was discussed as
early as 2015. 
2. The idea of combining radiative cooling with camouflage has been quite well explored. See, for example, H Zhu et. al.,
Nat Comm, 12(1), 2021. Deng et. al, Adv. Func. Mat., 2500122, 2025, and Wang et. al., Opt Mat Express, 13(10), 2023. The
authors’ material is likely more scalable than some of the previously deployed designs, and potentially better for humans.
But the concept is known. 
3. For humans, is radiative cooling outside the LWIR window practical? Previous works have shown that outside the
window, humans gain rather than lose heat.3 Indeed for vertical surfaces, it seems that it is better to have the opposite
spectral profile of what the authors suggest.4 Thus, the cooling ability of the design seems to be limited. 
4. Frankly, the use of “Bio-inspired” in the title seems somewhat overstated. Although the design is described as inspired by
cocoons, cocoons do not function by reflecting infrared radiation for camouflage, so the connection in terms of functionality or
material design is weak. 
5. While electrospinning can indeed be scaled to roll-to-roll production, the manuscript does not actually demonstrate any
roll-to-roll process. It may be advisable to reconsider how this is framed in the title. 
6. The work does not present any solar reflection data. Since aluminum absorbs to some extent in the UV–visible range and
has low thermal emissivity, it tends to heat up under sunlight. If the nylon fiber layer can scatter solar radiation and
significantly reduce absorption by the aluminum foil, this would be highly beneficial for practical temperature control under
solar exposure. Based on only the modest thermal emittance in the non-atmospheric window, the cooling effect in the
outdoor may not be so ‘efficient’ as claimed in the Abstract. 
7. Camouflage by reflection is tricky, because the reflected temperature has to be the same as the background temperature.
Given that the average diameter of the nylon fibers is only 0.1 μm, one can infer that they are transmissive in the longwave
IR, i.g. reflection by the underlying Al is primarily specular. For example, in Fig. 4f, the cold infrared signal observed at the
shoulder may originate from reflection of the sky’s thermal radiation. But does it not stand out from the warm background,
defeating the very point of the design? This is a potential issue worth discussing. Surface treatments of the aluminum foil
could perhaps mitigate this problem. 
8. There seems to be a clear tradeoff between LWIR camouflage and laser reflection at 10.6 μm. Can the design proposed
by the authors simultaneously do both well? That seems a somewhat doubtful. 
9. Does thermal camouflage and laser camouflage collectively qualify as multispectral camouflage? The first is multispectral
for their design, and the second actually occurs within the LWIR. So line 275 of their manuscript should be edited
accordingly. 
10. Is is not more likely for technologies like LIDAR (cheaper light sources and detectors) to be used to detect humans rather
than 10.6 μm lasers (LWIR reflective camouflage gear seems a relatively unused concept)? If yes, then would this textile not
be counterproductive?

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have addressed many of the questions that we had raised. While we commend them for their efforts, we are
unfortunately not convinced that the work represents a level of novelty suitable for publication in Nature Communications.
The most notable aspect, scalability, is perhaps better suited for a more specialized journal (e.g. a journal that focuses on
thermal sensing). On this point, we would defer to the editor’s view. 
Some specific points are given below: 
1. The points of novelty claimed – screening polymers based on functional groups, and angular characterizations, are hardly
novel. In other contexts, including radiative cooling, these have been explored many times. It seems that the authors are
straining themselves to find points of novelty here. The choice of PA66 offers a tangible advancement, but is it really a
breakthrough? In fact, the authors have not sufficiently addressed the question on the tradeoff between 10.6 μm reflection
and LWIR camouflage. Please see below. 
2. The authors’ response to the question of the tradeoff between laser reflection and LWIR camouflage is misleading. The
authors claim: “infrared detection by thermal imagers relies on the average emissivity from 8 to 14 μm while the laser
detection depends on the received back signal at a single band of 10.6 μm. Thus, if the absorption width of material at 10.6
μm is narrow enough, it will have negligible impact on the average emissivity in LWIR, which means infrared camouflage
and laser camouflage are compatible.” 
But Fig. 2D of their manuscript clearly shows that for absorptance at 10.6 μm to be significant (> 0.5), the entire LWIR
reflectance must drop. Their defence, based on narrow and broad ranges, has nothing to do with the evidence they present.
This is concerning to see. In fact, if the laser absorption were to be even higher, LWIR emissivity may rise to as low as 0.5. 
3. The authors response to the question of whether the textile is suitable for cooling human beings is not well supported. The
authors claim: “The aforementioned works are mainly aimed at extremely hot environments like summer daytime or thermal
localized scenes like heat island cities, where the ambient temperature is higher than human body and it is necessary to
isolate the external energy.” Neither of these are true. For example, net heating can occur at moderately hot environments
even when emitters radiate to the sky. 
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666386424003345. For their
design, its going to be much worse, as radiation to the window is minimized. The authors also compare the cooling
performance of their design to bare aluminium, which is a strange baseline to be using for a wearable. 
4. In response to remark 7 about camouflage via reflection being tricky, the authors note that “This problem can be solved by
regionalized customized design through the thickness of nylon fibrous membrane and the load mass of pigment particles”
but would that not change the emissivity? Isn’t a better solution to just make the underlying aluminium rough? The
information in Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24 is not clear. 
5. Related to #4, the authors’ work and the controls they use encouraged us to look at IR camouflage uniforms. It seems
Ghillie suits, which are loose fitting and thus closer to ambient temperature due to convection rather than temperature of the
object to be camouflaged, are quite good at thermal camouflaging. Existing thermally camouflaged variants of these designs
may already do the job quite well. It may be worthwhile to compare such designs (e.g.
https://blog.beezcombatsystems.com/predator-ghillie-spectralflage.html) against the design proposed by the authors. That
would be better than using aluminium as a control.
