Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Upon reviewing this manuscript, I find that its scientific foundation is significantly overshadowed by a style and tone that is
more reminiscent of a sales pitch than a rigorous academic contribution. The narrative is dominated by promotional
language—terms such as “set to revolutionize” and “unprecedented” are used liberally—while core scientific details,
comparative analysis, and critical discussion are either minimized or omitted entirely. This approach detracts from the
credibility of the work and raises concerns about academic integrity, as the manuscript appears more intent on persuasion
than on objective presentation of research findings. 
From a scientific perspective, the central claim of developing a permanent polyelectrolyte multilayer (PEM) coating for self-
cleaning textiles is not as novel as the manuscript suggests. The use of PEMs for surface modification is well-documented,
and the manuscript does not provide sufficient mechanistic evidence or comparative data to demonstrate a clear
advancement over existing technologies. For example, while the authors claim an “ultra-high sulfonate density” and superior
hydration-layer formation, these assertions are not substantiated with robust experimental data such as neutron reflectometry
or atomic-scale imaging. The supporting evidence, largely based on spectroscopic data, is insufficient to convincingly
establish the superiority of their coating over established alternatives like TiO
₂
 or ZnO-based systems (for imparting self-
cleaning, antibacterial, and UV-protective properties to fabrics). 
Furthermore, the manuscript fails to address the well-established sacrificial double-layer strategies widely used in the
commercial laundry sector, particularly those developed by industry leaders such as P&G and Unilever. These companies
deploy temporary, sacrificial polymer layers that are designed to be removed with each wash, effectively trapping soils and
preventing redeposition—a critical function in fabric care. For instance, P&G’s laundry pods utilize water-soluble films, and
Unilever’s detergent formulations include polymers that form rinse-away barriers, both of which are validated through
extensive industry testing. In contrast, the PEM coating described in the manuscript is intended as a semi-permanent
solution, requiring manual reapplication every 100 cycles. This is a significant practical disadvantage compared to the
automatic, per-wash reapplication of sacrificial layers in commercial detergents. 
The manuscript’s economic and environmental claims are also problematic. The cost analysis omits key factors such as the
energy required for multilayer spray deposition and the increased material use associated with industrial-scale multilayer
application. When these factors are considered, the purported cost savings are much less convincing, especially when
compared to the established cost-effectiveness of TiO
₂
 coatings or pod-based systems. The environmental benefits are
similarly overstated, as the authors do not provide a lifecycle assessment of the PEM materials—despite known concerns
about the toxicity and environmental impact of the polymers used. 
A particularly critical oversight in the manuscript is the practicality of the proposed multiple-layer spray application method. In
laboratory settings, spray-coating a few samples may be feasible, but scaling this process to an industrial level presents
significant challenges. The requirement to deposit multiple alternating layers uniformly on both sides of large fabric rolls is
not only time-consuming but also energy- and labor-intensive. Achieving consistent layer thickness and coverage across
industrial-scale textiles is notoriously difficult with spray techniques, especially when compared to established dip-coating or
padding methods that are already optimized for high-throughput textile manufacturing. The manuscript does not address
these scalability concerns, nor does it provide any data or discussion on process optimization, quality control, or the
potential for defects and variability in mass production. This omission further undermines the practicality and commercial

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

viability of the proposed approach. 
Technically, the durability and scalability of the proposed coating are not adequately demonstrated. The abrasion resistance
tests do not replicate the mechanical stresses of real-world laundering, and the coating’s performance degrades significantly
after a relatively small number of cycles. The incompatibility of the spray-coating method with roll-to-roll manufacturing
processes—standard in the textile industry—further limits the practical applicability of the technology. 
In summary, while the manuscript presents an interesting application of PEM coatings, it does not deliver the scientific rigor,
comparative analysis, or critical self-assessment expected of a high-quality research paper. The writing style prioritizes
persuasive, marketing-driven language over transparent scientific discourse, which undermines the credibility of the work.
For this manuscript to be considered a serious scientific contribution, it must shift away from sales-oriented rhetoric, provide
robust experimental validation, directly compare its approach to established commercial technologies, address the practical
limitations and trade-offs inherent to its proposed solution, and, crucially, confront the significant scalability and practicality
issues associated with multiple-layer spray application in industrial textile processing.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper describes a method to create a self-cleaning polyelectrolyte coating based on multilayer PDADAMAC-PVS films
on various surfaces including clothing. The resulting coated surface are not only dirt and biofilm resistant, they are also
much more readily rinsed that otherwise would be case, significantly reducing the need for water and/or detergents. 
In terms of impact of chemistry research, it is not often you see research presented as clearly as Figure 1 summarizes here!
Here we have a single research study (paper) that has the potential to safe enough water to meet +2x the drinking water
needs of 2-3 billion people that lack access to adequate drinking water supply. 
This work is a little outside my main field but given both the potentially huge societal impact of this work, the quality of the
manuscript (in the main) and how thoroughly the authors have characterized this system, I would like to recommend the
publication of this manuscript in the strongest possible way, subject to the authors addressing a few very minor issues: 
1) Regarding how the paper is organized – it would have been good to show Figure 2 a lot earlier, ideally after the first
paragraph or so in the Results and Discussion segment. 
2) On a similar note, paragraphs are often very long. The paper would be more readable if some of them are broken up into
smaller paragraphs.

will potentially suppress communal disease spread but also make it a lot easier for organizations responsible for these
spaces to clean the surfaces – one could simply get them very clean with a simple rinse! I bet many a bus and train company
director at least would love that!

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors propose a versatile self-cleaning fabric coating for water-saving, detergent-free laundry based on the layer-by-
layer (LBL) technique as an alternative to conventional washing with detergents. This is certainly an interesting and timely
topic. 
However, the manuscript suffers from a somewhat promotional tone. As a reader, I feel that some of the claims are
exaggerated and that the presentation aims more at persuasion than at balanced scientific discussion. For instance, in the
introduction the authors state that “today, average washing machines consume 60–140 L of water per load.” In reality,
modern household washing machines in Europe typically use about 30–50 L per load. Such discrepancies undermine the
credibility of the work. 
Moreover, the authors should acknowledge the existence of detergent regulations, such as Regulation (EC) No. 648/2004,
which sets requirements for the biodegradability and environmental impact of detergent products. Under this regulation,
surfactants must be at least 60% biodegradable within 28 days under standard test conditions, ensuring that they break
down in wastewater and natural environments. This context is essential for a fair comparison between conventional
detergents and the proposed coating technology. 
A self-cleaning coating is indeed a highly desirable development. However, the most convincing results in this context
would come from direct comparisons of washing performance using standardized tests. There are several DIN and ISO
standards that quantify detergent performance under controlled conditions. These standards assess the removal of typical
stains (e.g., grease, ink, protein) and account for environmental considerations such as surfactant biodegradability. The
current study lacks such standardized testing; while some comparisons are presented, they are not based on recognized
protocols. 
The authors employ the LBL technique originally developed by Gero Decher for surface modification of fabrics. However,
they do not provide an appropriate reference, such as: 
Decher, G. (1997). “Fuzzy Nanoassemblies: Toward Layered Polymeric Multicomposites.” Science, 277(5330), 1232–1237. 
Similarly, their use of spray coating for polyelectrolyte deposition should cite the relevant work by Joseph Schlenoff, who
introduced sprayed polyelectrolyte multilayers as a practical alternative protocol. The omission of these fundamental
references is disappointing. 
The authors should also recognize that the LBL technique inherently produces non-equilibrium structures whose properties
depend strongly on the experimental protocol. This sensitivity makes it difficult to extrapolate findings from model surfaces to
complex substrates such as fabrics. 
Finally, the presented SFG spectra are of poor quality and contribute little to the discussion. For charged surfaces, one
would expect a significant enhancement of the water vibrational band due to orientation in the interfacial electric field. In this
context, Raman spectroscopy would likely provide more meaningful information. 
As Reviewer 1, I would not be satisfied with the response. 
Reviewer 1 raises, in my opinion, valuable questions and suggestions. It is a comprehensive program that could help a
company decide whether to invest in this technology as a product or not. The program may, however, be too ambitious for
the scope of this paper. Nevertheless, some of these aspects should be implemented in the current draft, such as the use of
standardized cleaning protocols, which would lead to much more convincing results. 
In particular, I dislike the response to item 4. Spray coating creates a non-equilibrium state that is defined by the
experimental protocol. There is certainly room for improvement by following the suggestions of Reviewer 1. 
Honestly, in its present form, this paper does not meet the standards of a Nature article.

The authors propose a versatile self-cleaning fabric coating for water-saving, detergent-
