Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript titled “Ultra-Coherent Meta-Emitter Tailors Arbitrary Thermal Wavefront” presents a dual-funnel (DF) meta-
emitter architecture that achieves arbitrary thermal wavefront shaping, including self-focusing and holography, by leveraging
the synergistic coupling of lossy and lossless boundaries. Although coherent thermal emissions based on nonlocal
metasurfaces have been studied for a while, this is one of the few works that exploits the use of thermal emissions as a
coherent source for advanced wavefront engineering applications. The concept is physically insightful, and the experimental
demonstration in the Millimeter wave regime provides convincing validation. 
Overall, this study is interesting, provides valuable insights into the control and use of thermal radiations. Several technical
and conceptual issues need to be clarified. Once these points are fully addressed, the manuscript can be considered for
publication in Nature Commnications. 
1. In the manuscript, the following sentences are used: “These long lifetime photons correlates the scatterers in the “lossless
funnel” with designed phase-delay, thereby efficiently converting the temporal coherence (Wt) to the spatial coherence (Wx)
of tailored emission waves.” 
“The cavity mode’s quality factor (Q-factor) dictates the temporal coherence of transmitted thermal photons, which is
converted to the spatial coherence through the propagation of surface modes. Local scatterers on this radiation surface can
be strategically introduced as defect perturbations to contrive phase-correlated thermal emission.” 
In these sentences, the expression “converting Wt into Wx” does not represent a strict conservation-type transformation.
Rather, it should be regarded as an engineering picture, where long-lifetime photons are utilized to facilitate phase locking
across a large-scale array. For the sake of rigor, the term “convert” is not appropriate in this context and should be replaced
with a more precise wording. 
2. One of the main features of the structure is the resonant funnel, and efficiency around 10% is given in Fig. 2(d). However,
important information is missing here. What is the total length of the structure in the x direction? Does this length affect the
overall efficiency? Is the total input power on the lossy side taken into account? It is surpring to me of an absolute effiency
around 10% since only a single funnel is used. This value is much higher than the extrodinary light transmission originally in
Thomas Ebbessen’s work. 
Furthermore, the reported energy tunneling efficiency is below 10%. While this level is sufficient for proof-of-concept
demonstrations, it remains far from optimal. The manuscript would benefit from a discussion on: 
- Whether this efficiency is limited by mode mismatch, metallic loss, or FP resonance detuning. 
- What strategies (e.g., mode converters, impedance matching, higher-Q cavities) could realistically improve this value
toward >50% or higher. 
- Whether there is a theoretical upper limit to the tunneling efficiency in this architecture. 
3. The spatial coherence length is approximated as L ≈ τ·vg, where 1/τ = 1/τWG + 1/τsurf. For maximizing emission intensity,
it is desirable for τWG and τsurf to be matched for optimal coupling, while additional scattering losses from sidewall
roughness must also be considered. However, intrinsic loss imposes an upper bound on τ. A natural question is whether,
under the constraint of a fixed τ, the design of vg can be exploited to further enhance L. Providing additional simulations or
analysis regarding the tuning of group velocity would significantly strengthen this argument.

4. It seems to me that there is a mess in different coherence lengths. Please make the following terms more explicit:
temporal/spatial coherence, temporal/spatial coherence length. Please also compare the spatial coherence length with the
one calculated by λ/Δθ, where Δθ is the angular distribution of the thermal emission at a specific wavelength. 
5. In the self-focusing section, the ensemble-averaged focal line FWHM (1.03λ0), obtained from statistical simulations of 100
random dipole excitations, is shown to be very close to the diffraction-limited value (0.93λ0) observed in the intrinsic mode
simulation. However, it remains unclear how large the intensity fluctuations are across different statistical realizations. If the
fluctuation is large, how can one exclude the possibility that the apparent sharp focusing is caused by partially coherent
imaging? 
6. The authors implement polarization multiplexing by patterning grooves along orthogonal directions. Then, when the
groove width is narrow (e.g., ~λ0/10) and the depth is large (e.g., >λ0/4), will the localized SP (or spoof-SPP) modes on the
groove walls convert the Ex and Ey components into each other via corner scattering or higher-order mode coupling,
resulting in p↔s cross-polarization coupling? If significant cross-coupling exists, how is the crosstalk in the reported
“polarization multiplexing” estimated? 
7. Thermal focusing and holography rely on precise phase control from subwavelength grooves. However, the manuscript
lacks analysis of fabrication tolerance: 
- How do random errors in groove width, depth, or position affect focusing quality or hologram fidelity? 
- What is the maximum allowable error before performance degrades significantly? 
- Are the current CNC-machined samples near this limit? 
Including a tolerance analysis (via simulation or perturbation theory) would greatly strengthen the practical relevance of the
design. 
Somee minor suggestions: 
1. I suggest in Fig. 2(a) that only a single unit cell of structure is used to demonstrate the mode profile for the two cases of BM
and W-RM. Using multiple periods might be misleading. 
2. In line 247, the authors mentioned an order of magnitude longer of the cohernece length over that in Alu’s work. I don’t this
is a fair comparison because the loss associated with the spoof SP in this work is much lower.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I have read the present manuscript on thermal emitters with great interest, and commend the authors for their creativity, but
have a number of concerns that I will address below. Some are technical, some are more conceptual, with which I will start. 
My main concern relates to the way this work is presented: supposedly outperforming, in a direct comparison as shown in
Fig. 1b, thermal metasurfaces comprising a patterned emitter. However, this comparison to me is not a fair one: this work,
and for example reference 26, rely on thermal point sources (in this case the funnel), the radiation of which is then
manipulated via a metasurface. This is conceptually completely different from thermal metasurfaces where the entire
patterned surface is emitting. In a sense, the collection of thermal emission from the backside, funneling it through a
subwavelength hole, and then manipulating it, is still cascading different optical functions (generation, inducing spatial
coherence, and then shaping the wavefront sequentially). This is completely different from true thermal metasurfaces, where
the emitting electromagnetic mode contains fluctuating sources that populate it, resulting in a single step emission process
of designer functionality. 
I therefore strongly discourage the authors to make such strong claims and by making a direct
comparison with “standard” thermal metasurfaces, particularly because these results are interesting, but claiming this much
discredits the work. 
Furthermore, very strong claims are made about creating holograms, but in this case the holograms appear to simply be
stitched-together lenses as in the first example, given that the holograms comprise only straight lines and the sample
appears to be a number of combined lenses even in the photo. Again, this is an interesting demonstration, but this is not a
hologram in the traditional sense, where a continuous phase profile yields a structure a certain distance away from the
emitter that can comprise more than only straight lines (two cylindrical lenses next to each other also are not typically
considered a hologram). 
Some technical concerns: 
- One big practical difference is that thermal metasurfaces can reach unity emissivity for specific polarization states, but in
this case the subwavelength funnel fundamentally limits the amount of power that can be emitted (a single mode waveguide
is also bound by a blackbody limit, it cannot transmit more power than that). Thermal metasurfaces are already relatively
weak emitters even at unity emissivity (compared to coherent sources), so I think this is important to address, especially if

you have only one funnel per many wavelengths: how does this affect the emitted power? 
- How is the blackbody modeled and taken into account in determining the efficiency and size of the collection area? As far
as I can tell this is not mentioned, which makes me think the black body is not incorporated directly – however, if so, that
would result in a misleading collection size. 
- How does this extend to the near- or mid-infrared? Thermal radiation at 100 GHz is weak, and thermal sources are sought
after in the mid-IR. This is an interesting demonstration but has little practical implication: at high frequencies, one would
require a very lossless material combined with a single point thermal emitter, similar to the aforementioned reference. 
- What is the spot size for the NA of the lens? It apparently is near-diffraction limited, but the value that one would expect in
the ideal case is not mentioned as far as I can tell. 
To conclude, this is an interesting demonstration of an idea very similar to ref. 26, but it is pitched in a way that is far too
strong: it is a compactified set of cascaded elements (thermal source, pinhole, wavefront shaper) with very limited
demonstrations of wavefront shaping (as far as I can tell only focusing has been shown, including in a demonstration of a
“hologram” formed by multiple lenses), which is not what a thermal metasurface does, and I therefore don’t think it should be
pitched as such or compared with them.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the effort the authors have put into the manuscript in response to my and the other reviewers' comments.
However, regarding my main concern, I do not feel this has been addressed entirely. The authors claim that the physics
between this platform and a cascaded source/pinhole/metasurface stack is completely different, but I disagree. The role of
the tunnel/pinhole is clearly crucial, because it ensures a point source/spatial coherence – and with spatial coherence it is
trivial to manipulate wavefronts, which is the backbone of virtually all metasurfaces. As such, in my opinion, this work has not
addressed the inherent challenges to thermal metasurfaces, which the authors outline nicely, but instead they have
compressed the standard approach (lamp/pinhole/metasurface) into a much smaller footprint. 
Don't get me wrong: I like this work, I think it is impressive and should be published, but it currently is minimizing/dismissing
the achievements of the community starting with Greffet who are pursuing control over thermal radiation sculpting by
suggesting that this approach is superior. It is not, it is merely a different and smart approach to ensure spatial coherence
which enables easier manipulation of wavefronts. 
I would strongly encourage the authors to remove direct comparisons between the two approaches on the basis of e.g.
spatial coherence length, since, again, in the present metasurface the coherence is imprinted by the pinhole. Likewise, I
would ask the authors to explicitly mention in the last paragraph of the introduction and in the architecture section that the
key ingredient in enabling wavefront manipulation is the thermal point source, as in the other reference that proposes
thermal holography. 
Note that there are a number of typos in Fig. 1 and the caption.

Response: Thank you for your question. The definition and calculation of efficiency were 
presented in the Extended Method Section of the original manuscript. Although our design shares 
structural similarities with those employed in extraordinary optical transmission (EOT) systems, it 
operates in the near field, in co ntrast to the far-field nature of EOT. Consequently, the efficiency 
metrics differ fundamentally due to the distinct excitation conditions involved. In EOT systems, a 
coherent plane wave source is applied. The transm ission efficiency can be directly defined as the 
ratio of transmitted power to the incident power ( 𝜂 ൌ  𝑃୲୰ୟ୬ୱ/𝑃୧୬ୡ୧), measurable via a Vector 
Network Analyzer (VNA) for the microwave band or a spectrometer in the optical band. In our 
case, however, the excitation originates from stochastic thermal fluctuations. The total transmitted 
energy strongly depends on the dipole position and polarization, making direct quantification 
difficult. Therefore, we used the surface mode as the reference source and defined the coupling 
efficiency as the ratio of the energy carried by the top surface mode after tunneling through the slit 
to that of the bottom surface mode. This measures  the mode-to-mode transfer efficiency through 
the three coupled components: bottom surface waveguide, slit waveguide, and top radiation 
surface.  
We performed simulations in COMSOL Multiphysics as illustrated in Fig. S6a (reproduced as

framework relies on surface waves excited by the grooves around the slit to enhance transmission. 
Because periodic boundary conditions are not suitable for the whole structure (a single slit with 
periodic grooves), the transmission of the entire device strongly depends on the simulation size. 
For this reason, most previous works have only reported relative enhancement compared to an 
isolated slit, rather than absolute transmission.
