Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors proposed a twist-stacked black phosphorus detector capable of broadband bipolar circular polarization
detection. The theoretical analysis and experimental data are comprehensive, and compared with other circular polarization
detectors, this work demonstrates multiple performance breakthroughs, making the approach a valuable reference. I suggest
that certain aspects still require revision before publication. 
1. What are the main factors influencing the twist angle between the upper and lower layers? If the angle is changed, how
would the effect differ? The authors could provide further discussion and supplementation through calculations and
simulations. 
2. The circular polarization responses in Figure 3 differ greatly under different connection configurations, but the related
descriptions of the corresponding physical processes are not sufficiently detailed and should be further supplemented. 
3. The RCD signal is shown in Figure 3b. The authors should describe the measurement methodology used to obtain it.
How to evaluate the rationality of using this metric to determine the degree of chirality. 
4. From the RCD measurements in Figure 3b, it is evident that regions with strong chirality are observed. The bilayer-
overlapped region also exhibits certain chirality; however, it remains unclear why the measured circular polarization
response is significantly weaker than that of the trilayer-overlapped region device. 
5. Regarding Figure 4, why do intralayer contacts and interlayer contacts exhibit different I–V characteristics, and is this a
necessary condition in this context? 
6. Although this work demonstrates significant advantages in performance, the twist-stacked structure introduces
considerable complexity and challenges for device fabrication. How can this issue be addressed? 
7. Can similar results be obtained in other non-chiral materials, such as TMDs, in addition to black phosphorus? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Jiang H. et al. proposed that the twisted stacked BP structures can generate strong chirality and enable chiral detection,
which is quite interesting. This design is supported by both experimental and theoretical results. Besides, the detector shows
advantages in infrared chiral detection. Overall, the work is of high quality and deserves to be published in NC. Here are
some revision suggestions: 
1. As shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 3-2, despite the structural design, the device exhibits responses to both
circularly and linearly polarized light. The authors are encouraged to clarify how this trade-off is addressed, and whether the
response to linearly polarized light is intended to be preserved or suppressed. 
2. The authors should clarify how the twist angle affects the CPGE response and whether the angle presented represents a
unique solution. 
3. The work in Sci. Adv. 8, eabo0375 (2022) also reported that a BP detector exhibits a response to circularly polarized light.
However, the mechanisms in these two cases appear to be entirely different. A detailed discussion is therefore necessary. 
4. If other two-dimensional materials, for instance, isotropic ones, were employed in this system, could the interference from
the linear polarization response be more effectively suppressed? The authors are encouraged to discuss possible avenues
for improving the present device.

5. In the RCD measurements of Fig. 3, strong chirality is observed in both the bilayer and trilayer overlapping regions. The
authors are encouraged to evaluate how these differences manifest in the detector performance. 
6. How is the RCD measurement performed, and does it require an applied magnetic field? If not, the labeling in
Supplementary Fig. 2-3 should be corrected. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work reports a device for detecting circularly polarized light. Using twist-stacked black phosphorus as the sensitive
material, the authors were able to detect circularly polarized light and detect a shift in the photocurrent polarity. However, the
work’s innovation and the device's performance are substandard. Therefore, I recommend rejection. The reasons are as
follows. 
a. This work is not innovative enough. Many papers have already reported on the use of angled design in two-dimensional
materials to detect polarized light. Furthermore, the authors are aware that controlling the twisting and stacking of multilayer
two-dimensional materials at specific angles has become a common strategy for introducing optical chirality into achiral
systems. 
b. The authors claim that this work satisfies multispectral detection requirements in complex scenarios, but the results show
that it only achieves a wide spectral response and lacks multispectral detection capabilities. This is confusing. 
c. Judging from the optical photos of the device, the author’s control over the interface is very rough, and it is difficult to
ensure that these uncontrollable factors will have a negative impact on the device performance noise. 
d. The device’s response to blackbody radiation is also unsatisfactory. The blackbody responsivity is an order of magnitude
lower than the laser responsivity. What accounts for this discrepancy? The test setup lacks a filter. Therefore, when exposed
to a blackbody temperature exceeding 750K, the wavelength of light emitted is primarily within the visible spectrum.
Furthermore, the device’s photocurrent switching characteristics indicate insensitivity to blackbody radiation.

Comment 5. Regarding Figure 4, why do intralayer contacts and interlayer contacts exhibit 
different IhV characteristics, and is this a necessary condition in this context? 
Author Reply 5:  We sincerely thank the reviewer for this insightful question. The distinct I tV 
characteristics observed between intralayer and interlayer contacts originate from the trilayer 
black phosphorus (BP) architecture. In addition to enhancing the chiral response induced by 
twisted BP, an important motivation for designing the trilayer structure is to exploit vertical 
electrostatic symmetry to suppress the linear polarization (LP) response from individual BP layers.
As illustrated in Figure R1.4, the device consists of relatively thinner top and bottom BP 
layers and a thicker middle BP layer. Owing to the difference in work function between the thicker 
(middle) and thinner (top and bottom) BP layers, vertically symmetric homojunctions are formed, 
resulting in a symmetric potential distribution along the vertical direction. Since the transport of 
photogenerated carriers induced by linearly polarized light in the top and bottom layers depends 
on the interlayer potential, this symmetric potential design helps to mitigate the LP component of 
the photocurrent during detection. 
It can thus be inferred that the I tV characteristics between different layers are determined by 
the work function difference between the thick and thin BP layers in this configuration. This 
behavior differs from that of intralayer I tV characteristics and is, in fact, essential for achieving 
the desired device functionality.
