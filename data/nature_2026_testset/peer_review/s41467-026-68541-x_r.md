Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
General comments 
This manuscript presents a detailed mechanistic investigation of the one-pot catenation of cage-like monomers, with a focus
on the dimeric (DCC) and trimeric (TCC) cage-catenanes. The authors convincingly show that these processes display key
hallmarks of nucleation–elongation mechanisms, but with clear topology-dependent differences: MC-1 acts as the key
templating nucleus driving efficient DCC elongation, whereas TCC relies instead on partially catenated intermediates, with
MC-2 functioning only as a nucleation-stage intermediate. 
The study is thorough and carefully executed, combining equilibrium binding analyses, critical concentration studies, time-
resolved NMR kinetics, seeding experiments, and simulations. These methods collectively provide strong evidence that the
catenation process shares important features with cooperative supramolecular polymerization. The work bridges concepts
from supramolecular polymerization and mechanically interlocked molecules, providing valuable mechanistic insight and
design principles for future development of interlocked topologies. 
That said, while I find the nucleation–elongation framework largely convincing, I recommend some nuance in the
terminology. In classical supramolecular polymerization, “nucleation–elongation” refers to purely noncovalent growth from a
nucleus. In the present system, the “nucleus” and “elongation” involve coupled dynamic covalent (imine exchange) and
noncovalent templating steps, and in the case of TCC, autocatalytic pathways via partially catenated intermediates. Thus,
describing the mechanism as “nucleation–elongation-like cooperative catenation” may more accurately capture its hybrid
nature. However, I am not entirely certain whether my opinion is correct, so I would appreciate the authors consideration. I
would also like to respect the authors’ perspective. 
Overall, the manuscript is of high quality and potential impact. I recommend publication after the following clarifications and
refinements. 
Specific comments 
1. On the possibility of panel self-assembly (Fig. 2) 
At first reading, Fig. 2 could suggest that panels self-assemble into oligomeric stacks at higher concentration, whereas at
lower concentration monomeric panels react more readily with L1 to form MC-1. Such a scenario would imply cooperativity
arising from panel self-assembly. Only later (Fig. 9) do the authors clarify that panel–panel binding is extremely weak (K ≈ 15

connection with Fig. 2) to preempt misinterpretation by readers. 
2. Wording of the σ parameter description (page 10) 
The manuscript currently states: “A decrease in σ suggests a less pronounced nucleation process, as the induction period
shortens.” The expression “less pronounced nucleation” may imply that nucleation is more difficult, which is opposite to the
intended meaning. A clearer statement would be: 
“A decrease in σ indicates that nucleation is more facile, as the induction period becomes shorter.” 
3. Clarification of σ notation 
The symbol σ is widely used in supramolecular polymerization literature as the “nucleation penalty factor.” In this
manuscript, σ is newly defined as t_induction / t_half, which is conceptually different. To avoid confusion, I recommend
considering a different symbol.

4. Seeded growth of TCC (Fig. 7) 
The text explains that addition of MC-2 has no significant effect on the induction period, while preformed TCC markedly
reduces it. This is consistent with MC-2 acting only as a nucleation intermediate, while elongation requires partially
catenated intermediates. It would strengthen the argument to emphasize more explicitly in the main text (not only in the
Supplementary Information) that the cumulative binding constant of MC-2 is much lower than that of MC-1, thus rationalizing
why MC-2 cannot serve as an efficient seed. 
5. Terminology consistency 
The terms induction period and lag phase appear somewhat interchangeably. Harmonizing these terms would improve
clarity, especially for readers less familiar with kinetic analyses. 
6. The present study also reminded me of a work by Datta et al. (Nature 583, 400–405, 2020), which described the formation
of polycatenanes from supramolecular toroids through a secondary nucleation mechanism. While the molecular systems are
very different, both works highlight how nucleation–elongation-like processes can drive catenation into higher-order
architectures. Citing this study would help readers appreciate the broader context and underline the generality of catenation
phenomena governed by cooperative growth pathways.

Catenation”, reports on the nucleation-elongation mechanism in the one-pot synthesis of topologically distinct catenanes,

In supramolecular polymerization, the elongation phase leads to the formation of long polymers, which are typically observed as fibrous structures in TEM/AFM. However, in the present manuscript, the largest catenane cage observed is a trimer (TCC). Thus, how appropriate is it to call it as an elongation? We thank the reviewer for question, which is also mentioned by Reviewer 1. It is indeed about the distinction between supramolecular polymerization and the mechanism observed in our system (discrete catenanes). It is correct that elongation in many well-known systems (like actin, myloid fibrils and synthetic supramolecular polymers) is associated with the formation of long polymer chains. However, we have used the term "elongation" based on its fundamental mechanistic definition, rather than the morphology of the final product. Here is our reasoning:  (1). Fundamental definition: "Elongation" is defined as "the act of lengthening something." Our final product (e.g., TCC) is demonstrably larger (i.e., of a higher degree of polymerization) than the nucleus (e.g., partially catenated intermediates) from which it forms.  (2). Nucleation-elongation mechanism: it is characterized by two kinetically distinct phases, and the growth from our nucleus to the trimer (TCC) is this second, post-nucleation step. We are therefore using the standard, well accepted term for this specific kinetic pathway.
