Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
A novel tool, ORCA, is presented for the identification of RNA modifications in data generated through the direct sequencing
of native RNA. This is a very active area of research. Around 170 modifications exist, and tools have been developed that
are able to identify only a small subset of these modifications. New tools are then needed for a more comprehensive
mapping and characterization of the marks that make up the epitranscriptome. 
ORCA represents a promising solution for the comprehensive mapping of the marks that are visible through the lens of
Nanopore sequencing. It leverages deep learning methods aimed at collectively identifying these marks – through
adversarial training, i.e. forcing the learning of generalized features that are shared across modifications - and takes

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

advantage of large collections of RNA modification positions stored in public databases for annotating the identified hits. An
extensive set of validations through orthogonal methods and datasets is included. 
Altogether, the data suggest that ORCA could represent an important new method in this rapidly evolving while competitive
field. The manuscript is well written, and results are well presented and convincing. Major and minor comments are listed
below. 
MAJOR COMMENTS (1-13) 
1. TandemMod is one of the few tools currently available that aims at comprehensively identifying RNA modifications. I
would suggest a more thorough discussion of how ORCA’s features stand against it (including TandemMod model transfer
abilities), and a more extensive comparison of the results, vs some independent reference “gold standard”. Such
comparison, vs TandemMod, is currently largely limited to m6A predictions. 
2. The concept of adversarial training, i.e. forcing the learning of generalized features that are shared across modifications is
interesting. It remains to be clarified what is the level of control that the Authors have on which/how many modifications are
suitable for this approach according to the collected features. Are the “shared features” necessarily shared among all
modifications under consideration, or they can be shared among sub-groups of a larger set of modifications under
consideration? Eventually, what is the search space, how many modifications are eventually targeted, considering the ability
of the tool to proceed without training data for specific marks? 
3. What is the impact of the ELIGOS training set on ORCA's performance, in terms of experimental design (position,
composition and prevalence of the modification) and quality of the Nanopore data? 
4. What are ORCA's CPU-GPU/memory requirements? 
5. The code for easily reproducing all analyses the analyses should be made available as an integral part of the study 
ORCA employs a two-step strategy based on detection and annotation. The RNA modifications database (DBs) that are
considered are an important resource. However this rises a number of points that should be clarified: 
6. These DBs are heavily populated by data about specific modifications (particularly m6A) and in specific cell lines (such as
HEK and HeLa cells). How is the biased content of the considered modification DBs taken into account? 
7. Using modifications sites non-associated to DBs hits (unannotated sites) as negative controls, in the first stage, seems
like a problematic concept (especially for non-m6A modifications, see the previous point), even though these sites are
eventually re-considered, and potentially ex-novo annotated. The Authors should better discuss at which extent they need to
represent negative controls, and what are the implications of this clearly over-simplified categorization. 
8. How is the DB’s composition impacting the results presented in Figure 6, about the clusters of adjacent modifications,
which are heavily including m6A (being this over-represented in those DBs). 
9. More in general, how close ORCA's hits need to be to DB's records to be annotated by them? 
Sequencing depth: 
10. How is ORCA impacted by the sequencing depth at a given genomic site? 
11. When analyzing various samples or conditions, the number of reads could greatly vary depending on technical reasons
(e.g. the overall number of reads produced by flow cell) or for biological reasons (e.g. varying number of reads associated to
the same gene or genomic position as a consequence of differential gene expression between conditions/treatments). Is
ORCA in need to / able to get rid of this bias, for example by sub-sampling the reads of the larger dataset (at the sample
and/or at the gene or site level) or by any other means? 
Evolution of the Nanopore platform: 
12. What is the compatibility of ORCA in terms of RNA002 and/or RNA004 Nanopore chemistry? Not proving that ORCA is
able to work with RNA004 data would be a tremendous limitation. 
13. Regarding RNA004, a comprehensive comparison with Dorado's predictions of RNA modifications should be included. 
MINOR COMMENTS (14-24) 
14. Defining ORCA an "universal framework for RNA modification detection" is likely an overstatement, given the vast
prevalence of untested (and currently largely untestable) modifications 
15. It remains unclear whether reads-level predictions are returned by ORCA 
16. How were the features selected (Fig. 1a)?

21. The chosen color palette often makes it very difficult to distinguish the various colors, e.g. in Fig. 2e 
22. The results about pseudouridine were not so convincing, due to the very little number of hits. 
23. When comparing ORCA's predictions with m6A-SAC-seq (line 244) could the author better described how many hits
were called in total by ORCA and how many of these could be associated with the DRACH motif 
24. Unclear statements: “increased sequence variability” (line 101); ".. we restricted base-level features to discrepancies
between basecalled and reference sequences .." (lines 113); "RNA modifications without type-specific bias" (118); "LSTM
layers"; "RNA modifications of the same type" (line 128).

However, ORCA requires high-quality training sets with accurate ground-truth labels for both 
modification presence and stoichiometry. Most endogenous direct RNA-seq datasets cannot 
provide such stoichiometry labels, which cannot be reliably determined using current NGS-
based detection methods. Therefore, many widely occurring RNA modifications cannot be

generalization ability. As additional IVT transcriptome resources become available, ORCA can

17. ORCA focuses on a 9 nt window centered on each candidate site. What is the rationale, 
justification, and implications of this choice? 
Response: Thanks for the comment. During nanopore direct RNA sequencing, the signal is 
determined by approximately 5 nucleotides passing through the pore (PMID: 31931956). A 
single RNA modification can therefore perturb the current profile across five consecutive 5-
mers surrounding the modification site, which together span a 9-nt region. To capture this effect, 
ORCA extract signal and alignment features from these 9-nt windows to each candidate site. 
Furthermore, Although the official pore model for RNA004 is parameterized as a 9-mer, several 
studies have shown that the central five positions still dominate the current signal (PMID: 
40085000), so the 9-nt window used by ORCA remains a reasonable approximation for both 
RNA002 and RNA004 chemistries. In future work, the window could be extended, for example 
to 13 nt, to more fully exploit the extended 9-mer context of RNA004.
