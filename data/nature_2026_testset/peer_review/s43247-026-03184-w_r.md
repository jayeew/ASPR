Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript “Climate change and ocean acidification risk to underwater cultural heritage” addresses one of the most
pressing issues related to climate change: its impact on underwater cultural heritage. 
Major Claims and Novelty 
The authors present an initial quantitative assessment of the risks posed by climate change to underwater cultural heritage,
focusing on the vulnerability of historic stone materials to ocean pH variations. This is a relevant and timely study that
provides a much-needed framework for evaluating climate-driven deterioration processes. 
Strength of Evidence 
The study adopts a mixed field and laboratory experimental approach to monitor the amount and rate of stone surface
material loss and structural alteration triggered by natural processes of mineral dissolution and biodeterioration in
underwater environments. The experimental models describing stone surface changes are then combined with IPCC ocean
pH data to define decay trends and assess risks for pre-industrial, present, future, and far-future periods. This approach
provides a valid broad-scale projection. However, the dataset currently refers only to global ocean pH and does not
incorporate available Mediterranean-specific data, even though the experiments were conducted in the Mediterranean
basin. I strongly encourage the authors to add this regional dimension. This is particularly relevant because the
Mediterranean has already warmed significantly more than the global average, with mean temperatures rising by about
1.4°C since the pre-industrial era. As most underwater archaeological and historical heritage sites are located in the
Mediterranean, this point deserves emphasis. Useful information can be found, for example, in IPCC WGII AR5, Chapter 30
(https://www.ipcc.ch/site/assets/uploads/2018/02/WGIIAR5-Chap30_FINAL.pdf) and in recent studies such as
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-07858-x. 
Additional Considerations 
Besides acidification, Mediterranean warming has facilitated the spread of tropical species invading from the Atlantic and
Indian Oceans. These organisms could become important agents of biodeterioration, particularly in bioreceptive stone
materials. Moreover, in the context of marine bioerosion, it is known that growing environmental changes (eutrophication,
coral mortality, and ocean acidification) are expected to elevate bioerosion in the near future. Endolithic organisms such as
boring sponges, which erode carbonate-based submerged heritage materials, may accelerate their bioerosive activity under
conditions of increasing acidification. While this aspect has been more thoroughly studied in coral reef settings (see, for
instance, https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/74/4/895/3064242), it could be at least partially correlated with processes
occurring in the Mediterranean and deserves consideration here. 
Finally, I note that Figures 3, 4, and 5 are missing from the submitted material, while only Figures 1 and 2 are included.
These figures should be provided to ensure consistency between the text and the supporting material. 
Potential Impact 
The paper has the potential to make a significant contribution to the field of underwater cultural heritage conservation. With
the inclusion of Mediterranean-specific data and consideration of biological threats linked to warming, the study would
provide a stronger, regionally relevant, and more comprehensive assessment that could influence both conservation
strategies and future research.

 Line 33 “seawater acidity has increased by 26% “. According to Copernicous data it is 40%. I
wonder with kind of acidity measurement the authors use, only based on free H+ io ns? Maybe 
they can describe the acidity concept they used. 
Answer: For acidity we use just the common definition referring to H+ ion concentration. We consider 
as reference for the whole article the latest IPCC report, where it is stated that «The pH of gl obal 
surface waters has decreased from 8.2 to 8.1 since the pre -industrial era». Converting the 0.1 pH 
difference in H + concentration change, that amounts to +25.9% acidity. We have verified this also  
with the actual IPCC historical data (used for creating Fig. 4 of this paper).  We appreciate that the 
latest IPCC report is not updated to present pH measurements and that the current acidity increase 
is higher (considering the modeled data averaged from diffe rent IPCC scenarios, we have just 
calculated an acidity increase of 35% for year 2024). In order to account for the present situation 
while still being consistent and referring to IPCC, we have chosen to replace that figure with a more 
generic phrase: “at least ~30%”. This is also more cautious: we have read the Copernicus webpage 
(https://marine.copernicus.eu/ocean-climate-portal/ocean-acidification) citing the 40% increase, but 
that does not report references or precise ΔpH from the pre -industrial period (only from 1985); on 
the other hand, Feely et al. (2023) in Oceanography mention  that «Over the past two -and-a-half 
centuries, surface ocean pH has decreased by about 0.1 1, which is an increase of about 30% –40% 
in the hydrogen ion concentration (Orr et al., 2005; Gattuso et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2019)».
