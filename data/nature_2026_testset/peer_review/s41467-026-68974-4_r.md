Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this article, the authors primarily focus on two aspects of the research: On one hand, they designed an ammonia gas
sensor based on triboelectric nanogenerator probe (TENG-P) and detailed its working principles. Compared to the second-
level response time of traditional gas sensors, this sensor achieves a millisecond-level response time. Its integration with
deep learning technology also demonstrates exceptional accuracy in ammonia concentration detection. On the other hand,
they analyzed the advantages of A-droplet with a bilayer structure over conventional droplets, and demonstrated that using
ammonia-dissolved A-droplet as a medium can effectively enhance the output stability of the sensor. This study provides a
high-precision, rapid-response and stable solution for ammonia concentration detection in agricultural greenhouse
environments. However, the article still has several critical issues that need to be addressed before publication to enhance
its persuasiveness and scientific value. 
1. The article only provides a schematic diagram of the sensor structure in Fig.2-a, but omits photographs of the physical
devices. It is recommended to supplement images of the fabricated sensor to facilitate readers’ intuitive understanding of its
working mechanism. Additionally, it is also recommended to supplement the schematic diagram of the fabrication process of
the sensor. 
2. All sensor performance data were obtained under controlled laboratory conditions. Practical application scenarios, such
as greenhouse, have certain differences from the laboratory environment (e.g.,temperature and humidity), which often
fluctuate over time and other factors. Therefore, it is recommended to include performance tests under realistic
environmental conditions to enhance the credibility of the test data significantly, and further demonstrate the robustness and
stability of the device. 
3. The article does not directly compare the detection capabilities of multiple sensors, so it is recommended to add a test of
multiple sensors under identical conditions, and compare the consistency of their output. In addition, the short-term stability
of the sensor was tested through 100 cycles, but the long-term stability over weeks or even months was not explicitly
evaluated. Considering that the normal operation of the sensor relies on the electron-capturing function of the Teflon-based
tribo-layer, prolonged exposure may lead to dust and other contaminants adhering to its surface, thereby degrading sensor
performance. Therefore, it is recommended to add long-term stability experiments for the sensor. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a new method for ammonia monitoring based on a droplet triboelectric nanogenerator. The
reported stability is maybe a plus. However, I have some serious concerns, particularly regarding the claimed 2.1 ms
response time. It seems to me that the overall system response is fundamentally limited by the droplet down-drop frequency,
which casts significant doubt on that rapid response time 2.1 ms claim. Given this key limitation, I can't recommend this
manuscript for publication in Nature Communications. 
1. The sensor relies on the formation of A droplets from ammonia and sodium dodecylbenzene sulfonate. I'm wondering,
could the sensor's performance be affected by the presence of carbon dioxide or water vapor? Figure 5d shows a relatively
low response to other gases, which is encouraging. However, what happens when these gases are mixed with ammonia?
Will the sensor still accurately detect the ammonia concentration in the presence of CO2 and water vapor? This is a critical
point that needs clarification. 
2. While the sensor's intrinsic response time is fast, the overall system response is clearly limited by the slow rate of A-
droplet formation. It appears the response time of the sensor system isn't really governed by the TENG output speed, but

rather by the rate at which the A-droplets are generated and dropped. Given this bottleneck, how can the authors justify
claiming a fast response time for the entire system? 
To be precise, the response time should reflect the time interval between a change in the physical quantity being measured
(in this case, ammonia concentration) and the time it takes for the TENG sensor to register that change. So, how long does it
actually take for the TENG sensor to detect a sudden spike in ammonia concentration? 
Furthermore, the data presented in Figure 2d, where the ammonia concentration changes abruptly from 0 ppm to 50 ppm, is
concerning. The time it takes for the sensor to detect ammonia from 0 ppm to 50 ppm is also not the reported 2.1ms. The
sensor seemingly fails to capture any intermediate states. This suggests a significant delay or a lack of sensitivity to gradual
changes in ammonia concentration, which further undermines the claim of a fast response. The authors need to address this
discrepancy directly. 
3. Authors tweaked the height and angle to get a more stable reading. But I'm wondering, does that stability hold up when
the ammonia concentration starts fluctuating? 
4. Authors explain that the stability of A-droplet comes from two substances. I'm curious, can we apply this finding to other
droplet-based friction nanogenerators, or is this just a special case? 
5. The paper doesn't really get into optimizing the sensor's structure or materials to improve performance like sensitivity,
range, detection limit, or resolution.

(Remarks to the Author) 
In the proposed manuscript entitled “Bioinspired triboelectric droplet sensor for ammonia monitoring”, the authors have
developed a liquid-solid triboelectric nanogenerator for rapid and accurate sensing of ammonia gas. In the proposed setup,
the authors have utilized an air-cavity droplet (A-droplet) using a coaxial injection system and a triboelectric nanogenerator
probe (TENG-P) for sensing purposes. For optimization purposes, the ratio of the flow rate of liquid and gas, frequency of
impacting droplet, size of the top electrode, impacting height, and sliding angle are optimized to obtain the maximum stable
output. The proposed system has shown a remarkable enhancement in response time of the ammonia sensing and
integration of deep learning efficiently provided the recognition of ammonia concentration. Following are some comments to
improve the quality of this manuscript: 
1. In order to validate the enhancement in the diffusion rate of NH3 molecule on the triboelectric surface after interacting with
water molecules, the authors are suggested to provide the triboelectric current data upon direct exposure of different
amounts of NH3 gas to the triboelectric surface as similar to the different amount of NH3 given in A-droplet for sensing. 
2. In order to show the effect of NH3 concentration on hydrophobicity, the authors are suggested to provide contact angle
data of A-droplets with different concentrations of NH3. 
3. The authors are suggested to provide a discussion about the potential of the proposed ammonia sensing system for
usage in practical situations, such as collection of gas from targeted areas and implementation for sensing. It is also
recommended to provide the sensing data for gases collected from a real environment. 
4. It seems that the authors haven’t provided the details about how they have changed the concentration of ammonia gas in
A-droplet for sensing data provided in Fig. 6b. In order to make the manuscript more informative and enrich its
understanding, the authors should provide these experimental details. 
5. The authors are suggested to discuss the motivation for selecting Teflon as the tribo-layer and provide a systematic
comparison of sensing performance for different tribo-surfaces. 
6. Regarding the information provided about tribo-layer in the methods section (Teflon), and in Fig. 3b (FEP) is not
consistent. The authors are suggested to rectify this mistake. 
7. (a) Regarding the schematic presented in Fig. 6e related to deep learning, the authors are encouraged to briefly discuss
the CNN model architecture employed, details about the datasets used, and the methodology applied to validate the
classification results in the present study. (b) Given that the dataset shown in Fig. 6b appears relatively simple, the authors
are requested to clarify why a complex model such as a CNN was chosen over traditional machine learning approaches like
SVM or Random Forest. 
8. In Fig. 2a and Fig. 2c, the labeling for different components of TENG-P looks confusing because the color of the tribo-layer
is not consistent over Fig. 2a and Fig. 2c. Please rectify this kind of mistake to enhance the readability of this manuscript. 
9. Regarding Fig. 2b, where the authors have shown a change in the size of A-droplet upon change in the ratio of liquid and
NH3. However, despite the size of the A-droplet, the appearance of the A-droplet also looks different. The authors should
clarify that the changes in bright and dark areas are just a part of reflectance during photography or shape of air inside the A-
droplet is changing. 
10. The authors are suggested to provide the analysis of surface morphology and chemical compositions of tribo-layer
before and after the sensing of ammonia to show the stability and reusability of the proposed sensor as the authors have
shown generation of stable output over 100 cycles.

I have doubts about the data presented in the paper. The reported response time has been drastically revised from a claimed
2.1ms to 1400ms, a 666-fold increase. Furthermore, the critical advantage of TENG stability is undermined by inaccurate
representation and flawed experimental design. Figure 2f, purporting to show stability testing, appears to have been
conducted under conditions significantly different from those simulating the claimed high-speed response time 1400ms. The
response time and stability tests were performed under disparate experimental setups. High-speed droplet impact, which
can affect TENG stability (through thermal effects, material wear, interfacial charge accumulation and dissipation kinetics),
was not adequately considered in the stability testing. I do not recommend publication in Nature Communications. 
1.The stability test in Figure 2f exhibits a significantly different droplet impact frequency compared to the "response time"
tests in Figures 2d and 2e. Judging from the waveforms in 2d and 2e, the response time tests appear to have been
conducted at a rate of approximately three droplets per second, representing a relatively high droplet impact rate. However,
the stability test in Figure 2f, estimated from the data points, shows approximately 22 droplets impacting the sample over a
150-200 second period. This translates to an average droplet impact frequency significantly below one per second, starkly
contrasting with the implied high-speed impact in the response time tests. This disparate droplet impact frequency across
different testing phases constitutes a serious methodological flaw. Figures 3d, k, 4e-h, and 6b suffer from the same issue.
Furthermore, high-speed video of the electrical testing with droplets impacting the FEP membrane is required to ensure no
interference between successive droplets. 
2. Why does the ammonia concentration in the water within the tube stabilize after 1 second (Fig. S4)? This conclusion
requires verification with precise ammonia concentration detection equipment. 
3. The consistency shown in Figure 3i, with only 10 cycles, is far from sufficient. As a sensor, it needs to operate
continuously for a much longer duration. 
4.The molecular dynamics simulations in Figure 5 only demonstrate the principle of ammonia concentration sensing; they do
not provide a rationale for its high-speed and stable sensing capabilities. 
5. The paper's application of AI, while presented, lacks practical, real-world testing. There are no circuit, physical images, or
demonstration videos. The innovative aspect of the AI is unclear. The CNN model architecture is not explained. The
dimensionality of the input data is unknown. Real-world scenarios involve variations in ambient temperature, humidity, and
atmospheric conditions throughout the day and night. More physical photographs and videos are needed to demonstrate the
sensor's practical utility. 
6. Why does the sensor output remain constant despite changes in ambient temperature, given that the solubility of ammonia
in water is temperature-dependent? 
7. The video that has already been shown does not show a physical image of the sensor, nor does it display the electrical
signal output.

Response: We fully agree with the reviewer’s observation that the sensor must exhibit 
an intermediate state reflecting changes in the external physical quantity. In the revised 
manuscript, this intermediate variation has been experimentally validated (Fig. 2d), 
establishing the sensor’s response time at 1.4 s. We further clarify the origin of this 
intermediate state. Specifically, the dissolution rate of ammonia in water far exceeds 
the droplet formation cycle, meaning the ammonia concentration during the droplet 
release and monitoring process remains relatively stable. Additionally, electron transfer 
at the liquid -solid interface occurs instantaneously; thus, liquid -solid contact 
electrification does not induce a pronounced intermediate state. Therefore, the 
intermediate state observed in the sensor response originates from gas diffusion within 
the delivery tubing (Supplementary Fig. 4). In the tubing, high-concentration ammonia 
gradually diffuses into the surrounding air, causing sequential droplets to contain
