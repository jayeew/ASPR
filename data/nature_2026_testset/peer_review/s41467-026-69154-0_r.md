Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is an interesting article written by Yang et al using phage therapy in combination with Imipenem and Fluconazole for
the treatment of refractory K. pneumoniae peritonitis. To my knowledge, this is the first case that describes phage therapy
use in a peritoneal dialysis patient. For this article, I have several concerns (listed below) before considering this paper to be
published in Nature Communications. 
Major: 
• What was the legal basis for giving phage therapy? Is it according to compassionate use and some local regulatory
framework? Or is it under the Helsinki Declaration, Paragraph 37?

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

• It is hard to imagine that a patient with end-stage kidney disease, who has been on dialysis for 9 years, does not have any
other comorbidities attributed to CKD? Did the patient have anemia (was it treated?), or did the patient not have secondary
hyperparathyroidism? 
• What was the reason to treat the patient with no clinical improvement for 14 days in K. pneumoniae peritonitis episode? Is
this following local guidelines? 
• There is no information given on phage morphology, genetic typing, and nature–lytic? Or lysogenic? This information is
crucial for phage therapy. 
• There is no information/data given on phage interactions with antimicrobials used in combination for the treatment. This
influences the microbiological outcome and clinical response as phages commonly have a synergistic effect with antibiotics
but may also exhibit antagonism. 
• Why was only IgG tested? I would expect to see IgM and IgA curves as well. Additionally, the results indicate there were no
preexisting phage-specific IgGs. Why did the authors test for neutralizing antibodies/reactions only in the PD fluid? Why not
in serum? Demonstrating this would be essential to show the systemic effect of the immune response when administering
phages intraperitoneally. 
• There is a lack of data on the proposed phage strategy. I would suggest creating infection curves using phages and then
checking the susceptibility of phage-resistant mutants to antibiotics, as well as the susceptibility of the other phage used.
Also, it would be essential to show the cocktail combination effect on phage resistance development. 
• I do not see the discussion part of SCR for S. haemolyticus peritonitis relevant for this paper, as it focuses on phage
therapy of K. pneumoniae peritonitis.

• Why was only IgG tested? I would expect to see IgM and IgA curves as well. 
Additionally, the results indicate there were no preexisting phage-specific IgGs. Why

not in serum? Demonstrating this would be essential to show the systemic effect of

Response: Thank you for your advice. In this therapy case, the bacteremia did not 
occur in the patient, and the infection occurred in the local peritoneal cavity. And 
also, the phage cocktail was used in the peritoneal cavity. Therefore, we want to know 
whether neutralizing antibodies are produced, which could decrease the therapeutic 
effect of the phage. Neutralizing antibodies in serum seem not to be so important for 
the activity of phage in the peritoneal cavity; in other words, they could not affect the 
clearance of bacteria by phage in the peritoneal cavity. Furthermore, the production of 
neutralizing antibodies needs more time, usually several days. Therefore, we think 
that the detection of neutralizing antibodies in the PD fluid has more important 
significance. 
In fact, we did not specifically detect IgG. We used the dialysate effluent to 
complete the detection, which is a mixture of many Ig. However, though a large 
proportion of antibodies can bind to phage, they cannot close the activity site used to 
infect the bacteria, which can not affect the therapeutic effect of phage. In contrast,

“IgG” should be corrected to “neutralizing antibodies” in the third-to-last paragraph 
of the Results section. 
• There is a lack of data on the proposed phage strategy. I would suggest creating 
infection curves using phages and then checking the susceptibility of phage-resistant 
mutants to antibiotics, as well as the susceptibility of the other phage used. Also, it

Response: The advice is very interesting and helpful for the phage therapy strategy 
and clinical outcome of patients. In fact, we have built a big K. pneumoniae phage 
library, in which different K. pneumoniae phages can lyse different capsule types of 
K. pneumoniae. We have accumulated and known the match-up relationship between 
the bacteria and phage. In fact, the bacteria of the patient in this case can produce 
phage-resistance to ΦCP-p-KP-23240 within 4 hours and become the phage-resistant 
mutant (shown in Fig.3). We have detected the susceptibility of the other phage of a 
lot phage-resistant mutants (the original strains are isolated from different patients but 
have same capsule type), and these phage-resistant mutants can be lysed by ΦCP-p-
KP-21067 (cannot lyse the original bacteria). The addition of ΦCP-p-KP-21067 in 
this cocktail can make the resistant time extend to 24 hours or even longer (shown in 
Fig.3).

refractory peritonitis caused by Staphylococcus haemolyticus. Following your 
recommendation, we shortened the description and discussion of SCR for this patient, 
providing a detailed account of the patient's condition and the complex treatment
