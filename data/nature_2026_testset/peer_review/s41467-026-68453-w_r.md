Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their manuscript “BrainScale: Enabling Scalable Online Learning in Spiking Neural Networks” Wang et al. present a
substantive amount of work with three main contributions: 
• Derivation and implementation of another (two variants of) approximation of RTRL in spiking neural networks 
• A software platform for SNN simulation and training built on JAX, including methods for using their new RTRL
approximations 
• Validation of both the learning rules and the software on computational neuroscience models and neuromorphic computing
benchmarks 
Overall, the paper is very well structured and written and the results look sensible. However, I have some concerns 
Major: 
1. In the validation on neuromorphic benchmarks, the authors are not relating their results to the recent literature. To name
just a few works, 
a. On SHD: 
i. Hammouamri, I., Khalfaoui-Hassani, I. and Masquelier, T., 2023. Learning delays in spiking neural networks using dilated
convolutions with learnable spacings. arXiv preprint arXiv:2306.17670. 
ii. Bittar, A. and Garner, P.N., 2022. A surrogate gradient spiking baseline for speech command recognition. Frontiers in
Neuroscience, 16, p.865897 
iii. Nowotny T, Turner JP, Knight JC. Loss shaping enhances exact gradient learning with eventprop in spiking neural
networks. arXiv preprint arXiv:2212.01232. 2022 Dec 2. 
iv. Sun, P., Chua, Y., Devos, P. and Botteldooren, D., 2023. Learnable axonal delay in spiking neural networks improves
spoken word recognition. Frontiers in Neuroscience, 17, p.1275944. 
v. Pengfei Sun, Ehsan Eqlimi, Yansong Chua, Paul Devos, Dick Botteldooren, Adaptive Axonal Delays in feedforward
spiking neural networks for accurate spoken word recognition, arXiv:2302.08607 
vi. Yu, C., Gu, Z., Li, D., Wang, G., Wang, A. and Li, E., 2022. Stsc-snn: Spatio-temporal synaptic connection with temporal
convolution and attention for spiking neural networks. Frontiers in Neuroscience, 16, p.1079357.

3. Line 90ff : The concepts of “AlignPre” and “AlignPost” are not new and should be referenced to the literature

more than two lines of additional code (the red box in Figure 4F).” – unclear, how can a forward algorithm be made
“compatible with reverse-mode AD”? 
5. Line 251: “However, without surrogate gradients, BPTT failed to train successfully on this task, with its training accuracy
fluctuating around the 50% random baseline (Figure 5F). ” – unclear: how do you train using BPTT without a surrogate

(Remarks to the Author) 
The article presents a scalable algorithm for brain dynamics training. The authors defined three postulates regarding
scalability in relation to wide applicability, effective memory management and prototyping using target hardware
architectures. The authors performed tests on a relatively narrow set of LIF and ALIF neurons and various synapse
configurations described in Method 7.2. I positively evaluate narrowing the tests to such a set of models due to their use in
modern neuroprocessors. Additionally, the use of a sigma-delta network is worth considering in future analyses. Both the hot
topic of training spiking neural networks and the solutions proposed by the authors make the work valuable. I positively

evaluate the division of work proposed by the authors. The addition of Chapter 7 “Methods” and addition of “Supplementary
materials” make it much easier to navigate through the extensive material. The work is written carefully and is a reliable
study of the proposed approach. However, I think that the work lacks a reliable comparison of the results achieved by the
authors with the results of existing frameworks. The functionality of frameworks would also require comparison with existing
tools. Detailed comments below: 
1. I suggest organizing the numbering of the bibliography, e.g. according to the order of use. 
2. Please verify the content of the Supplementary materials section. Not all of its elements have links in the text, e.g. there
are no links (and therefore comments) to tables S1-S11. I suggest additionally increasing the font size on some charts in this
section, e.g. S12-S14. Please verify the notation in tables S3, S4, S8, S9, S10, S11 with the values V_v, V_rest, V_reset,
V_th (should be as in table S7). 
3. I suggest adding information about the access date to the used datasets in the Data availability section. 
4. I suggest adding a legend to Figure 2 explaining the meaning of the colors and symbols used in the diagrams. In the case
of Fig. 2A and 2B, I suggest introducing a nomenclature for the columns (before and after the merging process) 
5. I suggest verifying that all shortcuts are defined on first use. For example, DMTS appears in the caption of Figure 5 on
page 9 (linked to on page 8), but its explanation appears on page 10. The abbreviation BIC is not explained at all. 
6. I would suggest describing how the online learning program compiler illustrated in Fig. 4 corresponds to the general
concept illustrated in Fig. 1. 
7. One of the most recognized SNN learning algorithms is the ANN-SNN conversion (e.g. bootstrap algorithm). It turns out to
be competitive with many new and innovative approaches. On the other hand, conversion is often worse according to the
memory usage criterion. I believe that the summary of the BPTT, D-RTRL, ES-D-RTRL algorithms shown in Fig. 5JKL (data
length vs. memory/time per bach/accuracy) should be compared with the results for the conversion method. It may be
particularly valuable to compare the time per batch parameter, which is usually incomparably lower for conversion than for
backpropagation. Similarly, I would like to ask to supplement the data in Table 1 with the parameters obtained for the ANN-
SNN conversion. I would suggest bolding the solution with the best result in the list. It is also worth comparing with the
conversion algorithm the results of the accuracy for cognitive task performance analysis demonstrated in Fig. 6BC. 
8. The authors describe the compilation functionality for CPU, GPU and TPU. I believe that the results of the analyzes in Fig.
5JK should be presented separately for all three architectures. It is worth considering presenting such an analysis for the
accuracy parameter (Fig. 5L) - there are slight discrepancies in this parameter in frameworks depending on CPU vs. GPU. 
9. Please provide information about exactly which devices (CPU, GPU, TPU) the tests were carried out for. The "time per
batch" parameter is difficult to assess without detailed hardware data. 
10. The work advertises the BrainScale tool as a framework with extensive functionality. The work lacks a comparison in
terms of functionality of this tool with other existing ones, e.g. Sinabs, Spyketorch, Brain2, Lava-dl, Norse. To begin with, I
suggest comparing these frameworks in terms of functionality, e.g. available models and algorithms. It would also be
valuable for potential users to compare the performance of the selected dataset (e.g. accuracy and time per batch). 
11. As for the scalability postulates proposed by the authors, there are no hard arguments to prove their fulfillment by
comparing BrainScale's performance with existing solutions. And so: 
a) in relation to training scope - the statement "BrainScale significantly reduces model complexity" would be worth
supporting with a comparison of model complexity for models implemented using a conversion algorithm, which usually
proposes models of very low complexity 
b) in relation to memory efficiency - the statement "properties that can reduce online learning computations" would be worth
supporting with a comparison of e.g. the avarage or peak memory usage parameter with another algorithm e.g. STDP, which
for a small batch size may require relatively small memory resources 
c) with regard to implementation - the statement "facilitating wide adoptions of SNNs on various applications" should be
carried out through analyzes for various architectures, i.e. CPU, GPU and TPU

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Wang et al. introduce BrainScale, a framework for automatic differentiation for spiking networks, that internally uses an
algorithm called ES-D-RTRL to compute gradients for spiking networks. ES-D-RTRL is an application of approximate
diagonalized real-time recurrent learning (RTRL) to spiking neural networks. Wang et al. present some first results of this
new framework to demonstrate its functionality. The manuscript contains a number of nice concepts and is presented clearly.
However, BrainScale lacks novelty over existing methods and should discuss prior work more exhaustively. 
The need for software tools to automate the process of constructing large and complex spiking networks and suitable
learning mechanisms has been extensively explored in prior work, e.g. early on by Chris Eliasmith's Nengo framework [7],
Eshraghian et al., [2] or jaxsnn, Müller et al. 2024 [3], to name just a few (see e.g. Pietrzak et al. 2023 [1] for a review).
Clearly, BrainScales solves many problems in a different way, but in the light of this extensive prior work, the introduction
and discussion overstate the novelty of the proposed framework. Relevant related work should be cited alongside a
clarification of what is novel in BrainScale. 
The idea of using RTRL for spiking neurons is not new either. Note that even e-Prop (ref. 6) is a simple variant of spiking
RTRL. Mingqing Xiao et al. had a paper in NeurIPS two years ago that is conceptually very similar to the work presented
here. It is also cited in the abstract of the manuscript (ref. 82). They used a leaky-integrate-and-fire neuron model and derived
an approximate version of RTRL. The approximation used a very similar diagonalization to achieve linear scaling (as done

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

in the current manuscript). They show theoretical and experimental results for recurrent and feedforward networks. LIF
neurons are very similar to the exponential synaptic response kernel that is used in the derivation of ES-D-RTRL. In the light
of this similarity, the manuscript seems a bit unfair towards ref. 82. It is only cited in the abstract in the statement "... or use
oversimplified models [82], failing to capture the intricate brain dynamics." What exactly are the intricate brain dynamics that
BrainScale can capture and prior methods failed to address? A detailed side-by-side comparison would be in need here. 
Also the simulation results reported here are misleading in the sense that they miss a fair comparison with state-of-the art.
94% test accuracy on DVS gesture dataset is not state of the art. Modern machine learning method achieve 100% on this
data set, see: [4]. Why this gap? Should be discussed. Furthermore, Xiao et al. [ref 82] also reported superior results on DVS
gesture dataset (>96%). Please include and discuss their result and compare the difference in detail, given the relevance of
this work (see also comment above). They also presented compelling results for the more complex cifar 10 and 100 data
sets, among others. Would be interesting to include those as well here. Also on Spiking speech commands much better
results have been reported, e.g. [5, 6]. Why this gap? Please discuss. Furthermore, the comparison with BPTT without
surrogate is not very meaningful, since BPTT for spiking neurons in that case simply doesn't work. Also, why stop learning in
Fig.5E,F at 95%? What is significant about this number, and how would convergence behave after reaching that point?

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have made large strides in addressing the previous round of reviews. One of the main issues was to relate their
work to the recent literature on training SNNs on datasets such as SHD. The results of the proposed methods are now
reported to be much more competitive, presumably by using more advanced neuron models. 
However, the authors have chosen to rerun methods of competing works while removing some aspects of their methods -
most notably batch norm. They argue that this is a fairer comparison of learning methods as batch norm is incompatible with
online learning, which is their own aim. 
This is a problematic argument to make. Fair comparisons are notoriously difficult, but in this case, the offline, back-
propagation-through-time methods yield trained SNNs with the stated performance and any normalisation with batch norm
during the training phase can be absorbed into weights during inference. So, in essence, there is no fundamental argument
against the use of batch norm. Removing it to make the comparison with a method that can't employ batch norm more
favourable to that other method doesn't seem to improve fairness. 
Additionally, the authors are still missing at least one other training method which also achieves competitive performance
(without batch norm), such as the results based on EventProp (e.g. 10.1088/2634-4386/ada852). Eventprop is also missing
in table S1, though it is very competitive, and in their otherwise nice simulator comparison (table S2), (ml)GeNN is strangely
absent.

The authors were unable to carry out some of the comparative analyses suggested in the previous review. This is mainly
due to limited access to specialised computing equipment. I do not consider this to be a shortcoming, as devices dedicated
to SNN are niche solutions and access to them is severely limited in terms of time. I would like to thank the authors for their
efforts in preparing the comparative analyses included in the article. They are entirely satisfactory. 
The authors did not follow the suggestion to compare the results obtained with their solutions with the results obtained using
the conversion method (comment no. 7). The authors gave two reasons for not doing so. I do not entirely agree with the first
argument, which suggests that such analyses are very costly. A counterargument could be that conversion algorithms are
implemented in commonly used frameworks, where examples using the N-MNIST dataset, which the authors also use, are
often implemented. Therefore, conducting appropriate comparative tests should not be a very time-consuming task.
However, I agree with the second argument concerning the limitation of the research to online learning algorithms. I accept
the authors' decision and have no comments in this regard. I recommend, however, that future research consider the steps
suggested by the authors in the Limitations and Future Work section in lines 413-419. 
Finally, I have two editorial comments: 
1. Re: comment 2 point 3: 
I have doubts about the values V_reset, V_rest, V_th presented in tables S3, S4, S8, S9, S10, S11, S12. Values such as “1.”
or “0.” are given. This may be a source of confusion. I think it would be better to write “1.0 V” and “0.0 V”. 
2. The meaning of TPU is not explained in the text. For people outside the field of SNN, it may not be as obvious as, for
example, CPU or GPU. I think it would be a good idea to consistently explain all three abbreviations in the text of the article. 
In my opinion, after taking into account the above two minor comments, the work can be published.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I want to thank the authors for the clarifications. The presentation has improved, but my main concerns remain. 
- For the claimed main innovation, the online learning abilities, the approximations use in this work are already well known.
Esp. the approach taken earlier in Marschall et al. 2020 is quite similar and seems very relevant to contrast with the
proposed method, which is now presented as if it were original work. 
- I still disagree with the argumentation of the authors regarding the choice of benchmarks and baseline algorithms. If the
focus of this work is to demonstrate scaling, which the name of the model, the abstract, introduction and discussion suggest,
then scaling to state-of-the-art learning performance is an integral part of that. Being able to train large(r) models does not
prove that the algorithm scales, if the performance is not matched on hard tasks. Hence, if scalability of existing algorithms
was the problem, then it should be solved by the proposed method, which has to be demonstrated to support the many
claims regarding the superiority of the method. 
- To match common scientific standards, the comparison between algorithms should involve individual hyperparameter
tuning (not as the authors suggest, use the same hyperparameters), since choice of parameters may influence algorithmic
performance. I.e. if hyperparameter tuning was done for one model, the same hyperparameter tuning has to be done for all
others. Tuning parameters for one algorithm and then applying the same hyperparameters for comparisons is considered
bad practice. 
- As a side remark: I agree with the other reviewers that the name clash with BrainScaleS is very unfortunate. I also don't
think BrainScale is a particularly good and descriptive name for the proposed model.
