Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have proposed the SMEP pipeline for antimicrobial classification and MIC regression in 2023 and have now
extended it to FSLSMEP, incorporating a few-shot learning framework. The work addresses the key challenge of data
scarcity (only 148 known anti-A. baumannii AMPs) by leveraging a few-shot learning strategy: pre-training on a large corpus
via the ESM model, followed by a two-stage fine-tuning process on a broader but related dataset (1,186 anti-P. aeruginosa
AMPs) and finally on the limited A. baumannii dataset. The pipeline systematically filters a massive virtual library of 64
million peptides through empirical selection, classification, ranking, and regression modules, yielding a top-10 candidate list
for wet-lab synthesis and validation. 
Below are my detailed comments regarding the authors’ response to Reviewer #2, along with additional concerns: 
Response to Reviewer #2 
Q2.1. 
The authors’ reply reiterates the filtering pipeline but compares the entire GRAMPA corpus to external databases rather than
focusing on the A. baumannii subset. The reported numbers remain inconsistent. Please reconcile these figures using
updated and subset-specific sources to ensure clarity and reproducibility. 
Q2.2. 
This issue is addressed, but the manuscript would be strengthened by discussing strategies to mitigate heavy computational
costs. For example, pre-filtering the full sequence space with clustering approaches such as Linclust
(https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-04964-5) could improve efficiency. Without such pre-filtering, the FSLSMEP
paradigm may struggle when scaling to longer AMPs or broader modification spaces—areas where generative models
typically excel. 
Q2.3. 
The core evaluation issues remain unresolved. The classifier is still evaluated on ~29 positives within a highly imbalanced
dataset, using single-split Recall/Precision/F1 metrics without confidence intervals, repeated splits, or power analysis—this
yields essentially noisy estimates. Limited data is understandable, but this is precisely why robust resampling strategies
(e.g., repeated k-fold CV or bootstrapping) are necessary. 
Additionally, the “Top-k MSE” metric is methodologically flawed. As currently described, it selects the ground-truth top-k
peptides and computes the MSE over them. This introduces label leakage and only answers “how well do we fit already-
known good samples?” rather than “how good are the sequences the model recommends for testing?” For drug discovery,
the latter is the critical question. Instead, the top-k predictions from the test set should be evaluated against the ground-truth
top-k. Please justify or revise the “Top-k MSE” approach accordingly, as the current formulation risks inflating performance
estimates. 
A re-evaluation with proper statistics is needed, including repeated k-fold CV on the entire pipeline, especially in the
absence of a dedicated validation set. 
Q2.4. 
The authors have clearly explained why they did not adjust the MIC threshold for positive/negative labeling. This rationale is
acceptable. 
Q2.5. 
The reviewer suggested exploring correlations between experimentally tested peptide properties (e.g., MW, net charge) and
antimicrobial activity, rather than merely listing descriptors in Supplementary Table 2. A targeted analysis of these
correlations would better reveal potential structure-activity relationships. 
Q2.6. 
The authors provided predicted vs. experimental MIC values (Supplementary Tables 10–20), which show underfitting and

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

high bias. While this is understandable given the limited training data, and some peptides did show acceptable MICs, please
address the concerns raised in Q2.3 to avoid over-optimistic estimation of model performance. 
Q2.7. 
The ablation issue remains insufficiently addressed. Although prior work included wet-lab ablation studies, here an in silico
ablation on the test set with the comprehensive metrics outlined in Q2.3 would strengthen the manuscript. Since FSLSMEP
replaces the previous ML backbone with ESM, the noise characteristics and learned features differ; therefore, results cannot
simply be assumed to transfer. 
Q2.8. 
The authors used Needleman–Wunsch global alignment to report that most predicted AMPs share <55% identity with the
positive training set. However, since the training peptides range from 5–80 aa while predictions are only 6–8 aa, global
alignment may artificially penalize shorter sequences. Please provide the length distributions of query and training peptides
and discuss whether global alignment is appropriate. For example, RWWKIW and RWWKIWXXXXXXXX share only
42.86% identity under Needleman–Wunsch but 100% under Smith–Waterman. 
Other concerns from Reviewer #2 are adequately addressed. 
Additional Major Concerns 
Comparison with Generative Models 
There are now numerous generative models for AMP design, including diffusion-based
(https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adp7171) and autoregressive models (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-
36994-z, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-51933-2). In contrast, FSLSMEP relies on exhaustive screening with a
relatively simple pipeline. The authors should discuss the trade-offs between brute-force screening and generative
approaches, highlighting when FSLSMEP is preferable and where it may fall short. 
Model Choice 
Facebook Research has released the ESM-2 suite (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ade2574), which
reportedly outperforms ESM-1. Please clarify why ESM-1 was chosen as the backbone despite the availability of an updated
model. 
Dataset Provenance 
GRAMPA is a classic MIC dataset primarily designed for E. coli. However, DBAASP lists 939 A. baumannii entries, which
reduce to 346 after filtering (e.g., amidation, canonical amino acids), nearly doubling the 148 sequences used here. While
some reduction due to clustering (CD-HIT, mmseq2) is expected, the manuscript lacks a clear explanation of data
processing steps. Please detail how the final A. baumannii dataset was derived. 
Comparative Baselines 
The only non-deep models compared are those from the original SMEP pipeline, which is not a fair benchmark against an
ESM-based backbone. Prior studies (https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-025-02253-4, Table 1) show significant MCC
differences between ML and DL models. Why not train tree-based models (e.g., XGBoost, RF) directly on ESM embeddings
for a more rigorous baseline? 
Minor Issues 
Line 93: Instead of stating ML models “always” fail on limited data, use softer phrasing such as “they are likely to yield
suboptimal predictions without further tuning or regularization.” 
Line 193–195: If empirical filtering discards a large portion of chemical space upfront, it is misleading to claim FSLSMEP
“generalizes to the entire peptide library.” Please revise this claim. 
Line 244: The manuscript states that the ranking module selects the top 500 peptides, then the regression module refines
the top 10—yet it also says the regression module “replaces” the ranking model. These statements contradict each other and
need clarification. 
Figure 1B/C: Consider representing the ESM module with multiple Transformer blocks rather than simple FCLs. While the
classification head may be an MLP, the focus should reflect ESM’s Transformer architecture. 
Training Details: Please include essential details (epochs, early stopping, module sequencing, random seeds) in the
Methods rather than hiding them in the Supplementary file. 
Descriptors Section: The detailed descriptor listing in the Supplementary is not informative as-is. Consider summarizing key
features instead of pasting raw code output. 
Code Availability: Please provide a GitHub repository link rather than a static attachment. This allows readers to raise
issues, reproduce results, and contribute improvements. 
Overall Recommendation 
This manuscript has potential for publication in Nature Communications given the valid wet-lab results and a sound
conceptual framework. However, the current version suffers from critical statistical and methodological weaknesses in

—particularly proper model evaluation (Q2.3), dataset clarification, and a more rigorous comparative analysis—will
significantly improve the manuscript’s robustness and reproducibility.

[ANSWER] – Thanks for commen t. Screening models and generative models each

3. While I appreciate the acute bacterial pneumonia mice model, a systematic infection like

immune system in the animal, thus the cyclophosphamide injection. Could the authors 
provide evidence that neutropenia and other immunocompromised symptoms did happen

[ANSWER] – A classic approach to inducing mouse neutropenia involves  
intraperitoneal administration of cyclophosphamide at 150 mg/kg 3 days prior and 
100 mg/kg 1 day prior. This method is widely employed in recent studies (Cell, 2024, 
187, 5453; Nat. Biomed. Eng. 2023, 7, 797; Nat. Biomed. Eng. 2022, 6, 67; Cell Host & 
Microbe, 2023, 31, 1260). Additionally, our model has demonstrated significant in vivo 
bactericidal efficacy of EME7(7) compared with saline. Thus, we suggest that further 
assessment of immune system damage in mice following cyclophosphamide injection 
may not be necessary.
