Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript explores an antigravity-confined interfacial self-assembly (ACIS) strategy for growing cyclodextrin–alkane
nanofilms and compares it with conventional gravity-induced self-assembly (GIS). The topic is timely and potentially useful
to the soft-matter community, and the abstract plus the opening half of the Introduction are clear and engaging. Unfortunately,
the remainder of the paper lacks clarity, cohesion, and adequate evidence. The work does not warrant publication in Nature
Communications. 
Major issues: 
The narrative quality declines sharply after the mid-Introduction. Organization becomes erratic, ideas are repeated, and
awkward phrasing impedes comprehension. A careful, line-by-line edit is required to bring the latter sections up to the
standard set by the opening pages. 
The manuscript asserts that gravity fundamentally limits interfacial film formation, yet the key paragraph advancing this claim
provides no citations. Please substantiate the argument with representative literature or supply systematic data showing that
a broad range of interfacially assembled films fail under gravity. Without such evidence, the paper’s primary justification
remains speculative. 
Comparisons between GIS and ACIS are scattered throughout multiple sections. Consolidating these discussions—ideally
in a single figure or a dedicated subsection—would clarify ACIS’s advantages and remove redundancy. 
The mechanical and morphological improvements reported for ACIS films appear incremental when benchmarked against
prior interfacial films. The manuscript should state more explicitly where the present approach surpasses earlier work, or
adjust the claims to reflect a modest advance. 
Nearly every data panel is accompanied by multiple schematic drawings. While cartoons can aid understanding, they
currently overwhelm the actual measurements. Please reduce redundant schematics so that experimental evidence is more
prominent. 
Figure 1 functions as an overview montage, yet panel 1c duplicates Figure 3i and other elements reappear later. Streamline
Figure 1 to a concise concept graphic or remove duplicated content to avoid confusion. 
Page 3 contains a self-contradictory sentence claiming that the membrane “restricts CD mobility” while simultaneously
“increasing diffusion.” Clarify whether confinement accelerates diffusion along the interface (reduced dimensionality) or
slows overall transport. 
The formation mechanism can likely be explained by classical nucleation and growth driven by host–guest enrichment at the
interface. The quantum-mechanical calculations seem unnecessary unless they yield insights unattainable by simpler
methods. Consider moving these computations to the Supporting Information or demonstrating explicitly how they alter the
mechanistic picture. 
The experimental setup is described almost verbatim three times on page 7, disrupting the narrative flow. Consolidate these

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports a self-assembly strategy that utilizes the oil-water interface to dimensionally localize the association
between an n-alkane and a cyclodextrin, resulting in a 
self-supporting nanofilm. By 
sandwiching a water-infused, porous
nylon membrane and an oil-infused, porous Teflon membrane, the authors create a capillary-force stabilized, gravity-
inverting oil-water interface where cyclodextrin and dodecane assemble to produce the nanofilm. 
The authors demonstrate
that the approach allows the formation of large-area film with markedly improved order and stability. 
They further illustrate
the utility of the approach for applications across 
materials engineering, food processing, emulsion preparation, and oil
displacement in petroleum recovery. 
The approach presented is interesting, the findings insightful, and the applications promising. 
I recommend publication after
the authors have considered the following minor questions, addressing which I believe will enhance the work's reach to the
broader interdisciplinary readership of Nature Communications. 
1. The primary self-assembly strategy, which enables the interfacial synthesis in the present case mirrors previously reported
approaches. 
These include the assembly at the low interfacial tension ATPS interface (Sproncken et al. DOI:
10.1038/s41586-024-07481-2) and droplet interface membranes (Huang et al. 10.1021/ja802089s, Holden et al.
10.1021/ja072292a). 
Although the authors cite Sponcken reference, the relevance needs to be more clearly articulated. 
2. The mechanism stabilizing the interfacial assembly is inadequately addressed. 
Why does dodecane fare better than other
n-alkanes? 
Why b-CD? 
How the known cage-like and channel-like organization of alkane-conjugated cyclodextrin drive the
interfacial assembly and stability needs to be better described. 
3. 
Here, the descriptions on pages 11 and 12 are needlessly repetitious and confusing. 
They can be consolidated into a
simpler explanation. 
4. How are the films detached from the nylon and Teflon membranes? 
How does the inclusion geometry of cyclodextrin
influence their functions?

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the authors' efforts to address my concerns and the extensive revisions undertaken. However, after careful
consideration of the revised manuscript and rebuttal, I think further improvments are required. 
Regarding the evidence that gravity fundamentally limits interfacial film formation. The authors have misinterpreted my
request. The three references they added (Yang, Lehane, and Sproncken) describe electrochemical methods that fail at
ambient conditions because they require external polarization and electrodes, not because gravity prevents film formation.
These papers do not demonstrate that gravity is the limiting factor—they demonstrate that electrochemical driving forces are
necessary for those particular systems. Similarly, Table R1 shows only two examples from the authors' own CD-alkane
system. This does not constitute evidence that a "broad range of interfacially assembled films fail under gravity" as I
originally requested. 
The authors provide an elaborate justification for why the quantum calculations are essential—arguing that classical
nucleation theory is inappropriate for confined geometries and that interfacial tension cannot be measured in real-time.Yet
classical nucleation theory has been successfully applied to confined systems (crystallization in nanopores, nucleation in
microemulsions, etc.), and the inability to measure interfacial tension in real-time is an experimental limitation that does not
automatically necessitate quantum mechanical calculations. 
Regarding the applications, I appreciate that the authors are pursuing industrial collaboration for enhanced oil recovery.
However, this information should be integrated into the manuscript itself to strengthen the case for significance. If this
methodology is indeed ready for industrial application as stated in Reply 9, the manuscript should discuss why density-
inverted configurations are essential for EOR applications and why alternative approaches (microfluidics, electrochemical
methods, etc.) are inadequate. This would substantially strengthen the justification for the work.

Reply 9: We thank the reviewer’s professional comment on the formation mechanism. We believe 
that the analysis of quantum mechanics is still necessary . Classical nucleation occurs at 
macroscopic liquid-droplet interfaces, whereas our system involves nucleation at microscopically 
confined interfaces. Within such confined spaces, molecular aggregation is subject to geometric 
constraints. Furthermore, while the interfacial tension in classical nucleation can be directly 
measured, it cannot be determined in real-time for our system. Consequently, the conditions within 
confined spaces do not satisfy the prerequisites of classical nucleation theory. 
According to the assumptions of the classical theory (Crystal Growth & Design, 2016, 16(11):
