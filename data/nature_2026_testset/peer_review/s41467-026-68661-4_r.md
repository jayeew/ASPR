Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The presented study addresses a significant clinical issue, identifying SAMSN1 as a molecule that impairs NK cell antitumor
activity. However, the work in its current form has several limitations from both a scientific perspective and in terms of
manuscript writing. For instance, insufficient information on the datasets analyzed, the patients, the healthy controls,
experimental details, and statistical analysis makes it challenging to evaluate the manuscript adequately. Consequently, the
scientific relevance of the paper is limited. 
The main limitations include the following: 
1. The authors state in the title that SAMSN1 negatively regulates NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity in hepatocellular
carcinoma (HCC). However, this seems not to be specific for NK cells, as both CD4 and CD8 T cells appear to be affected
too (as also stated in the result paragraph “The regulation of immune function by SAMSN1 is not NK-specific”). Moreover,
only some of the results are related to HCC, the others refer to other tumor types. 
2. The abstract contains many errors. Some phrases are not correctly written. Moreover, the authors affirm that SAMSN1
expression in NK cells inversely correlates with immune checkpoint blockade responsiveness, but this has not been
demonstrated in the present manuscript. 
3. Is SAMSN1 associated with a specific subset of NK cells (both in mice and humans)? The authors evaluated the
expression of SAMSN1 in the tumor setting, which is its expression on circulating NK cells? 
4. Some results are not properly described. 
5. The authors reported the analysis of scRNA-seq data from published data, however the papers are not cited. Moreover,
there are no information about the patients included in the analysis and about the analysis performed. How were the
populations annotated? Is SAMSN1 differentially expressed between PT and IT? The authors indicate that SAMSN1
expression is higher in lymphocytes compared to other populations: how were the statistics calculated? 
6. Which is the HCC cohort analyzed for the survival analysis? There is no information at all about it. How has SAMSN1low
from SAMSN1high groups been defined? How has SAMSN1 expression been normalized on PTPRC? 
7. The authors reported that the analysis of the TCGA cohort is of a scRNA-seq dataset. However, the TCGA contains bulk
RNA-sequencing data. Moreover, they reported that they identified prognostic biomarkers in the blood, but no results were
shown, moreover TCGA does not contain data on peripheral blood. 
8. The resolution of immunohistochemistry images is not good. In the figure legend, the authors indicate that SAMSN1
staining is shown in lymphocytes, however, there is no indication of how lymphocytes are identified. Moreover, they state
that the negative control shows no significant staining, but no images are reported. How is the immunohistochemistry score
(IOD/area) calculated? 
9. It is not described how the t-SNE analysis has been performed. The authors state that the t-SNE analysis revealed distinct
clustering of immune cell populations in both PT and IT samples, however, the two t-SNE maps are very similar and no
clustering analysis has been performed. 
10. The cytokines expression has been evaluated only upon stimulation, which is their expression at basal levels? 
11. The authors in the results of CyTOF experiments reported only some of the markers included in the panel, what about
the other markers? Especially those that correlated with SAMSN1 expression from the TCGA analysis, as NKG2A, TIM-3
and LAG-3-. What are the differences between SAMSN1+ and SAMSN1- cells? 
12. It is not clear whether the difference in GZMB expression between SAMSN1+ and SAMSN1- cells shown in figure 2H
refer to PT or IT.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary 
The authors have elucidated the role of the SAMSN1 gene in NK cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity related to HCC. By
utilizing the CRISPR-Cas9 system, they implemented a knockout (K/O) of the SAMSN1 gene in human NK cells and a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

mouse model to conduct an in-depth study. This paper presents intriguing findings that could contribute to advancements in
next-generation cell-based cancer therapies. If several essential experiments are additionally performed, I would
recommend this manuscript for publication in Nature Communications. 
Major 
To investigate the function of the SAMSN1 gene, the authors used the CRISPR-Cas9 system to generate SAMSN1
knockout (K/O) human NK cells and mouse models and analyzed their phenotypes. Although CRISPR technology has
advanced rapidly in recent years, the authors did not specify which version of the CRISPR-Cas9 system was used in their
methods. While the consistency in phenotypes observed between human NK cells and mouse models enhances the
reliability of the findings, the potential for off-target effects when targeting the SAMSN1 gene using a standard CRISPR-
Cas9 system cannot be entirely ruled out. The authors did not provide sufficient off-target analysis for SAMSN1, leaving
open the possibility that the observed effects may have been influenced by modifications in off-target genes other than
SAMSN1. 
Minor 
The authors demonstrated the K/O of the SAMSN1 gene using CRISPR-Cas9 through an SAMSN1 expression profile.
However, please provide direct evidence of editing within the target SAMSN1 gene (e.g., indel formation). 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Chen et al. present an intriguing study on the role of SAMSN1 in regulating NK cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity,
particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The manuscript highlights SAMSN1 as a potential immune checkpoint that
suppresses NK cell activation and cytotoxicity, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion. The study identifies elevated
SAMSN1 expression as a marker of poor prognosis in HCC patients and demonstrates, through experimental models, that
SAMSN1 deletion enhances NK cell functionality, limits tumor growth, and prevents immune cell exhaustion. These findings
position SAMSN1 as a promising therapeutic target for improving NK cell-based cancer immunotherapy and enhancing the
efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). While the study presents novel insights, there are several areas requiring
clarification and improvement. Below, I outline both major and minor concerns. 
Major concerns 
1. Introduction: The introduction lacks depth and precision. Several references appear inappropriate or irrelevant, suggesting
a cursory literature review. For example, Reference 8 cites a low-profile nursing journal unrelated to the topic. Reference 11
is from an MDPI journal, which raises questions about quality; it should be replaced with a higher-impact reference.
References 18–20 are reviews rather than original research, which should be prioritized when citing specific findings.
Reference 23 (uterine NK cells) and Reference 24 (liver NK cells during homeostasis) are cited in contexts where they
appear irrelevant or inaccurate. The paragraph spanning lines 87–99 is particularly vague. For instance: "May function as a
tumor suppressor in certain contexts"—this requires specifying the relevant mechanisms described in the cited papers.
"Modulation of signaling pathways related to cell proliferation and survival in certain contexts"—similarly, this statement
should be clarified with detailed findings. "Suggested a role for SAMSN1 in regulating inflammatory responses"—again,
what was specifically discovered in the referenced studies? Additional references are missing for claims such as "reported
in various malignancies, including multiple myeloma and lung cancer." 
2. Figure 1: The rationale behind Figure 1B is unclear. The absence of t-SNE plots showing SAMSN1 expression across
population clusters weakens the analysis. Terminology like "Peritumor (PT)" and "Intratumor (IT)" is confusing. According to
the legend (lines 512–513), both PT and IT are tumor zones, making it unclear what is meant by "adjacent normal tissue."
Correlating SAMSN1 expression with NK cell scoring signatures (e.g., PMID: 31088844) would significantly enrich this
figure. 
3. Lines 144-146 mentioned Figure 1A-C, which analyzes scRNAseq data from published studies; however, the
corresponding references are missing and should be included. Additionally, Figure 1F illustrates the correlation between
SAMSN1 and other immune checkpoint receptors. It is unclear whether the expression of SAMSN1 and the immune
checkpoint receptors analyzed here is for the whole cell population in the dataset or specific on NK cells. If the analysis does
not specifically target NK cells, the findings presented may not directly support the role of SAMSN1 in NK cells concerning
tumor immunity, which is your focus of this paper.

mRNA, protein reduction (81.8% to 56.3%) is marginal and lacks statistical comparison in Figure 3C. Key data are missing,

Detailed information is provided as follows:
1) SAMSN1 Expression Predominantly Elevated in NK Cells in HCC cohort : 
Analysis of scRNA-seq data from 18 HCC patients (published paper, PMID 33357445) revealed significantly higher 
SAMSN1 expression in NK cells compared to T cells, myeloid cells, parenchymal cells, and tumor cells. Specifically, 
SAMSN1 expression in NK cells was 1.35-fold higher in intratumoral (IT) compared to para tumor (PT) regions, 
whereas T cells exhibited a modest 1.21-fold increase (Fig. 1a-d). This indicates a preferential upregulation of 
SAMSN1 in NK cells within the HCC tumor microenvironment. 
2) CyTOF Analysis Confirms NK-Specific SAMSN1 Upregulation: 
CyTOF data from 22 HCC patients demonstrated a significant increase in SAMSN1 + NK cells in IT regions 
(16.6% to 27.8%, P = 0.024) compared to PT regions. In contrast, SAMSN1 + CD8+ T cells showed no significant 
difference (ns). Furthermore, GZMB+ NK cells were reduced in SAMSN1 + NK cells (42% to 37%, P = 0.034) in 
IT samples, underscoring the negative regulatory role of SAMSN1 in NK cell effector function. This NK cell-
specific upregulation is functionally significant, as demonstrated by a strong negative correlation between 
SAMSN1 and GZMB expression in NK cells (r = -0.543, P = 0.0106, Fig. 2j), whereas weaker or no correlations 
were observed in T cell subsets. 
3) In vivo Validation in HCC Models (Samsn1-/- and Samsn1f/f-Ncr1Cre+ mice):

4c, e). Similarly, in Samsn1f/f-Ncr1Cre+ mice, liver weight relative to body weight decreased from 8.8% to 5.2% ( P
= 0.0017), and median survival increased from 27 to 36 days ( P = 0.0101) (Fig. 5g, k). Notably, Samsn1f/f-Ncr1Cre+

To investigate the function of the SAMSN1 gene, the authors used the CRISPR-Cas9 system to generate 
SAMSN1 knockout (K/O) human NK cells and mouse models and analyzed their phenotypes. Although CRISPR 
technology has advanced rapidly in recent years, the authors did not specify which version of the CRISPR-Cas9 
system was used in their methods. While the consistency in phenotypes observed between human NK cells and 
mouse models enhances the reliability of the findings, the potential for off-target effects when targeting the 
SAMSN1 gene using a standard CRISPR-Cas9 system cannot be entirely ruled out. The authors did not provide

Reply: We thank the reviewer for their insightful comments regarding the specificity of our CRISPR-Cas9 methodology 
and the potential for off-target effects in SAMSN1 knockout studies. We fully acknowledge the importance of rigorous 
validation in CRISPR applications, and we have strengthened our manuscript accordingly by incorporating additional 
details, experimental validations, and references to support the on-target specificity of our findings. Below, we address 
each point systematically, with revisions reflected in the updated Methods section (page 23, lines 522) and new 
Extended Data Fig. 6a-c. 
1) CRISPR-Cas9 System Specification: For human NK cell knockouts (NK92 line), we employed the standard 
Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9) ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex (NLS-SpCas9-EGFP, novoprotein Cat# 
1081061), delivered via electroporation. sgRNAs targeted SAMSN1 exon 2 and were designed using the Zhang 
Lab CRISPR Design Tool (MIT) for optimal specificity. We selected RNP delivery based on established evidence

efficient indel formation (~65% editing rate) specifically at the target site (Extended Data Fig. 6a). No unintended

locus, with no detectable disruptions in closely homologous genes, further corroborating specificity. 
These comprehensive measures, including spanning design, empirical testing, and functional rescue, collectively 
demonstrate that the observed phenotypes are attributable to SAMSN1 knockout alone, without confounding off-target 
effects. We believe these enhancements address the reviewer's concerns and bolster the robustness of our conclusions. 
The revised manuscript includes all new data and references for full reproducibility.
