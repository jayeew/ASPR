Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper provides a detailed study of the polymer dynamics and rheology of IDPs self-coacervates as a function of the
charge blockiness of their primary sequence. DPD simulations methodology is highly reliable and suitable for the problem
under consideration, and the results are sound. Decreasing local relaxation time of the blob with increasing charge
patchiness is qualitatively explained within the scaling picture of the self-coacervate and Zimm scaling for the blob
relaxation time. The most interesting and novel findings pertain to the effect of the local charge inhomogeneity, which results
in dynamic heterogeneity and different local relaxation times for the different fragments of the sequence. These results are
crucial for the interpretation of recent experiments on biocondensates and the microscopic understanding thereof. 
This paper definitely deserves publishing in CommsChem, but prior to that, the authors have to address the aspects listed
below: 
1. The authors would benefit from comparison to the recent theoretical work considering the same problem – the effect of the
primary IDP/polyampholyte sequence on the dynamics within and viscosity of the self-coacervate phase. [Macromolecules
2023, vol. 56, p. 5201; https://pubs.acs.org/doi/full/10.1021/acs.macromol.3c00512, see Section V] Some of the authors'
results are in accordance with the scaling theory developed in that work: 
a) Beginning of page 7. Sequence effects remain important even in the presence of salt. Sequence dependence of the self-
coacervate density and the resulting Rouse viscosity is indeed expected even in salt-added regimes. 
b) Bottom page 7. \Eta ~ \rho^{1.71}. Scaling theory predicts an exponent of 2 in theta solvent (that would change to 1/(3 \nu -
1) = 1.3 in athermal solvent, see Rubinstein & Colby, Section 8.5), which is very close to the simulation result. It is also more
natural to compare the viscosity of the coacervate to that of a neutral semidilute solution, not the PE one. Self-coacervates
are known to be quasi-neutral within the scaling consideration (see any review on coacervates and self-coacervates). This
also applies to the conclusion section. 
c) Page 8: Increase in the Rouse viscosity of the self-coacervate with increasing blockiness is in complete agreement with
the scaling results of the theoretical work, see eqs. 63-64. 
d) This work might also be mentioned in the introduction of page 2 because the developed scaling approach, as well as the
DPD simulations by the authors, accounts for “intricate relationship between charge sequence, hydrodynamics, and
electrostatics”. The authors may also highlight the advantages of their excellent DPD simulations over the scaling theory,
because the latter is incapable of determining numerical prefactors, i.e., crossover positions and regime width. DPD
definitely goes well beyond simple scaling analysis! This will help to emphasize the novelty of the excellent simulation study
performed by the authors. 
2. I was puzzled for a moment with the nomenclature. Different primary sequences in the literature are normally termed
“charge clustering” or “charge blockiness”, while the authors use “more charge-balanced blocks” (page 2). This may be
slightly misleading because I first thought about change-imbalanced IDPs/PAs. Adding synonyms of low
blockiness/patchiness in parentheses would be helpful to avoid confusion. 
I recommend publication of this manuscript pending satisfactory revisions addressing my remarks.

2. Similarly, the authors should mention a bit more about the ion model used in the work. Are ions modeled as WCA beads with charge? Reply: We thank the reviewer for clarifying our simulation model. In the present work, the model is termed GCMe-DPD, which is an extension of our previously developed GCMe (Gaussian core model with smear electrostatics) method. In GCMe-DPD (page 11, SI section A), solvent particles (and other monomers) are not modeled as Lennard-Jones (LJ) fluids but rather as   particles interacting with Gaussian-core repulsions. We choose our GCMe-DPD for the following main reasons:   1) Our GCMe-DPD method effectively couples long-range hydrodynamics interactions (HI) and electrostatic interactions, which are the two major factors for IDP condensate dynamics focused on this work.  To our knowledge, while other coarse-grained methods based on LJ potentials have their merits, including the general LJ-based methods ([B. Yu et al., Macromolecules, (2023)]; [D. Sharon et al., Macromolecules, (2022)]; [H. Liang et al., Macromolecules, (2022)]), and Martini force field ([M. Tsanai et al., Chem. Sci., (2021)]; [L. Wang et al., Nat. Commun., (2025)]), these methods (with or without explicit solvents)  fails to conserve local momentum and thus do not rigorously capture hydrodynamic interactions. Given the importance of HI on IDP condensate dynamics, our GCMe-DPD offers clear advantages.   2) The choice of using Gaussian soft-core model in the coarse-grained excluded volume and electrostatic potentials is due to their theoretically tractable formula, i.e., they can be analytically derived respectively from Gaussian-smeared mass and charges, as documented in our previous publication [B. Ye, S. Chen & Z.-G Wang, JCTC, (2024)]. Compared to LJ-based method, the soft-core potentials are appropriate to model highly coarse-grained systems that allow particles to slightly overlap.    In this work, we set the excluded-volume interaction to be the same among all the species, representing good solvent conditions for the polymers. This is the common practice in coarse-grained simulations of generic polyion solution systems.   In revision, we add the following sentence in the main text on page 3 to point the readers to the SI: “Other simulation details, such as water, ion, and polymer models, DPD thermostat, and electrostatic interactions, are given in the “Simulation details” section and in the SI appendix.”   3. The authors should show the density of protein, solvent and ions from the coexistence simulations they have run. From what can be gathered, the ionic concentration is assumed to be 0.1 M within the condensed phase. However, recent work [https://pubs.acs.org/doi/full/10.1021/jacs.4c08946, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451929423001535, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jpcb.0c10489 ] has shown that ions can partition very differently between the dilute and dense phase. Is it then appropriate to assume uniform

distribution of ions within the condensate? Further, how would different partitioning behavior affect the condensate dynamics?  Reply: This is an interesting question regarding ions partitioning in condensate and dilute phase. In our work, we set the excluded-volume interactions to be the same between ions and other species, which does not account for specific ion effects. Under this condition, the simulations (see example from Fig. R1) show that ion concentrations are higher in the dilute phase than the condensate phase, i.e., the tie-line is negative in the polymer-salt diagram, consistent with theoretical predictions ([P. Zhang et al., Macromolecules, (2018)]) and many experimental observations ([M. Radhakrishna et al., Macromolecules, (2017)]; [S. Friedowitz et al., Science Advances, (2021)]; [L. Li et al., Macromolecules, (2018)]). The negative tie-line is a consequence of the excessed (compared to the case of equal partition) ion electrochemical potential in the condensate phase arising from the chain-connectivity and high polymer concentrations.   However, specific ion effects may lead to positive tie-line ([A. E. Neitzel et al., Macromolecules, (2021)]; [C. E. Chee et al., Macromolecules, (2025)]) and non-trivial ion distributions within the condensate([Posey et al., JACS, (2024)]; [Y. Dai et al., Chem, (2023)]; [Zheng et al., JPCB, (2020)]), which may impact the local polymer dynamics. Nevertheless, capturing these effects requires all-atom simulations of specific systems, which is well-beyond the scope of current work on generic coarse-grained systems.   In homogenous systems, the effect of salt ions on condensate dynamics is mainly manifested by its influences on polymer concentrations of the dense phase, since it determines the viscosity. The ion mobility should have little effects on polymer dynamics, as the diffusion time scales for the two species are vastly different.
