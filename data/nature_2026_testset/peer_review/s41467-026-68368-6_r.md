Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Zhang and colleagues describes a novel ML approach to phenotype patients admitted to the ICU with
ACLF. Using an unsupervised clustering model on a cohort of 1,256 patients defined by NACSELD criteria, the authors
identify two distinct clusters that correlate with prognosis and appear to be influenced by acid–base disturbances. The
findings are supported by both internal and external validation cohorts. The manuscript is methodologically sound,
comprehensive, and well-written. The authors should be acknowledged for their use of advanced analytical methods in
addressing this complex clinical condition. However, several points still warrant clarification to strengthen the manuscript,
despite some limitations already being acknowledged in the Discussion (e.g., the use of GCS instead of West Haven criteria
for encephalopathy, defining respiratory failure based on the need for ventilation, and the absence of a dynamic assessment
for ACLF). 
Please find detailed comments below. 
Major comments: 
1) The clustering model is based on NACSELD-defined ACLF, but it is unclear whether it would be applicable to patients
classified using the EASL-CLIF criteria, which are more widely adopted internationally including also liver and coagulation
failures (bilirubin and INR). NACSELD criteria, originally developed in the context of infection-driven ACLF, tend to capture
patients with more advanced extrahepatic organ failure, whereas EASL-CLIF identifies earlier organ dysfunction, which still
carries prognostic value. Incorporating a broader definition of ACLF could improve the model’s generalizability. Notably, the
RF analysis (Suppl. Fig. 2) highlights liver and coagulation parameters among the most influential variables, further
supporting their relevance. This limitation should be more clearly acknowledged in the Discussion as it may not yet
represent a truly ‘holistic approach’. 
2) The manuscript does not account for liver transplantation, a key competing event in the natural course of ACLF. Excluding
transplant data may bias survival analysis, especially if transplant eligibility differed between clusters (e.g., patients with
respiratory failure may be less likely to undergo transplantation). The authors should clarify whether transplant information
was available and, if not, address this as a limitation. 
3) In real-world settings, ACLF is often diagnosed late, with patients transferred to specialized wards or ICU after
deterioration. This can result in missing or delayed lab values (especially for acid-base and hemodynamic status) limiting
the model’s prospective utility. The authors should acknowledge that while the model performs well in a retrospective ICU
cohort, it may need validation for earlier or less data-complete clinical settings. 
4) The Supplementary Material states that clinical and laboratory variables were collected within the first 24 hours of ICU
admission. This information should be included in the Methods section. Additionally, several variables used in the model are
likely influenced by timing and prior interventions. For instance, the relatively high MAP across clusters (despite bleeding
being a precipitating factor in 40% of patients) suggests many patients were already receiving vasopressors, fluids, or
transfusions, which may confound interpretation of hemodynamic and metabolic status. 
5) The authors state that the majority of data points were distinctly grouped (Figure
 
3A), yet Supplementary Table
 
7 shows
that 377 patients (30%) were classified as overlapping between clusters. This is a substantial proportion and suggests
considerable uncertainty in cluster assignment. This finding should be explicitly acknowledged and discussed, as it impacts
on the interpretability and applicability of the clusters. 
6) The internal validation method requires clarification. The manuscript reports that 1,334 patients with decompensated
cirrhosis were considered for validation, including 782 with ACLF, overlapping substantially with the 1,256 ACLF patients
used to derive the clusters. If these are not separate populations, the validation cannot be considered independent. The
authors should clearly state whether the validation cohort is distinct from the derivation cohort and, if not, discuss the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

implications of this overlap. 
7) The eICU cohort is presented as an external validation dataset, but the manuscript does not clearly state whether the
clustering model was applied to this cohort, and survival outcomes are not reported. To strengthen the external validity of the
approach, the authors should report whether similar prognostic differences between clusters were observed in the external
cohort as well. This is particularly important given that the eICU cohort differs from the derivation cohort in the rates of brain
and respiratory failure. 
8) The number of organ failures was significantly higher in cluster 1 which in turn was associated with higher mortality. Was
there consideration for not including number of organ failures in the model, to avoid this potential confounding? The
advantage of unsupervised clustering approach is that it allows to understand the different phenotypes, recognizing that not
all organ failures impact outcomes equally. It would be helpful to include a KM/survival analysis comparing the 2 clusters
based on number of organ failures (ie. Splitting the 2 clusters in figure 3c). 
Minor comments: 
1) The mortality observed in the validation cohort of patients with decompensated cirrhosis was higher than in the original
ACLF cohort (31.7% vs. 26.1% in Cluster 2, and 73.4% vs. 70.4% in Cluster 1). It would be helpful if the authors could
comment on possible reasons for these differences. 
2) The use of MIMIC-IV data up to 2019 may limit the generalizability of the findings, as ICU care and early recognition of
ACLF may have improved in recent years, including greater access to liver transplantation. This should be acknowledged in
the discussion. 
3) Line 109: please specify “in real-world ICU settings”. 
4) Figure 2B: The font size of the variables is small. Please consider increasing it for better readability if possible. 
5) Figure 2C is unclear. The axis labels and legend need clarification. 
6) Line 150: Please clarify what is meant by “traditional classification method in mortality risk prediction”. 
7) Please report the number of events and at-risk subjects in figure 3B and 3C.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Prognostication in ICU is difficult, especially in patients with cirrhosis. The authors have performed ML and other algorithmic
approaches in stratifying mortality risk in ACLF patients based on the NACSELD criteria. They validated this in an external
cohort and found clusters that associated with different mortality risks. 
The data used were also studied in a small group of
decompensated patients. 
General comments: 
a. Restricting the cohort to only NACSELD-ACLF patients selects for a very sick group already in the ICU. The authors
should expand this to all patients with cirrhosis in the hospital as well to better predict outcomes. 
b. A prediction model with a better AUC is needed to aid clinicians; 
RFA was performed with a poor AUC..can authors
clarify? 
c. Were the data used only at the time of admission or throughout the inpatient course? 
d. We need a section discussing how this model can be applied to clinical practice. If a patient is predicted to be in the
lower-risk cluster, there is still a moderately high mortality rate of nearly 30%. Can we further stratify risk? Additionally, some
of the important variables had high missingness rates (i.e. lactate with 14.65% missingness). Would clinicians be expected
to impute these variables themselves, or is there a way to still use the model with incomplete data? Finally, adding on to this
point, would clinicians be expected to enter all 50 variables manually in order to generate results? I worry that this might be
too time-consuming, and a streamlined model, perhaps including the top-10 or top-15 variables, could improve clinical utility.
Ideally one needs a model like this to be available publicly with as little manual input needed for busy clinicians. 
e. The authors should reference recent studies that also assess mortality in cirrhosis and discuss their impact vis-à-vis the
current experience. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0016508525057701
https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2772522 
Specific Comments: 
Lines 384-386: “For patients with multiple episodes of ACLF, only the most recent episode was included to avoid potential
biases related to repeated measures.” It is possible that prior hospitalization / prior ACLF history may affect mortality,
compared to a patient who is experiencing their first ACLF episode. Is it possible to quantify this and add it to the model as
another predictor? 
Lines 410-411: Is there justification for the use of k=5 in KNN imputation, and exclusion of pts. with >20 missing variables? 
Lines 415-416: Is there justification for transforming all continuous variables into categorial variables? Typically, keeping
continuous variables in their original form will preserve information. 
Lines 509-520: If I understand this correctly, the authors expanded the criteria for inclusion into the cohort to just be
decompensated cirrhosis, rather than ACLF, and then re-applied their model. For this validation, they included 1,334 pts. (vs.
1,256 w/ACLF used for model training). Does this mean that a large majority of the patients w/decompensated cirrhosis also
had ACLF, seeing as only 78 additional patients were included? If this is the case, then the authors effectively validated their
model on the same cohort that they originally derived the clusters from.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This study examined unsupervised clustering of 1,256 NACSELD-defined ACLF patients using MIMIC-IV, a publicly
available dataset. Acid-base balance variables were identified as major predictors of clustering. External validation was
done using the eICU database, another publicly available dataset. Here are my comments. 
Major 
1. The discrepancy in exclusion criteria for missing values between the training and external validation sets needs
clarification. While patients with 20 or fewer missing values were allowed in the MIMIC-IV database, the criterion was
reduced to 5 or fewer missing values for patients in the eICU database. Was that a typo? 
2. Consequently, it was noted that around 75% of patients in the external validation set were excluded due to missing data,
which may lead to selection bias. Missing data imputation using K-neighbor algorithm was only performed in the remaining
25% of the patients. 
3. How was k selected for the kNN algorithm? 
4. It would be good to compare the clinical characteristics of patients who were excluded due to having missing data in more
than 20 variables to those who remained in the analysis. 
5. [Figure 3D] What does that mean by an importance of 1? As shown in Figure 3C, it appeared that the number of organ
failures (ACLF-1, -2, -3) was an important predictor of mortality. Yet, it was not an important factor for clustering. 
6. A total of 50 variables are needed for identifying the clustering, and the HR was 3.6 comparing cluster 1 to cluster 2. If the
study aims to identify patients at risk of short-term mortality as mentioned in the Introduction, would that be possible to
perform a conventional survival analysis, e.g., Cox regression analysis, to identify important risk factors associated with
mortality in ACLF? While acid-base balance variables are useful in separating the patients into 2 clusters, they may not
necessarily be the most important in predicting short-term mortality. Consequently, maybe considering the number of organ
failures plus a few important factors (maybe acid-base balance variables) would already be able to separate the patients into
two groups with HR of 3.6. A clustering that requires 50 variables may not be applicable to many patients, as shown in the
substantial exclusion in the eICU database. 
Minor 
1. [Figure 1] k neighbors algorithm should be k-nearest neighbors algorithm. 
2. It would be good to justify the choices of the number of categories chosen for the study variables. It was noticed that some
variables were categorized into four groups, while some were in three groups or two groups. 
3. Please provide the number at risk for Kaplan-Meier analysis.

The revised manuscript by Zhang and colleagues shows some improvements, and the clarifications provided by the authors
are appreciated. However, several important issues (data selection, cohort definition, and the validation analyses) remain
unresolved, and new areas have emerged that may impact the clinical applicability of the proposed ML model.

1) As the authors pointed out, exclusion of prior LT is essential, however, LT post-ACLF diagnosis is important to consider.
In a time-to-event analysis, LT constitutes an important competing risk that precludes the occurrence of the primary endpoint
(death). 
2) The description of when variables were collected remains confusing, both in the manuscript and in the authors’
responses. The authors stated that, in the ACLF cohort, variables were collected within 24 hours prior to ACLF diagnosis,
meaning the data may not truly represent patients at the time of ACLF onset. It is also unclear how all 50 variables would be
available for patients not yet critically ill. Moreover, in the validation cohort, data were collected at the time of ACLF
diagnosis after ICU admission, which introduces an inconsistency with the derivation cohort. 
3) In the “decompensated cirrhosis cohort,” more than half of the patients had ACLF, which creates confusion regarding the
terminology of this cohort. It also remains unclear whether individual patients could have been included in both analyses:
first as decompensated cirrhosis patients and later (when organ failures developed) as ACLF patients, introducing possible
duplication. 
4) The absence of mortality data in the eICU validation cohort (as acknowledged in the new version) substantially limits the
capacity to validate the prognostic impact of the proposed clusters. Moreover, the exclusion of approximately 75% of the
eICU patients due to missing data further undermines the robustness of the findings. 
5) The Methods should clearly specify how cirrhosis was defined and identified in both the MIMIC and eICU databases.

Response: The reasons for converting continuous variables into categorical variables in th e 
study were as follows: 
1. Many clinical scoring systems (e.g., SOFA, MELD, CLIF-C ACLF) and guidelines rely on

influence of extreme values. 
3. The non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) algorithm we employed performs optimally 
when the input data are non-negative and typically discrete (e.g., count data or categorical 
variables). Although NMF can mathematically accommo date continuous variables, its 
convergence stability and robustness can be compromised when the data exhibit large-
scale variations, skewed distributions, or noise , common characteristics of raw clinical 
continuous measurements.  To enhance model robustness  and interpretability, we 
discretized the continuous variables during preprocessing (e.g., using clinically established 
thresholds or quantile -based binning) and converted them into ordinal categorical 
features. This approach aligns better with NMF ’s inherent assumption of additive, non -
negative combinations and helps mitigate the influence of outliers while improving feature 
comparability. More importantly, such discretization significantly improves the 
convergence stability of NMF, leading to more consistent and reliable clustering results.
