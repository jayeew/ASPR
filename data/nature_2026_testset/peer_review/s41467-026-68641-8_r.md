Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I have now read the revised manuscript, and after reviewing this version I am left with the same questions that were raised
before the revision. I find it an interesting study, and I appreciate that the authors have addressed several methodological
issues, which has improved the overall quality of the work. However, my primary concern remains: the observed effects
could be a result of the chosen model, and ultimately, we still do not know if the presented findings translate to PD. 
The authors chose to work with a PFF model, which I believe is one of the more relevant models of PD, but it should be
interpreted with caution given the conformational variability of recombinant assemblies that are used in different laboratories.
Mouse fibrils can be more immunogenic than human fibrils and may cause immune or complement activation to varying
degrees. Consequently, several of these effects could depend on the model employed—particularly the use of mouse
synthetic fibrils that are mechanically injected into the duodenal wall. At least some validation in human samples, whether
via immunohistochemistry or other techniques, would be prudent to confirm that the observed effects are genuine and to
further enhance the relevance of the work. 
Additionally, the study strongly emphasizes the connection between the gut and the brain. One of the key take-home points
is that early pathology in the gut can influence the progression of PD. However, the new Supplementary Figure 1 indicates
that there is no transmission from the gut. This raises several questions: How are the gut and the brain connected? How do
macrophages or complement activation contribute to the progression and central pathology in PD? What are the effects on
the brain? I understand that these questions now extend beyond the scope of the current study - as noted in the rebuttal -
and could be the focus of a follow-up investigation. Nevertheless, if there is no clear link between the gut and the brain, or no
demonstrable contribution from gut macrophages to central pathology, this limitation should be stated so that readers are
aware of it. The addition of study limitations in the discussion is a positive step in this direction.

In brief, the routes of connection between the gut and brain are well established. CN X 
connects the gut to the nodose ganglia/NTS (afferent) and the DMV (efferent). 
Additionally, sympathetic innervation of the gut provides an alternate efferent connection 
and visceral sensory neurons provide an alternate afferent connection to the brain. 
These neuronal circuits have been studied in the context of feeding behavior, motility, 
and (recently) gut inflammation There is also the circulatory system, which is leveraged 
by gastrointestinal hormones to signal satiety (e.g., GLP1). We agree with the reviewer

thorough review of this physiology is best covered in future work. In relation to PD, 
some groups claim that the spread of pathology is along vagal efferents back to the 
DMV. In contrast, one recent study found that spread of pathology along vagal afferents 
from enteroendocrine cells is possible, while yet another reported that pathology can 
spread from gut-to-brain via CD11c+ immune cells that assumingly travel via the 
circulatory system. All this to say that each anatomical route can contribute to the 
spread of pathology along the gut-brain axis, but we find no evidence of such spread 
here and hence did not investigate mechanism. As far as we are aware, the effects of 
macrophages and complement in this model are confined to the ENS, and more
