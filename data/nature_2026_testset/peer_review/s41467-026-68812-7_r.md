Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors claim that they have developed an improved sequencing technology for small noncoding RNA sequencing
called qMap-seq. The key improvements involve (1) the 11 random bases over the existing 8 random bases and (2) the
inclusion of RNA spike-ins with 2’-O-methyl-modified RNAs. The authors then extensively applied qMap-seq to a panel of
24 cell lines and 20 tissues from mice, and 18 tissues from crab-eating monkeys. With this sequencing data, the authors
characterized their findings, developed a machine-learning algorithm for bias-correction, and built a web interface called
SmRNAQuant for easy access to their computational results. The sequencing data that the authors provide especially on the
crab-eating monkeys is novel and interesting. However, the improvement in sequencing technology is marginal, the
innovation of their machine-learning algorithm is unclear, and the physiological significance of their findings in terms of co-
sequencing miRNAs and piRNAs, for example, is missing. 
qMap-seq is not the first sequencing method to use random nucleotides and unique molecular identifies. As the authors
mention in line 85, the qMap-seq method is a modified version of 4N-Xu method which also uses random nucleotides to
reduce ligation bias and is utilized as unique molecular identifiers to correct for PCR biases. Other small RNA sequencing
methods also use random nucleotides. The impact of using 11 random bases in qMap-seq over short random bases is only
presented theoretically in Figure S1A, but the empirical impact that this decision results in is unclear. 
The second innovation that the author claims is the use of RNA spike-ins for absolute quantification. In line 58-60, the
authors state that “none of these sequencing approaches permit absolution sncRNA quantification”. However, the use of
RNA spike-ins for absolute quantification and monitoring ligation bias has also been used in previous methods as well.
Other experimental optimizations including the concentration of PEG8000 are also done in previous studies, altogether
indicating that the innovations proposed by qMap-seq are marginal. 
Lastly the authors’ main comparison is with TruSeq and not the more advanced methods such as 4N-Xu and AQ-Seq that
the authors mention in the introduction. To argue for improvement, the authors must compare with more advanced methods
other than TruSeq. 
The fact that it is a single-tube sequencing protocol is interesting. It’d be interesting if the author tested on low RNA input and
its robustness in quantification. 
It is worth mentioning that there is another method called QMAP-Seq that was published in Nature Communications in 2020.
Here’s the link to the manuscript for reference: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-19553-8 
The comprehensive profiling of multiple cell lines, mouse tissues, and macaque tissues is very impressive. The discovery of
miRNA conservation between mouse and macaque tissues is novel but not surprising as it is already known that
microRNAs are conserved between mouse and human. Nevertheless, I do expect that this resource will be valuable for the
small RNA scientific community. 
The authors’ comment on 2’-O-methyl-modified sncRNAs was interesting. The authors mention that piRNAs and plant
miRNAs harbor 3’ 2’-O-methyl modifications in line 56. Applying their method to plant tissues and germinal cells would help
highlight the importance of using 2’-O-methyl-modified RNA spike-ins.

In line 24, the authors claim that they revealed substantial biases in miRNA abundance, strand selection and tissue specific
expression. However, this bias in TruSeq in particular has been shown previously, especially in AQ-seq which is one of the
previous methods that the authors cite. In fact, in Figure 4C, they compare with AQ-seq data and find that the performance of
both methods are comparable. 
Regarding their machine learning algorithm, the performance is interesting but the exact details of their approach and how it
corrects bias in small RNA sequencing data is unclear. For example, in the result and method section, the authors do not
describe how they conducted feature engineering before applying XGBoost. 
In Figure 2c, the authors show a high abundance of other RNAs in cell lines compared to tissue samples. Also, the amount
of rRNA is especially high in cell lines and mouse liver and pancreas tissues. The physiological significance of this is
unclear. Could it be that they are artifacts or noise from the sequencing method? 
In Figure 3a, the authors compare the number of miRNA species across cellines and tissues. However, the number of
miRNA species will also depend on sequencing depth and the amount of artifacts and technical noise (e.g., rRNAs) from
data generation. 
As for the web application, it’d be more applicable to the scientific community if it included the sequence alignments and
sequence modifications. 
Lastly, to claim absolute quantification, the authors must provide experimental confirmation that is not based on sequencing. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents qMap-seq, an optimized protocol designed to minimize bias in small RNA-seq. By incorporating
synthetic RNA spike-ins of known quantities, the method enables estimation of the absolute abundance of miRNAs in, e.g.
100 ng of total RNA. Leveraging this approach, the authors generate a comprehensive resource characterizing the miRNA
transcriptome across a wide range of cell lines, tissues, and species, offering potentially a more reliable catalog of functional
miRNAs. This manuscript also introduces a machine learning-guided model for bias correction, which holds potential for
retrospective correction of miRNA-seq datasets. Lastly, the authors provide an interactive platform (SmRNAQuant) to
facilitate access to the resource, which will be of value to the community. 
Although various methods have been developed to reduce bias in miRNA-seq, few have been applied systematically across
diverse biological contexts to generate a unified reference dataset. Furthermore, absolute quantification of miRNAs has not
been extensively explored. This paper addresses both limitations. However, I have several points for the authors to address: 
Major comments: 
1. The authors use 11 random bases for UMIs to collapse PCR duplicates. The collision rate was estimated to be 1.4%, but
this may be inaccurate if the authors assumed uniform ligation of all possible 11-nt UMI sequences. Given the known strong
ligation bias in miRNA-seq, some UMI sequences are likely overrepresented. I strongly recommend including a scatter plot
comparing miRNA read counts before and after UMI deduplication. As PCR is known not to significantly distort the miRNA
profile, substantial changes after UMI correction would suggest that 11-nt UMIs may be insufficient for accurate
deduplication. 
2. In Fig. 1F, the authors validate qMap-seq using miR-221-3p. In connection with point 1, I suggest including an additional
comparison between RT-qPCR and qMap-seq for the most abundant miRNA in average across cell lines. This would help
assess whether qMap-seq performs well for highly expressed miRNAs. 
3. qMap-seq still exhibits bias and therefore does not guarantee fully accurate absolute quantification (Fig. 1C). I strongly
suggest softening some of the more definitive claims throughout the manuscript. For example, consider modifying 
the title
from “absolute quantification” to “estimated absolute quantification,” and revising line 158 from “true” to “more reliable.” 
4. Most importantly, estimating absolute miRNA quantities is a central contribution of the paper. However, it remains unclear
what specific biological insights are uniquely enabled by these absolute estimates, as opposed to relative quantification. For
example, do absolute abundance estimates more accurately reflect miRNA activity? 
5. The authors highlight examples in which previous methods or databases misestimate 1) strand bias, 2) family-level
expression, or 3) tissue specificity. However, these observations are not experimentally validated, although qMap-seq still
has bias. I suggest validating at least one of these examples to substantiate the improved reliability of qMap-seq. 
Minor comments: 
1. Lines 47-50: PCR has been shown not to be a major source of bias in miRNA-seq. Notably, the addition of 50 cycles did
not substantially alter miRNA profiles (Hafner et al., RNA, 2011, Jayaprakash et al., Nucleic Acids Research, 2011). Thus,
please revise this section or provide a justification for diverging from these findings. 
2. Please acknowledge the prior use of Lambda exonuclease in miRNA library preparation (Hagemann-Jensen et al.,Nat.
Biotech., 2018). 
3. Please include strand information (e.g., -5p vs -3p) when referring to mature miRNAs throughout the manuscript unless
referring to families or precursor forms (pri- or pre-miRNAs). For example, lines 209-211 currently lack this information. 
4. The machine learning-guided model could potentially be further improved by incorporating information on 5’ and 3’ termini
of mapped reads. If the current model aggregates all reads mapped to a given miRNA regardless of their precise ends,

isoform-level resolution could be leveraged to refine the model. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their manuscript "An absolute quantification atlas of small non-coding RNAs across diverse mammalian tissues and cell
lines" Xiao et al describe their improved method to clone small RNAs with absolute quantification (qMap-seq), and use this
to generate an atlas of several dozen datasets of mammalian small RNAs, cell lines and tissues of several species. They
also describe a machine learning method to correct small RNA cloning bias. 
There are many small RNA cloning protocols, and many limitations of the early methods have been recognized and
improved. These include use of randomized adaptors on both ends, spike-ins, and UMI to minimize ligation bias and enable
absolute quantification of small RNAs. These have all been applied in other studies (eg from Narry Kim lab PMID 30605524
or Phil Zamore lab 30001700), and are implemented in many lab's current workflows. In addition, a new paper from Ildar
Gainetdinov lab also includes small RNA cloning absolute quantification (PMID 40436630). A difference with the submitted
paper is that they use a series diluted spike-in RNAs with defined concentrations to the total RNA, so they can make a
standard curve and calculate the absolute miRNA levels according to the curve, similar to absolute quantitive qPCR for
individual genes. This submission is >1 month later than the other recent one, so I would not make a big deal, but only to say
the general cloning methods are well-known, but they do a better job for absolute quantification. 
The authors apply this method to a wide range of mammalian tissues and cell lines, generating a large dataset and
providing a public resource. This dataset is useful, but just to note there are also extensive other data exist. 
This study also developed a machine learning method to help correcting the previous small RNA seq datasets and include
in their web database for users to adjust and do absolute quantification of these previous small RNA seq data. 
I think the study is technically very competent. I don't really have substantial feedback that would improve it or make it more
confident. It provides some advances but the methodology is not very novel, and the database is extensive but also other
small RNA databases exist. I think its an editorial decision whether to publish in Nature Communications. It could also be of
interest to a journal with a more focused readership in RNA (such as RNA or NAR), or perhaps genomics (Genome
Research or Genome Biology).

The authors’ revised manuscript now holds two main but moderate contributions to the RNA community: (1) a single-tube
experimental workflow for small RNA-seq, and (2) small RNA resources including miRNA and piRNA in cell lines, mice,

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have adequately addressed my previous concerns and revised the manuscript accordingly. 
However, for Supplementary Figure 2, please repeat the same analysis separately within each cell line and each tissue, and
present all corresponding scatter plots. To assess whether the 11-nt UMI is sufficient for deduplication, it is necessary to
compare the abundance of the most highly expressed miRNAs before and after deduplication. If the 11-nt UMI is insufficient,
the abundance of the most highly expressed—but not moderately or lowly expressed—miRNAs will be distorted after
deduplication. Importantly, even if the UMI length is insufficient for the most abundant miRNA(s), integrating multiple
datasets (as the authors have done) could underestimate or mask such distortion due to read counts from cell lines or
tissues where the same miRNA is expressed at lower levels. Therefore, the absence of distortion should be confirmed
independently for each cell line and each tissue.

RESPONSE: We thank the reviewer for the insightful comments.  
We agree that 4NBoost is not the first study to use random adapters and UMIs. 
Indeed, a series of studies since 2011  have demonstrated that  library preparation 
methods using fixed adapters can introduce severe ligation bias, whereas using 
random or pooled adapters can effectively mitigate such bias  (Giraldez et al., 2018; 
Hafner et al., 2011; Jayaprakash et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2019; Sorefan et al., 2012; 
Sun et al., 2011; van Eijndhoven et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2015; Zhuang et al., 2012) . 
Similarly, several studies have employed random sequences as UMIs to remove PCR 
duplicates (Belair et al., 2019; Fu et al., 2018; van Eijndhoven et al., 2023). The use of 
RNA spike-ins for absolute quantification of sncRNAs has also been reported using 
microarray (Bissels et al., 2009) , RT-qPCR (Brown et al., 2007; Rose et al., 2021)  or 
sequencing (Gainetdinov et al., 2018; Gainetdinov et al., 2021) . In addition, earlier 
works have demonstrated  that a high concentration of PEG can reduce ligation bias  
(Kim et al., 2019; Song et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2013). Overall, we acknowledge the 
reviewer’s point that our method is not entirely novel and that several of its individual 
components have been reported to some extent  previously. However, the primary 
purpose of our methodological section is not to claim the superiority over existing 
approaches, but rather to demonstrate its accuracy and robus tness in achieving 
absolute quantification of sncRNAs.  
To this end, we systematically integrated and refined findings from prior studies, 
and optimized key parameters—including the number of random bases in adapters, 
the required UMI length, PEG concentration, adapter concentration, by -product 
removal strategy, and the overall workflow. These refinements allowed us to establish

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors’ revised manuscript now holds two main but moderate contributions to the 
RNA community: (1) a single -tube experimental workflow for small RNA -seq, and (2) 
small RNA resources including miRNA and piRNA in cell lines, mice, monkeys, and 
now plants. This is vastly different from what the authors have originally proposed in 
their manuscript. 
The single-tube experiment is again interesting but unfortunately not applicable at 100 
pg level or less. Achieving this pg level technique would have opened the possibility of 
single-cell small RNA-sequencing. That being said, this single -tube experiment offers 
new opportunities for scalable small RNA -sequencing. Regarding it as a small RNA 
resource, I do believe that it complements existing small RNA databases, especially in 
terms of quantification. 
Response: We sincerely appreciate the reviewer ’s recognition of the contributions 
our work brings to the RNA community, and we thank the reviewer for the thoughtful 
comments that helped us further refine and highlight the key advances of this study. 
We would like to note that our initial goal was indeed to extend the method to ultra-
low-input or even single -cell applications. However, during method optimization, we 
found that the use of randomized adapters inevitably generates substantial by -
products, particularly when the input RNA amount is extremely low and extensive PCR 
amplification is required. Although lowering the adapter concentration can partially 
mitigate this issue, it compromises quantitative accuracy.  Future optimization efforts 
may focus on enhancing 3’ adapter removal efficiency or exploring alternative ligation 
strategies that could enable reliable picogram-level input processing.
