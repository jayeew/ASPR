Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript introduces a hierarchical picot-fiber hydrogel coating (PFHC), which mimics the layered structure of natural
soft cartilage to achieve ultralow friction and high wear resistance. By combining a lubricating surface layer with a tough
fiber-reinforced core, the authors overcome the inherent trade-off between low friction and durability in conventional hydrogel
coatings. The manuscript provides a comprehensive description of PFHC design, structure, mechanical properties,
lubrication, and wear-resistance, mechanistic insights, as well as biocompatibility and in vivo stability. Overall, the research
is well-designed, and the experiments are carefully executed, supporting the conclusions drawn. It can provide significant
insights into designing hydrogel coatings, potentially impacting various practical applications in biomechanics and related
fields. I recommend the acceptance of this manuscript after a few minor revisions. 
1. The introduction effectively highlights the challenges of implant wear and the potential of hydrogel coatings. However, in
the second paragraph, the description seems to imply that all hydrogels inherently possess ultralow friction. This may
mislead readers, as hydrogel–counterface interactions are influenced by multiple factors, including surface charge,
hydrophilicity, hydration layer stability, and interfacial electrostatic interactions. In practice, not every hydrogel achieves
ultralow friction. The authors should clarify this point and make proper distinctions to avoid overgeneralization. 
2. The authors attribute the layered morphology of PFHC to oxygen inhibition during polymerization, which results in distinct
surface and core structures. Figure 1f indeed shows clear morphological differences. However, it remains unclear whether
such differences are accompanied by measurable variations in mechanical properties. The authors should provide
nanoindentation data for both layers would strengthen the claim of a gradient structure. 
3. The tribological evaluation convincingly demonstrates ultralow friction in PFHC. However, the lubrication regime in which
this coating operates is not fully clear. Since a softer surface layer often promotes hydrodynamic or mixed lubrication,
analyzing the lubrication regime would substantially enhance the mechanistic insights. The authors are suggested to apply
models or theoretical estimates of film thickness to determine whether PFHC functions in boundary, mixed, or hydrodynamic
regimes under the tested conditions. 
4. The fluorescence tracing experiment is convincing, showing the dynamic self-assembly of picot fibers. Quantitative
analysis would make this result more robust. This would add an important layer of mechanistic understanding and allow
readers to better appreciate how dynamic fiber assembly contributes to the overall structural stability and function of PFHC. I
recommend including quantitative fluorescence analysis in the results. 
5. The degradation tests show that PFHC is remarkably stable, with minimal degradation observed even under challenging
conditions. This is highly beneficial for long-term implant applications, but it also raises concerns for contexts requiring
controlled biodegradability, such as tissue engineering. The authors may wish to discuss whether the low degradability is
primarily due to peptide sequence design and whether degradation can be tuned. Such a discussion would clarify the
broader applicability of PFHC materials. 
6. Since peptide-based materials can be sensitive to high temperature, ethanol, or UV treatment, it is important to ensure that

sterilization does not compromise peptide integrity or hydrogel performance. The clarification of sterilization methods applied
to PFHC and UPE before biological testing was recommended. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript describes the design principles and characterization of a picot-fiber-based multilayered material able to
mimic the properties of cartilage. The complex build-up of cartilage makes its mimicry even harder. Yet, in previous research,
the authors have disclosed how the hydration and wear can be guaranteed by means of peptide-embedding hydrogels
(PHFCs) to be interlocked/conjugated to a solid carrier. The materials in this manuscript are thoroughly examined, also in
terms of eventual biocompatibility. Although the design of the picot fibers has been disclosed by the authors in the past (see
Nat Commun 2023), the applicability of the PFHC-based materials is more extensively demonstrated in the current
manuscript. the major concern of the manuscript consists of the concise nature of the manuscript, meaning that many
aspects are mentioned and materials are described that come out of nowhere. This might also be due to the fact that prior
work did touch upon these aspects and they are just assumed to be known by the reader in this case. This concern will be
exemplified below. Points will be listed in chronological order and switching in between main text and supporting
information.

Reviewer Comments to the Authors 
This manuscript, “Hierarchical Picot-Fiber Hydrogel Coating with Ultralow Friction and High Wear Resistance,” presents a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

rigorous and insightful study on a bioinspired hierarchical picot-fiber hydrogel coating (PFHC) that effectively mimics the
structural and functional architecture of articular cartilage. By integrating a porous, lubricating surface layer with a peptide-
reinforced core composed of self-assembling picot fibers exhibiting reversible hidden-length extension, the authors achieve
an exceptional combination of ultralow friction (~0.009) and high wear resistance. The work stands out for its mechanistic
sophistication, quantitative performance, and translational relevance to biomedical coatings. Overall, the study is well-
conceived and experimentally robust, yet certain aspects, particularly the mechanistic quantification, long-term durability,
and contextual benchmarking, would benefit from deeper discussion and minor analytical refinements to fully align with
Nature Communications’ publication standards and strengthen its broader scientific impact. 
The authors should address the following comments 
1. The concept of “hidden length” as a molecular energy-dissipation mechanism is compelling and well-supported
qualitatively. To reinforce this claim, it would be helpful to include an estimation or discussion that connects the energy
associated with molecular unfolding events to the observed macroscopic fracture or toughness values. Even an approximate
calculation or supplementary note would help bridge the molecular and bulk scales and enhance the mechanistic coherence
of the work. 
2. The in vivo results over 7 weeks are promising, but readers would benefit from a clearer discussion of the expected long-
term stability of the PFHC. Please address the possible degradation kinetics of the peptide components and outline whether
the mechanical and tribological performance is expected to persist over several months. If feasible, preliminary data or
insights from accelerated degradation or enzymatic exposure studies could further support this discussion. 
3. The tribological testing currently focuses on a maximum load of 5 N (approximately 0.4 MPa), which is below the upper
range of physiological joint pressures (2–5 MPa). It would strengthen the practical significance of the study to clarify how the
coating performs under higher contact stresses, or at least discuss the expected performance envelope based on the
material’s mechanical characteristics. 
4. The comparison presented in Figure 3c is useful but would be more persuasive if normalized across key parameters such
as load, sliding frequency, lubrication environment, and cycle number. A supplementary table summarizing these test
conditions and corresponding performance metrics from relevant literature would provide valuable context for evaluating
PFHC’s relative advancement. 
5. To improve reproducibility and analytical rigor, please clearly indicate the number of replicates (n) for each experiment,
confirm that data distributions meet the assumptions of the statistical tests used, and specify whether corrections for multiple
comparisons (e.g., post hoc analyses) were applied. Standardizing this information throughout the figures and
supplementary materials would align the work with Nature Communications’ reporting standards. 
6. The strong interlocking interface between the coating and substrate is a critical feature of the design. However, its long-
term stability under dynamic or fluid-shear environments is not yet addressed. Additional testing, such as under oscillatory
shear or simulated synovial flow, or a reasoned discussion of expected performance under such conditions, would
strengthen the claim of durable adhesion and broaden the translational relevance of the work. 
7. Minor refinements could improve clarity and readability: 
o Define all abbreviations upon first appearance (e.g., PFHC-Gel). 
o Minor linguistic adjustments for conciseness are recommended. 
8. 
Additional Questions and Suggestions 
o Could the authors elaborate on the potential for functionalization or integration with medical-grade metals (e.g., Ti6Al4V
implants) beyond the test substrate? 
o Are there foreseeable limitations regarding scalability or manufacturing consistency of the PFHC coatings? 
o Consider adding a concise graphical summary illustrating the hierarchical design and decoupled lubrication mechanism
for a broader readership appeal.
