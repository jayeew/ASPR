Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors assess the performance of five published deep learning models for the prediction of drug responses to specific
cell lines. To this end they compiled a benchmark data set obtained from GDSC, containing around 1000 cell lines and
about 250 drugs. Additionally, the models trained on the GDSC data is also evaluated on novel reported drug responses
obtained from the literature. 
The author also introduce the experimental variability-aware prediction accuracy (EVAPA), which is meaningful, especially
in the context of the large variance of experimental values of activity data. 
The study is overall well conceived and is of considerable interest to the scientific community. However, there are some
substantial issues in the performance evaluation that should be addressed: 
- While it is meaningful to calculate the (mean) absolute percentage error APE for physical quantities like the IC50 resulting
in a unit-less value, APE is not meaningful for the logarithmic values or the min-max normalized variant (if this is the one the
authors use). The logarithmic values depend on the unit and might be negative or (close to) 0. The same goes for the [0,1]
normalized values, which would give increased APE for values close to 0. Instead the authors should report the mean (base
10) logarithmic error without normalization. This is an easily interpretable quantity, where 1 corresponds to a error by a factor
of 10 and 2 to an error by a factor of 100. 
- in particular with respect to the EVAPA score, it is extremely valuable to compare model performance to simple baseline
baseline models: 
1.) For the random split the baseline model always predicts the average pIC50 value of all the training drug responses. 
2.) For the NCC split, given drug D, cell line C, the model always predicts the average pIC50 of drug D (for all cell lines in
the training data). In this way, it can be assess to what extent a deep learning model is better than just predicting the cell-line
unspecific potency of a drug D. 
3.) For the NCD split, given drug D, cell line C, the model always predicts the average pIC50 of cell line C (for all drugs in
the training data). 
Likewise, here the extent to which a model is able to predict the suitability of a drug for a cell line better than a drug property-
insensitive baseline model can be evaluated. (This baseline can also be used when evaluating the performance of novel
compounds from the literature.) 
The proposed baseline models would allow a systematic evaluation to what extent these models are able at all to 
a) predict cell-line specific drug potency and 
b) predict drug-specific cell-line susceptibility 
Although the conclusions of the paper are reasonable, they could be better supported by the points raised above, as these
more clearly address 
limitations of current models.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a manuscript titled Benchmarking Deep Learning Models for Predicting Anticancer Drug Potency
(IC50): Insights for the Medicinal Chemist, in which they seek to avoid standard metrics in QSAR model measurements and
replace them with the absolute error percentage. In principle, this sounds good, considering that models relying exclusively
on R2 or cross-validation parameters do not analyze data error but rather the overall classification of all data, where
substantial errors in prediction can exist. However, the inputs used for the tested models do not provide much information
about the structure-activity relationship of anticancer drugs, and this is where Pearson correlations become vitally important.
These correlations help models have inputs associated with the output and provide independent information to avoid
overfitting the model. 
Now, the absolute error should be an additional measure of model error without eliminating the compensation coefficient,
Pearson compensation, and RSME values. 
Consider that the manuscript is only halfway through its work. As they conclude, more variables need to be evaluated so that
the resulting models have acceptable quality values that would qualify as predictive models. 
1) Eliminate over-explanations in the discussion of concepts widely discussed in the literature.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The revisions have improved the manuscript considerably, making it much more valuable for publication. The EVAPA score
for the R, NCC and NCD splits as well as the direct comparisons of the NCC and NCD splits to the baseline models nicely

The authors have addressed the concerns raised by the reviewers to my satisfaction and I would only like to point out a few

- I do not agree with the conclusions at the end of section 2.2 explaining the NCC performance by the ability to learn cell line

dependent on the cell line expressions. The authors might want to consider this in their discussion.

"The absolute base-10 logarithmic error (ALE) and its mean across, MALE, across all" 
Here, the first 'across' should be removed.
