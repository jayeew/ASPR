Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This extensive study combines psychophysics, fMRI, iEEG, and DNN modeling, investigating one-shot perceptual learning
as operationalized by object recognition in Mooney images. The psychophysical experiment quantified the effect of viewing
an unthresholded (grayscale) image on subsequent recognition of a thresholded (Mooney) version of the same image. By
manipulating the grayscale images, the psychophysical experiment found partial invariance to manipulations such as
reflection or translation, indicating sensory rather than conceptual memory. It also demonstrated invariance to image scaling,
inconsistent with an early visual representation. An fMRI study searching for such partially-invariant visual representations
indicated high-level visual cortex. An iEEG study detected a learning effect of viewing unthresholded images on the
representation of thresholded images, again indicating high-level visual cortex as the relevant site for perceptual learning.
Finally, a transformer-based DNN model reproduced some of the human behavioral patterns, and the perceptual history
captured by this model improved the prediction of fMRI responses to a sequence of images in the fusiform cortex. 
In general, I am convinced by the authors’ overall claim that the site of the perceptual learning involved with Mooney images
is indeed high-level visual cortex. This paper offers extensive converging evidence and demonstrates systematicity, vision,
and very high technical prowess of the team behind this work. However, as with many systems- and cognitive-neuroscience
studies, it is difficult to evaluate the statistical validity of dozens of custom-tailored, unregistered data analyses. A better data-
sharing policy could enable the community to perform efficient post-publication scrutiny of this extensive body of work. I
believe that uploading complete, well-documented repositories containing the human psychophysics and fMRI data along
with all relevant analysis code should be a mandatory condition for publishing this work. I have inspected the links currently
mentioned in the manuscript and they are either inaccessible, incomplete, or undocumented. Without robust data sharing, I
cannot confidently recommend this work for publication in Nature Communications. I understand that the human iEEG data
is more difficult to share, but I believe that at least anonymized time series (without associated imaging, but with anatomical
locations) can be shared with the community. 
Below, I list additional points that can be improved before this work is published in its final form. 
1. Quantification of invariance in the fMRI experiment (Fig. 3): This analysis detects representations that are at least partly
transformation-invariant. However, the study does not quantify how far from full invariance these representations are. This
can be done by computing dissimilarities between repetitions of each untransformed grayscale image and comparing these
dissimilarities to the dissimilarities under transformations. 
2. On a related note, the magnitude of dissimilarities is hard to interpret since it reflects both representational geometry and
signal-to-noise ratio. Using RDM-correlations is a better (and more established) approach to characterizing representations.
This issue is relevant to both Fig. 3c and Fig. 4d. Specifically, the posterior-to-anterior reduction in dissimilarity magnitude
shown in Fig. 4d is likely to reflect the SNR gradient of visual BOLD response and cannot be attributed to invariance without
comparing the dissimilarities to those expected by completely invariant representations. 
3. The model-based RDM compresses the results of the behavioral experiment into a very abstract description. Have the
authors considered forming a behavioral RDM based on image-pair-specific perceptual learning outcomes? 
That would
allow a much stronger linkage between the behavioral and fMRI experiments.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

4. The comparison of the DNN model to human behavior does not measure the correspondence between DNN and human
behavior on a trial-by-trial basis. Fig 5c summarizes the experiment by a few average performance estimates. Fig. 5d takes a
step in the right direction, but it is unclear why training linear classifiers from the DNN internal representation to predict
human behavior is justified. If the model is strong, its outputs should predict human trial-by-trial performance well without any
further parameter fitting. If the “zero-shot” alignment between the outputs of the DNN and human behavior is low, this
mismatch should be reported and analyzed. 
5. The result in Fig. 6c suggests that the recognition of Mooney images as faces (or more generally, animate beings)
mediates the perceptual learning effect in the neuroimaging and iEEG data. Can the authors test this hypothesis? 
6. The brief paragraph on the study’s limitations is insufficient and does not promote a deeper discussion. I am sure that the
authors have more to say here on where they perceive the limits of our current understanding, given their new results.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Neural and Computational Mechanisms Underlying One-shot Perceptual Learning in Humans 
## Summary 
This manuscript presents the results from a series of functional MRI experiments at 7T, intracranial recordings, and elaborate
computation modelling. The aim here is to understand the mechanisms underlying one-shot perceptual learning, as
exemplified by the ability of observers to recognise / understand so-called Mooney images: grayscale images that have
been binarised to obscure some information about shading, which makes them harder for observers to decode. 
The manuscript comes across as very polished, and the figures very nicely presented, but I found the apparent clarity in the
writing in contrast to the line of argument, which was very hard to follow. It is clear that a lot of work has gone into this study
and I think the data are very interesting. However, I am not entirely convinced that the results shed the light onto the
perceptual learning mechanism as clearly and conclusively as claimed by the authors. 
As a case in point (but not the only place in the manuscript), there is a manipulation of the stimuli to test for differences in
magno vs parvocellular processing - technically, this looks carefully done (color calibration, controlled visual stimuli, etc).
But to what end this manipulation and detailed analysis is included is not that clear. The major differences in M and P
processing are apparent very early in the visual processing pathways (retino, LGN, to V1) and as the authors themselves
state, both "systems", such as they are, have projections into IT. The invariance experiments / test make it clear that V1 or
earlier can't really be the site of the perceptual learning (as resizing the image cues still leads to one-shot learning). 
The absence of an effect of the M and P biased stimuli is therefore not that surprising? 
Another high-level comment is that the wording around "neural activity", cortical responses measured with fMRI, and implied
neural processing in a neural network are quite blurred. It is true that the dataset contains some neural recordings, but the
fMRI data has been "subsampled" into regions of interest, which are taken from a combination of probabilistic atlases, which
makes the comparisions between data obtained with the different methods more tenuous - to some extent the ROI
combination actually undoes the efforts of going to high spatial resolution with 7T fMRI (altough I am aware that there are
other reasons to go to high field). 
With some revisions to the text, the authors could bring out the exact connections between the data sets and modelling
efforts much more clearly.

would be more important to highlight the most interesting aspects of this dataset. In a revision, I’d be happy to provide more
fine-grained feedback on the analysis and results.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study employs diverse methods—behavioral experiments, psychophysics, fMRI, intracranial recordings, and
computational modeling—to investigate perceptual one-shot learning, specifically focusing on Mooney image recognition.
The authors propose that the high-level visual cortex (HLVC) is a crucial hub for encoding perceptual priors and facilitating
rapid learning. While their multifaceted approach is commendable, several concerns arise regarding their task design,
analysis methods, and modeling framework.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

General issues: 
The conclusions about HLVC's role rely primarily on correlational evidence from fMRI and iEEG data. While these methods
offer valuable insights, they do not establish causality. For this reason, many claims in the main text are overstated. 
The DNN model is presented as capturing top-down feedback mechanisms in learning, but the connection to biological data
is speculative. Simply comparing the proposed perceptual bias-update model and human subjects’ performance might not
be enough to conclude that the same mechanisms exist in the human brain. Model comparisons are required to evaluate
alternative hypotheses and in-depth analyses to show how the best-fitting model aligns with biological processes. 
The paper presents both fMRI and iEEG data, but these modalities are not sufficiently integrated. fMRI results show spatial
localization in HLVC, while iEEG provides temporal dynamics, yet there is little discussion of how these findings
complement each other. A clearer integration of these modalities would support the claims about HLVC’s role in both spatial
and temporal aspects of learning. 
The relatively small sample sizes (fMRI: n=10; iEEG: n=19) raise statistical power concerns. 
Several figures, particularly those representing fMRI and model data, are overly complex and hard to interpret. Simplifying
these figures or breaking them into smaller panels would improve clarity. For example, the DNN model architecture
(Supplementary Fig. 9) is detailed but overwhelming without a step-by-step explanation in the main text. 
The iEEG study involves epilepsy patients, yet the paper does not discuss how the patients’ clinical conditions might
influence the results. While some demographic details are provided in the supplementary materials, the main text should
address potential differences in neural responses due to underlying pathology. 
The authors confidently attribute the one-shot perceptual learning effects to HLVC plasticity, but alternative explanations are
not fully considered. Attention-related processes or contributions from other cortical areas (e.g., frontoparietal networks)
could explain some of the observed neural activity. 
Major concerns: 
[Section 1-2] 
Hypothesis 
How does the "perceptual prior" concept central to this study differ from basic object identity recognition (e.g., "dog" or
"cat")? Scale and position invariance are well-established properties of the visual/IT cortex, and these naturally extend to
perceptual learning since recognition is guided by learning in this paradigm. Furthermore, HLVC's role in processing high-
level concepts (priors) is already well-documented. So, it is not surprising that the findings presented in the first three
sections largely align with existing knowledge about HLVC function. 
If perceptual priors are indeed distinct from HLVC's invariant object recognition capabilities, then the invariance testing
should be expanded to include additional manipulations such as Gaussian noise, patch masking, contrast variation, etc. 
The authors propose HLVC as a candidate region for one-shot learning based on its purported manipulation invariance.
However, this premise needs to be revised, as HLVC responses are demonstrably affected by various image manipulations,
including inversion, rotation, and spatial shifts. Therefore, manipulation invariance cannot be considered a necessary
condition for one-shot prior representation. 
Analyses 
The authors perform pairwise comparisons between conditions (e.g., original vs. catch, original vs. manipulation) using
repeated-measures ANOVA. However, before conducting these pairwise comparisons, isn’t it standard practice to perform
an overall repeated-measures ANOVA across all three conditions: original, catch, and manipulation? This initial global test
would establish whether a significant overall effect exists across conditions. 
If the overall repeated-measures ANOVA reveals a significant effect, post-hoc pairwise comparisons between individual
conditions (e.g., original vs. catch, original vs. manipulation) would be appropriate to determine where the specific
differences exist. Without this initial step, the current pairwise comparisons may increase the risk of Type I error by treating
each comparison in isolation. 
Experimental design 
I had difficulty relating prior knowledge/one-shot learning to the experimental design. I might have misunderstood the nature
of experimental design, but in any case, the following points need to be clarified: 
(1) Figure 1b suggests that the experiment tests whether the manipulated grayscale image can recall prior knowledge
learned from the original grayscale image. However, it seems that each subject sees only one version of the grayscale
image per unique Mooney image (either original, catch, or manipulated). Therefore, there is little opportunity for prior
knowledge learned from the original grayscale image to be recalled during the manipulation condition, as the subject has
never seen the original image beforehand. The figure should reflect that the manipulated condition is independent of any
prior exposure to the original image. 
(2) The description of prior knowledge, particularly on Page 3 (last paragraph), seems inconsistent with the experiment's
design. The authors investigated whether the manipulated grayscale image can recall "stored prior knowledge." However,
no prior knowledge is stored in the manipulation condition because the original grayscale image was never shown.
Furthermore, presenting original and manipulated grayscale images for the same Mooney image would contradict the one-
shot learning paradigm, where a single exposure induces learning. 
(3) Figures 1 and 2, Experimental Design (page 4, 6): The current results suggest that HLVC facilitates one-shot learning
through top-down modulation of EVC. However, could this not simply reflect object recognition learning rather than one-shot
learning? The only invariant variable across the learning process was the size manipulation, while other conditions were
manipulated to make object recognition more challenging. Hence, could it be that the learning effect was low not because of
the reduction of the one-shot learning effect but due to difficulties in object recognition? Furthermore, this might explain the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

fMRI activation of the ventral stream (HLVC) and the fusiform cortex (FC), typically involved in object recognition. 
(4) The key difference between this one-shot learning experiment and previous designs lies in manipulating the images. It
seems that the manipulation was introduced to demonstrate that one-shot learning involves perceptual learning. If this is
correct, could you provide a more straightforward explanation for why such manipulation was necessary? 
(5) Additionally, to support the authors’ claim, it may be essential to reference prior studies that address the impact of
manipulation on perception, along with presenting relevant fMRI data. 
Interpretation of behavioral results 
In the third paragraph on Page 5, the authors state that "these results suggest that the perceptual priors cannot be encoded
in the early visual cortex." This conclusion conflates altered neural coding with the inability to encode perceptual priors,
which are not equivalent concepts. While the size manipulation may change the neural coding in the early visual cortex, this
does not necessarily imply that the perceptual priors cannot still be encoded and utilized effectively. 
For example, if the altered coding in the early visual cortex could not properly encode size-changed perceptual priors, one
would expect a significant decline in perceptual decision-making performance. However, the results shown in Figure 2d
show that the subjects' recognition accuracy for size-manipulated images was similar to that for original images, suggesting
that while the neural representation may have been altered, the perceptual system still successfully encoded and used the
prior knowledge. 
[Section 3] 
Potential issues in analyses 
There are no detailed descriptions of the distance comparison (Figure 3b-c) or the searchlight analysis (Figure 3d) in either
the main methods section or the supplementary materials. 
I am not sure if the distance comparison between within-image and cross-image distances is valid because the cross-image
distance distribution includes both same-condition and different-condition data. This potentially inflates the cross-image
distance values. To accurately assess whether neural encoding is condition-invariant but image-variant, the comparisons
should confined to within-image distances and same-condition cross-image distances. 
The behavioral invariance is observed during the one-shot learning task (Figure 1-2), but the neural invariance is tested
during passive viewing. The different cognitive demands between these two conditions may bias the neural encoding. 
The model-based representational similarity analysis (RSA) assumes that the similarity between different conditions for the
same image is equal. I am not sure this is a valid assumption; for example, the psychophysics results in Figure 2 show that
different conditions show distinct effects on recognition performance. 
The authors conclude that HLVC is the prime candidate region for storing perceptual priors. To fully support this claim, isn’t it
necessary to show that prior knowledge can be successfully decoded from HLVC neural signals? Following this, the neural
invariance (Figure 3) should be tested to confirm that HLVC encodes perceptual priors in a condition-invariant manner. 
Figure 3 (page 8) 
The learning effect was significantly reduced under the position shift and orientation change conditions (excluding size
manipulation), indicating that the learning was only partially invariant. If this is the case, should you not have limited your
fMRI analysis to invariant conditions (such as size and original conditions) to accurately identify the brain regions
specifically involved in one-shot learning? Analyzing all conditions together, as done in the current study, might make it
difficult to separate brain regions associated with one-shot learning. 
Following this suggestion, if authors assume that most conditions contributed to one-shot learning, wouldn’t it have been
helpful to use a control, such as catch trials, for comparison? If HLVC showed consistent activation in both manipulation and
original trials, compared to catch trials which has no one-shot learning effect, this would strengthen the authors’ claim. 
To further explain the top-down modulation from HLVC to EVC, I propose functional connectivity analysis in the fMRI data.
Rather than focusing on the connectivity between FPN and EVC, as in previous studies, demonstrating increased
connectivity from HLVC to EVC would offer stronger support for your findings. It might be beneficial to visualize the
connectivity analysis similarly to Figure 3b. 
[Section 4] 
The cluster-based permutation tests to identify the onset time of neural changes: While these tests are effective in identifying
significant clusters of neural activity, they do not provide precise information about the exact onset time of neural changes.
As demonstrated by Sassenhagen and Draschkow (2019) [1], cluster-based tests do not establish the significance of the
effect latency. Therefore, specific claims about neural onset times based solely on these results should be avoided.
(References: [1] Sassenhagen, J., & Draschkow, D. (2019). Cluster-based permutation tests of MEG/EEG data do not
establish the significance of effect latency or location. Psychophysiology, 56(6), e13335.) 
Page 9, "likely due to bottom-up visual activation that subsided quickly when recognition was unsuccessful.": This
assumption overlooks the likely involvement of top-down visual processing. Following predictive coding frameworks, where
top-down feedback is used to resolve discrepancies between sensory input and internal models, ‘unsuccessful recognition’
requires first interpreting sensory information and likely input from higher cortical regions to lower ones. 
The claim that prior-related information is transferred from HLVC to EVC is an overstatement. 
(1) Page 9, “that feedback from HLVC to EVC likely carried prior-related information.": The authors do not clarify what kind of
information is being transferred, nor do they provide sufficient evidence to support the claim about the direction or site of
information flow. Temporal precedence alone is insufficient to determine the direction of flow between brain regions, as
reciprocal connections and recurrent processing could also explain the observed patterns. To substantiate claims about
directional information flow, the authors should consider other conventional techniques such as Granger causality or
dynamic causal modeling (DCM), which are designed to infer causal relationships and information flow in neural circuits. 
(2) In the 365-483 ms range of EVC in fig4c bottom, post>0 (orange) is observed, but post>pre (black) is not. Additionally, a
cluster where pre>0 (blue) appears twice. One can interprete this as follows: "which can be explained by similar visual

features between Mooney images and their matching grayscale images, such as co-localized contours." Using this logic,
post>0 should also appear, but it did not. Thus, I am not fully convinced by the authors’ claim drawn from the results showing

[Section 5-7] 
Model justification 
The connection between the DNN model and biological mechanisms remains largely speculative. The DNN model seems
to be overly complex. The paper does not justify why this level of complexity is needed, nor does it sufficiently compare the
model to simpler alternatives. For example, what about the associative learning model or hierarchical Bayesian model?
There are variants of the Bayesian model with varying learning rates as a function of prediction error or attention level that
could replicate the behavioral findings of this work. Such models can also be used to implement top-down modulation of
HLVC; each intended to test alternative hypotheses, such as attention or prediction error-based one-shot learning. 
While it outperforms previous models like BLT and CORnet, the benefits of the added complexity are unclear, and the
architecture is not explained in a way that’s accessible to a broad audience. 
The differences between the proposed DNN model and baseline models, such as BLT and CorNet, should be more clearly
explained. The current description lacks sufficient detail regarding how the models differ in their architecture and approach
to one-shot perceptual learning. A more in-depth comparison would clarify the specific contributions of the proposed model. 
If the authors aimed to model HLVC→EVC top-down signaling as they claim, they should have specified which components
of the proposed DNN correspond to each region's role. 
Evaluation issues 
To validate that the prior acquired during the learning process of the DNN model is indeed similar to the one acquired by
humans, shouldn't the model be tested using the same psychophysics manipulations employed in the human experiments?
For example, the authors can test the DNN model with various manipulated grayscale images (as in Figure 2) to replicate
the learning effects observed in human subjects. This validation must be performed before any analyses using the internal
features of the DNN model (e.g., in Sections 6-7 and Figure 6) can be justified. 
One-shot learning performance was measured using evaluation accuracy. If that's the case, then it’s merely matching inputs
by updating only the memory state in a network with fixed weights, so it’s difficult to consider this as a one-shot learning
mechanism driven by synaptic plasticity changes. 
Page 11, "found that our model significantly outperforms them in this one-shot perceptual learning task (Supplementary Fig.
10).": Besides performance comparison between the model and humans, the authors should also demonstrate the model
can replicate the learning effect observed in human subjects. Based on Supplementary Fig. 10, the BLT model also appears
to replicate the increase in post-learning accuracy compared to pre-learning accuracy. This warrants further investigation, as
performance alone should not be the primary metric of success. 
Page 11, "To evaluate whether the internal representations of the model contain information relevant to how human subjects
recognize the Mooney images...": These analyses should be preceded by a correlational study between the model's and
human performance. Specifically, the authors need to fit the proposed model to the human data and determine whether there
is a significant correlation between the model's correct recognition rate and the corresponding human correct recognition
rate for the same images. Without establishing this relationship, the subsequent analyses on internal representations remain
speculative and lack grounding in direct human-model performance comparison. 
Page 11, Fig. 5c: The post-learning accuracy of the DNN model is lower than the pre-learning accuracy of human subjects.
This discrepancy raises significant concerns about whether the DNN model can truly represent human one-shot perceptual
learning. With such low performance after learning, it is difficult to accept the claim that this model is capturing human-like
visual processing.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I appreciate the authors’ detailed response. I particularly commend their efforts to open source the data and code, as well as
their introduction of new analyses. 
Despite the revisions, I remain concerned about unresolved major issues. 
1. An alternative explanation of the iEEG results—that the high-order cortical response reflects the outcomes of basic-level
categorization rather than the site of perceptual learning itself—has not been ruled out. Another reviewer has also raised this
parsimonious alternative explanation (‘How does the “perceptual prior” concept central to this study differ from basic object
identity recognition?’). I find the authors’ response to both inquiries unsatisfactory. If direct empirical disambiguation is not
feasible, the theoretical framing should be revised to acknowledge this potential confound and adopt a more nuanced
interpretation. 
2. Earlier visual regions elicit stronger neural population responses compared to later visual regions, consequently leading
to stronger hemodynamic responses in the former. This phenomenon has been consistently observed in numerous visual
electrophysiology and fMRI studies. This pattern is also evident in the steep decline of the yellow curve in Supp. Figure 5,
which indicates that dissimilarities decrease even within each viewing condition. This decline suggests that the reduction in
dissimilarity magnitude primarily reflects a decrease in response SNR rather than an increase in invariance 
Therefore, interpreting the downward trend in Supp. Figure 5 as evidence of increasing invariance (lines 252–260, main

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

text) is, in my view, invalid. One cannot establish that a representation is invariant solely by testing t-values against zero.
Indeed, using cross-validated distance to measure dissimilarities mitigates the problem of noise-driven inflation of distances,
and thereby gives meaning to the zero-point. However, this approach does not eliminate the confound of decreasing SNR
along the anterior-posterior axis. As an extreme example, consider a voxel sampled from the ventricles—it would also
exhibit dissimilarities indistinguishable from zero. 
For a representation to be invariant in a neuroscientifically meaningful way, it must meet two criteria: it should exhibit
minimal dissimilarities across viewing conditions of the same image while showing significant and substantial dissimilarities
between different images. Crucially, the former requirement cannot be supported by merely reporting p > 0.05. As the
authors know, NHST cannot prove the null hypothesis. Instead, established inferential methods such as equivalence testing
and Bayes factors should be used. 
I suggest that the authors either remove this section of the manuscript along with the associated figure (including the
searchlight analysis) or revise the analyses and text to avoid confounding increase in invariance with decrease in SNR. This
could involve formally quantifying the gap between the yellow line and the green bars in Supp. Figure 5. 
3. Since the model RDM contains many tied values, interpreting the Spearman correlation coefficient is challenging. How
were ties handled in the analysis? This problem relates to another reviewer’s concern that the model RDM assumes
equivalent dissimilarities. I recommend exploring alternative ordinal correlation measures that provide explicit control over
how ties are handled (e.g., the Kendall Tau family).

Remaining major issues after authors’ point-by-point responses, 
1) Re: Authors’ response: 
“Actually, the task condition during grayscale image presentation in both cases were relatively similar. During the behavioral
experiment, subjects viewed the Mooney and grayscale images and used button presses to respond recognition outcome
(Yes/No), followed by a verbal response about the identity of the image. During the fMRI experiment, participants passively

their recognition it would be “Yes” on most trials. We have previously shown that the one-shot perceptual learning effect
does not depend on cognitive effort4 and passive viewing was sufficient. Furthermore, we deliberately used a block design
with shuffled image orders so that a Mooney image is rarely directly followed or preceded by its matching grayscale image

Our investigation of magno- vs. parvocellular pathway contribution was part of behavioral Experiment 2, which addressed secondary questions. The M and P pathways originate in the LGN and project throughout the visual hierarchy, with stronger contributions to the dorsal and ventral streams, respectively. Our finding that there is no difference in the learning outcome for both manipulations compared to the high-contrast line drawing suggests that either pathway alone can contribute to prior representation. This is consistent with our main conclusion that HLVC supports prior representation, rather than an earlier region like V1. For example, what about the associative learning model or hierarchical Bayesian model? We agree that there are other cognitive models that could be used to explain the behaviors of humans during perceptual (learning) tasks. For example, a recent work (Jaffe et al., 2024) proposed an image-computable Bayesian model of speeded decision-making in a visual perception task. This type of model can be used to model human behaviors such as reaction time, correct rate etc. However, they are not appropriate for our analysis. These models are typically used to represent and test specific representational, structural, or functional hypotheses that underlie certain human behaviors; thus, they are usually directly fit to human behaviors, and the models’ abilities to explain human behavior in a formal model comparison are seen as lending credence to the hypothesis that generates said model.  On the other hand, in our analyses, the goal is not to represent a specific hypothesis with a computational model and test the validity of that hypothesis by fitting the model to human behavior. Instead, we are interested in developing a model that is trained using an objective cost function (instead of fitted to human behavioral data) and yet can predict the full pattern of human behavior (including accuracy, learning magnitude, image- and trial-level error pattern and learning outcome). Our second goal was to use the model to help localize prior representations in the human brain that contribute to perceptual learning, thereby providing complementary evidence to our empirical investigation.   We achieve our goals by training a network that performs strongly on the task of interest, and a natural choice here is to start from existing vision backbones that are known to perform well on image recognition tasks. As powerful as models such as hierarchical Bayesian models are at explaining human behaviors, they are not known for their strong performance on perceptual tasks. Given the representation-first nature of our computational analysis, the complexity of our model should not be compared with models intended to explain human behaviors; rather, it needs to be compared with models intended to achieve high performance on image recognition (such as convolutional neural networks or recurrent neural networks trained on vision tasks) and few-shot or zero-shot learning tasks. We are afraid that the answer to this question depends on the precise definition of “sensory input matching”. This concept is key to the predictive coding framework, in which “sensory input matching” typically means comparing the bottom-up sensory signals with internally generated predictions to compute a prediction error signal. For instance, Clark (2013) says, “Perception thus involves “explaining away” the driving (incoming) sensory signal by matching it with a cascade of predictions ...” and “Perception reduces surprisal by matching inputs with prior expectations.”  Importantly, under such a framework, the prior needs to be activated before such matching computation can be carried out. In other words, if we transplant such concepts to our task, the prior knowledge needs to be reactivated before it can be matched to the sensory input. And if the matching is unsuccessful (i.e., large residual error), an alternative prior may need to be activated until a successful match is found.   As we mentioned in the previous round of response to reviewers, we are unsure if the predictive coding framework is the suitable theoretical framework for conceptualizing one-shot perceptual learning. But the foregoing argument suggests that under the predictive coding framework, prior reactivation should occur before sensory input matching.   Another interpretation of “sensory input matching’’ is the process of identifying the correct prior (we recognize that this may be the reviewer’s definition). As mentioned above, under the predictive coding framework, this also requires the prior to be activated first, which is then compared against the sensory input to see if it’s a good “match”.   More broadly, from a biological perspective (and not constrained by the predictive coding framework), we believe that, in this task, identifying the correct prior and activating that prior occur at the same time and are the same process. That is, viewing the grayscale image induces synaptic changes within the prior-storing brain region, such that the relevant neurons in this region are activated preferentially when the degraded sensory input (driven by the post-Mooney image) arrives. In other words, identifying the relevant prior is the same process as activating that prior. This then shifts the neural processing of the Mooney image to be similar to that of the grayscale image both locally and in downstream regions through both feedforward and feedback connections. The core of the Mooney paradigm, as used in the human experiments throughout the manuscript, is that the "pre" phase requires the Mooney image to be presented before the subject has seen the corresponding grayscale image. Learning is defined by the perceptual change that occurs after this exposure. The described shuffling procedure breaks this critical temporal constraint. By fully randomizing the sequence, a grayscale image (e.g., G1) can and will frequently appear before its corresponding Mooney image (M1) is presented for the first time. This issue is evident in the authors' own example sequence: A. The grayscale image G1 appears once before the first presentation of the Mooney image M1. B. The grayscale image G2 appears twice before the first presentation of the Mooney image M2. This makes me wonder whether the model's resulting behavior can be genuinely compared to human one-shot perceptual learning.  The purpose of this shuffling is to ensure that the model is never directly trained on the test Mooney task format; rather, shuffling encourages the model to learn to use prior knowledge to solve the post-phase task without relying on any specific structures. The reason is that in shuffled sequences, the only salient task structure is that some images can be recognized using knowledge from prior images. This is the exact model behavior that we want to investigate.  Training a machine learning model on one paradigm and testing its extrapolation ability to a superficially different but fundamentally related tasks also have a long history in the field of machine learning. On the other hand, training the model directly on data identical to the test paradigm could encourage the model to develop very specialized solutions to this particular paradigm (overfitting), which is markedly different from human participants who undergo no substantial training before the Mooney task.   We also note that although pre-phase Mooney images are part of the human test paradigm, it has not been proven crucial for perceptual learning. Rather, pre-phase images are included as a way to establish baseline recognition performance without the knowledge of matching grayscale images. In neural network models where we have the ability to arbitrarily modify the internal activations, there is no need for a strict temporal structure of pre-gray-post. Whenever a grayscale image G1 appears before M1 is presented for the first time, both M1 following G1 should be considered post-phase.   5) Lines 828-830: Supplementary Fig. 11a shows model performance during the evaluation phase, using held-out image sequences following the same shuffled structure. >>>There appears to be a significant discrepancy between the evaluation procedure and its actual implementation in the code.
