Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents HIT-EC, a hierarchical transformer model for accurate and interpretable EC number prediction. It
addresses key limitations of existing methods—such as poor performance on rare EC numbers and lack of biological
interpretability—by leveraging a four-level architecture aligned with EC hierarchy and a novel learning strategy for
incomplete annotations. HIT-EC provides trustworthy, evidence-based predictions and outperforms state-of-the-art models in
benchmarks. identifying conserved motifs in enzyme families, offering value for drug discovery and metabolic engineering.
However, some major concerns remain to be addressed for considering by Nature Communications. 
1. For model interpretation, relying on a single example to validate the domain-specific evidence derived from the relevance
scores computed by the final HIT-EC model is neither rigorous nor sufficient. Since no wet lab experiments were conducted,
the authors should at least select one enzyme from each of the six major families for assessment. 
2. Regarding the CYP106A2 family, the selection criteria for the 13 analyzed sequences remain unclear. More importantly,
from a biochemical and evolutionary perspective, analyzing sequences with >90% sequence identity is not informative, as
such high similarity guarantees they represent the same enzyme functionally. Even without AI-based analysis, we can
confidently conclude they are the same enzymes just from different species. 
3. The primary value of E.C. number assignment lies in annotating understudied enzymes, correcting misannotated
enzymes, and identifying promiscuous enzymes with divergent catalytic functions - not in reanalyzing well-characterized
enzyme families like CYP106A2, whose functions are already clearly defined. However, the authors fail to demonstrate how
HIT-EC performs on these critical tasks, which are far more relevant for advancing enzymatic knowledge. I think it would be
more persuasive if the authors could validate at least one of these applications using wet lab experiments. 
4. The authors state that they conducted cross-validation experiments in Section 2.1 ("Cross-validation for performance
comparison"). However, based on the description provided, it appears that the experimental setup involves repeating the
training and evaluation process ten times with random data splits (80% training, 10% validation, 10% testing) rather than a
true K-fold cross-validation framework. While this approach can be considered a form of repeated hold-out validation, it does
not strictly conform to the standard definition of cross-validation, especially in terms of ensuring balanced sampling across
all folds and reducing variance in performance estimation. 
5. In Section 2.2 ("Ablation study"), the authors evaluate: (1) HIT-EC without incomplete annotations and (2) a baseline
encoder-only transformer model that lacks both hierarchical modeling and the incomplete annotation. While this provides
some insight into the contribution of each component, the ablation design seems incomplete and does not fully follow the
principle of single-variable control. Specifically, a critical missing condition is a baseline transformer model trained with the
masked loss mechanism for incomplete EC numbers (i.e., base transformer + incomplete EC number). This would allow for
a full factorial analysis: I recommend that the authors perform this missing experiment and update the ablation study
accordingly to strengthen the validity of their conclusions. 
6. In Section 4.1 ("Architecture of HIT-EC"), the authors describe the final prediction as an aggregation of local and global
predictions using a weighted combination (Equation 5). However, the manuscript does not provide a separate evaluation or
visualization of the performance of the local predictions and global predictions individually. As such, it is unclear how much
each component contributes independently to the overall performance, and whether either one alone would be sufficient.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript introduces HIT-EC, a novel hierarchical interpretable transformer model designed for trustworthy and
accurate Enzyme Commission (EC) number prediction. The authors address key limitations of prior methods, such as poor
performance on underrepresented EC numbers, lack of strategies for handling incomplete annotations, and limited model
interpretability. HIT-EC’s architecture, which mirrors the four-level hierarchy of EC numbers, integrates both local and global
contextual information from protein sequences and introduces a masked learning strategy to utilize partially annotated data.
Extensive evaluations, including cross-validation, external validation on newly registered enzymes, and species-level
assessments, demonstrate the model’s superior performance and robust interpretability compared to current benchmarks.
While the study is thorough and presents a promising advancement, further clarification is needed to fully assess the rigor
and generalizability of the approach. 
1. In the current version of the manuscript, details regarding dataset curation and model training are presented primarily in
the Methods section. However, for better readability, the authors should briefly summarize how they curated the dataset and
trained the model directly in the Results section, particularly in Section 2.1. These aspects are crucial for interpreting the
presented performance results and should be framed as part of the results rather than being deferred solely to the Methods. 
2. The statement “We excluded EC numbers associated with less than ten sequences” requires clarification. Does this refer
to EC numbers linked to fewer than ten distinct protein sequences? This exclusion appears contradictory to the manuscript’s
emphasis on HIT-EC's improved performance for underrepresented EC numbers. Were these excluded EC numbers
included in the evaluation of underrepresented groups (e.g., those with ≤25 sequences), or were they entirely omitted from
both training and testing? Please clarify how these smaller EC classes were handled throughout model development and
evaluation. 
3. Section 2.3 raises several important points that need further explanation: 
(1) Although the New-392 dataset is said to include sequences released after September 2022, did the authors assess
sequence similarity between this dataset and the training set to rule out potential data leakage? High similarity could
confound the evaluation. Also, does the statement that 192 of 392 sequences are “outside the coverage” of the 1,938 EC
numbers imply these were manually filtered based on their EC assignments? 
(2) CLEAN, which otherwise performs competitively, shows dramatically reduced performance on the New-392 dataset.
Could the authors explain the cause of this discrepancy? 
(3) The phrase “we only considered sequences within the coverage of the cross-validation models” is unclear. Does this
mean only sequences whose EC numbers are among the 1,938 classes were included? 
(4) Regarding the statement about “failures in EC number assignments”: does this refer to cases where the ground truth EC
number was within the model's coverage, but the model failed to make a prediction or predicted an incorrect class? 
4. Given that incorporating additional data from TrEMBL appears to improve model performance in later experiments, why
was this dataset not included in the initial training of the HIT-EC model? It would be helpful for the authors to discuss their
rationale—whether due to annotation quality concerns, computational constraints, or other considerations. 
5. Throughout the Results section, the term “cross-validation models” is used repeatedly but without specifying which model
architecture is being referenced. Presumably, this refers to HIT-EC trained under a 10-fold cross-validation framework, but
this should be explicitly stated for clarity. 
6. In the species-specific evaluations using KEGG genomes, what ground truth EC annotations were used for performance
measurement? Were these annotations manually curated or inferred via automated pipelines? The reliability of the ground
truth labels directly impacts the validity of the reported performance metrics and should be clarified.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, the authors put forward a hierarchical interpretable transformer model (HIT-EC) for trusted enzyme committee
(EC) number prediction. HIT-EC uses a four-layer transformer architecture that aligns with the hierarchy of EC numbers and
exploits local and global dependencies in protein sequences for multi-label classification tasks. The model also proposes a
new learning strategy to deal with incomplete EC numbered samples. HIT-EC generates plausible predictions by providing
biologically meaningful explanations. This toolbox may be useful for EC number prediction for proteins of unknown function.
However, there are now many toolboxes for EC number prediction and some of them are developed based on protein
language model. I think the progress in this paper is not significantly compared to previous toolboxes in protein EC number
prediction. Also, this work has no additional experimental evidences to verify the model prediction. The authors have done
some interpretability analysis of model prediction. The result is relatively primary and most existing toolboxes can also do
the similar analysis.
