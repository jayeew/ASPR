Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, the authors investigate the role of charge accumulation on operational stability through operando catalytic
modulation via near-infrared (NIR) toggling on a photothermal catalyst. The NIR irradiation enhances HER kinetics and
inhibits charge accumulation and ion migration in the perovskite layer. As a result, a record-high STH efficiency and
enhanced operational stability were obtained. The work is interesting and presents advances in the field. I recommend it for
publication after dealing with the concerns listed below. 
1. For the stability test in Figure.1d, a sudden photocurrent drop from ~20 mA/cm2 to 0 was observed after ~310 h tracking.
Similar results can be seen in Figure. 6d. The authors need to explain the reasons beneath. 
2. On page 8 line 180, how do the authors define the Jph fluctuation period? How are the Jph fluctuation period results
obtained from Sup. Fig. 16? 
3. On page 9 line 203, the authors showed an increased ECSA value under NIR irradiation. Were the results obtained from
the same device? I suggest the author monitor ECSA change on the same device during the continuous process from NIR
irradiation off to on and then to off. 
4. On page 11 line 252, the authors perform TPC analysis to observe charge accumulation. Please give more test method
details (e.g., bias conditions) in the Method section and give a specific explanation of how the charge flows to generate the
reverse current. 
5. On page 15 line 368, stability tests under galvanostatic conditions were performed. Since charge carrier and ion
distribution are related to bias conditions, will the different voltages influence the stability? 
6. Mott-Schottky analysis: The device capacity is quite different between Figure. S24 and S29 for the control and target
devices, is it because the slight differences in device architecture? 
7. If the solar simulator light intensity is decreased to reduce photogenerated charge carrier density, or the catalyst and the
electrolyte are changed to enhance the HER catalytic activity, will the charge carrier accumulation and ion migration be
inhibited? 
8. On page 19 line 454, the authors built up an unassisted water-splitting system. Will NIR irradiation have similar effects on
the OER side? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper looks at the effect of NIR irradiation on a catalyst modified cathode attached to a perovskite solar cell. I cannot
recommend the paper for publication at the moment as I have many questions about the reliability and accuracy of the
techniques used as well as questions about whether the results have been interpreted correctly. 
(1) The authors discuss that this is an encapsulated solar cell which is connected in series with a catalyst coated electrode.
There is some discussion of the configuration and why it was chosen, but I think there needs to be clearer explanation for
why it is better to submerge the solar cell, rather than keeping it outside the solution and connecting the photoelectrode with
a slightly longer wire? 
(2) Does the NIR heating of the carbon electrode create a temperature gradient and thereby cause a large increase in

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

convective stirring? Is there an increase in performance due to increased mass transfer of e.g. the reactants and products to
and from the electrode? Is it possible to differentiate whether the improvements are due to enhanced kinetics, or rather
enhanced mass transport? There are papers that suggest that the HER on Pt nanoparticles is mass transport limited,
increased mass transport would therefore legitimately increase current (e.g.
https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsenergylett.1c00246). As far as I can see this has not been investigated? The bubble
evidence supports this? 
(3) 
It is stated that NIR irradiation significantly enhanced both the photocurrent and the photovoltage. I can understand how
the photocurrent is affected, but can the authors better explain why the photovoltage (by which I mean the open circuit
photovoltage) is different? In this case the cell is at open circuit and no charge is being extracted through the contacts, how
can heating of the contact influence the open circuit photovoltage inside the device? 
(4) The increased catalytic activity is partly attributed to an increased surface area which is taken from measurements of the
double layer capacitance. A larger surface area is measured under NIR. I would like to see this measured several times (e.g.
control, NIR, control, NIR, control) to rule out other mechanisms such as irreversible roughening of the electrode? 
(5) Impedance spectroscopy shows that the low frequency arc of the control device has a larger diameter (attributed to higher
Rct) at the same voltage versus RHE. 
Given the complexity of the impedance spectroscopy of perovskites, can the authors
give evidence that the low frequency arc is measuring RCT and not some kind of ion modulated current? Would the
diameter of the arc not be expected to be quite different as at the same applied voltage versus RHE the two systems are in
different states? E.g. at 0.9 V versus RHE the control cell is effectively at open circuit whereas the NIR cell still has a
photocurrent of over 20 mA and may not have a linear response. 
(6) Mott-Schottky analysis is used to determine that there is no change in band-bending in the bulk perovskite. Mott-Schottky
very rarely measured band bending in perovskite cells (there are many papers showing just how problematic it is as a
technique for perovskites and proving that it rarely measures the space charge layer) and a great deal of work is needed
here for the authors to convince us that they are actually measuring band bending. I suspect that none of the Mott-Schottky
analysis here is valid? 
(7) IMVS is measured – but the interpretation is far from clear. It should mirror the EIS results at open circuit. 
(8) 
IMPS is measured and the data is fitted to extract ktrans and krec. All the EIS and IMPS plots need to be plotted
isotropically. IMPS of perovskite solar cells measured at high frequencies measures the RC time constant of the devices
(RseriesCgeo). Are the authors able to show that this is not what is being measured here? The data for both cells looks very
similar, especially when it is claimed that the NIR strongly changes the kinetics. It is straightforward to calculate the RC time
constant and to compare it with 1/RC from the semi-circle maximum? This needs to be ruled out. 
(9) Can the authors show that DLCP and CV methods actually measure the carrier trap density in their devices? Typically,
these techniques do not accurately measure traps in perovskite devices as the ion migration dominates the response. All the
same issues as for Mott Schottky analysis apply, there are many papers on this which are not discussed. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The present manuscript discusses degradation of perovskite-based photocathode caused by charge accumulation.
Comparison with and without NIR irradiation on HER catalyst and various characterization during the degradation found that
sluggish HER kinetics led to the charge accumulations in the semiconductor and its degradation. The present manuscript
would provide design guidelines for durable photocathodes which can be potentially considered in various semiconductors.
However, my concern is what would be the original cause of the degradation. Is it due to either the catalysts degradation
caused by bubbles or poor HER kinetics in the absence of NIR? Additional simple control measurements would further
clarify their claims before its publication in Nat. Commun. Please find the following questions. 
Fig. 1c 
The authors claim that “the photothermal effect induced by NIR irradiation increased the catalyst’s temperature, thereby
accelerating HER kinetics and enhancing both Jph and Von.” Why Von changes by HER kinetics? Von should be at 0
mA/cm2 where HER kinetics does not involve. Does the Von shift come from the thermodynamics of HER? The authors may
be able to discuss the shift from Nernst equations. It may also help to evaluate the local temperature on the catalyst not just
by IR camera. 
Fig. S19 
Tafel slope on Pt-C/CC/FTO was found to be reduced by NIR. If we simply follow Tafel equation or Butler-Volmer equation,
the slope should increase at the elevated temperature. The authors can discuss the observed change in detail. I may
consider that the improved kinetics helps to suppress the coverage of H leading to the smaller Tafel slope. 
See Sci. Rep. 5
(2015) 13801. 
Additionally, can they show LSV of Pt-C/CC/FTO after the stability tests? It helps readers to observe how the overpotential
increased after Pt detachment. 
Local heating 
Local heating by NIR seems to play an important role. It is also known that kinetic loss on electrocatalysts causes a local
heating. Can they compare NIR power density and local heating from HER catalysis? The authors can evaluate the local
heating (W/cm2) due to HER from the operating current density (A/cm2) and overpotential (V). Overpotential can be roughly
evaluated from LSV at their operating current density. This analysis helps the readers to get an idea on the power density
levels needed to heat up.

The huge bubbles and their detachments were claimed to be the cause of Pt detachment and degradation of HER. The

Implementation potential of NIR local heating technique

a unit area of perovskite, can we obtain sufficient NIR power after the split of natural sunlight? The authors can discuss its
potential based on the standard solar spectrum.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have improved the paper and have answered some of my initial questions. I am still not convinced that it is
possible to conclude that the NIR heating increases the reaction kinetics - the authors themselves state that the picture is
very complex. The stirred experiments are a nice control, but they will differ from increased convection very close to the
surface with NIR heating, as well as local mixing caused by the release of bubbles. It may be that the kinetics do increase,
but I don't think there is enough evidence to state it unequivocally. 
The EIS, IMVS and IMPS are still not plotted isotropically as they should be (same length and number of units on both the x
and y axes to make a perfect square plot). I am also still concerned that the EIS (and by implication also the IMVS) may have
been misinterpreted. The author response that 'However, if it (the low frequency arc) were related with ion or defect migration
within the perovskite layer, a decrease in arc diameter is expected since ion migration is most severe near open-circuit
conditions' is not correct for several reasons. Firstly ion migration is not most severe near open circuit (which is close to the
flat ion/flat band potential). The impact of ion migration on recombination may indeed be largest close to open circuit, but this
is not what the authors argue, they talk about field screening, which is largest close to short circuit not open circuit as stated
in the response. Further to this the impact of ions may be to either increase or to decrease the recombination resistance
close to open circuit – it depends on the specific cell and there is no clear link between the size of the arc and the
interpretation of whether it is caused by ions or charge transfer as argued by the authors. In my own experience, for many
cells ions reduce recombination (and hence increase recombination resistance) at open circuit. You would also expect a
voltage dependence of ion modulated recombination as changing the voltage changes the Fermi-level position and the
amount of recombination. I think the updated response further highlights the lack of clarity in the interpretation of the EIS,
IMPS and IMVS results. 
I also think that there is also some confusion in the response between steady state and dynamic
measurements and the resulting difference in ion behaviour. It is finally not true that a response between 1Hz and 50Hz is
too fast to be ionic. In my opinion the data interpretation for these techniques has not been improved as there are many
papers in the literature that show ionic responses of perovskite cells at these frequencies. The response on the IMVS and
IMPS is also not strong and I still doubt some of the interpretation.

Comment 4:  
On page 11 line 252, the authors perform TPC analysis to observe charge accumulation. Please 
give more test method details (e.g., bias conditions) in the Method section and give a specific 
explanation of how the charge flows to generate the reverse current. 
 
Response: 
We appreciate the reviewer’s valuable comment in respect to TPC analysis. In response, we 
have revised the Methods section to include more details of the TPC measurement conditions. 
Specifically, TPC was measured under 0.8 VRHE in 1-sun illumination at 0.5 M KPi (pH 7).  
Regarding the mechanism, the observed reverse current originates from the discharge 
of trapped charges when the light is turned off. During illumination, some photogenerated 
carriers are trapped at the perovskite/charge transport layer (CTL) interface. Upon light 
termination, these trapped charges are released and flow in the opposite direction relative to 
the photocurrent, which manifests as the reverse current in the TPC signal. For control sample, 
severe charge accumulation at the catalyst/electrolyte interface interfered the charge separation 
and transport inside the device, leading to enhanced charge trapping at the perovskite/CTL 
interface. As a result, a pronounced reverse current was observed when the light was turned 
off. In contrast, for the NIR-sample, the improved catalyst kinetics reduced charge 
accumulation at the catalyst/electrolyte interface, which in turn mitigated charge trapping at 
the perovskite/CTL boundary. Consequently, the magnitude of the reverse current was

markedly diminished or nearly absent. We added this demonstration to our revised manuscript 
to help the understanding of TPC results.

Comment 5:  
On page 15 line 368, stability tests under galvanostatic conditions were performed. Since 
charge carrier and ion distribution are related to bias conditions, will the different voltages 
influence the stability? 
 
Response: 
In this study, all galvanostatic stability tests were conducted exclusively on FTO/CC/Pt catalyst 
electrodes. This approach was adopted to avoid reliability issues in bubble dynamics 
observation, which could arise if potentiostatic conditions were applied, since varying current 
densities between the control and NIR samples would result in differences in bubble number 
and size. For typical perovskite-based water splitting cathodes, stability was assessed only 
under potentiostatic conditions. Nevertheless, even under galvanostatic operation, the overall 
stability trend is expected to remain consistent. Specifically, in order to maintain the same

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 15 -->

11 
 
current density, the control sample would require a more severe bias due to its lower kinetics, 
which would in turn accelerate ion migration. This inference can be supported by the J–V curve 
evolution shown in Fig. 3a, b, where the control sample exhibits pronounced decreases in both 
Von and Jph over time. Consequently, even if a constant current density were applied, the 
corresponding potential variation in the control sample would be expected to be substantially 
larger compared to NIR sample.

represents. Because our device uses a perovskite/spiro/Au stack, a positive LF arc attributable 
to recombination/ion migration in the bulk or at perovskite interfaces can indeed appear, as the 
reviewer notes. To disentangle contributions observed at the cathode, we fabricated two devices 
separating the components: (i) an encapsulated photovoltaic (PV) stack electrically contacted 
with a Cu wire at the catalyst position but without the catalyst itself, and (ii) a catalyst electrode 
(FTO/CC/Pt-C). For the encapsulated PV stack, the LF arc emerges and increases as the bias 
moves from open circuit toward short-circuit (Fig. R3b). Bode analysis shows this resistance 
appears around ~1–10 Hz and its characteristic frequency shifts to lower values as the bias

Supplementary Note 8: Low frequency arc of perovskite-based cathode 
The key question is what resistance the low-frequency (LF) arc represents in the perovskite-
based electrochemical devices. Because the device uses a perovskite/spiro/Au stack, a positive 
LF arc attributable to recombination/ion migration in the bulk or at perovskite interfaces can 
indeed appear. To disentangle contributions observed at the cathode, we fabricated two devices 
separating the components: (i) an encapsulated PV stack electrically contacted with a Cu wire 
at the catalyst position but without the catalyst itself, and (ii) a catalyst electrode (FTO/CC/Pt-
C). For the encapsulated PV stack, the LF arc emerges and increases as the bias moves from 
open circuit toward short-circuit (Supplementary Note Fig. 5b). Bode analysis shows this 
resistance appears around ~1–10 Hz and its characteristic frequency shifts to lower values as 
the bias approaches short-circuit (Supplementary Note Fig. 5c). These features are consistent 
with the Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination resistance originating from the bulk 
and/or interfaces, as reported in prior literatureS51. For the isolated catalyst electrode, an LF arc 
is also observed; however, its magnitude decreases as the potential is swept negative from the 
onset voltage, and it is further reduced under NIR illumination (Supplementary Note Fig. 5d, 
e). We attribute this behavior to charge-transfer resistance at the catalyst/electrolyte interface: 
both improved kinetics and mass transport under NIR illumination decrease this resistance. In 
the Bode plots, this LF contribution appears near ~1–10 Hz and shifts to higher characteristic 
frequencies as the potential approaches onset and the peak frequency increases further to ~10–
50 Hz with NIR illumination (Supplementary Note Fig. 5f). These control experiments 
demonstrate that (i) SRH-related and charge-transfer resistances can occupy similar frequency 
windows, and (ii) the LF-arc trend at the working cathode mirrors the charge-transfer behavior 
of the catalyst electrode but is opposite to the SRH recombination-related trend of the 
encapsulated PV . Consequently, interpreting the cathode LF arc as charge-transfer resistance is 
more consistent with the observed bias and illumination dependences. However, we need to 
explain why the SRH-related LF resistance observed in the encapsulated PV structure does not 
appear at the cathode. 
This interpretation aligns with prior PEC-EIS analyses, which recommend bias-
dependent assignments of the LF arcS52. Near the onset voltage, charge transfer is typically the 
dominant bottleneck and thus the most likely origin of the LF arc. But at higher overpotentials 
where the photocurrent is large or the catalyst’s onset potential is exceeded, recombination-
related impedances can become dominant in the LF domain. Our voltage sweeps were
