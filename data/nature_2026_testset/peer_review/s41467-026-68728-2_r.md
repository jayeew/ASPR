Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the submitted manuscript, the authors studied vertical chiral emission from dielectric metasurfaces that preserve out-of-
plane (sigma_z) symmetry. They conducted very detailed theoretical studies on how the polarization state of radiation from
an out-of-plane symmetric dielectric metasurface is influenced by anisotropic non-resonant background scattering (called
scattering by 'anisotropic continuum' in the current manuscript). The authors also verified their theoretical studies by
measuring chiral emission from the emitter-coated dielectric metasurface and reported very good experimental results for
vertical chiral emission. The current study surely enhances a fundamental understanding of planar chiral structures and
provides novel insights into chiral metasurface design. The manuscript is well written, and I think that the current study could
be of great interest to the relevant research communities. 
However, I see several important issues in the current manuscript. There have been several relevant studies in literature.
And the merits of the out-of-plane symmetric metasurfaces for chiral emission are still not clear. Specifically, I'd like to ask
the authors to clarify the following issues: 
1) It was previously noted that the polarization state of radiation from a dielectric metasurface (or photonic crystal slab) is
strongly constrained by the direct non-resonant scattering (or continuum scattering) process. For example, see 
- arXiv:1708.02197 (2017), "Polarization state of radiation from a photonic crystal slab" 
The discussions in the current manuscript seem closely related to this previous study. This article also commented that,
when the direct-scattering (or continuum-scattering) coefficients are the same for the two orthogonal polarization
components, the radiation from the dielectric slab should be linearly polarized. 
As another example, the following paper studied how to achieve maximal planar chirality using dielectric metasurfaces. This
article also noted that no chiral response can be obtained if the effective background only has an isotropic response (Section
II in Supporting Information). 
- Nat. Comm. (2022) 13:4111, "Planar chiral metasurfaces with maximal and tunable chiroptical response driven by bound
states in the continuum" 
Therefore, I think the authors should clarify the advancement and difference of the current manuscript compared to the
previous studies in literature. 
2) Terminology issue: The title includes the term 'non-chiral'. I suppose the authors wanted to stress that the current study
considered a more ideal case for planar chiral metasurfaces that preserved out-of-plane symmetry while previous studies
mostly considered structures that still involved out-of-plane symmetry breaking. However, the current study also used a
chiral geometry (non-zero alpha in Figure 1a) that breaks in-plane mirror symmetry, and this type of structure has been
commonly called planar 'chiral' structure in literature. Therefore, the use of the term 'non-chiral' could be very confusing. It
would be great if the authors could come up with a different term. 
3) In the current manuscript, the authors demonstrated vertical chiral emission from dielectric metasurfaces that preserve out-
of-plane symmetry. However, similar chiral emission in the vertical direction is also expected from planar chiral metasurfaces
on a substrate that break the out-plane-symmetry [e.g., when emitters are coated on the planar dielectric structures in the
article above, Nat. Comm. (2022) 13:4111]. Do out-of-plane symmetric metasurfaces have 'practical merits' for chiral
emission compared to other planar chiral structures? 
4) In Figure 4, the authors considered asymmetric parameter ‘alpha’ up to 0.09. The chiral response (degree of circular

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

polarization, DCP) gradually increased up to this point. Then, what happens when the alpha increases further beyond 0.09?
And what is the optimal value of alpha in the current design? 
5) Effect of absorption losses: In the current manuscript, I think lossless dielectric structures are assumed. But light emission
from emitters (either from dye molecules or from semiconductor materials) implies that absorption should also exist at the
same wavelength. Therefore, emitter-integrated dielectric metasurfaces should have some absorption losses at the emission
wavelength. How do the absorption losses affect the theoretical description of vertical chiral emission in the current
manuscript? Is anisotropic continuum still required for vertical chiral emission? For example, according to the current study,
gammadion structures made of lossless dielectric materials cannot be used for vertical chiral emission because they are
rotationally symmetric and do not support anisotropic continuum scattering. However, when emitters are integrated with such
lossless dielectric metasurfaces, the entire system becomes lossy. I am wondering whether vertical chiral emission is still
suppressed in this case (or it can be now achieved without anisotropic continuum). 
In a similar way, I suppose that plasmonic chiral metasurfaces may behave differently for vertical chiral emission due to their
intrinsic absorption losses (in addition to the absorption losses of emitters). I would be thankful if the authors clarify these
issues. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors report on the design and experimental demonstration of a planar metasurface of broken in-plane symmetry,
which hosts specific high quality factor photonic eigenstates emitting circularly polarized waves of opposite handedness in
the vertical up and down directions. They study how such a state arises in arrays of silicon nanobar dimers subjected to
weak symmetry breaking perturbations and attempt to relate the emission chirality with a birefringent background scattering
(here called anisotropic continuum). Such metasurfaces are then fabricated and demonstrate that indeed, upon cladding
with luminescent organic dye, the properly designed samples emit light with the degree of circular polarization (DCP) up to
0.9 in magnitude and of the opposite handedness in the opposite directions. The paper is accompanied by the
Supplementary Information (SI) elaborating proposed theoretical explanations formulated in terms of the temporal coupled-
mode theory (TCMT), describing the details of fabrication and optical experiments. Additional modeling clarifies the role of
material parameters as well as suggests how the proposed approach can be realized using differently shaped meta-atoms. 
The subject of the paper is very timely: high-purity sources of chiral light are desired for many emerging chiroptical
applications. The main simulations and experimental results look solid and support the conclusion that a planar metasurface
can emit circularly polarized light. However, this has already been shown in recent works published by essentially the same
group! 
I cannot recommend publishing in NatComm as I seriously doubt the qualitative novelty of this work. 
My doubts are further strengthened by the fact that the authors avoid citing their own papers, and only briefly mention one in
the SI. 
As a matter of fact, practically identically designed metasurface was used in [R1] to perform exactly the same task and has
reached DNC up to 0.74 of emission of a quantum dot cladding. The particular size parameters are different now to ensure a
somewhat different emission wavelength. However, the metasurface design is identical to that in [R1]. Compared to [R1], the
manuscript contains only more elaborate (and not always well justified) theoretical explanations and additional simulations.
Note also that in another recent Ref. [R2] the authors present a similarly designed trimer metasurface performing chiral
thermal emission. Therefore, I strongly doubt the novelty of the current findings. 
Having stated this, I must recognize the potential importance of the theoretical framework developed in order to explain the
simulated and repeatedly measured properties. As the authors will definitely look for opportunities to publish their work
elsewhere, I formulate a couple of related suggestions and critical remarks: 
1. Simulated (Fig 1b) and measured (Fig. 3) transmission spectra clearly resemble those generally predicted 20 years ago in
Ref. [52] for a pair of non-orthogonal modes of an open lossless resonator. More recently, such modes are termed “coupled
via-the-continuum” [48]. It seems much more reasonable to start with postulating the existence of two modes of the same
parity rather than to presume the existence of strongly birefringent background of unknown origin. Note that I am unaware of
a justified procedure of rewriting TCMT equations in order to include a state into the background. From the general
viewpoint, this may severely violate the energy conservation. Presumably, Section S3 of the SI attempts to describe such a
procedure, but it is hard to comprehend this section due to many undefined notations and possible typos. Please provide a
detailed explanation how a state can be phenomenologically included into the background. Otherwise, one can directly
follow the strategy of Ref. [52] and start with a pair of states. 
2. To clarify the peculiar eigenstate interplay, it might be useful to analyze them in metasurfaces subjected to different
symmetry perturbations as those used to produce Fig. 3. In particular, this could elucidate how the shape perturbations open
the state coupling via the continuum. Note also that the field distributions of the states are now given only on a single tiny
inset in Fig 2a. These can also clarify the states origin, their coupling and the role of symmetry breaking perturbations. 
3, Finally, to prove the TCMT applicability, one usually derives the model expressions for the transmission and reflection
amplitudes and shows how well they fit the simulated transmission and reflection spectra. Otherwise, the usefulness of

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In my first report, I recognized the timeliness of the topic addressed in the paper but expressed serious doubts regarding the
fundamental novelty of the reported results. My major concern was that the authors have already presented the very same
effect – chiral emission of light – performed by practically identically designed silicon metasurface in their recent paper. In
the original manuscript they managed to fully avoid mentioning this paper. Now it is cited as [12]. In addition, I formulated a
couple of suggestions regarding the theoretical background and presentation in case the authors decide to publish the paper
elsewhere. 
In response, the authors write: “The apparent similarity in metasurface geometry is intentional and crucial to the purpose of
this study. In our previous work, the metasurface was placed on a dielectric substrate that breaks out-of-plane mirror
symmetry and belongs to different classifications as our current work. To isolate the impact of out-of-plane symmetry, we
intentionally retained the same in-plane geometry” and also “We have no intention of repeating prior work. On the contrary,
our aim is to highlight the conceptual contrast by keeping geometry constant and varying only the symmetry condition” 
These rather strong statements, however, are not backed in the paper. Instead of resolving the issue, they raise even more
serious concerns: 
1. If the similarity to the previous work was intentional, why was it not stated anywhere in the text, and why was the relevant
paper not mentioned and cited? Where in the latest version can we read about the physical consequences of the
“conceptual contrast” between the previous and current metasurfaces? 
2. If the presence of dielectric substrate was so crucial in the previous work, why did the authors write there that “The out-of-
plane symmetry is nearly preserved in the current structure since the side wall of the silicon pillars is vertical and the
symmetry breaking induced by the silica substrate is weak.” (see page 2 in Ref. 12). Note that this is a very reasonable
statement. Typically, weakly refracting substrates marginally affect optical properties of silicon metasurfaces: one should
really search for very specific situations, when a substrate plays a noticeable role. 
3. Is there any scientific evidence of a qualitative difference in the performance of metasurfaces from the present and
previous works? 
4. If the out-of-plane symmetry is so crucial, to which extent should one preserve it for the reported effects to take place?
Note that the latest metasurface is neither fully symmetrically fabricated. This is seen in Supplementary Fig. S9 subplot 9, as
well as it is manifested by different emission spectra from both sides given in Fig. 4e). 
In response to my other comments, the authors extended the discussion of how the birefringent background scattering (they
call it anisotropic continuum) can be related to other low-quality-factor resonances. They reasonably note that there can be
several resonances contributing here. However, then in SI they attempt to formulate a temporal coupled mode theory
(TCMT) describing how the birefringent background can arise due to a single broad resonant mode. I expressed doubts that
this is possible, and now would like to warn the authors that their newly added statements are most likely erroneous. Such a
procedure expectedly violates the time reversal and energy conservation. This is now explicitly shown by the authors
themselves as they arrive at the necessity of Supplementary Eq. (22), which requires the equality of frequency omega to the
eigenfrequency omega_c. This particularly means that the time reversal is granted for monochromatic fields of one specific
frequency. Of course, the fundamental physical laws must be valid in the whole frequency range where TCMT is considered
applicable.
