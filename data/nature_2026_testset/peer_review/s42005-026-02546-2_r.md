Response to Reviewer #1’s comments  
 
The manuscript “Non-Hermitian skin effect in arbitrary dimensions: non -Bloch bands, geometry-
dependence, and critical skin modes ” by Ze-Yu Xing, Yuncheng Xiong and Haiping Hu presents a 
geometry-adaptive Amoeba approach which can unveil the localization of normal skin modes on 
different lattice shapes in higher dimensions. By updating the momentum basis in 2D from the square 
to the rhombus shape, their reformulation can also faithfully capture the changes in bulk spectra of 
these normal modes in the thermodynamical limit. On the other hand, from exact diagonalization, 
it is shown that critical skin modes serve as spectral instabilities in both the original and their 
geometry-adaptive Amoeba theories. 
 
[Reply] We thank the reviewer for the careful review of our work. In the following, we address the 
questions/comments raised by the reviewer in detail.   
 
Although these results carry useful information to the community, we view the current work as a 
minimal extension of the original Amoeba formulation in Ref.  [54] and it cannot be applied to a 
wide variety of complex scenarios giving rise to unconventional NHSE in arbitrary dimensions (even 
in 1D). Our perspectives are as follows: 
 
1. The theoretical tool, spectral potential in Eq.(2), has been proposed much earlier in [Sci. Bull. 
67, 1865, 2022] where it is used to design parent Hamiltonians from bulk real spectra. In current 
work, spectral potential plays almost the same role as in the original formulation of Ref.[54], 
apart from a momentum basis change adapted to a different lattice shape. The conjecture Eq.(14), 
is not surprising as well. Ref.[54] proves that the original Amoeba theory gives the boundary of 
universal bulk spectra, indicating it is a union of non-Bloch spectra across all lattice geometries. 
In our eyes, the main methods and some of conclusions in current work are already present in 
Ref.[54] and cannot be taken as new. 
 
[Reply] We thank the reviewer for pointing out the earlier use of spectral -potential (electrostatic) 
ideas in Sci. Bull. 67, 1865 (2022). We now cite this work  as Ref. [59]. The electrostatic-analogy 
method itself is a general framework based on logarithmic potential theory in the complex plane 
[Surveys in Approximation Theory 5, 165–200 (2010)]. Both Ref. [54] and the present work employ 
this analogy: the energy spectrum is obtained from the corresponding potential function via the 
Poisson equation. Within this common framework, the central task lies in determining the spectral 
potential in the thermod ynamic limit, which becomes particularly challenging for higher-
dimensional non-Hermitian systems. 
 
Next, we clarify how our work differs from Ref. [54] in its scope, formulation, and main results, as 
summarized below.

(1) Different scope  of study. Ref. [54] develops the Amoeba/Ronkin framework of non -Bloch 
bands. This formulation is geometry-agnostic, and the resulting non -Bloch spectrum is 
geometry-irrelevant. In contrast, our work focuses on the geometry-specified non-Bloch 
spectrum and the corresponding NH SE. The formulation is developed for a definite lattice 
geometry (e.g., square or rhombus in 2D), and analyses of different geometries are carried out 
using momentum bases defined by the lattice cuts. 
 
(2) Different formulation of the potential function. As mentioned earlier, the key to obtaining the 
non-Bloch bands lies in determining the spectral potential in the thermodynamic limit. In Ref. 
[54], the Amoeba formulation extends the 1D generalized -Brillouin-zone condition directly to 
the amoeba manifold by e xamining whether it has a central hole. The potential function  
Φ𝐴𝑚𝑜𝑒𝑏𝑎(𝐸) is then inferred inside the spectrum based on a minimization principle. However, 
in higher-dimensional systems, both the spectrum and the NHSE are highly sensitive to the 
system’s geometry. To obtain the non -Bloch spectra for different geometries,  geometric 
information must therefore be included as an essential input. In our formulation, geometry is 
specified through the momentum basis, which is defined by the lattice cuts of the given shape. 
Starting from the 1D potential function, we derive the higher-dimensional potential function Φ(E) 
hierarchically. Φ(𝐸) depends explicitly on geometry and i n general , Φ(𝐸) ≠ 𝚽𝑨𝒎𝒐𝒆𝒃𝒂(𝑬) . 
Furthermore, we prove that our potential function always satisfies Φ(𝐸) ≤ ΦAmoeba(𝐸), which 
leads to the theorem that the non -Bloch spectrum for a specified geometry is contained within 
the Amoeba spectrum [Eq. (13) in the main text]. It is worth mentioning that we have provided 
an exact solvable example [see Fig. S 2 in Appendix S4], where only our geometry -adaptive 
potential reproduces the exact non-Bloch spectrum, while the Amoeba formulation does not. 
 
(3) Difference in the main results. The development of non-Bloch theory in higher dimensions has 
been one of the central focuses and challenges in non-Hermitian physics over the past few years. 
Compared to the well -established 1D case, the complexity arises from  the diversity of lattice 
geometries and the variety of skin modes. Ref. [54] proposed the Amoeba formulation, whi ch 
represents the earliest theoretical attempt to generalize 1D non-Bloch theory. However, the two 
essential aspects (geometry dependence of non-Bloch spectra; the specific manifestations of the 
NHSE) were not addressed there. Our manuscript pertains to the non-Bloch spectra and NHSE 
for specific geometries . The main results include: (A) We develop a geometry -adaptive 
framework for non -Bloch spectra and NHSE in higher dimensions, which enables a precise 
determination of the non -Bloch spectra and skin modes for a specified geometry, validated by 
concrete models [Figs. 2–3 in the main text and Fig. S 2 in the Appendix S4]. (B) We establish 
an exact spectral relation [the theorem in Eq. (13) of the main text], which explicitly shows the 
geometry dependence of non-Bloch spectra and clarifies their relation and distinction from the 
Amoeba spectra. (C) We identify the presence of critical skin modes and show that they lead to 
spectral non-convergence and instability. Together, these results form a coherent framework 
for understanding geometry-dependent non-Bloch bands, NHSE and their stability. 
 
Therefore, o ur study differs fundamentally from Ref. [54] . We respectfully disagree with the 
reviewer’s opinion that “the current work is a minimal extension of the original Amoeba formulation 
in Ref. [54]. ” In addition, the electrostatic -analogy methods  and the  conjecture in Eq. (14),  as

2. As a minimal extension, the current formulation naturally inherits the limitations of the original 
Amoeba theory. For instance, it reproduces bulk spectra of normal skin modes and fails in the

[Reply] The reviewer ’s question concerns the limitations of our formulation  in the presence of 
critical skin modes. We agree that critical skin modes fall outside the scope of both the Amoeba 
formulation and our own. Both approaches yield definite energy spectra in the thermodynamic limit: 
the Amoeba formulation gives a geometry -irrelevant “universal” spectrum, while ours gives a 
geometry-specified one. 
 
We highlight that our formulation applies whenever the energy spectra converge. For the critical 
skin modes, their localization length increases with system size (in our data, it scales linearly with 
the boundary length ; see Fig. 4(b)). As the system grows, these modes broaden, and the energy 
spectrum becomes highly sensitive to the system size and to geometric details , e.g., the boundary 
ratio (see Fig. 4(d)). This leads to non-convergence or indefiniteness of the non-Bloch spectrum in 
the thermodynamic limit when only the lattice-cut directions are fixed. Such critical states also cause 
spectral instability, making the spectrum extremely sensitive to small perturbations (see Figs. 4(e)(f) 
and Fig. S 7 in Appendix S6). For the  critical case, it remains an open question in the community 
whether a non-Bloch description and a generalized Brillouin zone can be consistently defined. Our 
physical standpoint is pragmatic: as long as a  theory faithfully captures the converged and stable 
cases, it is OK ! We regard th is failure not as a limitation of the formulation  itself, but as a 
breakdown of the non-Bloch framework when the spectral limit ceases to exist.

2. The proposed method reveals the geometry-dependent GBZ for regular geometric shapes through 
a basis transformation. How would this formulation extend to irregular geometries, such as 
polygons with nonparallel edges or circular boundaries? 
 
[Reply] This is a very insightful and challenging question. Our current formulation focuses on 
regular geometries (e.g., parallelograms or parallelepipeds in 2D and 3D). In a dD regular geometry, 
there are exactly d lattice cuts, and each cut direction is specified by a lattice momentum 𝑘𝑗. These 
momenta are analytically continued as 𝑘𝑗 → 𝛽𝑗  to obtain the potential  function, where  |𝛽𝑗|  
determines the localization length of the skin modes along the 𝑗-th lattice cut.  
 
For irregular geometries (such as polygons with nonparallel edges or circular boundaries), the 
situation becomes more complex, since the number of lattice -cut directions exceeds the spatial 
dimension. [A circular boundary can be viewed as having infinitely many cut directions.] This leads 
to a rich coexistence of skin modes, possibly localized at multiple corners or along curved 
boundaries. In Appendix S5 (last paragraph), we discuss a promising strategy for treating such cases. 
One may decompose an irregular geometry into a set of regular shapes [see the orange 
parallelograms in Fig. R1 below]. The non -Bloch spectra should have co ntributions from all these 
subsystems, each governed by our theory for regular geometries. We expect the potential function 
to be a certain superposition of the potentials from each subregion, following an appropriate 
minimization principle. We plan to explore these more complex geometries in future work.

4. The geometry-dependent GBZ seems to contradict the conclusions of Ref. 53? Could the authors 
clarify whether the two approaches describe distinct limits of the non-Bloch spectra? 
 
[Reply] We have carefully studied Ref. [53] and discussed it with its authors. The “GBZ” defined 
in that work corresponds to a manifold constructed for each specific energy 𝑬𝟎  [see Eq. (6) 
therein]. For every point inside the OBC spectrum, one obtain an energy -dependent “GBZ.” This 
concept differs from the usual GBZ  used in Ref. [54] (the Amoeba theory) and ours, where the 
GBZ is a complex manifold composed of (𝛽1, 𝛽2) (for 2D systems), whose image under the Bloch 
Hamiltonian gives the entire non-Bloch spectrum.  
 
The geometry dependence of non-Bloch spectra and the NHSE has been under active debate in the 
recent literature (e.g., Refs. [53–58]). In Ref. [53], the authors mention in the Introduction that “the 
shape of OBC geometry can significantly affect the NHSE in two and higher dimensions … This 
suggests that the 2D GBZ theory, if it exists, will be highly dependent on the choice of OBC geometry.” 
However, later in Eqs. (11)–(12) therein, they a priori assume geometry-independent OBC spectral 
bounds. The challenge in resolving this issue arises from (1) numerical difficulties for large non-
Hermitian matrices and (2) the vast variety of possible geometries. 
 
One of our m ain results is to clarify precisely this point. The theorem (Eq. (13) in the main text)  
explicitly shows that the non -Bloch spectra (for a given geometry) depend on geometry and differ  
from the Amoeba spectra. Consequently, the associated GBZ that reproduces these spectra must also 
be geometry dependent. In our formulation, geometric information is included as an input . As long 
as spectral convergence holds, we obtain the genuine non-Bloch spectrum. Our potential function is 
rigorously constructed through a hierarchical derivation and verified by (i) finite -size analysis 
[Figs. 2(d1,d2)], (ii) exactly solvable benchmark models [Appendix S4, Fig. S1(b)], and (iii) direct 
comparison with the Amoeba theory [Fig. S2(c)].  
 
In contrast, Ref. [53] focuses mainly on the construction of “GBZ” and does not address the spectral

the study of high -dimensional NHSE remain s an ongoing and rapidly evolving topic in the

5. From an experimental perspective, perturbations and imperfections are inevitable. What physical 
insights can we gain from the spectral potential or density of states when the energy spectra are 
sensitive to boundary ratios, as shown in Fig.4? 
 
[Reply] Normal skin modes are stable against changes in the boundary ratio as well as perturbations 
or imperfections. In contrast, the sensitivity of the non -Bloch spectra to boundary ratios and 
imperfections is an intrinsic feature of systems hosting critical skin modes  [see Fig. 4 and 
Appendix S 6]. This reflects the non -local nature of non -Hermitian couplings, where boundary 
conditions can strongly influence bulk eigenmodes. The spectral potential or density of states (DOS) 
describes how eigenenergies are distributed in the complex-energy plane. When the boundary ratio 
varies, the deformation of the potential captures the redistribution of eigenmodes among different 
edges. This reveals a geometry -driven spectral flow and signals the breakdown of spectral 
convergence. Experimentally, such effects may appear as sudden switching of localization between 
edges or pronounced geometry -dependent changes in eigenmode intensity, providin g clear 
signatures of critical skin modes and their sensitivity to boundary geometry and perturbations.
