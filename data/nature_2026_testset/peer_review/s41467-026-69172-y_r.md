Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is an interesting, innovative, and well-written article that is conceptually rigorous, as one has come to expect from the
Bonomi group. I am not sure I’m 100% convinced bAles ensembles necessarily do a fantastic job at capturing the overall
ensembles of IDRs, but I am very much convinced the conceptual approach is rigorous, and that for local secondary
structure, it does well (something not well captured by other extant models). Overall, I am highly supportive of the
publication, although I do have a number of comments. 
Major comments 
One minor concern I have is that ensembles lacking secondary structure elements may still deviate from their experimental
behavior. For Abeta prior work has estimated the Rg to be ~1.5 nm [1]; it would I think, be useful to ask if the ensemble here
matches that. By eye, the predicted ensembles are substantially larger than this. 
More generally, there are quite a few IDRs that lack any clear secondary structure bias, yet still posses long-range
interactions (e.g. Sic1 as characterized extensively by the Forman-Kay group, or hnRNPA1 variants charaterized by the
Mittag group) [2,3] - I think if the authors want to make the point that ensembles that lack structural biases are well
recapitulated, it would be important to illustrate this for sequences where there are strong biases away from random-coil like
global dimensions. 
The agreement with the synuclein SAXS data and the bAles derived scattering does not seem super close - it’s a little hard
to see, but in the q<0.1 region, especially, deviations are a bit concerning. It would be useful to quantify this deviation and
also perhaps show the distribution of global dimensions with the experimental Rg superimposed. 
It would be important to offer a comparison of performance for ensemble generation - the authors make claims regarding
throughput, but I would like to see performance comparisons (structures/second) for different methods compared. 
What is the structural diversity of conformers generated? Does the method yield only a small number of unique
conformations? Quantifying this in some way is an important analysis to include. 
The code sharing is excellent - please ensure all ensembles for all the methods presented here are shared in a Zenodo
repository for easy future analysis and comparisons 
Minor comments 
The use of excluded volume random coil models for IDP ensemble generation (as a reference state) via a modified atomistic
forcefield has been used quite extensively previously (which is not a problem at all, obviously!) but just for clarity it might be
useful to compare this back to the so-called “excluded volume” (EV) model used by the Pappu lab (described here [4]) just to
clarify if this is the same idea or if there are meaningful differences. 
In figure 4, there is not obvious explanation of what the RMSE in 4a is actually referring to - this figure should be further
expanded to clarify what’s being compared here. Also, how are NMR observables being calculated from a one-bead per
residue CG model in the case of CALVADOS? This should be explicitly mentioned in the results so these can be
interpreted. 
I greatly appreciated the author's comparison with the metainference work - this was useful and strengthens the paper
overall. 
I also appreciate the measured, non-overstating, and careful language used throughout the manuscript - this was very nice
to see and reflects the rigorous science being presented.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
We are very grateful to Nature Communications for the opportunity to evaluate the manuscript by Schnapka et al. We believe
that the topic of generating biologically relevant protein ensembles for intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) is highly
important, as it benefits both experimental and computational structural biology. In particular, we find the idea of combining a
machine learning approach (AlphaFold) with a physics-based method (random coil force field) very compelling. The two
approaches complement each other: AlphaFold represents the state-of-the-art for globular domains, providing stable
conformations at a fraction of the computational cost of all-atom simulations, whereas its limitations in capturing the
functional aspects of IDRs are mitigated by a force-field model capable of exploring conformational heterogeneity. The
integration of the two approaches through Bayesian theory, together with carefully chosen priors and correction strategies, is
a clever way to address both the single-state bias of AlphaFold and the approximations inherent in physics-based models. 
Overall, the manuscript presents sufficient material to explain and support the quality of the methodology. However, we have
a number of recommendations that could significantly improve the clarity, rigor, and impact of the work, as detailed below. 
We also wish to emphasize that one of the key strengths of the method lies in its ability to provide ensembles of comparable
quality to atomistic molecular dynamics simulations at a fraction of their computational cost, as stated in the abstract. Yet this
claim is not quantified. For readers unfamiliar with MD simulations or specialized HPC platforms such as Anton, providing
such an analysis - together with estimates comparing CPU and GPU performance - would be extremely valuable. 
Methodology
 
The authors introduce a new random coil model based on a modified atomistic force field. While the justification provided—
that the model generates diverse conformations suitable as priors in the Bayesian formulation—is reasonable, it remains
unclear how this compares with existing (and potentially faster) approaches such as FM, RanCH, AfFlecto, or
IDPConformerGenerator. Although benchmarking random coil generators may be outside the main scope of the paper, even
a simple comparison would clarify why a new model was designed rather than adopting established techniques. 
The authors convincingly demonstrate that bAIes captures flexibility where AlphaFold overpredicts structure, as in the N-
terminal region of α-synuclein, and that it can correct AlphaFold errors. They also describe the example of PaaA2, where two
helices are correctly predicted due to AlphaFold’s high-confidence pLDDT scores. However, the manuscript lacks a case
where bAIes identifies motifs within IDPs that AlphaFold fails to capture. Is it possible that the random coil model alone
could reveal such motifs? If not, the authors should clarify that bAIes inherits AlphaFold’s limitations in this respect. 
The criteria for filtering distograms - specifically sequence separation and distance cutoffs - should also be discussed in
more depth. While the rationale for sequence separation may relate to excluding helical hydrogen bonds, the choice of
distance cutoff is less obvious and requires further explanation. 
Method description
 
The section describing the theoretical framework of bAIes requires substantial clarification. In particular, the relationship
between equations (6) and (7), as well as the role of the simplified noise model (bAIes-N) in equation (11), is unclear. How is
EAF2(X) calculated in equation (11)? We did not find a reference for this step. A more structured and less jargon-heavy
explanation would help readers follow the Bayesian reasoning without losing the thread amid technical details. One
possible approach is to first explain, in simple terms, how AlphaFold and the random coil model are combined, and only
then introduce details on marginalization and noise model simplifications. 
The description of distograms is also too brief. For example, in Figure 1D it is not clear whether the plots represent

distributions of inter-residue distances (Cb-Cb) for residue-type pairs (ALA–ALA, ALA–VAL, etc.), as in statistical potentials,
or for specific sequence positions (i, j). Adding labels or variables to panels C and D would greatly improve interpretability. 
Finally, the authors should explain how AlphaFold distograms are obtained, as they are not available in AlphaFoldDB nor in
the default ColabFold outputs. 
Comparison with other methods
 
The use of RMSE as the main evaluation metric is straightforward and acceptable. However, for SAXS comparisons, the
reduced χ² would provide a more informative measure of fit. Reporting distributions of Rg and secondary structure propensity
(ex. helix/beta content per residue in the ensemble) 
would also offer intuitive insight into ensemble properties. The authors
might also consider X-EISD score to show the goodness of fit (doi:10.1038/s42004-020-0323-0). 
For multi-domain proteins, benchmarking bAIes against the previous CASP ensemble challenge (domain-linker-domain
targets) would be extremely valuable, as discussed in the recent preprint (doi:10.1101/2025.04.27.650885). 
Although the manuscript discusses differences between AlphaFold metainference and bAIes, no direct comparison is
shown. Including an example would make the contrast more concrete and informative. 
Figures
 
Figure 1 (especially panel D) - Clarify what is shown. Do the Cb pairs represent all residue-type combinations (20 × 19 / 2)
or all residue pairs in the sequence (N × (N – 1) / 2)? 
Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure 6 - Experimental error bars are not visible; consider adding transparency to overlapping
lines. 
Supplementary Figure 3 - Red dots are missing (only blue markers are shown).

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present bAIes, an elegant Bayesian framework for generating conformational ensembles for intrinsically

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

disordered proteins (IDPs). bAIes relies on a random coil prior and integrates predicted structural information in the form of
distograms derived from AlphaFold2 models. The question at hand is very significant and timely, as recent progress in
protein structure prediction has not yet been able to fully uncover conformational landscapes. The method is tested on a
benchmark dataset of 21 proteins with different degrees of disorder and most of which have available experimental data. It is
compared to classical all-atom MD simulations, as well as two other recent methods, CALVADOS2 and BioEmu. The
random coil model also serves as a reference. The authors discuss that bAIes is robust to AF2 errors (thanks to modeling
errors explicitly), and that integrating experimental data in bAIes (or other methods) through reweighting improves agreement
with experimental data, as could be expected. Finally, they test bAIes for its ability to sample linkers between domains in
multi-domain proteins. The discussion is honest and provides interesting perspectives to this work. 
The manuscript is clearly written and easy to follow. The methods are detailed and clear. The demonstration of bAIes
accuracy is convincing. 
Only a few points (listed below) remain to be clarified or explained. 
In results section "introducing structural information from AlphaFold" and discussion: I do not think AF2 structural information
can be summed up through its distograms only. Please amend the text of results and discussion accordingly. 
Comparison with all-atom MD simulations: although the authors state in the abstract and introduction that their method is
much more computationally efficient, it would be helpful to show by how much. Can the authors show the compared cost
(runtime, on which setup) of both approaches? 
Similarly, comparison of the computational cost of bAIes vs. CALVADOS2 and BioEmu would be very informative. 
These data are important to guide users in choosing the best available method/compromise for their purpose. 
Ensembles of multi-domain proteins and agreement with SAXS data: The authors state that "The agreement with
experimental SAXS profiles, while not perfect, exceeded our expectations". What were these expectations? In other words,
can the authors quantify how much better their method is, in terms of agreement with SAXS data, compared to a
baseline/reference (that needs to be defined)? 
Methods 
AlphaFold: Why choose the model with highest global pLDDT score? Is it most relevant to use a single model? 
Random coil model: Are residues in coil regions from PDB structures behaving similarly to residues in IDPs/IDRs? 
The authors state in their discussion that the ability of AF2 to learn IDP/IDR features despite being trained on folded proteins
could stem from training on coil regions. Here, they use coil regions as a dataset for parameterizing their random coil model. 
Modeling uncertainty in AF2 predictions: could confidence metrics provided by AF2, such as pLDDT (or maybe other metrics
such as the pairwise PAE scores), be taken into account in the model? Not as strong restraints, as mentioned in the
discussion regarding Metainference, but as a modulating factor? 
Minor comments 
Beginning of results: please add the number of benchmark proteins featuring each type of data 
Methods - Maximum Entropy reweighting: I think it should be "one for each experimental data type" instead of "once..." 
(Remarks on code availability) 
Unfortunately, I did not have enough time and resources to read the code in detail or to install and run the code, given the
software requirements and dependencies. However, I appreciate the fact that this code is integrated in a well-supported
framework (Plumed). Additionally, I was able to review the associated documentation, which provides a clear description of
the way to install and run the code and a detailed tutorial.
