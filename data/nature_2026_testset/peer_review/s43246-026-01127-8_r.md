Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This is a review of the paper “ Colossal modulation of the phase transition dynamics via solid state gating” 
In the article the authors measure a thin 3 terminal device (7 nm) of the metal insulator transition VO2 which is gated by a
Schottky barrier of the doped TiO2 substrate, which was detailed in their previous paper (ref. 15). In this study they focus on
the time scale of the gate and temperature mediated transition and find the results can fit a thermodynamic model with an
activation barrier that is controlled by both temperature and VO2 doping (that is modified by the gate voltage). The final
conclusion is that these types of materials with collective behavior can be switched fast and with low energy, which is known
and was demonstrated in other papers. 
The main idea and the thermodynamic model is nice and reasonable. However I find their analysis methodology not
stringent enough, where unintended human dependent analysis can strongly effect the results. But more important, there is
one part of their experiment that I need them to explain, since it does not make sense to me. Below I elaborate on this issue,
and what are the other problematic parts. This version is not adequate for publication, but a majorly revised version where
the critique was addressed may be viable. 
Main issue 1 
In the measurement for the metal to insulator transition (MIT) the authors start at high temperature and 5V gate, then cool the
sample to a temperature ABOVE the MIT, e.g. 316.6, then they change the gate to some voltage below 5V to Vg-down. If I
understand correctly, and looking also at ref 15 and 16, it seems that if one would measure the transition at Vg=0 then at this
temperature the sample should be metallic. Thus switching Vg-down to 0V should not change the state of the system. They
actually need to go to temperatures slightly below the T_MIT, where the reason the system is still metallic is due to the gate
voltage, and then changing the gate should have an effect. 
Is this a simple misspoken on their behalf, and they meant the temperature is below the T_MIT? If this is not the case then it
indicates the reason for the transition is something different. 
Main issue 2 
The methodology of defining the current slope and the time delay is problematic. Looking at Fig 2c,e the choice of the slope
is almost arbitrary. Even in the example in figure 2e I could choose the slope to be higher, which would cause the delay time
to be shorter. Looking at fig 3a,b, I don’t understand how they define a linear slope for these lines. Additionally, in 3b, it
seems the initial slope is very similar. 
The situation is like charging a capacitor using a different voltage and then saying the charging time is different due to the
initial slope. Of course they have the same time scale for the exponential charging, but the initial slope will be different. 
Regarding the delay time, there is an assumption that the delay is for the case that the sample will reach the final state. But
this is clearly not the case, so the dynamics of the system are different then what they claim. 
To conclude, I don’t see how this analysis procedure can claim to evaluate a system having a phase transition from one
state to the other and just overcoming a potential barrier, when the results do not appear as if this is the case. 
Additional issues 
In the introduction. The effect of gate on ion motion in these oxides was shown to cause effects in 3 terminal devices. For
example Science 339, 1402 (2013) and PR APPLIED 19, 034057 (2023), both in VO2. For completion of review, these
citations should be added, and compared to this case where the gate effect seems to have an electronic component. Maybe
also theorize why this case is different. 
When the authors choose different initial temperatures they also change the initial Vg 
so that the initial current was the same.
I don’t understand why this is done. It seems that they regard this is bringing the system to the same thermodynamic state,
but this is not well explained. It would be also interesting to see the effect from other base voltages. Also, it is important to
compare the final effect of the final voltage and the dependence of the initial voltage. Does it depend? If there is a
dependence, then the analysis as if the system reaches thermodynamic equilibrium is incorrect. This should be shown
(could be in sup. Mat. 
The authors write Vb= 0.8 V. Where is this number from? Reported work function and electron affinity or measurements? 
There is an underlying assumption that the system is very homogenous, however this is probably not the case, as there is a

is still applicable despite having a heterogenous system. 
Regarding the 2D nature of the film. This is a thin film, so even if the film extend at the same pace in the lateral and
horizontal direction, it will be essentially 2D in nature. 
Regarding the difference between cooling and heating. The samples are prepared at high temperatures, thus in the metallic

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript reports on the measurement of the transition speed between a metal and an insulator (or semiconductor)
under the influence of a gate effect. Throughout the introduction, the importance of the transition speed is emphasized, and I
agree with the authors’ emphasis on this point. However, the measurement approach contains a critical flaw. To accurately
determine the transition speed, thermal effects must first be eliminated. Yet, the applied pulse width is as long as 10
seconds. Under such conditions, capturing the spike at the transition point—such as that observed near zero time in Fig. 2(c)
—is essential. The characteristics of this spike, including the derivative dI/dt, should be thoroughly analyzed. Unfortunately,
the research deviates from this key focus. Although Fig. 3(a) displays spike peaks near zero time at gate voltages of 2V and
1V, these features were not given sufficient attention. The remaining measurements and explanations appear to be
secondary effects. As it stands, this paper does not adequately address the transition speed, and the research should be
reconsidered and redesigned from the ground up. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors report on an experimental work on the modulation of the time dynamics of the phase transition of VO2, when
gated with a solid-state material. 
The manuscript is well presented. It also contains original results that deserve to be
published. However, I have quite a number of concerns on the manuscript, and some important information is missing. You
will find below a list of my main concerns. 
1. The paragraph explaining the TTT diagram and how it is constructed is not clear at all and actually painful to read and
understand. It is hard to understand how the equation 1 was built. Furthermore, the authors use this equation and its
constitutive terms to explain the physics, but I would rather have an explanation of the physics with clear words and
sentences. 
2. The authors mention a drastically accelerated speed, but the overall speed of the transitions is quite slow compared to
other works on VO2. Please explain why and discuss how to improve this metric. 
3. As this transition is slow, what is the advantage of using a solid-state gating compared to an ionic one? 
4. What conclusions are obtained from the in-situ observation of phase transition? And how do they link to the TTT diagram?
5. To what extent the conclusions drawn are ‘universal’ for VO2? To me they are just true for that particular system and
device. 
In conclusion, I recommend a major revision before publication in Communications Materials.

"gate capacitance" as demonstrated in Ref. 16. When the gate capacitance of this device is plotted as 
1/C², it shows a linear relationship over the measured voltage range, as shown in Fig. R4 (Fig. 2 of

the measurement, suggesting that the gate effect is electronic. Because the system we are using is an 
epitaxial system unlike the high-k or SiO2 gates, and a dry system unlike the liquid gate, it seems that 
ionic reactions involving oxygen or protons are less likely to occur. These discussions have been added 
to Supplementary Information 1.

transition rate caused by a pump-probe laser is sub-ps scale (Refs. 20-22), and the transition rate in a

work. For example, Ref. 20 induces the transition using an ultrahigh-intensity laser of 50 mJ/cm²

induces the metallic transition by exciting many electron–hole pairs and collapsing the bandgap. The 
phase transition in two-terminal devices (Ref. 23) is induced by Joule heating that raises the device 
temperature by several tens of degrees. Furthermore, it is also reported in a relevant material that the 
thermal runaway accompanying the metallic transition can lead to temperature rises exceeding 100 K 
within a few nanoseconds (Blockman, Nature Nanotechnology, 2014). In contrast, the effect of the 
gate voltage in this paper corresponds to a temperature increase of only about 𝛽𝑁ଶୈ= 1 K when 
converted into an equivalent thermal effect. As a result, the transition rate is slower than that of laser 
and Joule heating.

finely enables a systematic evaluation of the transition speed with respect to overshoot, allowing us to 
construct a TTT diagram for the first time in VO2. This has also enabled the extraction of information 
on the thermally activated energy barrier. In contrast, if the external stimulus is too strong, as in 
previous studies, the process becomes fully non-equilibrium, preventing the investigation of thermally 
activated barrier that is crucial for device design.

Thank you for the question. In ion-gated devices, such as those reported in Science 339, 1402 (2013), 
it is difficult to clearly separate the transition dynamics from the device response because slow 
responses, such as interfacial reactions and ion diffusion, occur simultaneously with the phase 
transition. In contrast, in solid-state devices, the electronic RC time constant and the transient 
characteristics of the phase transition can be clearly separated. It enables a precise and systematic 
investigation of transient behavior during the phase transition, allowing the construction of an accurate 
TTT diagram, as demonstrated in this work.

our goal is to elucidate the mechanism of transition dynamics. The fact that the switching appears 
slower does not diminish its significance. Instead, we focus on the important finding that a very small 
gate modulation can induce several orders of magnitude change in the transition speed. Understanding 
this mechanism is the core theme in our work.
