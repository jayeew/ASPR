Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work the authors analyse how cells deal with the deregulated licensing of origins of replication caused by the
depletion of Geminin. Unlike previous studies, where defective licensing was driving large amounts of re-replication in
cancer cells, this study focuses on milder levels of DNA re-replication seen in immortalised cells, such as hTERT-RPE1
cells. 
Through an siRNA screen, the authors identify FANCD2 as key in suppressing DNA damage and supporting cell
proliferation upon Geminin depletion. They then show that FANCD2 loss causes faster fork speed and accumulation of S1-
nuclease sensitive stretches of DNA, likely ssDNA gaps. Genomic analysis reveals that in Geminin depleted cells, FANCD2
is not only at difficult and late replicating DNA regions, 
but it is also seen at early replicating regions, in the proximity of
highly transcribed genes and clusters of early firing replication origins. 
Surprisingly they find that dampening of transcription or R-loops digestion significantly supresses DNA damage in Geminin
depleted cells. 
This study suggests a new mechanism for the generation of genomic instability upon reactivation of replication origins within
the same cell cycle, which now directly implicates transcription and collision between the “re-replication” and the
transcription machineries. Such a mechanism is likely to occur in pre-malignant cells leading to tumorigenesis. 
The experiments are well performed and controlled, combining both classical cell biology and genomics approaches; the
results are clear-cut and important. The manuscript is well written and easy to read.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Initiation of eukaryotic DNA replication is tightly controlled and coordinated with cell cycle progression to ensure only a
single round of DNA synthesis. In metazoa this occurs primarily through careful regulation of CDT1 and Geminin levels.
Defective regulation of CDT1/Geminin can lead to re-replication of the genome which is mutagenic, toxic, and is implicated
in oncogenesis. However, the exact molecular consequences of CDT1/Geminin dysregulation are unclear. Badra-Fajardo et
al have investigated the consequences of Geminin dysregulation and identified an important role for FANCD2 in preserving
genome stability. They find that loss of FANCD2 causes DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and possibly cell death in
Geminin-deficient cells. Mechanistically, these consequences are proposed to arise from accumulation of single-stranded
DNA gaps and formation of DNA breaks that are a downstream consequence of re-replication. Furthermore, these effects
may be unequally distributed throughout the genome. Overall the authors have connected FANCD2 to defects in Geminin
function but there are major concerns about the mechanism proposed, the interpretation of the data, and the overall novelty
of FANCD2 function supported by the data. 
Major concerns 
1. The manuscript seems to imply a unique role for FANCD2 in responding to Geminin loss. However, the upstream
(FANCA in Fig 5) and downstream (RAD51, BRCA1, BRCA2 in Fig 1) components of the Fanconi Anemia pathway behave
similarly. It would thus seem that the role described for FANCD2 here reflects the Fanconi Anemia pathway’s function and
not something more unique about FANCD2. Because a major role of the FA pathway is to coordinate DNA breaks in a
controlled manner (by XPF-ERCC1) this would seem to contradict the model that FANCD2 is preventing breaks. The data
would certainly support a model that FANCD2 prevents break accumulation, but not that it prevents break formation. 
2. It is well established that cancerous cells are much more readily undergo re-replication following Geminin/CDT1
dysregulation. hTERT-RPE1 cells are examined in Figure 1 but the remainder of the manuscript focuses on cancerous cells
(U2OS, MCF7, 293T, HCT116). It’s unclear how much of the observed effects are specific to cancerous cells. 
3. The manuscript focuses on re-replication but this is only directly measured once (Fig. S1A). For the remainder of the
manuscript, nuclear size is used as a proxy. Not only is nuclear size very indirect but it is unclear whether it serves a
reasonable proxy: in Fig S1A the fraction of cells that exhibit increased nuclear area is much greater than the fraction of cells
that exhibit >4n DNA content. The lack of direct measurements of re-replication and concerns about the suitability of cell size
as a proxy for re-replication call into question most of the authors’ conclusions that relate to re-replication. 
4. Geminin knockdown is likely to have effects other than re-replication of the genome. The mechanism of re-replication by
geminin is loading of additional MCM complexes followed by origin firing on the already-replicated DNA. There is no reason
this could not also occur in the unreplicated region of the genome, which could manifest as unscheduled/excess origin firing
and may account for some of the effects observed (e.g. PMID29466339). Taken together with point (1), above, it seems very
possible the authors are documenting breaks induced by replication-transcription collisions and repaired by the Fanconi
Anemia pathway. 
5. The is an ~24 hour delay between FANCD2 and gamma H2AX focus formation (Fig. 3A). Because the cell cycle in U2OS
cells (used in Fig 3A) is approximately 24 hours it would seem very unlikely that the FANCD2 levels and damage are both

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

occurring within S phase or G2, as would seem to be implied by the model that FANCD2 protects forks from excessive
breakage. The H2AX foci would at the very least need to arise from the following S phase, but the simpler explanation would
be that they arise from breaks during mitosis. 
6. In several figures the authors report increased number of foci of different DNA damage response proteins following
Geminin knockdown (Figs 1c, 2d, 3a, 4a, 4d, 5a, 5d, 7d-e). Because Geminin increases nuclear area there would be
expected to be some increase in foci per nucleus independent of any effects on DNA damage. The same issue may also be
true for nuclear signal intensity (depending on how it was calculated). As the data stands, many of the increases in foci (and
possibility nuclear signal) could be a consequence of increased nuclear size. 
7. Similar to the concerns about nuclear size, re-replication is expected to result in more DNA per nucleus and thus an
increase in replication proteins on DNA (E.g. RPA in Fig 4A-B). The same would also be true for DNA damage foci because
more replication would increase the level of spontaneous DNA damage. It is thus not clear how meaningful increased DNA
replication/repair foci are under conditions where re-replication may be taking place. 
8. The genome wide distribution of FANCD2 presented in Figure 6 may not be particularly meaningful because there is no
correction for the prevalence of different genomic features. For example, the prevalence of FANCD2 peaks at intergenic
regions and introns in Fig 6E could simply be because this constitutes most of the genome. Similarly, the approximately 50%
overlap between FANCD2-bound genes in Fig. 6D is very similar to what would be expected for any two randomly selected
sets of genes. As things stand, many of the conclusions drawn from genomic analysis may not be meaningful. 
9. The effects of Geminin and FANCD2 knockdown on cell survival seem to be purely additive (Fig. 1D), in contrast to the
effects on the DNA damage response which are clearly synergistic (Fig. 1C). The DNA damage data fit with the authors’
model of FANCD2 responding to Geminin knockdown but the viability data do not. It is not clear whether Fig 1D supports a
role for the authors’ proposed mechanism in ensuring cell viability. 
Minor concerns 
10. The legend for Figure 1 refers to panel c but appears to actually be referring to b. 
11. For Figure 2A it is not obvious that Geminin knockdown causes increased FANCD2 recruitment to chromatin. The
difference is modest and total cellular FANCD2 is increased so the increased binding could just reflect higher protein levels. 
12. It is unclear what the asterisk in Fig 2C corresponds to. 
13. The analysis of Cbio data in Fig. 2E cannot be used to draw a causal conclusion because this data only demonstrates
correlation. Also it is unclear how this analysis constitutes n=3 as the legend would seem to indicate. 
14. When discussing Figure 5 the authors conclude that Geminin loss “promotes activation of canonical FA signaling and
recruitment of monoubiquitinated FANCD2”. They did demonstrate a role for FANCA following Geminin knockdown and can
infer importance of the FA pathway following Geminin knockdown, but they cannot infer any altered signaling (especially
because they did not measure signaling). 
15. For Fig 7d-e how do the authors know that the RPA signal is due to gaps and not single-stranded DNA arising directly
from R-loops? 
16. In the results section, the authors state “Finally, we tested whether increased formation of ssDNA gaps in Geminin and
FANCD2 double-depleted cells would result in chromosome fragility and high levels of genomic instability.” There is no
direct measurement of gaps and the intervention (Geminin depletion) is highly indirect so they cannot make this conclusion. 
17. For Fig S1A the graphs of cell counts should be labeled to indicate which is control vs which is Geminin depleted

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In my view, the authors have satisfactorily addressed most of the issues raised by the three reviewers. The third reviewer
essentially reiterates their overall negative assessment of the manuscript, but this seems to rest mainly on minor or
interpretative criticisms. Importantly, none of the specific points raised appears to identify any substantive flaw in the study. 
Point #1: The data convincingly demonstrate that FANCD2 prevents replication fork collapse in Geminin-depleted cells
independently of the XPF-ERCC1 pathway. There is no substantive rationale to support the reviewer’s speculation that re-
replication would cause increased interstrand crosslinks. Even if a partial dependence of FANCD2 function on XPF were
correct, this would represent a secondary consideration rather than a major limitation. 
Point #2: The reviewer infers that the absence of increased re-replication upon FANCD2 depletion implies an independence
of FANCD2’s role from re-replication. However, the authors’ objective was to identify factors that safeguard genomic stability
in Geminin-depleted cells, not necessarily to define factors acting alongside Geminin to prevent re-replication. Clarifying this
conceptual distinction is important for properly framing the study’s design and conclusions. 
Point #4: This point concerns a minor methodological issue that has been adequately addressed by the authors and does
not affect the main findings. 
Point #5: The reviewer correctly states that S-phase accumulation alone does not prove re-replication. However, the authors
did not rely solely on this parameter. They provide data showing cells with over 4C DNA content and EdU incorporation,
which are widely recognized as indicators of re-replication. The expertise of the research team in this area is well
established and lends credibility to their interpretation.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author):  Initiation of eukaryotic DNA replication is tightly controlled and coordinated with cell cycle progression to ensure only a single round of DNA synthesis. In metazoa this occurs primarily through careful regulation of CDT1 and Geminin levels. Defective regulation of CDT1/Geminin can lead to re-replication of the genome which is mutagenic, toxic, and is implicated in oncogenesis. However, the exact molecular consequences of CDT1/Geminin dysregulation are unclear. Badra-Fajardo et al have investigated the consequences of Geminin dysregulation and identified an important role for FANCD2 in preserving genome stability. They find that loss of FANCD2 causes DNA damage, chromosomal aberrations, and possibly cell death in Geminin-deficient cells. Mechanistically, these consequences are proposed to arise from accumulation of single-stranded DNA gaps and formation of DNA breaks that are a downstream consequence of re-replication. Furthermore, these effects may be unequally distributed throughout the genome. Overall the authors have connected FANCD2 to defects in Geminin function but there are major concerns about the mechanism proposed, the interpretation of the data, and the overall novelty of FANCD2 function supported by the data.
