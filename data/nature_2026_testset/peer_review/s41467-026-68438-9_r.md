Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors studied unusual anisotropic magnetoresistance (UAMR) of WTe2(9nm)/Fe3GaTe2(26 nm) heterostructure. The
main finding is the observation of an exceptionally huge UAMR of 289% at 5K when the magnetic field rotates in the yz-
plane (perpendicular to the current). Below 60K, more current (up to 90%) flows in Fe3GaTe2 layer. Significant UAMR was
observed below 100K. From the first-principles calculations, the authors showed a large spin-orbit coupling (SOC) exists in
WTe2 with the hybridization of W d-orbits and Te p-orbits. In addition, authors show the temperature-dependence of UAMR
follows a generalized logistic function. Large MR is a crucial quantity in spintronics that is important for magnetic sensing
and data storage. Thus, the topic is of great importance in the field of spintronics. I find the results also very interesting and
useful for the general readers of NC. However, I need to resolve some puzzles before making my formal assessment. 
1) Large UAMR in WTe2 was already observed in Refs. 17-22. For example, even bigger UAMR was reported in Ref. 20. Is
not it nature to have large UAMR of WTe2(9 nm)/Fe3GaTe2(26 nm) heterostructure? 
2) Huge MR in WTe2 was also attributed to the exact balance between electrons and holes in Ref. 17, the same as here in
addition to the SOC in WTe2. Other than SOC which I will also discuss below, I do not see the fundamental difference
between two mechanisms. 
3) In terms of the role of SOC, I do not see how can it lead to large MR in general and UAMR in particular. Can authors
provide a convincing argument? I believe that it is important. 
4) In Ref. 20, UAMR in WTe2 was claimed to be absolute cosine(\beta) not square of it although the data are similar. Authors
should compare their fitting and data against those in Ref. 20. It can help readers to have better understanding of the
systems. 
5) It is also unclear to me how exact balance of electrons and holes and SOC leads to the specific expression of UAMR. A
quantitative sketch like what was done in Ref. 17 about large quadratic B dependence of MR from two carrier model.
Although two carrier model cannot really explain the linear MR reported in Ref. 20 and UAMR, it at least provides a possible
scenario for unsaturated quadratic B phenomenon. The manuscript will surely make a contribution to the field and the topic if
authors can do so. 
6) It is not clear to me why Fig. 5d has the contribution from topological Hall effect (THE). THE is proportional to skyrmion
numbers. Can authors show the existence of skyrmions in their samples? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors reported a giant, unusual anisotropic magnetoresistance (UAMR up to 289% in WTe2/Fe3GaTe2
heterostructure. In contradistinction to conventional magnetoresistance mechanisms, the authors claimed that the UAMR is
the result of a distinctive interplay between charge compensation and magnetisation dynamics at the interface, thereby
facilitating Coulomb-unconstrained spin angular momentum transfer. Authors justified further that the WTe2 layer,
distinguished by its near-perfect balance of hole and electron carriers, dynamically neutralises internal electric fields under
magnetic field application, resulting in persistent nonequilibrium scattering and large, non-saturating MR. The observed
angular and temperature-dependent UAMR deviates significantly from the classical cos^2θ behavior, especially below 80 K,
where hole-electron resonance is most active. The main issue is that the origin of UAMR is not very clear due to following
reasons.

- Unsaturated MR can arise from different sources. How authors make sure such MR in their device is from e-h

- Without the e-h compensation, several materials show UAMR. 
- MR in WTe2 itself is very asymmetric and only B|| to c shows giant value., and other directions are either negligible or very
low, identifying the role of anisotropic Fermi surface rather than e-h compensation. 
The comments above make it very clear that the behaviour of electrons and holes, which is related to their Fermi surfaces, is
more important than the spins of electrons and holes in this study. So, what the authors say is very premature.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD), WTe2, has attracted a lot of attention as an ‘anomalous’ semimetal that exhibits
a striking linear magnetoresistance up to very high magnetic field. Various studies of the electronic states and electrical
transport in this material suggest that ‘electron-hole resonance’ (nearly balanced electron and hole populations) may be
responsible for the observed magnetotransport. 
The present manuscript aims to explore the effect of this electron-hole
resonance on spin transport. The motivation is not based on a rigorous theoretical prediction but rather relies on the intuitive
notion that spin transport under conditions of electron-hole resonance should yield interesting phenomena. There is a
reasonable rationale for this since all prior spintronics platforms have centered on materials wherein one carrier type
dominates. Thus, the theoretical analyses and fundamental understanding of spin scattering, spin diffusion, spin
accumulation etc. has generally been confined to the regime of single carrier types — or at best uncorrelated, single particle
physics of two carrier transport. Thus, the approach undertaken in this manuscript potentially offers an opportunity to open up
a new area for spintronics. 
The key claim made here is the observation of a “giant” or “exceptionally large” and “unusual” anisotropic magneto-
resistance (AMR) in WTe2/Fe3GaTe2 hybrid heterostructures in the temperature window where electron-hole resonance
occurs. The authors draw this conclusion by measuring electrical transport as a function of magnetic field (orientation,
magnitude) and temperature. I would urge the authors to be cautious about the excessive use of such superlatives unless
the data clearly warrant it. For example, a reader of this manuscript will look at the data shown in Fig. 2 and wonder what (if
anything) is remarkable about the AMR of the hybrid WTe2/FGT devices when the control devices of purely WTe2 and FGT
show almost the same behavior. It is certainly true that there are qualitative differences in the dependence of the AMR on
magnetic field orientation (angle with current and sample plane), but the ‘large’ effect of “289%” is only revealed after
subtracting other contributions. The authors are to be commended for doing a careful job of analyzing the data and teasing
out the hidden physics. However, this does not warrant the use of hyperbole to describe the observations… If one aims to
exploit the claimed discovery of an “unusual” and “giant” AMR in this material system, the raw signal would need to have the
enhanced attributes. Even though there may be some very interesting physics lurking in the data shown here, the
observations do not show much chance of making any technological impact unless the phenomenon can stand on its own. 
The authors also report the observation of chiral transverse transport in the hole-dominated regime. This is also (in principle)
quite interesting from a fundamental aspect and the authors accompany their observations with a DFT calculation that
potentially provides a foundation for understanding the phenomenon. The authors attribute ‘chirality’ to the interfacial
coupling between hole-electron resonance and magnetic moments or spin texture formation due to DMI. 
The authors also
mention that the chiral transport originates from the hybridization between the d-orbital of W and the p-orbital of Te, which
induces spatial symmetry breaking in the spin-orbit coupling (SOC) strength. However, this entire discussion is not very
clear or rigorous (e.g/ “This anisotropic SOC distribution could mediate anisotropic interactions ….” In the absence of a more
rigorous calculations, the use of illustrations or cartoons (in the supplementary) would be helpful. 
Overall, the work presented here provides a first glimpse of spin transport and spin-charge interconversion in a previously
unexplored regime that combines several interesting factors arising from the presence of electron-hole resonance in a a
strongly spin-orbit coupled 2D material interfaced with a 2D ferromagnet. In principle, this could elicit broad interest because
of the potential implications for spintronics with 2D materials. The manuscript in its current form falls somewhat short of
achieving this goal. 
Drawing potentially impactful insights into spin transport in this interesting materials combination is
especially tricky since the claimed behavior does not really stand out in its own right but is rather subtly cloaked by other
transport contributions that are dominant even in the individual WTe2 and FGT. The authors would be well advised to stick
to clearly highlighting the novel aspects of the physics they are trying to reveal and avoid unnecessary hype with claims of
‘large’ effects without context. There should also be a high threshold to inventing new technical jargon such as “unusual
AMR” or UAMR. 
The term AMR is well understood by the spintronics community and the term “UAMR” as coined here is
imprecise and not particularly meaningful. There are also several detailed questions that need to be addressed before this
manuscript could be suitable for publication in Nature Communications. These are listed below. A major global concern is
that the conclusions drawn 
in this manuscript are based upon data on a single device (with measurements also shown on
two control devices). Were additional hybrid devices measured to make sure the observed unusual aspects of AMR are

1. Large UAMR in WTe2 was already observed in Refs. 17 -22. For example, even bigger UAMR

Reply: Thank you for the comment. The UAMR observed in WTe2 and WTe2/Fe3GaTe2 originates 
from different mechanisms. The former stems from electron -hole compensation and is 
magnetism-independent, while the latter mainly comes from the scattering between carriers and 
the magnetic moments. The correlation of MR with magnetization is a critical feature for 
advancing non -volatile magnetic memory applications, as it enables magnetoresistance to be 
dynamically tuned by magnetic states.
Specifically, in WTe 2(9 nm)/Fe 3GaTe2(26 nm) heterostructure, the resistivity of the WTe 2
layer is much higher than that of Fe 3GaTe2, resulting in an in -plane charge current in Fe 3GaTe2
that is approximately 14 times larger than that in WTe2 (Supplementary Materials S4). Considering 
the UAMR values of the individual WTe2 (486.18%) and Fe3GaTe2 (−1.07%) layers, the estimated 
angular-dependent magnetoresistance based on a parallel -circuit model, which accounts only for 
the bulk contributions of the two layers, is approximately 31.48%. This value is far smaller than 
the observed MR of 288.78% in WTe 2/Fe3GaTe2, highlighting the critical role of the magnetic 
interface in determining the large UAMR. 
 To further substantiate this point, we fabricated a WTe 2(17 nm)/hBN(26 nm)/Fe 3GaTe2(36 
nm) heterostructure incorporating an hBN interlayer to isolate the magnetic interface, while 
maintaining a comparable in -plane charge current. As shown in Fig. R1(b), the UAMR of the 
WTe2/hBN/Fe3GaTe2 device measured in the yz-plane exhibits a nearly ideal cos 2β dependence at 
low temperatures, consistent with the behavior of bulk WTe 2, and in sharp contrast to the 
pronounced deviations from cos 2β observed in WTe 2/Fe3GaTe2 bilayers. The UAMR of

2. Huge MR in WTe2 was also attributed to the exact balance between electrons and holes in Ref.

Reply: Yes, the large magnetoresistance in WTe 2 originates from the balance between electrons 
and holes. However, in the WTe 2/hBN/Fe3GaTe2 heterostructure, the pronounced UAMR 
primarily arises from the interaction between the magnetic interface and charge carriers under the 
hole-electron balanced condition. Detailed explanations are provided below. 
Under the hole-electron resonance state, electrons and holes shift and accumulate to one side 
under the magnetic field due to the Lorentz force. This suppresses the formation of a built-in 
electric field that balance the transverse migration of carriers. Therefore, the transverse scattering 
continue increasing, the carrier mean free path decreases in longitudinal direction, ultimately 
leading to a huge, unsaturated longitudinal resistance change. When a magnetic interface is 
present, the sustained interaction between carriers and the magnetic interface allows the 
generation of giant UAMR. Additionally, the anisotropic magnetoresistance (AMR) is a 
relativistic effect arising from the Zeeman-like interaction between the carrier spin and a 
velocity-dependent effective magnetic field that is transverse to the electron wave vector, or 
equivalently, to the average current direction [Sci. China-Phys. Mech. Astron. 64, 217531 (2021)]. 
It essentially arises from the combined effects of SOC and the broken symmetry of the system’s 
ferromagnetically ordered state [Phys. Rev. B 80, 134405 (2009)].

2. The temperature dependence of UAMR has been fitted using a “generalized logistic function.” It 
would be helpful to provide a physical justification for using this specific functional form. Is the 
choice of this model based on an underlying theoretical framework, or is it purely empirical?

Reply: We thank the reviewer for raising this point. The generalized logistic function is primarily 
used here as an empirical fitting tool, as it naturally captures the characteristic temperature 
dependence of UAMR, transitioning from a low-temperature plateau to a high-temperature plateau. 
The extracted parameters, such as 𝑇0and 𝑝, can be interpreted as a phenomenological measure of 
the characteristic temperature and the sharpness of the transition, potentially reflecting the evolving 
contributions of hole-electron resonance and scattering mechanisms with temperature. We clarify in 
the revised manuscript that the function is not derived from a microscopic theory, but provides an 
accurate description of the observed temperature dependence.
