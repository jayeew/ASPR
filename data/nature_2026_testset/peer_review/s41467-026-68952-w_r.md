Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript describes the concept of strong metal-base interaction based on the results of Na-doped Rh/TiO2 catalyst
for hydrogenation reactions. The tackling point discussed in the manuscript is timely and quite interesting in heterogeneous
catalysis. In particular, providing the experimental evidence for suppressed hydrogen spillover to the TiO2 surface would
make this study offer a rational approach for designing efficient hydrogenation catalysts. 
Nevertheless, I have to raise some critical issues for revision as follows: 
Q1. The authors claim the presence of amorphous Na species (possibly Na2O) onto the Rh particle, which makes
arguments in two aspects. 
Q1-1. How is it distributed along Rh particle? Only on-top, corner or else? If a thin layer is formed as depicted in Fig. 1b, it
may be able to block the adsorption of gas and liquid reactant molecules. On the other hand, CO chemisorption experiments
showed the similar Rh dispersion between Rh/TiO2 and RhNa/TiO2, whereas the spectrum intensity in CO-DRIFT spectra
(Supplementary Fig. 7) appear larger for the former catalyst compared to the latter. If both observations are correct, they are
not consistent each other. This question should be cleared out during revision. 
Q1-2. Do all Rh particles have the same layer of Na species? Otherwise, what is the fraction of Rh particles having the
corresponding layer? Furthermore, how can the authors distinguish different Rh particles with and without the Na2O layer? I
hope the response is made on the basis of activity results and characterization data. 
Q2. The given simulation (Fig. 1d-f) is based on Rh single atom that is an extreme case for the simulation model. If the
authors want to make the SMBI concept powerful, the structure sensitivity of RhNa/TiO2 depending on the Rh size should be
investigated. 
Q3. The characterization data of the samples “pristine” and “H2@673 K” are shown in Fig. 2, while those of the samples “He
pretrt.” and “H2 pretrt.” are in Fig. 3. Particularly, the pretreatment “H2@673 K” or “H2 pretrt.” greatly affects catalytic
performance. Considering the presented results, the given pretreatment can be linked to activity evaluation experiments. 
Q3-1. For the gas-phase reaction, that is, CO2 hydrogenation, H2 pretreatment at 673 K was conducted. What is expected
when He pretreatment at 673 K is done? Are the results consistent with the characterization data? If the consistency is
found, then the authors can clearly confirm the effects of H2 pretreatment on the product yields and further the hydrogen
activation discussed in the context. 
Q3-2. For liquid-phase reactions, it seems that H2 pretreatment before activity tests was not conducted, according to the
main text and SI. If it is correct, I suggest that the authors should confirm the effects of H2 pretreatment on the hydrogenation
performance using three different substrates. 
Q4. In Figure 4, RhNa(1.0)/TiO2 was used for CO2 hydrogenation, whereas RhMa(0.2)/TIO2 was for the hydrogenation of
C=C, C=O and N=O bonds. This argues that the molar ratio of AM to Rh 
can change the conversion and product selectivity.
What is the most important factor to govern such a change? I think this should be discussed for readers to know about how to
tune the SMBI effect.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The role of alkali metal additives in catalysis has long been a significant topic of research. This manuscript explores the
synergistic effects between alkali metals and metals, with a primary focus on the impact of H
₂
 dissociation and hydrogen
overflow. The authors propose that alkali metal oxides interact with H
⁺
 ions, leaving H
⁻
 on Rh to form electron-rich metal
centers, which prevents hydrogen overflow and enhances performance in hydrogenation reactions. Based on this, the
concept of Strong Metal-Base Interaction (SMBI) is introduced. However, the manuscript lacks a thorough analysis of
catalyst structure and the relationship between reaction mechanism and performance. As such, it can be not recommended
for publication in Nature Communications. The following comments are provided to help strengthen the work. 
1. The spatial and coordination structure between Na2O and Rh is unclear. In Fig. 1b, how are the amorphous Na species
identified? Could additional evidence, such as EELS or EDS line scanning under H2 atmosphere, be provided? Is there any
SMSI effect between Rh and TiO2? Providing EDS elemental mapping images at higher resolution (for example 20 nm or 10
nm scale) would offer a clearer spatial distribution (Fig. 1c). Is there a direct bonding interaction between Na2O and Rh? 
2. The assignment of the infrared vibrational frequency of the Rh-H bond and its shift to lower wavenumbers as an indicator
of bond weakening - is this supported by literature or theoretical models? 
3. In Fig. 2e, it is suggested that H2 undergoes heterolytic dissociation at the Rh-Na2O interface on the RhNa/TiO2 sample.
What is the direct evidence for this? How can the possibility of homogeneous dissociation on Rh, followed by overflow onto
Na2O, be ruled out? Supplementary calculations should be provided to compare the H2 dissociation process on Rh-Na2O
interface and Rh nanoparticles. 
4. The manuscript claims that “Rh/SiO2 exclusively produces CO, suggesting that the methanation activity observed on
Rh/TiO2 likely arises from the influence of SMSI”. This contention, however, is debatable. Previous studies indicate that
SMSI formation creates a protective structure that inhibits H2 and CO adsorption, which is more favorable for CO formation
during CO2 hydrogenation reactions (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2022, 144, 11, 4874-4882). 
5. The primary product from Rh/SiO2 is CO, which significantly deviates from prior findings (J. Catal., 1996, 162: 54-65.). For
instance, Rh/Al2O3 (Appl. Catal. B, 2012, 113-114, 237-249) and Rh/S-1 (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2019, 141, 8482-8488) are
both irreducible supports, and their main product is CH4. The other properties of the support, rather than just its reducibility,
determine the CO
₂
 hydrogenation selectivity. 
6. Structural changes in Rh/TiO2 catalysts during CO2 hydrogenation have been reported (ACS Catal. 2025, 15, 12133-
12147; Nature Chem., 2017, 9, 120-127). Does the presence of Na have any effect on the structural changes?

Response 1-4. As correctly noted by the reviewer, the optimal AM/Rh ratio differs between CO₂ 
hydrogenation and liquid -phase hydrogenation reactions. In our study, RhNa 1.0/TiO₂ showed 
negligible promotion in vinyl acetate hydrogenation, whereas RhNa 0.2/TiO₂ exhibited the 
strongest enhancement among the tested samples. This indicates that the optimal AM loading 
depends on the reaction phase (gas vs. liquid), the nature of the substr ate (e.g., C=C, C=O, N=O 
groups), and even the identity of the alkali promoter. 
The most important factor governing these variations is the volcano-type dependence of SMBI on 
the extent of AM coverage. Insufficient AM results in only weak modification of Rh sites, whereas 
excessive AM coverage can mask Rh surfaces, suppressing H2 activation which is an essential step 
for initiating SMBI and for overall catalytic turnover. Thus, the optimal Rh:AM ratio reflects a 
balance between (i) interfacial modification to induce SMBI and (ii) preserving sufficient Rh 
accessibility for H 2 activation. For this reason, it is thought that the Rh:AM ratio that induces 
optimal interface changes is different in gas -phase CO2 hydrogenation and liquid-phase vinyl 
acetate hydrogenation. 
To clarify this point, we have added CO₂ hydrogenation results of the RhLix/TiO₂ series, analogues 
of Na promoted catalysts and more striking selectivity differences  (Supplementary Fig. 13) and 
revised the corresponding discussion in the manuscript. Additionally, the results of vinyl acetate 
hydrogenation over RhNa1.0/TiO2 catalyst have been added in Supplementary Fig. 15. 
Line 341: Determining the optimal promoter content is another essential consideration in 
practical catalyst design. CO 2 hydrogenation over the RhLi x/TiO2 series clearly illustrates this 
point (Supplementary Fig. 13). Although Li doping consistently enhances CO selectivity, the

magnitude of the effect increases with Li content, whereas CO2 conversion gradually decreases at

identifying the optimal AM/Rh ratio requires balancing interfacial modification with the

Hydrogen spillover generally involves the migration of dissociated hydrogen species, in which H 
atoms activated on metal surfaces are further separated into H+ and e−, followed by the migration 
of H + along surface oxygen anions  (O2−) of the oxide support (e.g., TiO 2) while electrons move 
along reducible metal cations. For spillover to occur, there must exist (i) a high hydrogen surface 
coverage on the metal site and (ii) adjacent oxide sites capable of stabilizing H + (i.e. sufficiently 
basic surface oxygen anions) an d (iii) accepting electrons ( i.e. reducible cations). These t hree 
factors jointly provide both the thermodynamic driving force and the kinetic feasibility of spillover. 
Therefore, the suppression of hydrogen spillover by SMBI becomes highly intuitive when 
considering its physicochemical origin. The O 2− species in Na 2O are far more basic than those in 
TiO2, thus more strongly stabilizing H +. Meanwhile, Na+ is not reducible, preventing e− transport 
through the Na2O layer. Overall, these interactions rationalize the inhibition of hydrogen spillover 
in the presence of Na+ dopants.

mechanistic context. While the initial activation of H 2 likely proceeds via homolytic cleavage on 
Rh due to its low dissociation barrier, the subsequently formed hydrogen species rapidly undergo

the revision, we investigated the thermodynamic preference of homolytic cleavage and heterolytic 
cleavage through DFT calculations and observed that heterolytic cleavage is slightly preferred  
(Please see revised Fig. 4). This ultrafast secondary process is difficult to observe directly, but the 
final stabilized configuration is best described as H − on Rh / H + on Na2O, which corresponds to 
heterolytic H2 cleavage in its net mechanistic outcome.

As mentioned in more detail in response 3-1, w e have conducted additional STEM -EELS

Taken together, these results indicate that while alkali metals suppress TiO x overlayer formation 
during H ₂ pretreatment and that no significant structural morphology changes after CO ₂/H₂ 
hydrogenation. The H ₂-reduced configuration therefore provides an acc urate representation of

Jung et. al. study Na-doped Rh/TiO₂ as a model to propose Strong Metal–Base 
Interaction (SMBI) characterized by Na2O species bound near Rh particles that promotes 
heterolytic H₂ splitting (H+ captured by Na2O  and H- remains on Rh), producing electron-
rich Rh and suppressing hydrogen spillover to TiO₂. The manuscript presents a potentially 
important mechanistic picture (SMBI) to explain alkali-metal promotion in hydrogenation 
catalysis. The combination of DFT calculations, NAP-XPS, DRIFTS/TPD, isotope 
experiments and catalytic testing is impressive and largely consistent with the SMBI idea. 
However, a few critical concepts in the proposed mechanism remain in question and 
require addressing in a major revision to meet the quality of publication expected in Nature 
Communications. 
 
1) Overall, the main weaknesses of the study are the following. 
 
a) The SMBI mechanism does not explain how the presence of Na species suppresses 
hydrogen spillover to TiO2. The presence of alkali metal species and the inhibition of 
hydrogen mobility were merely demonstrated to happen together, but the 
mechanistic causality between them is not directly and clearly explained. 
 
b) There is no clear evidence for the highlighted heterolytic cleavage of H2 on the Rh-
Na interface. The data do not exclude the possibility of homolytic cleavage on the Rh 
surface and subsequently migration of activated H species to the Rh-H interface.

sites?

analyses might also confirm if their models could validly represent the experimental 
forms of their catalyst materials.
