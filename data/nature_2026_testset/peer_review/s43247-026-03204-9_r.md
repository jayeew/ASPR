REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The paper investigated the temporal and spatial changes in sea level rise around Africa, the spatial domain is quite broad.
They used sea level observations from satellite altimetry, and found that the sea level around Africa significantly increased
in relation to 2023-2024 El Nino. The title, honestly, was misleading, because when I saw it I though the paper would reveal
some underlying mechanisms associated with the El Nino that drove the rapid sea level rise, e.g., oceanic variability or
atmospheric forcing, or determine the contributing sources, e.g., how much the ocean mass variations contribute or the steric
sea level rise changes over time, such knowledge would be appreciated by the community, but unfortunately, there was no
relevant investigation. I must say that only quantifying the sea level rise or acceleration does not warrant a publication,
especially in communication earth & environment, I therefore have to reject it, and suggest the authors to consider a suitable
journal. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript investigates sea level anomalies in the African Marine Domains over 1993-2024 using gridded 1/8 degree
satellite altimetry data over 1993-2024. The authors report: 1) record-high sea level anomalies during 2023-2024 El Niño
event; 2) regional mean sea level trend and acceleration that exceed the global means; 3) ENSO influences accounting for
~25% of the detrended sea level variance vs. ~14% non-detrended sea level variance; and 4) a regime shift around in 2009
where sea level trend increases by 71%. They attribute the record 2023-2024 sea level anomalies to nonlinear amplification
that causes larger sea level variability in a warming ocean. They further suggest that the PDO is linked to this nonlinear
amplification and the region shift, implying a fundamental change of sea level dynamics around Africa. 
Major comments: 
By documenting of the 2023-2024 record-high sea level anomalies around Africa and quantifying sea level changes across
the Africa Marine domains over 1993-2024 makes a valuable contribute to the sea level science community and to broader

stakeholders, with important societal implications. The analyses are sound, and the conclusions are generally convincing. 
However, additional evidence is needed to support the claim of “nonlinear amplification” is behind the 2023-2024 record-
high detrended sea level anomalies. The detrended sea level anomalies during the nine ENSO events (their Figure 6) show
markedly temporal (event-to-event) and spatial heterogeneity. For instance, many regions in the African Marine Domains
exhibit larger sea level anomalies during the 1994–1995 ENSO event than during 2023–2024, even though the global
ocean was cooler in 1994–1995. 
The authors also state that the Niño 3.4 correction is novel: they regress sea level anomalies on the Niño 3.4 index and
remove the fitted associated signal. However, this approach is standard for isolating and removing the contributions of
climate modes from sea level anomalies (e.g., Hamlington et al. (2021), Earth’s Future,
https://doi.org/10.1029/2020EF001839). 
Minor comments: 
Page 2, paragraph 1: SLR is not defined. 
Page 2, paragraph 1: Delete “with global mean sea level (GMSL) rising at ~3.45 mm/yr from 1993 to 2024.”, as it repeats
what is just stated on page 1, paragraph 3. 
Page 5, paragraph 5: “These patterns highlight the importance of including marginal seas in regional SLR assessments,
despite their lower population density.” 
Here, the authors generalize the observation of lower population density in the Red Sea to other marginal seas; this is
inaccurate. 
Page 7, paragraph 3: MAM has been defined earlier. 
Page 9, paragraph 3: “To statistically … long-term acceleration”. There are essentially redundant sentences. 
Page 10, paragraph 1: “High correlations … effective coastal planning.” This sentence needs rewording. It is not clear what
“this correction” is, the GIA correction or the Niño 3.4 correction? Assuming the correction is the Niño 3.4 correction, it is also
not clear what exactly the high correlations between the two would “highlight the correction’s role in clarifying the climate-
driven trajectory”. 
Page 13, paragraph 2: “Even modest anomalies (e.g., 27.04 mm in 2023–2024) can trigger … an elevated mean state.” Even
though the sum of the ENSO-driven SLAs and the elevated mean state is large, it does not mean the ENSO-driven SLAs
trigger the outsized impacts. 
Page 14, paragraph 2: “These findings reveal a nonlinear intensification mechanism: even moderate ENSO signals now
produce disproportionately large impacts due to the elevated 
baseline of sea level.” As specified in the major comments above, it needs more evidence to support that there is nonlinear
intensification mechanism. The large impacts reflect the cumulative effect of ENSO-driven SLAs together with an elevated
sea-level baseline; they are not due to ENSO alone. 
Page 15, paragraph 3: “120°-170°W, 5°N-5°S”. Need to fix the order of the coordinate range. 
Page 16, paragraph 16: “50°-70°E, 10°S to 10°N”. Replace “to” with “-”. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The paper entitled “2023-2024 El Niño Drives Record Sea Level Surges in African Marine Domains” discusses regional sea
level trends along the coast of African and the contribution of the El Niño Southern Oscillation to regional variability. One of
the main conclusions is that El Niño is a significantly increasing driver of coastal changes, which can impact coastal
communities through higher rates of sea level rise and associated flooding as well as the fishing industry due to decreased
upwelling and nutrient supply negatively influencing the coastal fish population. The paper aims to demonstrate how the
2023/2024 El Niño event in particular, caused a stronger response in the coastal ocean than previous events, which seems
to be a sign of an alarming trend when comparing to past events. 
While the paper raises an interesting point it lacks a bit in clarity and structure. It reads like a collection of facts and numbers
and could benefit from re-structuring to tell a coherent story. I would recommend a new structure that a) addresses the
physical and mathematical analysis and b) a more clear description and illustration of potential impacts. 
With regards to a), I think the analysis of the contribution of ENSO in the area needs to be put more into perspective of other
climate modes that are influencing sea level variability in coastal Africa. The authors elude to papers that describe impacts
of IOD, Atlantic El Niño, PDO, TSA, and NAO but don’t directly include these indices in their analysis. I think a more multi-
modal regression or EOF analysis will be required to tease out the relative contributions. An analysis such as Fasullo et al.,
2013 provided (Fig. 4, reference below) could be helpful in this case. As the warming trend is clearly masking the
contribution of the interannual variability, I would recommend having the trend removed in most of the analysis and discuss
the variability of the modes and their contributions to the detrended time series as this reflects the growing impact more
accurately. I suggest taking the following steps (or similar): 
Step 1: distinguish between general warming and ENSO – regress linear and remove 
Step 2: multimodal regression and/or showing the various indices that impact the area in one plot (e.g., like Fasullo et al.,
2013), which should give a more complete picture of what mode contributes how much, in particular, for the 2023-24 event 
Regarding b), I see two main areas that can be highlighted more clearly: 1) low-lying areas, areas already impacted by
flooding, or ongoing subsidence and 2) areas impacted by the consequences to the fishing industry through the warming
and reduced upwelling effect. New plots that highlight those factors in addition to the already plotted population density
could help support the argument that risks for coastal populations are increasing while also helping decision makers to
understand more visually which areas are at risk and through what process.

the potential risk.

outlined above, I'd like to see a more detailed attribution of the peaks vs. an analysis of only the El Nino event periods.
