Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript introduces a 128-channel, 2 mm-diameter "micro-endoscope" capable of recording smooth-muscle action
potentials approximately 25 mm into the distal colon of mice. The authors present in-vivo recordings from both healthy and
diseased animals, corroborated by video-synchronised ex-vivo validation. By demonstrating concordance between
multichannel signals and a suction electrode, pharmacological sensitivity, and a correlation with luminal diameter, the work
convincingly links the detected spikes to genuine myogenic activity, thereby providing a new tool for interventional
gastrointestinal electrophysiology. The principal recommendations are as follows: 
1. Raw traces were high-pass filtered at 10 Hz to isolate spikes < 10 ms, yet the displayed spectra span 0–60 c.p.m. The
manuscript should state that these low-frequency components derive from the RMS envelope and justify this processing
choice to avoid misinterpretation. 
2. Only the high-frequency (> 10 Hz) components were analysed, with slow-wave activity deliberately omitted. Because this
approach is atypical in gastrointestinal electrophysiology, the authors should explain their rationale for not analysing or
discussing slow waves. 
3. In-vivo and ex-vivo maps differ appreciably. Given the colon's proximity to the heart and diaphragm in mouse models,
concurrent electrocardiographic and respiratory monitoring is advised to exclude potential artifacts, especially from strong
cardiac signals. 
4. Based on the high impact of the journal, a more detailed description of the signal-processing pipeline is needed to
enhance reproducibility—for example, how the blue and orange rectangles in Fig. 3E were calculated. Given the non-
standard processing, sharing analysis scripts as open-source code would be valuable. 
5. Recommend to provide a synchronised video (colon diameter changes plus RMS trace) as supplementary information to
illustrate how spike bursts couple with colonic motion. 
6. Figure-specific suggestions. 
- Fig. 1: Recommend to add a labelled cross-sectional schematic indicating outer diameter, insulating layer, electrode layer,
substrate, and mucosal interface for intuitive visualisation of the circumferential array. 
- Fig. 1 impedance testing: Include a combined Bode magnitude–phase plot and a Nyquist diagram in the main text or
supplementary materials. 
- Fig. 2d: Add a colour-scale legend for the spectrogram.

We also present briefly the slow wave data from the endoscope in the newly -added 
Supplementary Information, which are - to us at least – certainly intriguing. The point is 
that indeed we do see “logical” slow waves in our recordings. However, we are also very 
aware that movement of tissue relative to the electrode, or even movement of the 
downstream cable, may itself introduce waveforms into the data. By simple physics, 
these motion artifacts perfectly reflect in their time course and amplitude the actual 
physical movements. This is well known to us from our past work on brain-computer 
interfaces for movement decoding from EEG of awake moving subjects.  
On the other hand, in traditional gut electrophysiology, electrodes are tightly attached to 
the serosa, and if the serosa moves, the electrodes move in concert with it, not relative to 
it. In our case the slow waves we record could represent classic subthreshold slow 
waves, well defined in gut electrophysiology. However, it is not possible at this point to 
rule out involvement of tissue movement-related motion artifacts. Therefore, at this point 
we refrain from making claims about the slow wave activity we see in the endoscope data. 
Consequently – to err on the side of caution – in the present manuscript we solely make 
claims about the spikes, i.e. part of the data that is certain to be pure electrophysiological 
activity.  
Incidentally, we are working to go beyond this in the future: thanks to the transparency of 
the endoscope we are developing optics to put inside it and image the gut and its 
movement from the inside concurrently with electrophysiology. Consequently – equipped 
with such “ground truth” on gut movement - we hope to be able to develop algorithms to 
control for any motion artifacts and responsibly include the slow waves in the “official” 
data provided by the endoscope.

The conclusion is that we can robustly remove the ECG artifact and are certain that 
neither it is ECG which is responsible for the differences between in  vivo and ex vivo data,

see the new Supplementary Information).     
Electrophysiological artifacts generated by breathing (diaphragm muscle) present a 
different story. We do see them in some of mice. They are easily identifiable by their 
steady, fast (much faster than gut dynamics) rhythm, unique spectral signature, and 
spatial decay along the oral to aboral axis. They are also easily correlated with the ground 
truth mouse chest movements captured in video recordings we have performed. We have 
briefly referred to these artifacts in the original submission (see Figure 3). However, the 
Reviewer is absolutely right that these artifacts deserve a more in-depth treatment. Thus 
we now provide explicit data on them in the new Supplementary Information and refer to 
them specifically in the revised manuscript and: 
“In some mice we also see electromyographic (EMG) activity related to breathing 
on the distal channels of the endoscope. Breathing EMG forms very distinct 
patterns both in temporal (signal waveform) and spectral domains (see Figure 4 
and Supplementary Figure 3) and thus can be readily ignored. Nevertheless, it also 
can obscure some colonic signals and further work is required to develop an 
algorithm to remove it from the data.” 
The conclusion is for now that the breathing artifacts – if they do appear in a given animal 
- can be readily identified and ignored, but at the potential cost of obscuring some colonic 
signals. As demonstrated in Supplementary Figure 3, one workaround can be achieved by 
inserting the endoscope less deep in the mice where the endoscope tip reaches the 
artifacts.  
However, our data analytics team believes that all the distinct characteristics of breathing 
artifacts – and the fact that their source is distant, not local - should allow their more 
“intelligent” suppression. This could be done through the development of algorithms 
based on volumetric modelling of electric fields coupled with independent component 
analysis. We plan to release all our algorithms as a companion software toolbox to our 
endoscope. We added a suitable paragraph also in the Discussion: 
“The in-vivo spike data do, however, still contain artifacts of physiological origin, 
namely electrocardiographic (ECG) signals and – in some animals - 
electromyographic (EMG) activity related to breathing. We found the ECG signals 
easily distinguishable and readily dampened to a negligible level by simple signal 
processing methods (see Supplementary Figure 2AB). Additionally, they can be 
easily extracted to serve as a precise heartrate readout (Supplementary Figure 
2C). Breathing EMG forms also very distinct patterns (see Figure 4 and 
Supplementary Figure 3) and, while it thus can be ignored when interpreting the 
data, further work is required to develop a more sophisticated suppression 
algorithm. Given the well-defined geometry of the endoscope, a promising 
approach involves volumetric modelling of electric fields coupled with 
independent component analysis (Głąbska et al., 2014; Łęski et al., 2010), which, 
which would allow limiting the signal content to sources within a certain distance 
from the endoscope.”

As regards the calculation of the blue and orange boundaries Fig. 3E statistical 
significance, we agree that the procedure we used in the original manuscript is not 
standard. We used it because it has served us well in previous work, and we do believe it 
offers a better false-positive/false-negative tradeoff than conventional parametric 
methods. However, we also understand it may also “startle” readers as unusual and thus 
undermine / detract from the main message of the paper.  
Therefore we recalculated the significance with the most well-known procedure for 
multiple comparisons control, i.e. the Bonferroni correction. Although it is very 
conservative – too conservative in many opinions - nevertheless, we found that the data 
are so unambiguous that the results don’t change except for one lost region of statistical 
significance; we adjusted the claims in the manuscript accordingly: 
“Furthermore, the decay of atropine downregulation was followed by a localized 
(across topmost seven channels) increase in spiking activity.” 
 We also call the procedure out it in the Figure 3E legend: 
“(bank of 1920 Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, cluster-based permutation 
p<0.0000052, i.e. Bonferroni-corrected p<0.01/1920 test, see Methods 
Supplementary Information for details)” 
Thus in the revised paper we now use this most established procedure and present its 
exact application in detail in the Supplementary Information: 
“To test the statistical significance of changes in action potential activity in-vivo 
across all channels - as measured by the RMS - brought on by our pharmacological 
manipulations presented in Figure 3, we compared each post-first-stimulus 
window of the RMS to the distribution of the RMS from baseline windows in the 
corresponding channel. This results in a very large number of singular statistical 
tests, requiring rigorous control for multiple comparisons (the false discovery 
error). To this end we used a two-dimensional (time / channel) implementation of 
the cluster-based permutation test (Sobolewski et al., 2011), first devised for one-
dimensional time series by Maris & Oostenveld (2007). In brief: we ran a t-test on 
every time RMS window on every channel. Individual significant (p < 0.001) 
windows which were adjacent – either in time or across neighboring channels - 
were clustered. The data were then randomly permuted (mixing post-stimulus 
values with pre-stimulus baselines) 1000 times, with the above clustering and 
summing repeated for each permutation. The largest cluster size (window count) 
was taken from each random permutation to obtain an empirical random

“2. Is there any observation of the ability to measure relaxation or evidence of increased 
tone at sphincters? This may have significant relevance in future potential application to

Thank you again for the pertinent and highly relevant question from the clinical 
standpoint. Our device measures electrical activity and not mechanical activity or tone. 
The interplay between electrical and mechanical activity of the GI tract is more complex 
than, e.g. in cardiology in that the neurohormonal modulation is predominant. However, 
the link is well known and predictable. Impeding motor activity (phasic contractions) is 
preceded by (and requires) spikes. Concerning tonic activity, the sphincter tone is due to 
the basal myogenic tone (basal tone of smooth muscle is in depolarized state) and it is 
influenced by cholinergic excitatory inputs (via Ach from enteric motor neurons). 
Sphincters do not present slow waves (unlike adjacent gut segments). Relaxation of 
sphincters requires suppression of the basal tone (the main inhibitory mediators are the 
Nitric Oxide and the Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide) via hyperpolarization of the smooth 
muscle cells, reduced Ca2+ influx and relaxation. The main electrical features of 
relaxation are: 1) inhibitory junction potentials and 2) loss of spike potentials. 
Theoretically, our device is perfectly capable of detecting the impeding relaxation state or 
hypertone of sphincters. Within this preliminary study, we didn’t evaluate the electrical 
activity and potential extrapolation of the sphincter tone. However, experimental studies 
in animal models of anal sphincter incontinence to correlate manometric changes to 
electrophysiology recording and to evaluate some interventions to treat fecal 
incontinence is definitely something we are discussing with a Surgical Unit of the Geneva 
Hospital. We have added a suitable passage in the Discussion:  
“We also plan to test utility of our novel device in identifying electrophysiological 
correlates of anal sphincter tone, with the aim of comparing them with 
manometric changes in animal models of anal incontinence for evaluation of 
potential experimental treatments.”

We would like to thank the Reviewer for their positive feedback on the pharmacological 
interventions we used. Regarding GMCs,  the CMC in mice is the rodent (see:  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33085903/) correlate of the GMC. At this point, we 
would like to refrain from making grand conclusions about the GMC with respect to the 
mouse CMC.  
The GMC originates typically in the proximal colon and travel downstream to trigger 
peristaltic contractions and promote fecal mass movement and defecation. They 
present, in humans, a speed of 1cm/sec and high amplitude at high resolution manometry 
(approximately 100mmHg) and they last approximately 10-30 seconds. In rodents, the 
colonic migratory motor complexes are more rhythmic than in humans and propagate at 
the speed of 0.5 to 2 mm per second (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31296967/). 
Reduced GMCs are linked to slow transit constipation, while exaggerated GMCs are 
observed in diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome.  
In our experiments, we observe a rhythmic electrical activity underlying CMS, that is highly 
likely to mimic the pattern of activity underlying GMCs. As shown in Figure 3 and 4, in wild 
type mice we observe regular ~10s bursts of spikes, approximately every 2 minutes, 
separated by periods of quiescence (~20s). This phenomenon is initiated shortly after the 
insertion of the endoscope and develops in intensity (RMS amplitude of spike bursts 
increases) over the first 10~20 minutes. Following this period, rhythmic bursting activity 
reaches a robust steady state which continues for as long as we have recorded up to now 
(>1h).  
In healthy rodents, the colonic motor complexes occur about every 2-4 minutes in 
isolated whole colons (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20142273/) but less frequently 
in vivo because of extrinsic neural modulation 
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31296967/). While we don’t have the manometric 
measurements counterpart, we can posit the hypothesis that these very regular spike 
bursts were induced by the obstruction / distension created by the endoscope itself, just 
like GMCs. Experimentally, balloon distension in the rectum generates high-amplitude 
contractions mimicking GMCs. One can hypothesize further that the spike bursts develop 
increased amplitude over time, in the attempt to overcome the obstruction induced by  
the endoscope.

“1. In response to my previous Comment No. 2, the authors state: "To mitigate this, we

We see that the use by us of the “shorthand” term “time constants” in the previous reply 
was misleading. We will now be more explicit. The peak-to-peak duration of the main 
wave of the action potential is indeed ~10 ms or a little more (see below figure). On 
average, however, the shape of the action potential is of course not a perfect sine wave 
(see below figure). This means its spectral signature does not simply translate to 100 Hz 
(=1s/10ms) nor actually  – seeing it’s a near bi-phasic waveform – to 50 Hz (=1s/(2*10ms)). 
The spectral signature of an irregular waveform has to be determined empirically. If we do 
this, we can easily see that the majority of the spectral content of the average action 
potential is actually contained between some 10 Hz and 60 Hz (see below figure). Thus 
setting the high-pass filter to 10 Hz on the one hand ensures we are not losing significant 
action potential information, while on the other hand puts us safely outside the range of 
any gut movement artifacts: peristaltic motion cycles do not happen at the speed of 10 
times per second, nor anywhere near that value.   
Accordingly, we have made the following relevant change in the text: “[…] we restricted our 
analysis to high-frequency events, specifically action potentials, with time constants in the range 
of 10-20 mswhose spectral signature is mostly above 10 Hz, […]”.
