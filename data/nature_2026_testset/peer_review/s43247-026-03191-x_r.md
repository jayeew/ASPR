REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Widespread enhancement of ecosystem carbon and water fluxes during early soil dry-down 
Comments 
The authors investigate the impacts of soil moisture dry-down events, periods of no rainfall following a precipitation pulse, on
vegetation carbon and water flux. They use data from 215 flux tower sites, global FLUXGPP GPP, ERA5 soil moisture, and
historical GPP and soil moisture from CMIP6 earth system models (ESMs), comparing dry-down period with the equivalent
period without dry-downs. They find stronger carbon and water fluxes in the initial days of dry-downs, especially in non-
dryland ecosystems, and show that the ESMs underestimate these GPP enhancements. The research design is
comprehensive, and the topic is interesting for readers. The responses of vegetation to soil moisture dry-down are indeed
underexplored, not due to lack of data or understanding of plant water stress physiology, but due to the ambiguity of defining
comparable soil moisture situations during and outside of dry-down events. 
Specific comments: 
1. Title 
The title may be misleading. The observed enhancement of GPP and ET in early dry-down stages is not directly caused by

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

the dry-down itself, but rather occurs under conditions where soil moisture remains above θcrit, i.e. plants are not water-
stressed yet. Further, although the water fluxes are mentioned, ET is not studied deeply as is done with GPP. 
2. Abstract 
The abstract could better reflect the scope of the study. It only partly covers the empirical findings of the research, such as
observed GPP, ET, and EF variations during dry-downs as observed from flux tower, satellites, and historical simulations
from ESMs, and explanations of GPP changes using RF and Shapley values. The comparisons between dryland and non-
dryland, and other tentative explanations, like critical SM (θcrit), are missing in the abstract. 
3. Use of top layer SM 
The author used top layer SM, instead of root-zone soil moisture. This is not representative for many plants. The authors
should justify this choice and discuss its implications. 
4. SMcontrol, θcrit, θwilt 
It remains unclear whether SMcontrol is above or below θcrit and the wilting point (θwilt). Is the SMcontrol above or below
θcrit and wilting point (θwilt)? Fig.1 shows that SMcontrol has a very large spread, overlapping the SMdrydown mostly,
especially at the early dry-down stages. The observed difference in vegetation responses may not stem from meaningful soil
moisture contrasts or dry-down events. 
5. Modelling SM decay 
A more intuitive and physically meaningful approach might involve modelling the SM decay curve using, e.g. exponential
decay https://soilwater.github.io/pynotes-agriscience/exercises/drydowns.html#model-description, and examining non-linear
vegetation responses along the continuum, rather than using a binary comparison (dry-down vs non-dry-down). 
6. CMIP6 evaluation 
The claim that ESMs underestimate GPP during dry-downs is interesting but incomplete without evaluating SM differences
(SMdrydown and SMcontrol) in the models versus ERA5. Biases in soil moisture simulations could contribute to GPP
biases, potentially affecting the evaluation of CMIP6 model performance. 
L92, Avoid mixing median with standard deviation (SD). Prefer either mean ± SD (for symmetric distributions) or median ±
MAD (median absolute deviation) for skewed data with large outliers. 
L140-142 The explanation regarding land–atmosphere coupling and photosynthetic capacity differences between dryland
and non-dryland ecosystems is unclear. Please elaborate or rephrase. 
L 211-L214 I don’t understand the logic: “ΔGPP becomes negative, SM levels are significantly below θcrit” and “ΔGPP
remained positive in some cases...SM being lower than θcrit”, suggest existing a θGPP, a threshold lower than θcrit, limiting
photosynthesis. Here depends on what SMcontrol is. If the SMdrydwon and SMcontrol are both below θcrit, and
SMdrydwon>SMcontrol , the ΔGPP is most likely positive, so where comes θGPP? 
L237 The phrase “controls and adjusts to changing...” could be clarified. Perhaps use: “...controls and adjusts to changes
in...” 
L239-240 Saying “our ability to...has been limited to date” seems overstated. Many studies exist on vegetation responses to
water stress. What’s novel here is the focus on natural dry-down events before water stress sets in—this should be
emphasized. 
L282-283 Specify which soil layer from ERA5 was used. ERA5 provides four SM layers; clarify which depth was analyzed. 
L290 The control period definition is ambiguous. Is it defined by the absence of decreasing SM, or absence of prior rainfall?
If the latter, how many days after the last rainfall qualify as a control period? Clarify the logic used to identify "non-dry-down"
conditions. 
L296 “10” days is redundant in “10, 15, and 20 days”. 
L362 (Fig. 1). There is inconsistency in the location of ΔSM = 0 in aggregated figures. For consistent samples, ΔSM = 0
should occur at SMdrydown=SMcontrol. Please clarify whether these summaries use matched samples or different site
groupings. 
L546 (Suppl. Fig. 15), The presence of negative SIF values in shaded area is problematic. Physically, SIF should be ≥ 0. 
L552. (Suppl. Fig. 16 c) The logic and derivation of the θGPP line in the conceptual diagram are unclear. Is it empirically
estimated or hypothetical? Provide a clearer rationale. 
L584 (Suppl. Fig. 21) Consider summarizing and comparing SMcontrol, SMdrydown, θcrit and θwilt across sites. Current
pooling masks potentially important differences in moisture regimes. Classifying only by aridity index may oversimplify the
actual soil moisture context. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
General Assessment: 
This study addresses an important question—how soil dry-downs affect ecosystem carbon and water fluxes. Understanding
these processes and the underlying mechanisms is critical to improving ecosystem modeling under climate change
scenarios. The authors analyze these impacts using FLUXNET observations and evaluate the performance of current
models in capturing these dynamics. The topic is novel, and the manuscript is generally well written and well organized. 
The manuscript could be further strengthened by quantifying uncertainties associated with the comparison design,
presenting results in more detail (e.g., by vegetation type or region), and deepening the discussion of key findings and their
implications. I hope the comments below are helpful for further improving the manuscript. 
Major comments: 
Methodology:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

The comparison between “treatment” and “control” periods is central to assessing the impacts of soil dry-downs on carbon
and water fluxes. Ideally, a true control would match all environmental conditions except for rainfall, which is not feasible in
natural ecosystems. This study instead uses the same calendar period in other years as the control, with multi-year
averaging to reduce interannual variability. While this is a reasonable approach, it may still introduce differences in key
factors such as temperature, radiation, and vegetation conditions (e.g., LAI), which could influence the fluxes independently
of dry-down effects. 
These differences do not invalidate the study’s overall conclusions, but acknowledging and quantifying the uncertainties
introduced by such variability would add robustness. For example, analyzing the differences in climate variables and
vegetation indices between treatment and control periods can help contextualize observed flux changes. 
Additionally, the authors might consider testing an additional comparison approach: analyzing fluxes during a time window
immediately before and after dry-down events. While this method also introduces challenges (e.g., potential differences in
solar radiation and temperature), it may provide further insights into the short-term flux response while reducing inter-annual
variability. 
Results and discussion: 
The findings on the effects of soil dry-downs on carbon and water fluxes are intriguing and appear robust. However, results
are primarily presented by aggregating across all sites. For better applicability to ecosystem modeling and management, it
would be valuable to explore how these responses vary across regions, climate zones, and vegetation types. 
For example, the study notes that LAI is the dominant predictor of daily ΔGPP. Since LAI varies substantially by vegetation
type, this finding may partly reflect differential responses among plant functional types. Similarly, drylands and non-drylands
differ markedly in vegetation structure and land-atmosphere coupling, which may explain the observed contrasts in flux
responses. Given that forests dominate many non-dryland regions while drylands are often grassland- or savanna-
dominated, vegetation type likely plays a critical role and warrants explicit analysis. 
Aggregating all vegetation types together may obscure these underlying mechanisms and lead to generalizations that mask
important variation. Stratified analyses or interaction terms in statistical models could reveal more nuanced patterns. In
addition, given that the random forest model explains only ~60% of spatial variation, it is possible that key ecological or
climatic drivers are missing from the model and merit further exploration. 
While the results are well illustrated, the discussion section could benefit from more in-depth interpretation of key findings.
Several results—such as the role of LAI, contrasting responses across ecosystems, and model limitations—are not fully
contextualized or explained. Providing more mechanistic insights or hypotheses would make the findings more meaningful
and useful for the broader modeling and ecology communities. 
Technical comments: 
Terminology: The manuscript often refers to “higher carbon and water fluxes” without specifying the direction or type of flux
(e.g., net ecosystem exchange, evapotranspiration, etc.). Since these fluxes can represent both inputs and outputs, using
precise terminology (e.g., “increased net CO
₂
 uptake” or “higher evapotranspiration rates”) would improve clarity. 
Line 62: consider rephrasing with The “pulse-reserve” hypothesis, which posits that there is a transient increase in plant
growth, has been documented in …. 
Line 64: the sentence seems incomplete. How about …, leaving a gap … 
Line 65: at the site scale 
Line 73: consider adding a brief explanation of how differing plant behaviors (e.g., stomatal regulation, rooting depth)
influence land-atmosphere feedbacks. 
Line 74: the term “strength” is ambiguous. Need a clarification. Besides that, the logic of the hypothesis is odd. You
hypothesize that the differences generate a weaker response. Do you mean that due to the differences, drylands exhibit a
weaker response to dry-downs? 
Line 102-106: the observed higher SM in dry-down periods compared to control may be due to the design where dry-down
follows rainfall, while control periods may not. This is worth noting more explicitly. 
Line 106-107: Could the differences observed during the dry-down period be attributed to warmer temperatures and higher
radiation levels? The mechanism of this difference would be valuable for the model community. 
Line 302: Repeated words 
Supplementary Fig 21: It shows the variability in SM changing trends across sites. A similar figure showing site-level carbon
fluxes change is suggested. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
[Overall] 
This paper exams ecosystem carbon and water fluxes during dry spells (consecutive dry days after rainfall events) at fluxnet
sites across different climate regions and biome types. It reports some “interesting” results. However, the presented analyses
appear to lack necessary depth and the results are questionable. Therefore, I do not recommend the publication of this
paper. 
[Specific Comments] 
1) The title and the way the paper phrases its results, “soil dry-downs significantly influence terrestrial carbon and water
fluxes” (first sentence of the Abstract), is at least mis-leading. The causal direction is the opposite: surplus of soil water (by

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

rainfall events) enhances terrestrial ecosystem growth, and the enhanced water (and carbon) fluxes in turn causes soil
moisture to dry down. This is well-known “pulse-reserve” phenomenon, which the paper correctly recognizes. The paper
surely can use “dry-down” to correlated with GPP/ET fluxes but should not be confused with the causal relationship. I
strongly suggest the authors rephrase similar statements throughout the paper to avoid the potential confusion that “soil dry-
down enhances ecosystem carbon/water fluxes”. 
2) Methodology. The comparison between the “dry-down” with the “control” periods (L147-149) may not be robust as thought.
The interactions between vegetation and soil moisture are a dynamic system, where the initial conditions of the system play
a critical role. That is, GPP and ET during the dry-down period are significantly influenced by the states of soil moisture and
vegetation (e.g., LAI) at the beginning of the dry spell. Such dependence can be very nonlinear (as the relationship between
soil water content and soil water potential). Therefore, calculation of the simple mean of the GPP/ET fluxes (e.g., Eq.(2)) may
not correctly represent their sensitivities to the driving force (soil moisture). 
3) Example: in contrast to the paper’s argument (L147-149), the more pronounced GPP/ET enhancement over the non-
dryland in Fig. 2 may actually indicate that these vegetation ecosystems are less sensitive to rainfall events. Note that the
initial condition of soil moisture at the beginning of the dry-down period can be lower than the “control” mean (Fig. 1, for US-
Var). For a dryland site like US-Var, if delta GPP responses linearly to soil moisture anomalies, the positive and the negative
GPP anomalies will cancel each other and therefore the mean response in Fig 2a is close to 0 (i.e., the control). On the other
hand, for the non-dryland sites, if vegetation more positively responses to sunnier/warmer conditions, it will be not regulated
by negative soil moisture anomalies (which may be far from the critical threshold). Therefore, the positive GPP anomalies
can persist throughout the dry spell. Therefore, these results are consistent with what previous studies suggested.
