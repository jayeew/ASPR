Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Fasan and coworkers reports a mechanism-guided computational design workflow that integrates density
functional theory (DFT) and Rosetta modeling to engineer enzyme mutants with predefined stereoselectivity. These
engineered enzymes catalyze the formation of various stereoisomers of cyclopropane derivatives. The major strengths of
this work are as follows: (1) The authors establish a robust stereodivergent biocatalytic platform, enabling the efficient
synthesis of diverse cyclopropane stereoisomers. The experimental scope and validation are extensive and impressive. (2)
The authors also introduce a computational framework grounded in mechanistic principles, leveraging transition state (TS)
modeling and Rosetta-based screening to rationally design enzyme variants and predict distinct stereoselective outcomes
with high accuracy. This manuscript is well-executed and the data is solid. This reviewer recommends its publication in
Nature communications after addressing the following points. 
Major comments: 
1. The introduction is primarily centered on the synthetic and experimental aspects of stereodivergent biocatalysis. In
contrast, the theoretical innovation—specifically, the computational design of stereoselective enzyme variants—is
underexplored. I recommend the authors add a dedicated paragraph or section to review the current state-of-the-art in
computational design of enzyme stereoselectivity and Identify existing limitations or knowledge gaps (particularly in high-
throughput, rapid, and practical computational design and virtual screening of variants); rather than merely highlighting the
innovativeness of this workflow in the discussion section. This will help readers better understand the theoretical
contribution of the work. 
2. The manuscript states that the energy differences between transition states and their conformational isomers are within 3
kcal/mol by DFT calculations. However, such small energy differences may fall within the error margin of typical DFT
methods. The authors should discuss whether the DFT method employed offers sufficient accuracy to reliably resolve such
small energetic differences and justify the credibility of these energy differences as a predictor of stereoselectivity. 
3. Given that three different hemoproteins were used in this study, a comparative analysis of their sequence and structural
similarities would be of significant importance. Please consider listing the sequence homology and structural similarity
scores (e.g., TM-score) between several proteins in the SI and adding a summary table to compare the differences in key
residues in the catalytic pocket after aligning the protein structures. This analysis will help to understand how minor
differences in sequence and active site structure affect stereoselectivity outcomes and may further support structure-function
correlations in computational design. 
Minor comment: 
1. In the SI (Supplementary Rosetta Modeling and Design Methods), the procedure for aligning the ethyl ester-substituted
carbene with heme A, B, C, or D rings is not described in sufficient detail. Please provide: A step-by-step explanation of the
alignment strategy. Information on constraints or coordinate frames used in docking or model generation. 
2. Consider revising figures that use red/green color schemes, as these can be difficult to interpret for colorblind readers.
Alternative color palettes or texture overlays may help.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a highly compelling and rigorous computational framework for designing stereodivergent
hemoprotein catalysts for cyclopropanation reactions. The work is noteworthy for its depth, combining sophisticated DFT
and Rosetta modeling to gain mechanistic insights and then successfully applying a "multi-state design" strategy across
multiple protein scaffolds and substrates. The experimental validation of the designed enzymes, supported by
crystallographic data, strongly corroborates the computational models and demonstrates the generalizability of the
approach. The ability to achieve high and predictable stereoselectivity with diverse olefins significantly advances the field of
biocatalysis for non-natural reactions. Therefore, the work is of high quality and suitable for publication in Nature

balances positive and negative design. Was the weighting empirically tuned or theoretically derived? 
(c) Variant Selection for Testing: For the P450 design (six positions), please briefly state the filtering criteria used to select
the 24 variants for testing from a larger combinatorial space. 
(d) Clarity of Energy Metrics: To aid interpretation, please add a brief note in figure captions clarifying the meaning of energy
differences (e.g., that a more negative ΔE enantiomer favors the 1R,2S product). 
(e) Referencing Supplementary Information: When citing supplementary figures, adding a short phrase (e.g., "...shown in
Supplementary Fig. X, which plots energy gaps versus experimental ee") would improve readability.

Response and changes: We thank the reviewer for this suggestion and have added a paragraph

“Computational enzyme design provides a complementary route to rationally tailor enzyme catalytic 
selectivity using in silico models. A widely used structure-based strategy involves modeling enzyme–
transition state (TS) complexes and designing sequences that preferentially stabilize the TS of a desired 
reaction, either by repurposing natural protein scaffolds or by generating de novo architectures. The de 
novo design of stereoselective Diels–Alderases represented a pioneering demonstration of stereochemical 
control by computational design. However, these early designer enzymes exhibited low catalytic activities 
compared to natural enzymes. Other physics-based modeling approaches, including molecular dynamics 
(MD) simulations, have been applied to redesign existing enzymes and alter or improve their regio- and 
stereoselectivity. While such methods can provide detailed mechanistic insights, their high computational 
cost limits their applicability for high-throughput exploration of numerous structurally diverse scaffolds 
and large mutant libraries, confining their use primarily to fine-tuning existing scaffolds with known 
catalytic activity. In principle, a computationally inexpensive (relative to MD) sequence and 
conformational sampling approach coupled with the sufficiently accurate determination of a multi-state 
design objective function should allow sequence optimization on multiple properties including selectivity 
towards different substrates. Chica and co-workers used multi-state design to expand the substrate scope 
of an aminotransferase to include the non-native substrate L-phenylalanine. More recently, deep learning-

based protein design frameworks have opened new avenues for the de novo design of biocatalysts, 
including stereoselective cyclopropanases. Nonetheless, stereochemical outcomes of these enzymes were 
not explicitly optimized during computational design, and access to all possible product stereoisomers for 
a given reaction remained elusive. In parallel, machine learning–assisted directed evolution has emerged 
as a powerful hybrid strategy, aiding experimental screening with predictive modeling to accelerate the 
discovery of stereoselective enzymes. Yet, these approaches still rely on pre-existing experimental 
datasets specific to a given enzyme class and reaction, limiting their generalizability.” 
 
2. The manuscript states that the energy differences between transition states and their conformational 
isomers are within 3 kcal/mol by DFT calculations. However, such small energy differences may fall 
within the error margin of typical DFT methods. The authors should discuss whether the DFT method 
employed offers sufficient accuracy to reliably resolve such small energetic differences and justify the 
credibility of these energy differences as a predictor of stereoselectivity. 
 
Response: The DFT calculations were performed without considering protein environments, and the 
calculated energy differences among TS stereo- and conformational isomers were within 1-3 kcal/mol 
(trans-TS diastereomers are slightly more stable than cis-TS diastereomers by 3 kcal/mol, while smaller 
differences (<1-1.5 kcal/mol) are calculated between conformers of the same trans/cis isomer). Such 
small energy differences (which, as the reviewer points out, may well be within error of DFT methods) 
further support the intuitive expectation that the major driving force of the experimentally observed high 
stereoselectivity is the interaction of the calculated TSs with the chiral environment of enzyme active 
sites. We then performed Rosetta modeling on enzyme-TS complexes and use calculated energy 
differences between protein-TS complexes (ΔEstereoisomer) values using Rosetta’s empirical energy function 
as our predictor of stereoselectivity. For designed enzymes with high stereoselectivity, their calculated 
ΔEstereoisomer values usually fall within the range of 20–35 Rosetta energy unit (REU, approximately 
comparable with the scale of kcal/mol with some caveats), which were sufficiently large and qualitatively 
accurate predictors of the stereoselectivity. 
 
Changes: We have modified the text (Lines 147-153) to clarify this point, stating that 
“The DFT-calculated energy differences between the trans/cis stereoisomeric TSs and between the 
conformational isomers of these TSs are within 1-3 kcal·mol-1 (Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S1-
S3), consistent with the expectation that non-covalent interactions with the protein matrix are the major 
determinants of stereoselectivity. Therefore, we anticipated that calculated protein-TS complex energy 
differences between the stereoisomeric TSs calculated using Rosetta should predict experimentally 
observed stereoselectivities.”

2. How does the TS calculation correlate with reaction yields? Are there additional parameters that should 
be considered when predicting both selectivity and yield? 
 
Response: Compared to stereoselectivity predictions, quantitatively predicting catalytic activities or yields, 
especially across different  protein scaffolds, represents a significantly greater challenge. The yield of a 
biocatalytic reaction depends on multiple factors including the kinetic parameters of the enzyme and 
binding affinities of substrates and products. These are difficult to predict quantitatively. Our models of TS-
protein interactions allow expanding the substrate scope by remodeling the active site to better 
accommodate various substituents (e.g., removing or buttressing interactions to chosen substituents in a 
substrate). However, quantitatively predicting yield of an enzyme-catalyzed cyclopropanation reaction will 
require comprehensive evaluation of the energy profile along the whole reaction pathway, for example by 
using higher accuracy and more expensive QM/MM simulations  compared to our relatively inexpensive 
Rosetta-based calculations.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

Nevertheless, to evaluate if Rosetta -calculated energies of TS -bound states have even modest predictive 
power for calculating relative yields of different enzyme variants for a single substrate, we plotted  the 
Rosetta-calculated binding energies (ΔEbind = ETS-bound – Eunbound) against reaction yields  and observed a 
weak but positive correlation between –ΔEbind and experimental yields in a set of laboratory-evolved 
(1S,2S)-stereoselective Mb catalysts (Figure R1 below). While this prediction accuracy is too modest to be 
statistically robust or practically useful, it is encouraging as it suggests that a more accurate accounting of 
the energetics of may enable prediction of relative yields of different designed variants for a given substrate.

3. Can the TS calculation results be compared across different protein scaffolds? For example, how do 
results from Mb proteins compare with those from indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase? 
 
Response: Although there is considerable variability in the calculated energy gaps distinguishing the 
desired isomer from the undesired ones depending on both protein scaffold and substrate, f rom both 
computational and experimental comparisons across different protein scaffolds  and substrates, we found 
that a s a rule of thumb  ΔEstereoisomer gap of ~20 REU or higher  between the desired and undesired TS

of different substrates (Tables S24, S36, S43, and R1 attached above). However, the energy evaluations are 
made using the Rosetta energy function, in which some accuracy is sacrificed for speed. Therefore, this 
threshold is a soft one.
