Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Ghasemi and co-authors presents a thorough cryo-EM structural analyses of PEDOT:PSS in solution and
in the solid state with and without secondary dopants. 
The authors aim to shed some light on the morphology of PEDOT:PSS, which is still needed for understanding and
controlling the properties of this benchmark polymer. 
The manuscript is a beautiful demonstration of the abilities of cryo-EM (used mostly for proteins observation or molecular
self-assemblies) and of the new generation of high-end microscopes. 
Although the PEDOT:PSS molecular structure has not been fully resolved, the 
real space images of PEDOT:PSS
morphology could help 
a broad audience (including those interested in organic/wearable electronics, bioelectronics, 
and the
EM community,…) to 
better understand how properties vary in the presence of a dopant or humidity. 
In general, the method is well-detailed, but additional data and images should be provided, in particular, to rule out artefacts. 
However, the heterostructures (fibers consisting of micelles surrounded by a thin layer of PEDOT:PSS lamella) proposed by
the authors is unclear. 
The coexistence of these heterostructures with crystalline domains (not aligned with the fibers) in the doped samples is also
unclear. 
Schematics of these heterostructures (for the non-doped and doped PEDOT:PSS) would help the reader. 
Please see below for more questions and issues that need to be addressed before considering the manuscript for
publication. 
While Heraeus keep certifying that the PH1000 dispersion contains micelles of 30 nm size, it is now the third research works
(to the best of my knowledge) observing fibrils in the dispersion thanks to the use of cryo-EM. (Nat. Commun., 2020, 11, 1–8 ;
J. Mater. Chem. C, 2023, 11, 7802-7816 ; and Matter 2024, 7, 1071-1091 (cited in the manuscript)). 
The authors are not the first ones to observe fibrils by cryo-EM and the previously reported references should be cited. 
I would advise the authors (and the community) to mention the batch number and date of receipt of the PH1000 they used in
their study to find out whether or not these fibrils are an effect of a new synthesis pathways recently developed by the
supplier. 
1) Morphology of neat PEDOT:PSS in solution 
The schematic of Figure1c (neat PEDOT:PSS) does not correspond to what is observed in the corresponding TEM image
(Figure 1e) where elongated fibrils are observed. Elongated fibrils may be composed of micelles (a periodic electronic
contrast along the main axis of the fibrils is seen, but no isolated micelles are observed on the TEM image). The
corresponding description page 5 line 127 should also be adjusted. Otherwise, the authors should provide a more
convincing TEM image showing isolated micelles.

2) Morphology of doped PEDOT:PSS in solution 
Although the images of solid films are more convincing, the term 'lattice planes' used by the authors in Figure 1 is open to
question. 
How can the authors be sure that these ‘lattice planes’ are not Fresnel fringes (particularly in blue squares Figures 1 h, k, n)?
Fresnel fringes (Fukushima et al. J.Phys.D:Appl. Phys., vol 7, 1974, 257-268) are characterized by a first intense signal
close to the specimen, followed by an attenuated distribution outside the specimen. The periodicity of Fresnel fringes is also
known to evolve with the defocus. Such intensity and periodicity variations are noticeable in the cryo-EM images (from the
nearest to the furthest from the fibril). 
The authors should specify the defocus at which the images were taken, their CTF and provide the same images with a
lower defocus value to confirm whether the so-called lattice planes exist. 
Why are the lattice lines so far away from the fibrils (mostly visible in Figure 1o)? 
These features can also be seen in Figure 1e (bottom right), for the non-doped sample. How do the authors explain this? 
How do the authors explain the co-existence of fibers and lattice planes? (A schematic would help the reader.) 
It looks like the PEGDA and EMIM-TFSI images have not been taken at a same defocus. (which could explain the variation
in micelle size and the lattice parameters). Can the authors study the object size at a similar defocus (for non-doped, and Li-
TFSI, PEGDA and EMIM-TFSI samples)? 
How can the authors be sure that Li-TFSI or EMIM-TFSI do not self-assemble (and are not at the origin of the so-called
lattice planes)? 
p17: The authors conclude that ionic dopant leads to a breakdown of micelles. This is not consistent with what is observed
in Figure 1. Elongated fibers are thinner in diameter with ionic dopant. But the fibers still show a periodic electronic contrast
along their main axis (which may be micelles). 
Isolated ‘micelles’ might even be seen in Figure 1n and 1p.

What is the defocus used for the cryo-EM of solid films (as compared to the solution cryo-EM)?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Ghasemi et al report on their characterization of different PEDOT:PSS formulation both in the solution and thin film using

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

electron microscopy. The authors show the hierarchical structure of PEDOT:PSS, with micelles, nanofibers, and
semicrystalline and disordered regions. I find the work very exciting. It is incredible to clearly see structural features in the
solutions and films that normally must be inferred from indirect measurements. This methodology is certainly the future of
polymer microstructural characterization, and I expect this work will be important in the field. 
However, the conclusions drawn from the analysis of the images are not entirely clear, and there are questions about the
sample preparation and the assumptions made when classifying the features seen in TEM. For this reason, I recommend a
revision prior to accepting into Nature Communications. My specific comments are below. 
First, the paper needs to explain what exactly is being imaged for the vitrified PEDOT:PSS dispersions. Presumably during
blotting, some of the water is absorbed into the paper, concentrating PEDOT:PSS in the dispersion. This likely drives the
aggregation into fibril structures that is observed. How representative are these images of the dispersion itself? 
Is there any
evidence for fibers forming in the as is dispersion before the blotting process? And how does the aggregation of
PEDOT:PSS micelles during blotting relate to aggregation during other common processing methods (spin-coating, blade-
coating, dropcasting, etc.)? 
It doesn’t appear that there is much variation in particle size within a sample, why is that? DLS characterization of particle
sizes in PEDOT:PSS dispersions have shown a wide dispersity [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcis.2020.09.070]. 
I’m a little confused about the proposed structure in Fig 1c. The cartoons show a micelle with a lot of messy, tangled
polymer, with a PEDOT rich core and PSS rich shell. However, the size of the micelle in the images is 2.7 nm up to 4.7 nm,
is on the order of 2-3x the PEDOT:PSS lamellar spacing distance when the molecules are tightly packed. How would all of
that polymer fit into such a small volume, especially when it isn’t tightly packed? In panel j, the micelle diameter is smaller
than two repeated lamellae of the shell of the nanofiber. Is this picture physically feasible? 
Also, in the cartoons, why are the PEDOT chains drawn as very short segments in the neat film, but very long (spanning
several micelles) in the secondary doping case? Shouldn’t the Mw of PEDOT:PSS be constant? What is the image intended
to convey? 
In the FFT insets in Fig 3a and Fig S3, there are several rings all the way out to ~3 nm-1. It is surprising to see so many
strong peaks since the GIWAXS 
~4 peaks which are fairly weak. What is the physical origin of these rings in the FFT
images? 
Why do the HRTEM images in Fig 3 look so different from images in Fig 2b and 2c? Is the imaging mode different? The
scale of the images in Fig 3 appear to be similar to the insets Fig 2. Does this mean some of the contrast in the low-res
images is due to imaging artefacts? 
While the paper shows several interesting modalities for cryo-EM characterization, there is very little continuity in the sample
choice. First, 4 different secondary dopants are investigated for solution imaging, but only two of the four are measured as
solid films. 
The authors then show humidity dependent crystal size but only apply the analysis to one formulation (Li-TFSI
secondary dopant) and compare the results to GISAX measured at two different humidities from the TEM experiment
humidities. Finally, they show SETM-EELS to explore where moisture is located within solid films, but the sample changes
again and the authors introduce PEDOT:PSS + Li-TFSI with salt-removed. A clear demonstration of how these techniques
complement one another and can be combined to build up a holistic microstructural picture for a single materials system
would be much more convincing than the current variety of samples explored here. I was also surprised that the PEGDA
doped PEDOT:PSS was not included in the mechanical analysis since the authors discussed in the introduction that
PEGDA is usually added to improve stretchability.
