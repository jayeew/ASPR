Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors investigated whether the reprogramming TFs turn on downstream genes that serve as a non-pre-
existing barrier to reprogramming. They found that three reprogramming TFs, Ngn2, Ascl1 and Neurod1, all turn on Olig2
very rapidly post transduction. They then go on to demonstrate that Olig2 is turned on in astrocytes, and that by knocking
down Olig2, more neurons are labeled by their tdTomato reporter (see major issue below). The tdTomato+ neurons are
electrically excitable, especially those in which Olig2 is first knocked down. Using scRNA-seq, the authors demonstrate that
Ngn2 and Ngn2+shOlig2 have distinct effects on gene expression within astrocytes, which can be broken down into 5
clusters. They further go onto to perform CUT&Tag to identify the Olig2 binding sites, and demonstrate that Olig2 binds to
and represses neurogenic genes, including Ngn2 itself. 
Taken together, this is a nice study, and the identification of Olig2 as a barrier to reprogramming is a novel finding. However,
there are several issues that need to be addressed prior to publication: 
Major issue: 
The authors have not acknowledged the issue with the AAV-GFAP vector, which is that when coupled to a neurogenic TF,
such as the bHLH TFs used herein, the GFAP promoter loses its astrocytic specificity and transgene expression is observed
in endogenous neurons (Revisiting astrocyte to neuron conversion with lineage tracing in vivo. Wang et al. 2021. Cell. 184,
5465–5481). Thus, for all the analyses of tdtomato+ cells in this study, especially after 7 days post-transduction, the majority
of the labeled cells will be endogenous neurons in which transgene expression has been activated. 
This caveat affects the following analyses and conclusions: 
1. To trace converted cells, it is essential to use the pre-labeling of glia, which was not done in this study. Thus, for Figure
2e-i, without assessing pre-labeled astrocytes, it is unclear to which extent the tdT+ cells were endogenous neurons that
expressed tdTomato, versus astrocytes that had converted into neurons. The loss of astrocytic marker co-expression with
tdTomato reflects at least in part a change in the promoter activity from driving astrocytic to neuronal expression. 
2. The electrophysiological studies in Figure 3 that targets tdtomato+ cells. For these analyses, while the shOlig2 improves
the electrophysiology of labeled neurons, it is not clear whether it has also affected the extent of endogenous neuronal
activity. These studies could be performed in a pre-glial labeling model, with tdTomato and the pre-labeled glia (e.g., GFP+)
targeted for recordings. 
Finally, it is essential that the Wang et al. study above is highlighted, and the review by Magdalena Gotz that highlights a
Call for Obligatory Controls and Standards. 
Bocchi R, Masserdotti G, Götz M. Direct neuronal reprogramming: Fast forward from new concepts toward therapeutic
approaches. Neuron. 2022 Feb 2;110(3):366-393. doi: 10.1016/j.neuron.2021.11.023. Epub 2021 Dec 18. PMID: 34921778.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Review of Lai et al. on Olig2 as a Barrier to Ngn2-Mediated Astrocyte-to-Neuron Conversion 
Summary: 
Lai et al. present a study investigating the role of Olig2 in astrocyte-to-neuron (AtN) conversion mediated by proneural
transcription factors (TFs). Olig2 has previously been highlighted as a potential barrier to neuronal fate conversion following
cortical stab wound injury (Buffo A, et al. 2005. PNAS). Here Lai et al. make a very interesting observation that Olig2 is
upregulated in astrocytes following forced AAV-mediated expression of bLHL proneural TFs (Ngn2, Ascl1, Nd1) in the
absence of injury and propose that here too, Olig2 may act as a inducible barrier to neuronal conversion. While this is an
intriguing idea, we find significant issues in the experimental evidence and interpretations. Without addressing these
concerns, the central claims of the paper cannot be substantiated. 
Major Concerns: 
1. Lack of Robust Evidence for Astrocyte-to-Neuron Conversion 
The central claim of the paper is that downregulation of Olig2, combined with ectopic expression of proneural bHLH TFs
(Ngn2, Ascl1, NeuroD1), induces AtN conversion in adult mouse cortical astrocytes. However, the evidence presented is
insufficient to establish true lineage conversion for the following reasons: 
• Contextual Regulation of hGFAP Promoter: The hGFAP mini-promoter in the AAV vector system is now well appreciated to
show variable and highly contextual expression in endogenous neurons (Wang et al., 2021, Cell; Le et al., 2022, Front. Cell.
Dev. Biol.; Xie et al., 2023, eBioMedicine; Enbar et al, 2024, Sci. Rep.). Thus, the observation of viral reporter protein
(tdTomato) fluorescence in NeuN+ cells is not sufficient evidence to demonstrate the cells arise from astrocytes. 
• Alternative Hypotheses Not Addressed: The authors argue that the increased percentage of tdT+S100B-NeuN+ cells
(neurons) and reduced percentage of tdT+S100B+NeuN- (astrocytes) observed following Olig2 downregulation (but not in
scrambled shRNA control) is evidence for astrocyte to neuron conversion. However, the authors do not consider any other
alternative hypotheses. For example, these data would also result from the death of astrocytes following the intervention with
leaky expression of tdTomato in endogenous neurons. 
• Absence of Robust Fate-Mapping Experiments: To validate the claim of AtN conversion, it is imperative that - at a minimum
- robust, viral vector independent fate-mapping are performed. Ideally, these should use well validated tools such as
Aldh1l1-CreERT2 mice crossed with Cre-reporter lines. The current experiments do not exclude the possibility of
fluorophore expression in endogenous neurons or other non-astrocytic cells. Given the importance of the claim the paper is
making, we would also recommend that efforts are made to label endogenous neurons prior to AAV injections to confirm that
tdT is not being expressed in endogenous neurons. There are several ways researchers could go about this (e.g. using

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

transgenic excitatory or pan-neuronal cre-driver lines with a GFP/YFP reporter, EdU/BrDU labelling in utero, or using
additional viral tools). 
• No Evidence for Intermediate Stages: In figure 2L, authors present a schematic of astrocytes passing via an intermediate
stage to neurons, however, this is highly misleading as no substantive evidence has been presented for intermediate stages.
Whilst authors note that there is an increased number of tdT+S100B-NeuN- cells when Olig2 is downregulated, the absence
of S100B is not of itself evidence that these transduced cells will go on to acquire neuronal properties and there is no
positive identification of immature neuronal genes (e.g. Dcx) in the histology or scRNA dataset provided. As such, it the
tdT+S100B-NeuN- cells have not been identified and should not be presented alone as an intermediate step of a conversion
process as there may be other explanations (e.g. we note that in the scRNA dataset many Oligodendrocyte lineage cells
express the viral transcript. Alternatively, astrocyte toxicity may result in absence of detection of S100B downregulation, etc).
Finally, there is no morphological characterisation or clear imaging data presented that would corroborate an intermediate or
immature neuronal stage. 
• scRNA-seq Analysis: Authors self-report that they were unable to collect reprogrammed neurons that express viral
transcripts. They speculate this may be for technical reasons or due to limited cell numbers converted. Either way, this data
is not capable of supporting the central claim of AtN conversion. Additionally, the scRNA-seq analysis revealed the
presence of multiple cell types, including mural cells, endothelial cells, microglia, and oligodendroglia (which express viral
transcripts at broadly levels similar to astrocytes). This could demonstrate a lack of specificity in the AAV targeting astrocytes
or issues around cell sorting. Together these technical issues somewhat limits what can be concluded from these data but
there are still some interesting observations. Namely, downregulation of Olig2 along with AAV-mediated expression of Ngn2
seems to result in a more neural stem-cell like transcriptome, however, this should not be over-interpreted this as a step
towards to neuronal conversion, given that currently no evidence is provided for a trajectory towards lineage converted or
immature neurons. 
2. Issues with Olig2’s Role and Mechanistic Insight 
• CuT&Tag Analysis of Olig2 Binding: The authors attempt to investigate Olig2’s function as a reprogramming barrier using
CuT&Tag experiments. Surprisingly, the canonical Olig2 binding site is not enriched in the genomic regions identified as
bound by Olig2. Whilst we accept that this could suggest Olig2 binds non-canonical sites, the data are more plausibly
explained by a failure of the CuT&Tag experiment. Promoters are enriched in GC regions, and the 27% of enriched regions
containing Gs or Cs likely reflect promoter capture rather than specific Olig2 binding. To support the authors claim of Olig2
binding non-canonical sites, the authors should include additional replicates of the CuT&Tag experiment and validate
binding through ChIP-qPCR or band shift assays for targets lacking canonical E-box sites. 
4. Additional concerns/observations: 
• Marker Expression Over Time: The authors claim that the hGFAP in AAV9 is "highly specific" for targeting astrocytes.
However, the authors find that even following conversion (and at late, 45dpi, timepoints) there is expression of tdTomato in
putative converted NeuN+ cells. It is unclear how the authors rationalise ongoing tdTomato expression in reprogrammed
neurons if the hGFAP promoter is specific for astrocytes? How can authors distinguish between genuinely converted cells
and mislabelled (i.e. leaky tdTomato expression in) endogenous neurons in experiments (e.g. in electrophysiology)? 
Recommendations: 
To address these concerns, the authors should: 
1. Include Fate-Mapping Experiments: Use transgenic mouse lines (e.g., Aldh1l1-CreERT2) with Cre-reporter systems to
ensure the origin of induced neurons is astrocytic and to exclude targeting of endogenous neurons. 
2. Demonstrate that tdTomato is not expressed in endogenous neurons: (or characterise the extent of this and show how
authors distinguish between these and astrocyte-derived cells). E.g. label endogenous neurons prior to AAV injections (e.g.,
using pan-neuronal Cre-driver lines or BrdU/EdU labeling) to confirm tdT is not expressed in pre-existing neurons. 
3. Show morphology: Microscopy should demonstrate the morphology of putative lineage converted cells, ideally with some
intermediate/immature morphological stages. 
4. Use Intermediate Markers: Demonstrate the progression of reprogramming using markers (e.g. DCX) for
intermediate/immature neuronal stages. 
5. Replicate & validate CuT&Tag result: Authors should do further work on this aspect as the current claims are strong
considering that failure to identify canonical Olig2 binding sites may be consistent with experimental failure. 
Conclusion: 
Lai et al. provide an interesting observation regarding Olig2 upregulation during ectopic expression of proneural TFs in
astrocytes. However, the evidence presented does not support the claim of astrocyte-to-neuron conversion or establish
Olig2’s role as a barrier to reprogramming. Since this is the central claim of the paper, more experimental work is needed
before this could be published. If the claims made were confirmed to an appropriate standard, the findings would be of
considerable interest.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Our primary concern with the manuscript revolved around unsatisfactory scientific evidence for the central claim of the paper,
namely that AAV-mediated expression of Neurogenin2 and Olig2 knockdown converts adult cortical astrocytes into neurons.
Due to widespread acknowledgement that many molecular tools to genetically label astrocytes suffer from “leaky”
expression in neurons, robust scientific evidence is required to ensure labelled neurons are derived from astrocytes.
Unfortunately, whilst the authors have acknowledged these concerns to some extent, we find the quality and nature of
evidence and rebuttal provided in response to our concerns continue to fall short of providing sufficiently robust evidence to
support the central claims of the paper. 
1- Lack of Robust Evidence for Astrocyte-to-Neuron Conversion 
Some of our concerns involving neuronal expression from the hGFAP promoter have received attention in the discussion.
However, authors were unable to provide additional evidence to either demonstrate that reporter transgene labeled neurons
do not arise from endogenous neurons (see recommendation 2) or quantify the extent of this. 
Authors did attempt to follow Recommendation 1, to perform fate-mapping of putative reprogrammed neurons. However, the
experimental design chosen raises additional concerns and ultimately fails to provide compelling evidence of lineage
conversion. We outline these concerns below: 
- In Figure 4 in the amended manuscript, authors remove the fluorescent reporter from the viral constructs. They use a Ai14
transgenic reporter cre line with the Aldh1l1-CreERT2. 
- This is a highly surprising choice of experimental design, since it would have been far more intuitive to use the same viral
vector used elsewhere in the paper with a YFP/GFP cre-reporter line. The rationale has not been discussed. 
- Authors point out that there is a modest increase (from around 5 to 12% of tdTomato+ cells that express NeuN in Olig2
knockdown condition compared to Ng2 alone. This is the primary basis for the claim that these cells are astrocyte derived. 
- However, one consequence of this experimental approach is that there is now no way from these data presented to identify
whether neurons analyzed were even transduced by the AAV. As such the authors are not fate-mapping viral transduced
cells per se, or at least no evidence is provided to demonstrate these were viral transduced cells. No discussion or evidence
is provided that even explains how authors knew that the regions analyzed were from the viral injection site, nor how many
cells have been analyzed. 
- Ai14 is generally appreciated to be more easily recombined that some other cre-reporter lines (e.g. Álvarez-Aznar, et al.
Transgenic Res. 2020) and can lead to some neurons being labelled with tdTomato+. 
While authors have shown the vast
majority of tdTomato+ cells stain for astrocyte markers, they have not quantified the percentage of tdTomato+ cells in this line
that express NeuN+ in control AAV or no virus conditions. 
- Finally, if there were rare recombination events in endogenous neurons (resulting in small fraction of endogenous neurons
expressing tdTomato) then an alternative hypothesis for a reasonably small increase in the percentage of tdTomato+NeuN+
cells reported could be a modest increase in astrocyte cell death due to Ng2 expression and Olig2 knockdown. 
- A robust approach would present clear evidence of co-expression of viral reporter and a genetic label of astrocytes and
improved quantification. At minimum, this would include the proportion of neurons expressing AAVs that co-express lineage
tracing reporter and compare this to the overall recombination efficiency of the line across all conditions (control, Ng2+shNC
and Ng2+shOlig2). Currently, the evidence presented is ambiguous and difficult to interpret although potentially promising. 
Aside from concerns over demonstrating the astrocyte origins of NeuN+tdT+ neurons (both in the original manuscript and in

the revised lineage tracing figure), we had several concerns around absence of compelling evidence for immature or
intermediate neuronal phenotypes, the presence of which would both support lineage conversion as well as fill mechanistic
gaps. We discuss the authors response to these below. 
Whilst the high-resolution confocal images are somewhat useful (recommendation 3), the crop and lack of morphological
analysis do not greatly advance the underlying point that motivated this recommendation. In other words, these imaging data
could still be consistent with gradual accumulation of expression of viral reporter transgenes in pre-existing endogenous
neurons. However, since the authors do not rely on morphological analysis to make their central claim, this remains a minor
problem – although had they provided more solid morphological characterization it could have given the central claim
considerably more weight. 
More pressingly, we raised concerns about the absence of robust evidence of immature or transitional molecular neuronal
phenotypes which must presumably exist for a bona fide conversion process. The timepoint of scRNA sequencing
corresponds to the peak of the so-called "intermediate stage" (as defined by 2i), when neuronal commitment should already
be underway. Absence of early neuronal genes (e.g., Dcx, Sox11, Neurod1) in the scRNA data is disappointing. Authors
acknowledge failure to capture reprogrammed neurons expressing viral transcripts in scRNA-seq, citing technical
limitations, early sampling (~10% conversion efficiency), and fragility of induced neurons during dissociation. However,
authors also failed to detect Dcx at the protein level – which would not be affected by this technical limitation. Instead,
authors found evidence of Vimentin and Nestin expression in both scRNA-seq and validated at the protein level. Both
Vimentin and Nestin are broad progenitor markers, insufficient to support neuronal lineage commitment or maturation. These
markers may well indicate a stem-like or reactive astrocyte state but do not demonstrate transition toward a neuronal fate.
Overall, the new immunohistochemistry data match very well with the scRNA-seq data and could support a hypothesis that
Ng2 + shOlig2 may induce astrocyte reactivity or stem-cell like progenitor state, but don’t contribute further evidence of
neuronal fate commitment or conversion. 
We do acknowledge that the authors comment that observations of some single spiking neurons constitute evidence of
neuronal conversion. It would have been useful to present these traces in the main figure to assess the quality of that claim.
However, we don’t believe that this supersedes the need for additional evidence to support claims that observed neurons
derive from astrocytes. 
2 - Issues with Olig2’s Role and Mechanistic Insight 
While we acknowledge that CUT&Tag was an appropriate method given the limited number of isolated cells, the authors do
not provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the results reflect genuine Olig2 binding in astrocytes, rather than
technical artifacts. As such, any conclusions drawn from this experiment are not adequately supported. Although the Olig2
CUT&Tag data in oligodendrocytes may be valid and potentially informative for understanding the differential role of Olig2 in
oligodendrocytes versus astrocytes, no definitive conclusions can be drawn from the astrocyte data. 
Conducting CUT&Tag in a different model system does not constitute strong evidence that the original CUT&Tag
experiment in astrocytes was successful. Although motif enrichment analysis identifies the closest matching motifs, the
reported enrichment of Sp1 and Klf—both of which are G/C-rich and commonly located in promoter regions—is not sufficient
to validate the experiment. Promoter regions are typically accessible and may be overrepresented in assays such as ChIP-
seq, CUT&RUN, or CUT&Tag when the target protein does not directly bind DNA or when antibody specificity is
compromised. Thus, the presence of these motifs alone cannot be taken as evidence of successful Olig2 binding in
astrocytes. A second biological replicate of the same experiment, or complementary binding assays as previously
suggested, would be required to substantiate these findings.
