Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents an interesting tackling of the important and timely problem of CO2 capture using responsive
organic solids. Simple monoalkylated monoethanolamines (Cn-MEAs), with the best performing of them being already
reported by some of the authors in ref. 37, allow an efficient, scalable, and cheap strategy for the capture of CO2 at ambient
conditions and desorption at mild and accessible temperatures. The work could potentially be interesting for the broad
audience of Nature Communications, however several aspects of the work need to be revised before being published. The
major concern is the validity of the “chain melt” hypothesis. 
Here below I have listed the issues to be addressed before considering the manuscript further for publication. 
1) “Chain melting” simply refers to order-disorder phase transitions involving alkyl side chains/fragments. See some
literature examples: (1) Nat. Commun. 2015, 6, 8810; (2) Nat. Commun. 2023, 14, 1304; (3) J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2024, 146,
46, 31911–31919. 
If the authors want to use this qualitative definition, as it is already used in the literature (see ref 45 for example), I suggest to
clearly define what “chain melting” means because it could be confusing for the readership of Nature Communications. 
In ref 45, “chain melting” refers to order-disorder transitions in asymmetric resulting in disorder phases that were not fully
structurally characterized because of the side-chain disorder and twinning of the crystalline samples. In the present
manuscript, the authors seem to refer to the “chain melting” as a qualitative mechanistic understanding of the phase change,
see Scheme 1. In particular, the authors write: “This is attributed to the exothermicity of the CO2 uptake process (~50 kJ/mol)
that is sufficient to induce chain melting (m.p. of C10-MEA = 38.1 °C) and initiate recrystallization to C10-MEA-CO2. The
crystal phase change qualitatively occurs without liquefaction, resulting in a solid-to-solid transition to form a crystalline CO2
adduct.” Indeed, they were able to solve both phases (also thanks to the great facilities at their disposal), and while the CO2-
loaded crystals show indeed disorder, they do not appear to be melted in any way. To corroborate the fully crystalline nature
of the loaded compound (and somehow contradicting their mechanistic model), the authors write: “Single-pulse MAS 13C
NMR experiments highlight the high mobility of C10-MEA, while C10-MEA-CO2 was characterized by broader, although still
relatively sharp and well-defined 13C resonances, being indicative of increased rigidity and formation of a more crystalline
structure. A single peak in the carbonyl region (158 ppm) was assigned to the CO2 adduct as a carbamate. A single-pulse
1H MAS NMR experiment revealed high mobility in pristine C10-MEA, characterized by the sharp peaks in the spectrum,
whereas significant broadening of the spectrum of C10-MEA-CO2 indicated markedly increased rigidity, confirming a
structural distinction from the parent compound.” To obtain a more informative picture of the order-disorder mechanism, the
authors should turn their attention to different experimental techniques such as low-frequency Raman spectroscopy and/or
THz spectroscopy, see for example the works of Omer Yaffe (Weizmann Institute of Science) and Michael Ruggiero
(University of Rochester). 
2) Given the substantial discussion of porous and guest-responsive crystals, please engage more fully with foundational
work from Susumu Kitagawa and co-workers on soft, porous crystals/flexible MOFs and related gate-opening/breathing
phenomena, to situate the present contribution within that context. A brief paragraph in the Introduction would help readers
outside the subfield. 
3) Figure 2 panel C the molecular structures are barely readable. Increase their size and/or change their color. Additionally,
it is not particularly clear when the desorption process starts and why it is happening at different times for the various
molecules.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents a study on nonporous hydrophobic organic crystals (alkylated 
monoethanolamines, e.g., C10-MEA) for CO₂ capture via a reversible solid-to-solid phase 
transition, which is relevant to advancing carbon capture technologies. Below are specific 
comments to strengthen the work.

Response: Thank you for the comments. For regeneration, based on our  thermal 
analysis (TGA and  DSC analysis), it is evident that the molecule undergoes rapid and 
significant changes in terms of heat exchange - fusion and recrystallization. Since we 
couldn’t record any observable surface area, we concluded that the compound is not 
porous therefore, the observed CO2 capture performance should be relied on the diffusion 
of CO2 gas on the surface , adsorb, and then infuse in to  the crystals to induce 
rearrangement of the whole crystal, affording quantitative structural changes as observed 
by all spectroscopic analysis as shown in the manuscript.  
Figure 4F shows the isothermal carbon sorption process using a BET surface analyzer. 
We couldn’t record desorption steps under isothermal conditions (0-25 oC), which is also 
an indication that the mild heating is necessary for efficient desorption. To make it clear, 
this was not to confuse the regeneration method, but it is merely to accommodate a 
common characterization method for carbon capture materials to show the capacity.   
For absorption and desorption cycles, the material performs better under continuous feed 
of exchangeable gas – it could be N2 or CO2, or a mixture.  
For application, it is plausible to plug in our capture method to biogas carbon capture for 
biogas upgrading, where the temperature of the flue gas is much lower. Even for a post-
combustion CO2 capture scenario, we can envision using our capture method after heat 
exchange of the flue gas down to 40 -60 °C, where a very narrow TSA will allow us to 
regenerate the sorbent. For this, we are currently working on the stability of C10 -MEA 
and other molecules for SOx and NOx conditions.
