Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors reported an electrocatalytic system to synthesize amine from HMF and methylamine using Ag
nanoparticles (denoted as Ag NPs). Although the Faraday efficiency and production rate are considerable, this reaction is
not novel and extremely non-challenging. The most critical issue is that the C-N coupling reaction is likely to be a
spontaneous reaction process, where the electrocatalyst mainly completes the hydrogenation reaction step, which conflicts
with the proposed mechanism in this manuscript. As a result, we can not recommend that the current form of the manuscript
be published on the Nature Communications. The following questions should be considered and be clearly reported: 
1. Given the well-documented rapidity of amine-aldehyde condensation reactions under ambient conditions, a critical
question arises regarding the authors' reported system containing concurrent HMF and methylamine: does the imine
intermediate predominate in the reaction medium under these conditions? To conclusively address this mechanistic
consideration, the authors should provide supporting evidence including (but not limited to) time-resolved 1H NMR
spectroscopic characterization of the reaction mixture, complemented by comparative kinetic studies and/or mass
spectrometric analysis of intermediate species. 
2. A second mechanistic concern pertains to the potential influence of stoichiometric parameters. Specifically, the rationale
for selecting the current methylamine/HMF molar ratio requires clarification. Have the authors systematically explored
reactant stoichiometry optimization (e.g., elevated methylamine concentrations)? To substantiate the claimed catalytic
specificity, control experiments comparing Faradaic efficiencies across (i) varied methylamine concentrations and (ii)
different metal electrodes (including non-noble counterparts) should be supplemented, alongside detailed kinetic isotope
effect analyses. 
3. The obvious difference between Figure 4c and Figure 4d is puzzling. Even if there are differences in the dominant
adsorption configurations on the catalyst surface, it does not mean that other adsorption configurations do not exist.
However, we could not observe any signals around ~1024 cm-1 which could be attributed to the furan ring of C-H, we
recommend that the authors clarify this issue clearly. 
4. Given the established morphological sensitivity of electrocatalytic systems (e.g., Ag nanoparticles vs. nanocubes
differentially exposing {111}/{100} facets), has rigorous morphological characterization (including crystallographic orientation
and particle size distribution) been performed to ensure structural homogeneity across the metal electrodes in Figure S1?
This is particularly critical as substrate-dependent variations in active site geometry and surface energy landscapes could
artificially modulate Faradaic efficiency trends. 
5. Computing energies of adsorbed species uses the harmonic approximation, which can be very sensitive to low-frequency
modes. If the used cutoff is too small then the entropy of the adsorbate will be substantially over-estimated. The authors do
compute the energy, and the many difference is only 0.2 eV, which is much close to the typical DFT error of ~0.2 eV, and this
conclusion could easily change if a different functional or U value is used. In other words, it is not conclusive evidence. 
6. Computational Refinement: Although the theoretical calculations align with experimental outcomes, they fail to rationalize
the high selectivity toward MAMF. We suggest: (i) Incorporating energy profiles for methylamine’s nucleophilic attack on the
aldehyde group to quantify activation barriers. (ii) Comparing energy differences between hydrogenation and nucleophilic
pathways to elucidate competitive reaction dynamics. 
7. Substrate Scope Expansion: Additional substrate screening experiments are necessary to validate the universality of their
design strategy for aldehyde-based C–N coupling reactions. Testing structurally diverse aldehydes would clarify whether the
observed mechanism is generalizable.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports on modulating the adsorption behavior of substrates by tailoring the surface structure of silver (Ag)
catalysts, thereby significantly enhancing the electrochemical C–N coupling of biomass-derived 5-hydroxymethylfurfural
(HMF) with methylamine to produce 5-[(methylamino)methyl]-2-furanmethanol (MAMF). The optimized Ag nanoparticles
(NPs), predominantly exposing the (111) facet, exhibit an impressive Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 89.2% and a selectivity of
90.1% for C–N coupling at −0.45 V vs. RHE. This performance is among the best reported to date. Systematic in situ
spectroscopic investigations combined with DFT calculations reveal that HMF adsorbs onto the Ag(111) surface in a distinct
η¹(C)-aldehyde configuration. This specific adsorption mode increases the electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon, facilitating
C=O bond polarization and promoting nucleophilic attack by methylamine, thereby enhancing catalytic activity. Overall, this
work offers a novel strategy for modulating reactant adsorption to achieve selective electrochemical organic synthesis. It
holds the potential to inspire the development of high-performance electrocatalysts. I recommend the publication after
revisions, as outlined in the following comments. 
1.Since the Ag NPs and Ag NCs exhibit different morphologies, their electrochemically active surface areas are likely to
differ, which could affect their catalytic performance. The authors are encouraged to provide ECSA data to clarify whether
this factor plays a substantial role in the observed activity differences. 
2.The effect of methylamine concentration on the reaction performance is not discussed. As the adsorption of methylamine
may also influence the C-N coupling process, the authors should consider including a discussion or experimental results
addressing this variable. 
3.The manuscript presents charge-transfer resistance values derived from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS),
but lacks information about the fitting procedure, including the equivalent circuit used and the associated standard errors.
Providing these details would strengthen the credibility of the analysis. 
4.The manuscript reports selectivity values, but does not clarify whether these are calculated based on carbon-containing
products, nitrogen-containing products, or total product yield. The authors should explicitly state the basis for the selectivity
calculation. 
5.The authors present a comparison of linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves for the C-N coupling and HMF reduction
reactions on Ag NPs (Supplementary Fig. 14). However, the corresponding data for Ag NCs are missing. Including this
comparison would provide a more complete picture of the performance differences between the two catalysts. 
6.Several key experimental procedures are insufficiently described. For instance, the rotary steaming step used before liquid
product quantification (mentioned in Supplementary Fig. 11) is not included in the Methods section, and specific operating
parameters are not provided. Additionally, variables used in Equations (2)-(6) are not clearly defined. The authors should
revise the manuscript to address these omissions. 
7.Error bars are absent in the Faradaic efficiency data presented in Fig. 3e,f. To demonstrate reproducibility and data
reliability, the authors should include error bars. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript describes tailoring the surface structure of Ag catalysts to modulate HMF adsorption behavior significantly
enhances C–N coupling selectivity. It presents a compelling study on facet-dependent C–N coupling but requires revisions
to address methodological gaps, clarify mechanistic claims, and contextualize performance metrics. Following are some
comments on this work. 
1. The manuscript is generally well-structured, but the figures and schemes need clearer labeling. For example, Scheme I
(page 3) is referenced but not included in the main text, which may confuse readers. Figure 2d and 2e are described as
showing "potential-dependent production rate" and "stability tests," but the axes labels in the figure are unclear. Please
ensure all axes and legends are explicitly defined. 
2. The introduction lacks a focused discussion on recent advances in electrochemical C–N coupling, particularly for
biomass-derived substrates. Highlighting specific challenges in HMF amination (e.g., overhydrogenation, dimerization) and
how this work addresses them would sharpen the novelty. 
3. While facet-dependent adsorption modulation is not novel, its application to electrochemical C-N coupling of HMF is
innovative. However, the introduction lacks a critical comparison with recent works on similar systems (e.g., Cu or Pd
catalysts for HMF upgrading). A table comparing FE/selectivity with prior studies would better contextualize the
advancements. 
4. The synthesis methods for Ag NP and Ag NC are well-documented, but the absence of TEM/SEM images in the main text
(relegated to Supplementary Figs. 2–3) weakens the immediate validation of morphology. Key characterization data (e.g.,
lattice spacing, XRD peaks) should be highlighted in the main text. Stability tests over 10 cycles (Fig. 2e, f) are insufficient to
claim long-term durability. Additional data (e.g., 24-hour chronoamperometry) would strengthen the practical relevance. 
5. The authors attribute the high selectivity to η¹(C)-aldehyde adsorption but do not fully address how methylamine
adsorption influences the reaction. Competitive adsorption between HMF and methylamine could alter pathways; this should
be discussed. Product quantification relies on LC/GC, but potential intermediates (e.g., aldimine) are not tracked. Including
time-dependent product distribution would clarify reaction kinetics.

6. The DFT calculations lack methodological details (e.g., functional, basis set, slab models). A brief description in the main

7. The proposed mechanism assumes direct nucleophilic attack by methylamine on adsorbed HMF but does not address the
role of protonation states or pH effects. Experiments varying pH could elucidate how proton availability impacts aldimine

8. The manuscript does not consider potential surface reconstruction of Ag NPs/NCs under operating conditions. XPS or

In reviewing the responses, we note that the authors are trying to improve their work and have corrected several issues.
However, our major concerns remain unresolved. We still hold the view that the authors seem to be insufficiently informed
regarding the issues we highlighted in our previous comment. Moreover, the revisions to the mechanistic discussion and
theoretical calculations lack justification, which is highly irresponsible and further exacerbates our concerns regarding the
reliability of the data. The following comments should be taken for more consideration. 
1. The authors emphasize that the difference in exposed crystal facets between Ag NPs and Ag NCs is key to altering
selectivity, suggesting that the (111) facet dominates the high selectivity in hydrogenation reactions. However, due to facet
heterogeneity, we believe that this interpretation overstates the data. The potential contribution of the (200) facet, or even
involvement of the (220) facet, cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, synergistic effects between different crystal facets may also
play a role. Clearly, attributing the regulation of selectivity solely to a single facet is unreasonable. 
2. The authors propose that the adsorption pattern of furan on the Ag surface is critical for selectivity. However, the current
experimental evidence may not sufficiently support this claim. Therefore, we believe the authors should expand the
substrate scope and verify the generality of their proposed synthetic strategy, particularly by exploring furan-related
structures. 
3. Regarding the experiment tracking intermediates via 1H NMR, we find the results puzzling. If the imine is a key
intermediate, its concentration should remain relatively stable during continuous generations. The authors need to explain
this observation. 
4. Additionally, concerning the imine intermediate, we suggest that the authors should directly use it as a reactant in
electrocatalytic selective reduction to validate the mechanism. We are particularly concerned about whether this species can
remain stable under alkaline electrolytic conditions. 
5. Furthermore, the central challenge of this reaction remains unclear. While the authors assert that controlling selectivity is a
major obstacle in the direct reduction process, this issue would be bypassed by using the pre-synthesized imine as the
substrate. Moreover, the formation of the imine itself does not appear difficult, and the authors acknowledge its spontaneous
formation under specific conditions. Hence, the significance of this study remains unclear to us. 
The authors should address the aforementioned questions seriously and directly and persuade me about the reliability and
validity of all the data. Additionally, the findings presented here starkly diverge from the rigorous expectations and stringent
standards that are synonymous with the high-impact, prestigious Nature Communications. These discrepancies underscore
a significant departure from the normative benchmarks that the scientific community anticipates from publications within this
esteemed domain, highlighting a need for alignment with the journal's commitment to publishing only the most
groundbreaking and impactful research.

Response to R1 -5: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comments and apologize for not 
clearly articulating the central challenge and innovation of our work. We agree that employing 
pre-synthesized imines is a convenient strategy in thermocatalysis to bypass the competing 
hydrogenation pathways of HMF. However, under electrocatalytic conditions , the situation is 
different. During electrolysis, OH – is continuously generated and accumulates at the electrode –
electrolyte interface, leading to a markedly elevated l ocal pH ( ≈12.6 at 20 mA cm –2). The 
increased local alkalinity promotes hydrolysis of the imine back to HMF and methylamine, thereby 
redirecting the reaction network toward direct amination of HMF with m ethylamine and leading 
to BHMF and BHH formation. In addition, at high interfacial imine concentrations, competing 
self-condensation of imine molecules can yield diamine and triamine  by-products, further 
complicating the product distribution and diminishing overall selectivity. Such over-condensation 
behavior has been documented not only in thermocatalytic systems but also under electrocatalytic 
conditions (Chem Catal. 2023, 3, 100500; Chem. Commun. 2023, 59, 4818–4821; Org. Biomol. 
Chem. 2020, 18, 5832 –5837). Moreover, some imines exhibit lower solubility in aqueous 
electrolytes than the corresponding amines and aldehydes, which restricts their effective interfacial 
concentration and further limits their applicability as isolated substrates. 
From a practi cal standpoint, the conventional synthesis of the imine requires refluxing in 
organic solvents at elevated temperature for several hours, resulting in relatively low productivity. 
By contrast, our strategy enables a faster, simpler, and more selective route by directly constructing 
the C–N bond via in situ control of the HMF adsorption configuration on Ag catalysts, without the 
need to isolate and handle a metastable imine intermediate. 
To substantiate these arguments, we synthesized the imine and exami ned its stability in 
electrolytes of different pH by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 24). The imine 
remained largely intact at pH 11.0, corresponding to the bulk K 2HPO3 electrolyte used in our 
electrocatalytic experiments. However, at pH 12.6 , representative of the locally alkaline 
environment near the cathode at 20 mA cm –2, the 1H NMR spectra revealed gradual, partial 
hydrolysis, indicating reduced stability under these more strongly alkaline conditions. This 
observation implies that when a pre-synthesized imine is used as the starting substrate, a significant 
fraction will hydrolyze back to HMF under operating conditions, effectively steering the reaction 
network back toward direct coupling between HMF and methylamine at the catalyst –electrolyte 
interface.
