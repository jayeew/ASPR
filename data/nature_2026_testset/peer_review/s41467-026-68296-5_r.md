Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
A sufficiently detailed understanding with new quantification of the dynamics of temporal pathways of LLPS of polymers, as
influenced by initial state of the ensemble of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes in salt-free aqueous solution is reported.
The results are original and of significance to a broad audience across various fields. 
The DPD method of simulation used in correct (including the hydrodynamic pumping) and the methods are appropriate. The
system (simulation box) dimensions, polymer chain lengths, DPD parameters for chain structure and charges, the simulation
times, are all appropriate and consistent for the right representation of a reasonable physical system. 
All necessary details are provided appropriately in the manuscript and supplemental information section. The paper is well
written. The theoretical model (Poisson's equation) for the 1-D 
flux-pathway is simple yet provides an explanation within the
scope of this study, some theoretical basis for understanding the origins of the dynamical behavior for this particular case. 
The work carried out in this study supports the conclusions and claims of the authors, and there is no additional evidence
needed. This work meets the expected standards in the field, and also in suitable for the journal Nature Communications.
The work is relevant to the field of synthetic polyelectrolytes, polymer science and engineering, natural sciences, and to a
little extent (though restricted extent and in a qualitative sense) to processes that occur in biology. 
The noteworthy results of this study are the new dynamical scalings of LLPS of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes towards
the formation of polyelectrolyte complexes (PEC) reported for the flux-pathway and for the well-mixed pathway (which is
driven by network formation initially itself), and the distinctly different dynamical scalings observed both these pathways for
initial and later phases of PEC formation. 
These results are remarkable and useful to further our understanding of the
dynamics of formation of such complex systems that occur in nature. 
I recommend publication in the present form.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Manuscript Summary and Key Findings 
In this manuscript, the authors present a study on the effects of mixing protocols on the dynamics of liquid-liquid phase
separation of polyelectrolyte complex coacervates. The authors use molecular dynamics simulations to evaluate the domain
growth of the coacervate droplets for two distinct initial mixing conditions. The first is a well-mixed solution of oppositely
charged polyelectrolytes. The second scenario involves a system where the polycations and polyanions are separated into
domains that are put in contact with each other, the authors called this scenario the flux pathway. 
The authors found that the two initial mixing scenarios produced different domain growth behaviors. The first scenario, the
well mixed pathway, showed the formation of a network structure in the early stages that would then break down into
droplets which eventually merge into a single droplet. In the flux pathway, coacervate droplets will form at the interface
between the two domains, which will then merge and draw in free chains to grow the domains. The well mixed pathway
showed early-stage growth with scaling of t1/2, and the flux pathway showed an early-stage domain growth with scaling t2/3.
The authors also evaluated the polymer concentration effects on the domain growth mechanism and dynamics and found
that the network structure that leads to enhanced early-stage growth requires a sufficiently high concentration to observe the
enhanced growth dynamics. The authors showed that the initial mixing conditions can be used to accelerate coacervation
dynamics, and the implications of system size are discussed for the various initial mixing conditions. 
Review 
This manuscript provides valuable insight into the dynamics of polyelectrolyte coacervation that will be of considerable
interest to the coacervate and polyelectrolyte complex communities. The findings in this paper are, to my knowledge, novel
and important. I believe the methods used by the authors are valid for the study, and the findings presented within the
manuscript are well reasoned and supported by the data. The manuscript is clear and concise, and provides the context
necessary to understand the significance and motivations of the study. The references cited in the manuscript are
appropriate and many are recent. The information provided in the supplementary materials is appropriately placed as
supplemental and provides more details regarding the simulation methods and data analysis. The methods have been
sufficiently described to allow for reproduction of the study by other researchers. 
The modeling and simulation methods used in this study was developed and previously published by the corresponding
authors, and addresses some key issues known within community. Molecular dynamics simulations of complex coacervates
can be complicated by the length and timescale requirements to observe phase separation, as well as the nature of the hard
cores of the Lennard Jones potential and the treatment of bulky ions as point charges within the center of the ion bead. The
Gaussian Core Model with Smeared Electrostatic Interactions methodology presented by the authors addresses these three
potential issues while maintaining the ability to evaluate dynamics and consider hydrodynamic interactions. This
methodology could be used by other researchers studying coacervation and other solutions of charged macromolecules,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

particularly for out of equilibrium and dynamics studies. 
It is my belief that the data from which the scaling exponents are drawn from are high quality and robust. The authors in this
manuscript provide well-reasoned explanations with mathematical descriptions for some of the findings in the study. For
example, they found two different late-stage domain growth dynamics for the well mixed pathway that they attributed to the
initial degree of mixing. The authors provided a method for evaluating the degree of mixing and found that indeed the value
for this degree of mixing correlated with the late-stage domain growth dynamics. Another example of the providing rigorous
explanations for their findings can be found in their explanation for the driving force that leads to the t2/3 domain growth
scaling observed in the flux pathway. The authors mathematically described the electrochemical potential and showed how
the potential difference between the interface and the two initial domains decreases over time to lead to a reduction in the
domain growth dynamics between the early stage and later stage of the flux pathway coacervation. 
The authors’ discussions and findings regarding the concentration dependence on early-stage domain growth dynamics and
the system size implications are very insightful. 
For example, the authors extrapolated the scaling for macroscopically large
systems, and found that the flux pathway can undergo coacervation in a matter of seconds, but found that the baseline
thermodynamic pathway would take 47.5 years to form a coacervate domain of the same size. 
It is not entirely clear to me the level of significance the broader audience of Nature Communications would find in this
manuscript, though I believe this topic is of considerable interest to the soft materials scientific community. Coacervates
represent a potential environmentally friendly and sustainable solution for many materials applications, where these
characteristics are becoming increasingly sought after. I believe this manuscript is of high significance and high quality and
should be published in Nature Communications. I have a few questions for the authors to possibly consider discussing
within the manuscript. 
Questions for the authors 
1. One key finding in this study is the enhanced domain growth as a result of network formation. This network formation is
concentration dependent, and I am wondering about the implications of the inhomogeneous polarizability throughout the
system that will be more prevalent at higher concentrations. How does the model account for these local changes in
polarizability between water dominant regions and polymer dominant regions? 
2. What are the implications of using the same bead size for the salt counterions as the other species, on the dynamics of
coacervation? How accurate are the salt ion mobilities captured in this model and could deviations to the salt ion mobilities
lead to significant changes in the mechanisms and dynamics of coacervate domain growth? 
3. It seems to me that the interface surface tension between the coacervate domain and the solvent and free chain domain is
important for the dynamics of droplet coarsening. Is this the case and how accurately is this interface surface tension being
modeled with this coarse-grained model? Would you expect the structuring of water around the coacervate droplets to have
an impact on the droplet coarsening dynamics? 
4. I am wondering about the domain size calculations from the structure factor and the error in the domain size as a result of
noise in the structure factor profiles. Do you have a sense for how sensitive the domain size calculation is to the choice of q-
vector binning? Presumably, larger systems would allow for obtaining structure factor profiles with less noise, and so I am
wondering if the systems studied are sufficiently large to get accurate domain sizes from the structure factor.
