Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the submitted manuscript, the authors present theoretical calculations of generation of VUV vortex beams via near
threshold harmonics (NTH) in argon driven by Laguerre-Gaussian NIR beam. The main result of the paper is the detailed
analysis of the generation process and characteristics of the generated NTHs. In general, vortex beams in the VUV range
are of interest for the community. However, when reading the manuscript, I have concerns regarding the presentation of
novelty: 
1. The way that abstract and Introduction (until line 111) are written is misleading. Eg. sentence in Introduction “To date, the
generation and characterization of short-wavelength vortex beams have been successfully demonstrated using large-scale
facilities” ignores previous HHG developments. It sounds that VUV vortex beams can only be produced at large scale
facilities. However, production of vortex beams with HHG has been experimentally demonstrated more than a decade ago: 
Zurch et al. : Nature Physics 2012 https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys2397 
With a follow up theoretical study: 
Hernandez-Garcıa : Phys Rev Lett 2013 https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.111.083602 
And mixed theory/experimental study in Ref [17]. 
I would say that vortex beam generation by HHG is getting pretty well established, judging by the number of publications on
this topic (e.g. references [34-43] in the submitted manuscript, which refer to recent work from years 2022 – 2025; references
above and other publications since 2012). 
The novelty of the submitted manuscript lies in addressing the spectral range in the VUV with rather low photon energies:
~10 eV – 17 eV (HH7 – HH11), which has not been investigated much. Typical HHG investigations start with around HH13
and higher. Therefore, a solid theoretical understanding of generating vortex HH7-11 is of potential interest for the
community of HHG and VUV applications. The authors should clearly write in the Abstract and Introduction that they focus
on a much unexplored spectral range, that vortex HHG has been already established and specifically discuss potential
applications of vortex beams in this spectral region. 
The method used by the authors is to solve the time dependent Schrodinger equation and calculate the propagation and far-
field profiles of the beams. The calculation appears to be solid and should be reproduceable. 
A large part of the text contains a lengthy and mundane description of the figures (describing in detail the ring structure in the
profiles, which the reader can directly see). 
2. The authors should add a discussion and explanation of the physical processes and some connection to experiment and
actual application of the vortex beams. For example, addressing separately H7.08 and H7 (in the supplement) is not
practical, because when a single harmonic is selected by a multilayer optic in the experiment, contributions of H7.08 and H7
will come together. 
Besides the fundamental issues raised above, I have also these smaller comments to the manuscript: 
3. Introduction Line 110: “perturbative nonlinear process, namely high-order harmonic generation (HHG)”. HHG (well above
threshold) is a nonperturbative process. State it clearly. 
4. Methods: what is the atomic density or Argon in the calculation?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript describes simulations regarding the spectral and spatial properties of near-threshold harmonics (NTHs)
generated in Argon by a near-infrared fundamental laser with a Laguerre-Gaussian (LG) mode profile and an orbital angular
momentum of 1. The research seems thorough and the results are presented in detail, and overall this is relevant work for
the field. However, I do have several questions that should be addressed, relating to the novelty and general nature of the
results. 
• My main concern is how general applicable the results of this work are to LG-driven NTH generation. Effectively, the
authors study one harmonic below threshold, one at threshold, and one above threshold. In the simulations, H7 happens to
be near the 4s Rydberg state in Argon, and H9 displays complex multi-orbital interference behaviour, leading to very specific
generation conditions. How do these results extend to e.g. other gases, a different fundamental wavelength, or in general
other harmonic generation conditions? Or is this work more of a showcase of what can happen in this specific situation? 
• Another aspect that should be addressed more prominently is the relevance of the LG mode (and specifically the nonzero
OAM) to the results. I would expect that the spectral features shown in Figs. 2 and 3 are mainly intensity-dependent, and
therefore would be very similar for Gaussian beams, is that the case? On the other hand, Gouy phases and spatially
dependent dipole phases would be different for LG beams, influencing the phase matching and beam profiles in Figs. 4-6.
Currently, only the results for the LG01 mode are given, but the paper would benefit from a discussion on the specific
influence of such beams. 
Other, more technical points: 
• It would be good to mention critical parameters such as the Rayleigh length and the beam waist at focus in the main text,
as these are crucial to understand the properties of the harmonic beams. 
• Attributing the frequency shift of H7 to the AC Stark shift seems reasonable. But that would make the exact frequency
intensity-dependent: repeating the analysis at another intensity would strengthen this argument. 
• H9 is analyzed in detail in Fig. 4, but in terms of intensity it must be very weak, as it seems almost absent in the spectrum in
Fig. 2. The authors suggest that this is due to multi-orbital interference. How sensitive are these spatial profiles to the exact
interference contributions? Would this profile be very sensitive to other parameters such as fundamental wavelength, or
atomic species? 
• In the phase profiles in Figs. S2 and S3: is there a defocus phase term subtracted (particularly for the far-field profiles), and
of so is that subtraction the same for all sub-figures? Please add some discussion on the observed phase profiles, as the
differences in curvature seem interesting. 
• Line 631: why does the harmonic emission split into three branches ‘due to the diverging nature of the driving laser’? Just a
diverging driver should not lead to complex splitting. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors of “Generation of vacuum ultraviolet vortex beams via near-threshold harmonics in argon gas driven by infrared

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

Laguerre-Gaussian lasers” present a theoretical analysis of macroscopic near-threshold harmonic emission—i.e., harmonics
generated close to the ionization potential of the target—driven by Laguerre-Gaussian vortex beams. The study explores
both the generation of such harmonics in the near-field and their propagation to the far-field, emphasizing in the differences
from the more extensively investigated high-order extreme ultraviolet (EUV) vortex harmonics. 
The manuscript aims to bridge the gap between studies addressing the up-conversion of optical vortices in the perturbative
regime (e.g., second- and third-harmonic generation) and investigations of high-order harmonic generation (HHG)
processes. However, the results presented do not appear to represent a fundamental or technical breakthrough in the field,
nor does the discussion exhibit sufficient depth to constitute a meaningful advancement. Therefore, I cannot recommend this
manuscript for publication in Communications Physics in its current form. For potential reconsideration upon resubmission, I
suggest that the authors address the following fundamental points. 
First, the authors should provide a more thorough justification of the novelty and necessity of generating such optical vortices
through this specific approach. As currently presented, the process of generating vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) vortex beams
appears conceptually similar to previously reported up-conversion of orbital angular momentum (OAM) beams in HHG
[Phys. Rev. Lett. 111, 083602 (2013); New J. Phys. 17, 093029 (2015)]. 
Second, the manuscript would benefit from a clearer and more convincing discussion of the distinct generation mechanisms
associated with the 7th, 9th, and 11th harmonics. As far as I understand, the primary physical distinction between near-
threshold and higher-order harmonics lies in the generation mechanism: the 7th and 11th harmonics are attributed to
multiple-orbit interference, whereas the 9th and 13th arise from the standard multi-rescattering process. The only supporting
evidence for this interpretation is the synchrosqueezing transform (SST) shown in Fig. 3, with no additional evidence
provided. It is difficult to understand from that figure why the emission mechanism should be attributed either to multi-orbit
interference or to multi-rescattering. Moreover, the labeling in Fig. 3(a) indicates “orbital interference” around the 9th
harmonic, while the main text (line 376) states that the dominant mechanism is multi-rescattering. In addition, the distortion of
the 7th harmonic form in (SST) is attributed to the Stark shift without sufficient supporting evidence or explanation. 
Third, several results throughout the manuscript are presented without adequate explanation or discussion of the underlying
physical phenomena, which raises questions about the robustness of the authors’ conclusions. For instance, in Figs. 2(d–f),
the macroscopic spectra are shown for different gas-jet positions, clearly indicating that the 7th harmonic is slightly shifted to
7.08. However, the origin of this spectral shift is not discussed. Furthermore, the authors should clarify why the intensity of
the 9th harmonic is orders of magnitude lower than that of the 11th harmonic and lacks of a clear peak.

[A] While we are naturally disappointed that the referee does not recommend
publication in Communications Physics, we respectfully note that their concerns are
not with the technical quality, validity, or clarity of our work, but rather with its
perceived scope and breadth of impact.
We believe that our findings on vortex NTHs have broader implications than the
referee suggests. The generation mechanisms we describe are relevant not only to
structured light and ultrafast science, but also to fundamental questions in nonlinear
optics, topological photonics, and light-matter interactions. These are topics of wide
interest to the diverse readership of Communications Physics. Furthermore, the
methodological approaches and theoretical insights presented in our work may find
applications beyond their immediate context, including in areas such as optical
manipulation, information encoding, and the study of singular optics.
Nevertheless, we respect the referee’s perspective and recognize that editorial
decisions regarding scope ultimately rest with the journal. We maintain that the
revised manuscript is technically rigorous, clearly presented, and contains results of
sufficient interest to warrant publication in Communications Physics.
