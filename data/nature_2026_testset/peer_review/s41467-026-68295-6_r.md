Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study utilizes Microwave-alkali co-activated persulfate technology to generate highly oxidative free radicals for the
humification of food waste into FLA with good growth-promoting effects. The method is simple, efficient, and cost-effective,
making it a very meaningful study. The work is also comprehensive and reliable. However, there are a few suggestions for
the authors to address further revisions: 
1. How exactly do free radicals participate in the humification reaction pathway? It is recommended to add direct evidence of
the interaction between free radicals and organic matter, such as the identification of intermediate products. 
2. Line 156: The authors attribute the detection of •OH/•SO
₄⁻
 to thermal activation (line 156), which overlooks the impact of
microwave non-thermal effects on the free radical pathway. It is suggested that the authors add experiments to verify the
non-thermal effects, such as comparing the differences in free radical production between the oil bath and microwave
systems at the same temperature. 
3. While the addition of KOH increases the pH (beneficial for •SO
₄⁻
 generation), the competition between the decomposition
products of PS under alkaline conditions and the humification intermediates for oxidation has not been considered. Line 248
shows that PS is completely decomposed in 4 minutes. Such a rapid consumption rate may lead to a deficiency of free
radicals later in the process. 
4. The PS system may produce sulfur-containing by-products. It is recommended to analyze the sulfur species in the
products to exclude the potential plant toxicity risks from organic sulfur residues. 
5. This study uses a household microwave for small-scale trials and industrial equipment for scaling up. The authors should
clarify the power density conversion logic! Additionally, does the temperature distribution of the materials have an impact? It
is suggested to supplement the study with infrared thermography images for validation. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Food waste represents a significant pollution source globally, making its efficient recycling a critical challenge. This study
addresses limitations of traditional disposal methods by introducing a microwave-alkali co-activated persulfate process that
achieves minute-scale conversion of food waste into high-yield fulvic-like acid fertilizer. The novelty of this technology is
evident when compared to conventional methods like composting or anaerobic digestion, particularly regarding its
unprecedented speed. Furthermore, it demonstrates improved chemical efficiency and lower costs relative to the KOH/PS
system previously reported by this group. The authors comprehensively validate the technology through process
optimization, product characterization, pot trials, and scale-up tests, effectively demonstrating its efficiency, mechanistic
basis, and application potential. Accordingly, I recommend acceptance for publication after minor revisions addressing the
points below. 
1. Would this process produce tail gas? If so, what are the main components? Please explain in detail. 
2. Was the pH value of the product alkaline? For fertilization evaluation tests, was the pH adjusted before use? Please
provide further explanation. 
3. For the discussion on its application modes, the safety of the agents (i.e. strong alkali and oxidants) used need to be
considered especially in household scale. 
4. Please notify the investment cost of microwave equipment and compared with composting. 
5. The nitrogen loss during the humification process is recommended to be determined and compared with composting. 
6. It is recommended to remove Raman and Rayleigh scattering from all 3D fluorescence spectra. 
7. Delete the blank line at line 287.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

8. The humification method in the manuscript primarily employs KOH and persulfate. Therefore, returning such fertilizers to
fields may lead to changes in soil pH and cation exchange capacity. Furthermore, since alterations in soil properties are
closely associated with microbial activity, microbial measurements are necessary to better demonstrate the fertilizer's
efficacy. 
9. The manuscript highlights the temporal advantages of this technology. However, its market potential depends on the
overall process costs. Therefore, the costs throughout this technology's entire process should be compared with existing
conventional technologies to demonstrate its economic viability and environmental friendliness. 
10. What is the pH of 2 wt% KOH? What is the composition of the residue after reaction (solid/liquid)? Furthermore, what are
its primary components? 
11. Regarding the calculation methods for HLA and FLA yields: Generally, both HLA and FLA are generated simultaneously
yet independently during artificial humification. The authors should explicitly differentiate between these two components.
Moreover, the differences in their respective efficacies require further explanation. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is ann interesting paper that aims to convert food waste into plant fertilizer. We applaud the authors for designing a pot
experiment to begin to test the real-world applications of their product. However, a fundamental issue exists with their
experimental design. While a negative control was included, there appears to be no mention of positive control. That is,
there is no way to compare their results to a treatment group that received ample nutrition from either a standard,
commercially available slow-release fertilizer (e.g. polyon/osmocote) or conventional fertigation. This inclusion would not
affect the results of the authors’ study, but it could strengthen their claims by providing evidence of their product or SRF
being equal to or better than a conventional method. We encourage the authors to discuss this in this paper and take this
step in future studies. Similarly, the cost analysis should also include a comparison against these positive controls. 
We are additionally concerned about the statistics in Figure 7.H. Based on the spread of the data, we would not expect there
to be a statistical (or frankly physiological) difference in chlorophyll content between the WP, blank, and K2SO4 treatments.
We encourage the authors to double-check the post-hoc analysis for this parameter. 
In addition to the main concern over an absence of a positive control, there are a number of other concerns that we feel
should be addressed before considering this for publication: 
- The authors use a lot of abbreviations—a list of abbreviations (perhaps in the Supplemental File?) would be useful. 
- 180 W of microwave power is indicated in making the formulation. But the microwave is rated at 800 W, thus it appears to
have been operated at 20% of max: so was this continuous or pulsed? (most commercial microwave ovens achieve reduced
power by pulsing on/off at maximum power output). In Fig 2 the indicated microwave time is for 180 W net power over the
indicated time? To achieve 180 W from a microwave rated at 800 W usually involves pulsing the irradiation. Was this the
case? 
- How was temperature measured? Microwaves are known to have non-uniform heating patterns (hence why milk or formula
for babies is recommended to not be heated in microwaves) An industrial 40 kW microwave was also indicated in the
Materials section for scale up. Did product preparation scale as expected between the two microwaves? 
- Slow release fertilizer was created using attapulgite (ATP) followed by granulation (milling) and coating with ASO to make
5mm particles. Do these particles release cargo by surface erosion, or bulk dissolution, or both? It is interesting in Fig.
6(C,D) that the release rates of FLA (MW range of “300 to 7370” Da) are almost identical to release rate of K+. 
Can the
authors provide some discussion regarding the release mechanisms? 
- Do the authors have images of “Blank” SRF particles? Did they also show the same surface transition to form “rod-like
structures”, or do these only appear in loaded particles? 
- Regarding use of the term “Blank”, the controls referred to as “Blanks” were actually “Untreated”. When considering a
delivery vehicle for slow-release, the more appropriate “Blank” is the “empty delivery vehicle”. 
- Considering the absence of a blank SRF vehicle, the statement on lines 312-314, “Notably, SRF treatment further amplified
the fertilization effect, resulting in better overall growth (including a 17.4% higher fresh weight) compared to the Product”
would need to be reconsidered to subtract any effect of the delivery vehicle on the soil properties and plant health. While the
delivery vehicle is not the active ingredient, it may still have an effect. 
- In Figure 7(l), the pH of the soil at the start and end of each treatment should be given. What type of soil (clay, sand, silt
content) was used? Was it from an agricultural field? Was it previously planted and previously fertilized? Please provide
some soil characterization details as this information is important in determining how the components added to the soil may
be adsorbed or desorbed. 
Plant growth: 
- Light exposure duration and intensity during growth? Watering regime? 
- Were the growth pots porous at the bottom to allow drainage? What was the watering regime? 
- As discussed previously, empty vehicle controls should employed to account for chemical / physical property changes in
the soil by the vehicle. 
- The images of the plants in the growth plots show significant differences across treatments, but also show a significant
level of differences within a given plot--even for the blank treatments. Why is there such a discrepancy in germination and
growth for a given treatment? Were the top set of images in Fig S6(A) taken on the same day as the images of the harvested
plants in the lower half of Fig S6(A)? The germination and growth of the plants in the aerial views appears to be less than 15
