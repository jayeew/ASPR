Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript highlights how the study fills a major gap in understanding long-term human–environment interactions by
reconstructing five centuries of land-use and ecological transformation in the karst landscapes of Southwest China. Using a
powerful combination of pollen and phytolith evidence, isotopic dating, archival historical documents, and modern satellite
observations, the researchers trace how a once-forested landscape underwent a profound and lasting shift due to human
activities. The core finding emphasizes that before the 18th century, the region was largely covered in dense, intact forests
and experienced minimal human disturbance. This ecological stability was dramatically altered with the introduction of
maize cultivation, a new crop that enabled intensive agriculture on steep karst terrain. Coupled with rapid population
expansion, maize-based farming accelerated large-scale deforestation, setting in motion a transition from a closed-canopy
forest ecosystem to a more open, degraded landscape. This ecological shift is strongly supported by biological proxies: a
pronounced rise in Dicranopteris spores (~11%), an indicator of disturbed, degraded understories, and a significant increase
in herbaceous pollen (3.7–18.8%), reflecting expanding open vegetation and farmland. One of the most critical insights the
manuscript conveys is that areas subjected to intense historical human disturbance show very limited ecological recovery
today. Even after two decades without human intervention, tree densities remain nearly unchanged, revealing the resilience
of degradation and the difficulty of reversing historical land-use impacts in karst systems. Finally, the abstract provides an
important policy implication: ignoring long-term land-use legacies can undermine ecological restoration programs. Efforts
that do not account for centuries of transformation may set unrealistic goals, adopt misguided strategies, and ultimately
waste valuable resources.

The manuscript highlights how the study fills a major gap in understanding long -term 
human–environment interactions by reconstructing five centuries of land -use and 
ecological transformation in the karst landscapes of Southwest China. Using a powerful 
combination of pollen and phytolith evidence, isotopic dating, archival historical 
documents, and modern satellite observations, the researchers trace how a once -
forested landscape underwent a profound and lasting shift due to human activities. The 
core finding emphasizes that before the 18th century, the region was largely covered in 
dense, intact forests and experienced minimal human disturbance. This ecological 
stability was dramatically altered with the introduction of maize cultivation, a new crop 
that enabled intensive agriculture on steep karst terrain. Coupled with rapid population 
expansion, maize-based farming accelerated large-scale deforestation, setting in motion 
a transition from a closed-canopy forest ecosystem to a more open, degraded landscape. 
This ecological shift is strongly supported by biological proxies: a pronounced rise in 
Dicranopteris spores (~11%), an indicator of disturbed, degraded understories, and a 
significant increase in herbaceous pollen (3.7 –18.8%), reflecting expanding open 
vegetation and farmland. One of the most critical insights the manuscript conveys is 
that areas subjected to intense historical human disturbance show very limited 
ecological recovery today. Even after two decades without human intervention, tree 
densities r emain nearly unchanged, revealing the resilience of degradation and the 
difficulty of reversing historical land-use impacts in karst systems. Finally, the abstract 
provides an important policy implication: ignoring long -term land -use legacies can 
undermine ecological restoration programs. Efforts that do not account for centuries of 
transformation may set unrealistic goals, adopt misguided strategies, and ultimately
