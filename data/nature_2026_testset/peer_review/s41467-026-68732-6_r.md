Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors propose a prognostic staging system for Alzheimer's disease (AD) that integrates cognitive status, traditional
risk factors, and multiple plasma and imaging biomarkers to stratify progression risk. They identified prognostic subgroups
using survival prediction modeling within each cognitive status, mapped them to specific outcome stages, and unified them
into a comprehensive staging framework. Key discriminators varied by stage: GFAP for cognitively unimpaired (CU)
individuals, hippocampal volume for those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and age for those with dementia due to
Alzheimer's disease (DAT). Plasma pTau217 consistently served as a secondary discriminator across all groups. This
integrated staging system preserves the strengths of individual outcome-specific stages while providing a single, clinically
interpretable scale. The paper emphasizes the importance of stage-specific prognostic factors for precise risk stratification
and highlights the potential of a comprehensive, biomarker-integrated staging system. While very interesting there were
several points that limited enthusiasm 
1) Preclinical AD Stage: It seems the authors missed addressing the significant preclinical stage of Alzheimer's disease
(AD).Biological diagnosis and pathological confirmation of AD typically involve amyloid and tau markers, yet the study only
indirectly considers these through pTau 217. The complexity of the proposed staging system may hinder its intuitive
understanding and clinical application. 
2) Non-Specific Markers: Both GFAP and nFL are markers not exclusive to AD and are influenced by age and other
diseases. A detailed discussion about the limitations of these non-specific biomarkers should be included. 
3) Staging System Analogy: The authors draw an analogy to a TNM system for AD, but the existing ATN criteria already
serve this purpose. The spatial distribution and magnitude of these biomarkers need proper consideration, which the
authors' model lacks. 
4) Age as a Predictor: Using age alone as the best predictor of dementia due to Alzheimer's disease (DAT) is questionable,
as it suggests all elderly individuals would have DAT, which is not accurate. Many individuals do very well at older ages and
these are not considered in the current model. 
5) Mixed Pathology: The authors should address that the diagnosis in their samples often includes mixed pathology. 
There
is no consideration of vascular measures, genetics (APOE), or social determinants of health, all of which can impact AD
progression. This significantly limits the generalizability of the results. 
6) Sample Representation: The ADNI subgroup used (290 participants) may not be representative of the broader cohort.
More details on the selection process are needed. 
7) Comparison with Existing Models: The authors should compare their model against current models that include amyloid
and tau biomarkers to show it is superior. 
8) Non-Specific Markers and Pathology Alignment: Even pTau 217 levels increase with age; overall, the study's reliance on
non-specific markers complicates their use for classification aligned with AD pathology. 
9) Clinical Application and Validation: The claim that the model could guide monoclonal antibody treatment is doubtful, as
current therapies depend on amyloid presence, which the model doesn't fully capture or evaluate for treatment response. 
10) External validation from diverse, population-based cohorts is missing, limiting generalizability. The exploratory nature
and lack of formal sensitivity analyses suggest further validation studies are necessary to confirm long-term predictive
accuracy.

The authors propose a prognostic staging system for Alzheimer's disease (AD) that 
integrates cognitive status, traditional risk factors, and multiple plasma and imaging 
biomarkers to stratify progression risk. They identified prognostic subgroups using 
survival prediction modeling within each cognitive status, mapped them to specific 
outcome stages, and unified them into a comprehensive staging framework. Key 
discriminators varied by stage: GFAP for cognitively unimpaired (CU) individuals, 
hippocampal volume for those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and age for those 
with dementia due to Alzheimer's disease (DAT). Plasma pTau217 consistently served as 
a secondary discriminator across all groups. This integrated staging system preserves 
the strengths of individual outcome-specific stages while providing a single, clinically 
interpretable scale. The paper emphasizes the importance of stage-specific prognostic 
factors for precise risk stratification and highlights the potential of a comprehensive, 
biomarker-integrated staging system. While very interesting there were several points 
that limited enthusiasm 
 
1) Preclinical AD Stage: It seems the authors missed addressing the significant 
preclinical stage of Alzheimer's disease (AD).Biological diagnosis and pathological 
confirmation of AD typically involve amyloid and tau markers, yet the study only 
indirectly considers these through pTau 217. The complexity of the proposed staging 
system may hinder its intuitive understanding and clinical application. 
 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s emphasis on the biological definition of preclinical 
AD. In the revised manuscript, we now clarify that our study adopts an operational definition 
aligned with major secondary prevention trials, in which preclinical AD refers to cognitively 
unimpaired individuals with abnormal amyloid biomarkers (Aβ+), irrespective of tau status. 
This approach reflects current clinical frameworks and is consistent with recent evidence 
demonstrating that plasma pTau217 robustly predicts both Aβ and tau PET positivity and 
captures early disease-related changes. Indeed, our separate study published in European 
Journal of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (2025) showed that plasma pTau217 
achieved high accuracy for predicting Aβ (AUC 0.96) and tau (AUC 0.90) PET positivity and

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 17 -->

effectively distinguished longitudinal cognitive decline across plasma-based AT profiles. 
These findings support the use of plasma pTau217 as a sensitive marker of early AD-related 
pathology even in the absence of direct tau PET measures. Nonetheless, we agree that 
pTau217 represents an indirect proxy rather than a direct measure of tau pathology, and we 
now explicitly caution that the proposed staging system does not constitute a biological 
staging framework and should not be interpreted as a substitute for AT(N)-based 
classification. We have expanded the Discussion and Limitations sections accordingly to 
emphasize this point and to acknowledge that the complexity of the framework may limit 
intuitive clinical interpretability, particularly in preclinical stages. 
In the Background section, 
“In this study, preclinical AD was operationally defined as Aβ+ CU individuals, consistent 
with current secondary prevention trials. Plasma pTau217 was used as an indirect correlate

not constitute a biological staging system based on combined amyloid and tau positivity.” 
 
In the Discussion section, 
“Indeed, plasma pTau217 has demonstrated independent associations with both amyloid and

comorbidities, and other neurodegenerative conditions. In the revised manuscript, we have

Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comment. We agree that the A/T/N 
framework already provides a biological classification system that parallels the role of TNM 
in oncology, and we do not intend to propose a replacement for, or duplicate of, the existing

incorporate regional tau patterns or a hierarchical biological ordering like A/T/N, and should

replace—biological frameworks by providing a survival-based, outcome-oriented structure.

biological staging, were not modeled due to the lack of tau PET in the derivation cohort.

necessary to connect our prognostic framework more directly to pathobiological staging. 
“Unlike biological staging frameworks such as A/T/N24, our model does not incorporate 
regional tau PET or quantitative pathological burden and therefore should not be viewed as a 
biological classification system. Instead, it is an outcome-driven, prognostic framework built 
from T1 biomarkers, non-specific plasma markers, and known clinical risk factors—intended

Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. We fully agree that age 
alone cannot be interpreted as a general predictor of dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease 
(DAT), nor does it imply that all older adults are at elevated risk. In our study, age emerged as 
the strongest discriminator within the dementia subgroup, but this reflects well-established 
pathophysiological characteristics of early-onset AD rather than a universal effect of aging.  
 
Specifically, numerous studies have shown that individuals with early-onset AD exhibit: 
 markedly higher amyloid positivity rates, 
 greater tau burden on PET, and 
 more severe and widespread cortical atrophy 
compared with late-onset AD. These biological features—not age per se—are strongly linked 
to a more aggressive clinical course, which explains why younger individuals with dementia 
showed faster progression in our cohort. 
“In dementia, younger age emerged as the strongest predictor of faster progression3, a 
pattern consistent with extensive evidence that early-onset AD follows a more aggressive 
clinical course. Individuals with early-onset AD typically show markedly higher amyloid 
positivity rates31, greater tau burden on PET32, and more severe and widespread cortical 
atrophy33 compared with late-onset AD. These biological differences—rather than 
chronological age itself—likely account for the accelerated progression observed in younger

5) Mixed Pathology: The authors should address that the diagnosis in their samples 
often includes mixed pathology. There is no consideration of vascular measures, genetics 
(APOE), or social determinants of health, all of which can impact AD progression. This 
significantly limits the generalizability of the results. 
 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comment regarding mixed pathology and 
additional determinants of AD progression. In our study, we attempted to reduce the influence 
of severe non-AD pathology by excluding individuals with extensive cerebrovascular disease. 
Furthermore, among the candidate prognostic features, we incorporated several key vascular 
and genetic factors—including age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and APOE ε4 carrier 
status—as well as education level, which is a well-established proxy for cognitive reserve. 
However, we acknowledge that the present framework does not include more detailed social 
determinants of health (e.g., socioeconomic status, environmental factors, occupational 
history, or quality of education) nor comprehensive vascular imaging markers such as white 
matter hyperintensity burden, lacunes, microbleeds, or cerebral perfusion indices. The 
absence of these broader contributors limits the model’s ability to fully capture mixed 
pathology and the multifactorial nature of cognitive decline, thus constraining 
generalizability to more diverse populations. We have revised the manuscript to clearly note 
these limitations and to emphasize that future work integrating detailed vascular imaging 
features, broader genetic contributors, and multidimensional social determinants will be 
essential to enhance the robustness and clinical validity of biomarker-integrated prognostic 
staging. 
 
“Although individuals with extensive cerebrovascular disease were excluded, only limited 
vascular and genetic variables (age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, APOE ε4 carrier status, 
and education) were included, and detailed vascular imaging markers or social determinants 
of health were not available—factors that may contribute to mixed pathology. (Discussion)”
