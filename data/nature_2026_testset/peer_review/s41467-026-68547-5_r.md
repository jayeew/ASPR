Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Here Henry et al. attempt to understand how ether lipids influence various cellular processes including ferroptosis
endocytosis and cancer cell stemness. They show that lipid properties are altered in response to the deletion of the ether
lipid precursor biosynthetic enzyme AGPS and, that the loss of this protein results in change in membrane composition by
lipidomics and changes in cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis. The authors linked these changes to fundamental changes in
membrane biophysical properties (lipid packing and elasticity) that impact in turn affect cellular endocytosis. 
This study represents an exciting investigation into the fundamental role of the lipid membrane in divergent cellular
processes. The research is conducted quite rigorously, and the conclusions are largely supported by the data. That being
said there are a few issues that need addressing. 
The major deficiency in the study is the observation that loss of AGPS results significant changes in clathrin and dynamin
independent internalisation yet the authors perform only limited characterisation of this pathway. Henry et al show that loss
of AGPS results in a significant downregulation of CD44 internalisation of hyaluronic acid as well as a reduction in uptake of
Dextran (70kDa) without impacting TfnR internalisation despite significant differences in iron uptake between control and
AGPS lines. This is a very intriguing result. They discuss that CD44 has been proposed to be internalised through the
CLIC/GEEC pathway (Howes et al., JCB 2010) but that uptake of 70kDa dextran is implicated with macropinocytosis (Li et al
Cell Biol Int 2015)– these are two very distinct pathways with two very different cellular functions. Given the dynamics of the
observed rate of internalisation in Fig 3b. it would be surprising if this is a consequence of changes in CLIC/GEEC
internalisation. But this remains to be demonstrated/ determined. A clear characterisation of the endocytic function between
the PyMT-1099 Wt and AGPS KO with a panel of endocytic markers and inhibitors is necessary to determine the effects on
endocytosis. Does inhibition of endocytosis impact the total amount of iron in these cells. As an aside to this point, I would
like to see the microscopy data to assess the quality of the imaging rather than simply the quantitation. This point pertains
specifically to figures 2, 3 and 4. These can be included as supplementary data. 
Minor Points: 
1) The authors observed that AGPS knockout results in a reduction in a metastatic like phenotypes. While this is a very
interesting result from the data presented, I think the conclusions need to be toned down or further discussion be added to
the text. Largescale changes in endocytosis and bulk changes in membrane composition will have wide-ranging and
diverse impacts on the receptor availability/stability/dynamics on the plasma membrane and within intracellular trafficking
pathways. It is hard to draw a direct line between changes in ether lipid abundance and metastatic capacity. 
2) Please provide a better explanation of the reasoning behind switching between cell lines ( ie
1099/pB3/OVCAR8/HUVEC) 
3) Fig1C and D and extended data 1B required rescue controls. 
4) The authors have home made their RhoNox-M - is this probe functional? Additional controls are required.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Herny and collaborators reports an interesting finding, namely that breast or ovarian cancer cells with
high metastatic potential and stem cell-like traits utilize AGPS-mediated ether lipid biosynthesis to maintain low membrane
tension and high membrane fluidity, which foster their metastatic potential. By using cancer cell models with deletion of
AGPS and elegant biophysical assays the authors uncovered that ether lipids modulate the level of intracellular iron and
ferroptosis susceptibility by CD44-mediated iron endocytosis. 
Strikingly, the authors found that loss of AGPS abolishes ether
lipids, while increasing the levels of several proposed pro-ferroptotic PE-PUFAs, suggesting that ether lipids function as the
main effectors of ferroptosis in these cellular settings. Further in vitro and in vivo studies convincingly indicated that loss of
AGPS blunts metastatic burden and cancer cell stemness. 
Altogether the manuscript presents some very interesting data
unraveling 
a mechanism by which cancer cells control their metastatic potential, by linking remodeling of ether lipid
composition of their membranes with the CD44-mediated uptake of iron. While the experimental design and the quality of the
data are sufficiently robust some issues should be further addressed by the authors to validate the main conclusions of the
manuscript. 
MAIN COMMENTS: 
1) Since PUFAs also promote PM fluidity, to corroborate the main finding of this study, namely that CD44-mediated import of
iron is selectively affected by ether lipid abundance, can the author further discriminate whether is the increased PUFA
availability or ether lipid deficiency that affects CD44-mediated iron import in AGPS KO cells? This could be assessed by
supplementing PUFAs in AGPS KO +addback and measuring CD44-mediated iron import or looking at CD44 internalization
upon supplementing ether lipids in AGPS KO cells. 
2) Previous studies reported that genetic inhibition of AGPS decreases cellular motility and invasiveness, in both breast and
melanoma cancers and reduces cell survival in breast cancer cells (pnas.org/cgi/doi/10.1073/pnas.1310894110 ). These
studies also showed that inhibiting AGPS has a broader impact beyond ether lipid pathway and affects fatty acid metabolism
and several oncogenic signaling lipids, including eicosanoid signaling. Can the authors exclude that these metabolic
changes following AGPS KO, may contribute to the ferroptosis-resistant and the low invasive cancer phenotypes they show
here? 
3) Iron level is higher in the ferroptosis-sensitive WT cells upon TGF-B treatment (Figure 2c) compared with the untreated
cells. However, ether lipid levels are not affected by TGF-B treatment in these same cells (Figure 1d). This suggests that the
increased iron transport is necessary to trigger ferroptosis but also that CD44-mediated iron transport is not only dependent
on ether lipid levels. Please comment on this point. Are there other possible mechanisms by which CD44 can mediate the
iron uptake? Please also add CD44 in the WB of Fig. 1b and related ones. 
4) The authors explored the effects of AGPS and CD44 ko on metastatic dissemination and cancer cell stemness, and they
also implicated the ether lipid biosynthesis pathway as a key factor in ferroptosis susceptibility of breast cancer cells. CD44-
expressing breast cancer cells with a cancer cell stemness phenotype, are susceptible to ferroptosis, and the in vivo data on
the reduced levels of iron in pB3 AGPS KO cancer cells relative to their WT counterparts or pB3 AGPS-add-back predicts a
differential ferroptosis susceptibility of these tumors. 
It would be interesting to see if loss of AGPS in these cells or other
breast cancer models explored in this study, makes these tumors resistant to the therapeutic effects of inducers of ferroptosis
due to the reduced iron uptake. 
MINOR COMMENTS: 
1) Please check 1f: pmol/µg? 
2) Can the authors represent beyond the PE subspecies shown in (Fig.1J) the relative total level of other non-ether PL
PUFAs in their AGPS settings? 
3) Some experiments are not fully described in the main text, e.g. Figure 2g, h liproxstain-1 has not been mentioned in the
text and the rationale behind its use as well. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Henry et al. explores the role of ether lipids in regulating Fe uptake that is associated with the acquisition of
EMT and CSC programs coupled to tumor development and metastatic progression. Additionally, the increase in ether lipid
concentration predominantly occurs in cancer cells (CSCs) harboring mesenchymal phenotypes, thereby rendering these
cells more susceptible to ferroptosis. Notably, the authors showed that cellular depletion of ether lipids suppressed the
endocytosis of CD44 and dextran, indicative of ether lipid engagement of clathrin- and dynamin-independent cargo transport
across the plasma membrane. Finally, ether lipid depletion enhanced membrane tension, which negatively impacted breast
cancer cell extravasation, metastasis, and CSC phenotypes. Although the paper is well-written and well-controlled, the study
itself is largely phenomenological and needs additional mechanistic insights to expand our understanding of the
connections between Fe uptake and ether lipid biosynthesis, particularly with respect to the cellular pathways operant in
stimulating these events. Additionally, how these events coalesce in driving EMT/EMP phenotypes is underdeveloped, as
are the studies examining CSC self-renewal and function necessary to traverse the metastatic cascade. Indeed, attempts to
improve the rigor of the CSC and in vivo analyses are warranted, as are more careful monitoring Fe and ether lipid
abundance in metastatic lesions. Finally, it is unclear why ether lipid and Fe analyses were not extended to human breast
cancer cell lines and/or PDOs/PDXs, as these findings are essential to moving the needle in understanding how ether lipids

participate in the pathophysiology of CSCs. Additional comments and concerns are presented below under “Specific
Comments.” 
Specific Comments: 
1) The primary weakness of the this potentially interesting study relates to the actual connections between ether lipids and
their involvement in enhancing CD44 and Fe transport as breast cancer cells acquire EMT/CSC phenotypes. For instance,
the literature is replete with studies associating CSC (“mesenchymal phenotypes”) reliance on Fe, an event that makes them
susceptible to ferroptosis. Likewise, enhanced Fe transport can occur via mechanisms involving either canonical TFR1/TF
or noncanonical CD44/hyaluronate pathways. Thus, while linking elevated ether lipid abundance to these processes is
interesting and novel, the downstream consequences are somewhat predictable based on previous findings (i.e.,
involvement of CD44, hyaluronate, clathrin-independent internalization, etc.). What remains unclear is the necessity of ether
lipids to the acquisition of EMT/CSC phenotypes. As presented, it is unclear as to whether alterations in ether lipids are an
essential facet for EMT/CSC transitions, or whether they simply represent passenger events that are a consequence of
cellular plasticity. Figure 1b argues for the latter in that cells lacking AGPS expression, and consequently those possessing
low abundance of ether lipids and Fe, still appear to competent to complete EMT transitions in response to TGFbeta. As
such, does Zeb1 or Snail1 KOs in PyMT-1099 cells impact ether lipid production and CSC phenotypes? Conversely, does
overexpression of either Zeb, Snail1, or AGPS in unstimulated PyMT-1099 cells induce spontaneous EMT/CSC transitions?
Similar manipulations should be undertaken in cells that possess a more permanent post-EMT/mesenchymal phenotype to
determine whether diminished ether lipid abundance can restore a more epithelial phenotype. 
2) A second weakness of the study relates to an understanding of the cellular pathways and signaling events that stimulate
ether lipid biosynthesis - i.e., AGPS levels are unaltered between pre- and post-EMT states, which begs the question as to
what specific signaling events and effectors initiate these responses. Likewise, do alterations in ether lipid content impact
TGFbeta signaling. Some attempts to strengthen these aspects of the study are warranted. 
3) Some attempts to strengthen and extrapolate these finding to human breast cancers is warranted - i.e., additional human
breast cancer cell lines, including non-TNBC subtypes, and/or analyses of human breast PDO/PDX materials. 
4) Figure 2: The overall magnitude of TGFbeta-mediated enhancement of lysosomal Fe abundance is unaltered by AGPS
KO (i.e., ~2.5-fold in WT vs AGPS KO cells), which argues against a direct effect of TGFbeta signaling in mediating these
cellular changes. Given the linkages between TGFbeta and CSC generation, some attempts to quantify ether lipid and Fe
abundance in non-stem vs stem-like populations should be undertaken. 
5) Figure 4: Membrane tension is clearly affected by AGPS depletion, such that alterations in membrane tension and CD44
endocytosis are capable of being rescued by administration of ether lipid-containing liposomes. However, it remains unclear
as to whether CD44 and its effectors also contribute to changes in membrane tension seen in AGPS KO cells. Thus,
changes in either lipid abundance or membrane tension should be monitored in CD44 KO cells. 
6) Figure 5: The CSC analyses are underdeveloped and need more accurate monitoring of stem cell frequencies, as well as
serial culturing of mammospheres. Additionally, visual representation of CSC vs non-CSC populations should be assessed
and presented by flow-cytometry. 
7) Figure 5: Panels K and I show PyMIT-1099 cell findings derived from NSG mice at day 121, while Panel M shows
representative pulmonary tumors produced by these same cells at day 121. Is this correct? It seems unlikely that NSG mice
would exhibit overlapping tumor kinetics with those observed in immunocompetent C57/BL6 mice. This needs to be
clarified. 
8) Cancer cells possess plasticity with respect to their production of ether lipids and their sensitivity to ferroptosis. Thus, the
results of Fig. 2i need to be extended to metastatic lesions to monitor ether lipid and Fe content, especially since
proliferating lesions are anticipated to exhibit a more epithelial phenotype (i.e., do these events revert to a more pre-
EMT/non-CSC state?). 
Minor Comments: 
1) Does AGPS and FAR1 knockout have other affects besides ether lipid depletion that might contribute to some of the
observations in this paper? 
2) Extended Figure 4: What cells were used and how long were the tumors allowed to grow?

The study performed by Henry et al. investigates the role of ether lipids in modulating cancer cell fate through mechanisms
dependent on membrane biophysical properties, specifically affecting iron uptake via a non-clathrin dependent pathway.
While the work is significant, several aspects require further clarification to strengthen the findings and address
contradictions. 
A major assumption in this study is that ether lipids alter the biophysical properties of membranes by increasing fluidity,
decreasing tension, and stabilizing lipid rafts. However, it is surprising that lipids that cause higher fluidity and lower tension
can also stabilize ordered (raft) domains. Furthermore, according to the data presented, these cells are enriched in
polyunsaturated ether lipids, making it even more unexpected that these lipids would stabilize lipid rafts, an observation that
contradicts the lipid raft paradigm. 
Therefore, the finding that polyunsaturated ether lipids can stabilize lipid rafts necessitates further studies. Model
membranes with controlled lipid composition should be employed to: 
1. Study how the major ether lipids present in the cells under study change membrane fluidity and tension. These studies
are essential, especially given that the major conclusions of this work center on the effects of ether lipids on membrane
properties. 
2. Understand how ether lipids affect raft stability in controlled systems so that proper conclusions can be drawn from cell
studies. 
Moreover, the current experimental strategy to assess lipid raft stability has potential artefacts that should be further
considered. The authors use GPMV obtained by membrane blebbing, which might not accurately mimic plasma membrane
lipid composition, thus not reflecting what is measured in membrane fluidity and membrane tension experiments. Performing
lipidomic analysis on GPMV could provide better insight into their composition and better relate the findings to the actual
lipids present in the GPMV. Additionally, the stability assessment relies on counting 25 to 50 vesicles per temperature,
which may not be statistically representative. The size and position of lipid domains might also influence detection accuracy.
More reliable information might be obtained through techniques such as DSC, fluorescence spectroscopy, or even Laurdan
GP. 
This study also lacks a comprehensive analysis of the changes of different lipids upon AGPS KO. While an Excel file with
lipidomics data is provided, an analysis of this data is necessary to confirm that the observed changes are indeed due to
ether lipids and not other lipid species. It seems that several lipid species (CE, TAG, PG, PE, etc.) are impacted by AGPS
KO, which could also contribute to the observed differences in membrane properties, internalization, tumor-initiating
potential, and metastatic capacity reported in this study. This issue should be further addressed. 
It is also important to determine if the increased internalization observed upon exposure to liposomes composed of
polyunsaturated ether phospholipids is specific to ether-containing liposomes or if it also occurs with liposomes containing
other lipids. For instance, lipids with PE headgroups are typically associated with non-lamellar phase formation and
membrane negative curvature, which is crucial for endocytosis. Therefore, this study should address whether the increased
internalization observed upon cell treatment with liposomes is specific to ether lipids or if it is a general property of
(phospho)lipids with PE headgroups. 
The determination of Laurdan GP values presented in this study did not take into account the correction factor (G factor). As
reported (e.g. 10.1038/nprot.2011.419) Laurdan GP values determined by microscopy require calibration. Without this
correction, comparing data from different images and experiments might not be accurate. Therefore, calibration images
should be acquired and Laurdan GP data must be corrected accordingly. 
Representative microscopy images used to assess membrane biophysical properties (tension, fluidity and raft stability)
should also be presented.

Reply: We thank the reviewer for raising these important points. The effects of lipids bearing 
polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA lipids) on raft phase stability have been previously investigated 
in synthetic biomembrane models (10.1016/j.bpj.2016.03.012)( 10.1529/biophysj.104.044552), 
computer simulations (10.1021/acs.jpcb.6b06815), and also in GPMVs 
(10.1016/j.bpj.2016.03.012) and live cells (10.1126/sciadv.aao1193). Across these studies, 
PUFA-lipids have been shown to stabilize raft domains.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 17 -->

The reviewer is correct that PUFA-containing lipids fluidize membranes. However, there is no 
contradiction between this effect and its stabilization of raft domains. This is because in phase-
separated membranes, DHA-containing lipids are strongly excluded from the more tightly packed 
raft domains and therefore accumulate in the more disordered (non-raft) domains. The resulting 
effect confers additional fluidity to the non-raft domains. The effect on the ordered/raft phase is 
minimal and tends to make it even more packed, as the cholesterol is expelled from the non-raft 
phase by PUFA-lipids. The combined effect increases the contrast, i.e. difference in 
packing/order, between the raft and non-raft phases, which enhances their likelihood to separate 
and therefore the stability of raft domains.
