Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper has a qualitative and exploratory character. The results derived from the interviews enable the authors to
diagnose the inadequacy of the current building resilience actions. Further, the barriers and the conditions for change are
identified. Notably, the authors point out tensions between the desired reforms and solutions. These are noteworthy results.
However, in conclusion, the authors offer a somewhat commonsensical summary. Collaboration, consensus building, and
cooperation between actors are non-controversial and known generic solutions. Exhibiting more of the tensions presented in
Table 3 would lead to a bolder conclusion. This result is currently underutilized in the main text. Presenting how these
tensions impact the scaling of climate-resilient solutions seems a genuine result, consistent with the paper’s qualitative and
explorative profile. Currently, there is not enough attention drawn to that. 
The paper deals with policies and measures and related actors' interplay to enhance buildings' resilience in the US coastal
communities exposed to climate change-related risks. The paper is original in its focus as compared to the existing literature,
which usually takes into account separate risks, such as fire (e.g. Syphard et al., 2013), or particular policy measures, such
as taxes (e.g., Gorji et al., 2023) or measures related to land-use planning (e.g. Syphard et al., 2019), welfare policy (Rao &
Enelamah, 2024) or housing action plans (Fuchs et al., 2022). Additionally, the existing literature mostly focuses on urban
areas, while the paper concerns the coastal US region of housing. Therefore, the paper's attempt to identify multiple
adaptation policies/measures, taking into account various actors in the coastal US region and identifying barriers and
enablers of the policies and measures, is novel. This approach may appear significant and provoke discussion due to its
emphasis on the institutional aspect of resilience production. In turbulent times, the transformations of the institutions and
policies may be as important for resilience as the risks in the physical sense. However, the paper offers a slightly general
picture. Specifically, a more direct research gap should be articulated to position the study vis-a-vis the literature. 
The papers' conclusions align with the results but are mostly generic. This makes the paper mostly descriptive. It is a pity
because the collected data has interesting aspects. In particular, table 3 seems to offer a source for in-depth, counterintuitive
claims. Moreover, the conclusion part can also be strengthened by a clear identification of the research gap in the
Introduction. 
The analysis have been presented appropriately. However, a few issues would require clarification. It is not explicit what the
rationale for the separation of the SM3 and SM4 is, as legal mandates can also be considered barriers. It seems to be
inconsistent as SM3 regards legal mandates and SM4 – both legal and other policies/measures. The study identified a
number of factors that are important in terms of resilience; however, little pattern appears in the analysis. The relative
importance of the factors is not discussed – except the role of the government. 
The methodology is proper for the purpose of the study, and it is satisfactorily described in terms of the recruitment process,
the interview circumstances, and the list of questions. It is unclear whether the interviews had a face-to-face form or an
online one. This should be clarified. Moreover, the individuals interviewed are called experts. The details are provided, but it
should be explained why experts were recruited and not practitioners or – professionals (for a reference in the environmental
field, see, e.g., Roche & Campagne, 2019; Riechers et al., 2017). How deliberate was the choice on the methodological
level, and how far the actual list of interviewees fulfilled the plan? Related to the above, not enough attention has been paid
to the conceptual framework in the paper. In particular, the notions of policy, action, resilience, adaptation, and government
are not defined, while they have various meanings. Further, a definition of enablers (SM5) is needed, clarifying a distinction

Comments to both Reviewers: 
We thank both reviewers for their insightful comments on how to improve the paper. We are now 
pleased to share a revised paper that we feel is more effective in (1) communicating a clear 
literature gap and (2) exploring the tensions across our findings, potential solutions, and the 
significance of this work.
1. Communicating a clear literature gap
We agree with both reviewers’ assessment about the need to clarify the research gap this study 
addresses. This paper now articulates a clearer research gap within the climate–housing 
literature. For decades, research on climate–housing has focused primarily on climate mitigation 
efforts within housing such as net-zero targets, energy efficiency, and decarbonization. While 
these efforts are critical, they have overshadowed the urgent need for scholarship on 
adaptation—particularly how housing systems can withstand and recover from the physical and 
transitional impacts of climate hazards. Few studies to date address the intersection of climate 
risk, housing market dynamics, and resilience policy. Our multi-sectoral approach seeks to 
address this gap by integrating diverse perspectives —across government, finance, insurance, 
non-profits, academia, and more—to understand what progress has been made in integrating risk 
reduction into housing policy as well as barriers and enablers for advancing these solutions.  
To clarify this gap, we have added the following paragraph and key sentences throughout the 
Introduction:  
“The existing climate and housing literature has primarily addressed climate mitigation 
concerns through carbon emission and energy-use reduction or “green building” practices 
associated with resource efficiency, especially in the U.S. and other high- income nations11-14, 
and not climate adaptation. Likewise, in practice, widely known industry standards that enhance 
building performance such as Passive House standards 19 and LEED certification from the U.S. 
Green Building Council (USGBC)20, are not well-suited to address the physical risks presented 
by climate hazards such as flooding, wildfires, or sea-level rise, though a recent LEED guidance 
update now integrates climate risk assessment21. As such, research that examines the intersection 
of climate adaptation and housing is a pressing need22–24, one that low-to-middle income 
countries appear to be demonstrating more urgency in addressing25–27. Adaptation research in 
the U.S. should shift towards examining the climate risk-housing nexus, assessing how climate

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

risks (both physical and transitional) impact systems that influence housing development such as 
land-use, finance, design, and federal- to-local governance.” 
In the following paragraph, we added in bold, 
“To date, climate risk and housing adaptation research has generally focused on understanding 
household preferences for adaptation measures; evaluating disaster recovery policies24, 
exploring managed retreat30–35; improving exposure and vulnerability assessments36–38, and not 
evaluating opportunities for integrating risk reduction into housing policy. Research on these 
topics is beginning to gain momentum28,39, as public and private markets are responding to the 
recent decline in availability and affordability of insurance in high climate risk areas40–42.” 
In the following paragraph, we also added,  
“However, interactions among real estate developers and housing construction companies, 
mortgage lenders, and insurance companies as well as federal-to-local governments have 
created a complex space for advancing solutions. As such, adapting housing to become resilient 
to future climate hazards should consider not just increasing structural resilience, but a 
system-wide approach that addresses the interconnectedness of land use, financial systems, 
development incentives, and multi-level governance.” 
And finally, we added a new statement introducing our research. We state, 
“Here, we provide a multi-sectoral examination of the progress, to date, in the integration of 
climate risks into housing development policies and actions. We present research asking four 
main questions: what actions have different sectors across the climate risk–housing nexus taken 
to enhance the resilience of new construction within private housing markets in coastal 
communities within the United States? What barriers have they encountered? What factors have 
enabled action? And finally, what solutions do experts think should be pursued? ”
2. Expanding upon the tensions across our findings, potential solutions, and the significance 
of this work.
We also agree with both reviewers’ comments on need to further articulate the significance of 
this work, either through a discussion on the tensions or through potential solutions. We added 
discussions of potential solutions and further contextualized the four tensions highlighted in 
Table 3, drawing from real-world examples that are currently in effect, or remarking on potential 
hard limits to adaptation.  
In response to the 1st tension surrounding building codes, effectiveness, and affordability, we 
added,  
“Threading the balance between building affordably and resilience will likely remain a 
challenge for years to come. Amid already challenging market and regulatory conditions in 
many coastal and urban areas of the U.S., new construction faces additional strain from

inflation, tariffs, high-interest debt financing, and continued labor shortages. However, 
California is leading the way by enacting reforms to CEQA, or the California Environmental 
Quality Act, an environmental protection law that has been often misused to block or delay 
construction projects, which add time and legal costs to development. The new reforms include 
exemptions for certain high-density projects from CEQA review, if they are not located on 
environmentally sensitive or hazardous sites, and reforms that would streamline rezoning in 
cities for more housing 47. These reforms intend to reduce costs associated with permitting and 
reviews and are a prime example of how state governments can use their power to reform a 
single stage of the housing development process in ways that generate broader, system-wide cost 
savings in construction, and perhaps resilience especially where these efforts decrease sprawl.”
In response to the 2nd tension surrounding insurance and inadequate incentives and pricing 
mechanisms, we added,  
“The challenge referred to by interviewees reflects specific limitations in the insurance 
industry’s current ability to price adaptation, but the State of Alabama’s adoption of FORTIFED 
standards has demonstrated the ability and willingness of government, non-profit, the insurance 
industry, and property-owners to work together to reduce disaster-related losses and insurance 
costs. The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS), a non-profit organization 
funded by the insurance industry to design and test construction standards that lead to risk 
reduction from various hazards, introduced the FORTIFED designations in 2010. The State of 
Alabama created the Strengthen Alabama Homes grant program, which subsidizes the cost of 
installing FORTFIED roofs, combined with insurance discounts for wind coverage. An analysis 
of FORTFIED standards after Hurricane Sally (2020) shows that homes with varying 
FORTIFED designations performed better than conventional construction and reduced loss 
frequency by 55% to 74% and loss severity by 14% to 40%,, and estimates that, if all affected 
homes had been retrofitted to a FORTIFIED roof, policyholders would have saved $32.6M, or 
61%, of all deductibles paid 48. This model could be replicated for other hazards, such as 
flooding and wildfire, but will require willing state governments and their departments of 
insurance to work with insurers to incentivize adoption, and time to study the impacts and 
quantify the benefits. Indeed, the California Department of Insurance in 2022 moved to require 
premium discounts for fire-proofing homes, but thus far the program had produced little savings 
for policyholders49.” 
In response to the 3rd and 4th tensions addressing climate information asymmetries and tensions 
surrounding asset devaluation, we explored a fundamental tension that poses an impediment to 
solutions. We state,  
“Addressing the challenge of information asymmetry and the broader question of who ultimately 
internalizes and bears climate risk underscores a fundamental tension in climate adaptation: 
making climate risks transparent produces loss for some despite the intention of reducing losses 
overall. For example, Gourevitch et al. (2023) estimates that residential properties in the U.S. at 
risk from  flooding are overvalued upwards of $200B 55. Correcting climate-related market 
distortions will lessen future damages and benefit future owners, but presents severe transitional

risks to both property owners and the public through property value loss, municipal credit 
downgrades, and reduced tax bases. As one Advocacy participant stated, “Everyone is looking to 
eliminate financial losses rather than absorb financial losses,” attesting to a potential hard limit 
in realigning incentives for more resilient development.”
