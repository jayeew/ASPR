Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a comprehensive study on the tunability of superelastic behavior in a (TiZrHf)50Ni50-xCox high entropy
alloy system. This work stands out for its elegant demonstration of continuous and reversible transitions between Hookean
and non-Hookean superelasticity, achieved through remarkably minor compositional variations (<2 at.%), unifying the two
phenomena previously observed only in different alloy systems. By systematically varying the Co content, a reversible
transition between Hookean and non-Hookean superelasticity was achieved, with recoverable strains up to ~8%. The
combination of sophisticated experimental characterization and thorough theoretical calculations provides a convincing
physical understanding of the underlying “hidden strain order” governed superelastic mechanism. 
Here are a few technical comments for the authors: 
1. In the phase diagram presented in Figure 2, what is denoted by the blue dashed line? A brief note in the caption would be
beneficial. 
2. Based on the results shown in Figure 3, it can be observed that crystal orientation has a significant influence on
deformation behavior. Why do [001] orientated single crystals exhibit linear elastic deformation, while those with [011] and
[111] orientations demonstrate nonlinear superelastic behavior? What is the underlying mechanism behind the effect of
crystal orientation on deformation behavior? 
3. The DFT models (SQS) are well-justified, however the binary-like models (ZrNi/ZrCo) are used to represent the full HEA
system in Fig. 5. The authors should briefly discuss the influence of other elements such as Ti and Hf. 
4. It's better to ensure consistent use of the alloy naming convention. The text primarily uses “Co15, Co20...”, but in some
cases (e.g., Page 3, “Co23 and Co25 alloys”), the “alloy” suffix is dropped. A consistent style (e.g., always using “Co25 alloy”
or simply “Co25”) improves readability. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript titled “Tuning superelasticity in high entropy alloy via a hidden strain order,” Yang and colleagues have
fabricated (TiZrHf)50Ni50-xCox high-entropy superelastic, characterized the transformation behaviors, superelastic stress,
recovery strain, and carried out microstructural analysis and DFT calculations. Overall, the authors have gained solid and
useful data and conducted the analysis. However, the following issues exist and need to be addressed. 
1. Justify the phase diagram. Based on the limited dataset from Figure 1, the authors propose a composition-temperature
phase diagram. The readers may wonder about the validation of the phase diagram, although the variable is set to be the
composition of Co. The phase diagram should have been constructed from a well-supported dataset, either experiments or
simulations. If possible, the authors are advised to add relevant experiments or discussions. 
2. Clarify the discrepancy between the stress-strain curves. In Figure 3, the macroscopic stress-strain curves show an elastic
strain of ~5% and the micropillar stress-strain curves show an elastic strain of ~10%. The discrepancies and connections
ought to be well ascertained with microstructural features. Also, the impact of different testing routes should be explained
well. In particular, the artifacts associated with the micropillar test should be cautioned.

3. Reconcile the temperature-dependent stress hysteresis. The authors have done a good job of showing the characteristic
superelastic behaviors. From the results of Figures 1 and 2, the readers may wonder how the superelastic behaviors vary in
the wide temperature regime, and in particular, how the stress hysteresis depends on the temperature. The authors are
expected to explain, and even better, provide an experimental pathway to justify. 
4. Strengthening the connection between atomic-scale strain and atomic-size mismatch. The authors have provided in-situ
TEM observation of lattice strain through atomic-scale strain mapping. Later, the authors have claimed the complex interplay
of atomic-size mismatch and chemical ordering to be the main factor in the fundamental mechanism, but the connection is
weak. The authors are advised to strengthen the connection, for example, by in situ correlation experiments. 
5. Assess the reversibility. Reversibility and stability are of great importance for the applications of superelastic materials.
Because the fatigue life is closely related to stress hysteresis and the (TiZrHf)50Ni50-xCox high-entropy alloy has quite a
large stress hysteresis (depending on the temperature), the audience may wonder what the reversibility and stability of the
high-entropy superelastic alloy would look like. If possible, the authors are advised to add relevant experiments or
discussions.

2. Based on the results shown in Figure 3, it can be observed that crystal orientation has a 
significant influence on deformation behavior. Why do [001] orientated single crystals exhibit linear 
elastic deformation, while those with [011] and [111] orientations demonstrate nonlinear 
superelastic behavior? What is the underlying mechanism behind the effect of crystal orientation on

Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful question. The martensitic transformation (MT) 
is well known to exhibit strong crystallographic anisotropy, as widely reported in the literature [1–
2]. The orientation dependence observed in our Co22 alloy is consistent with the transformation 
characteristics of other B2 austenite systems, such as CuZr-based SMAs [1–2]. In B2 austenite, the 
B2 to B19’ martensitic transformation proceeds through a lattice shear associated with the 
{011}<100>B2 shear system [3]. The activation of this transformation is therefore governed by the 
resolved shear stress on these transformation systems, quantified by the Schmid factor 𝑚 =
𝑐𝑜𝑠(𝜑) ∙ 𝑐𝑜𝑠(𝜆). As shown in Fig. R1, the Schmid factor reaches a high value (~0.5) along the 
<111>B2 direction, decreases toward <011>B2, and drops sharply to nearly zero when approaching 
the <001>B2 axis. The [001] loading direction is nearly perpendicular to the transformation shear 
directions, resulting in a very low Schmid factor for all transformation variants. As a result, the 
stress-induced MT is effectively suppressed, and the deformation is dominated by purely elastic 
deformation, yielding a linear elastic response. In contrast, the [011] and [111] orientations exhibit 
significantly larger Schmid factors, allowing efficient activation of transformation shear. This 
favorable geometric alignment promotes the nucleation and growth of martensitic variants, giving 
rise to pronounced nonlinear superelasticity with characteristic transformation-induced strain and 
hysteresis. To clarify this point, we added more discussion on the orientation-dependent elastic 
response in the revised manuscript.

recoverable strains compared with bulk tests due to the removal of microstructural constraints.

and reflects the intrinsic mechanical behavior. In the revised manuscript, we have added further

3. Reconcile the temperature-dependent stress hysteresis. The authors have done a good job of 
showing the characteristic superelastic behaviors. From the results of Figures 1 and 2, the readers 
may wonder how the superelastic behaviors vary in the wide temperature regime, and in particular,

Response: We thank the reviewer for this important comment. To clarify the temperature 
dependence of superelasticity and stress hysteresis, we performed additional cyclic micro 
compression tests on [011]-oriented Co22 micropillars using a TI950 nanoindenter equipped with a 
heating stage. The results, now included in Fig. S9, reveal a clear and systematic evolution of the 
deformation behavior with temperature. At low temperatures, the Co22 alloy exhibits pronounced 
nonlinear superelasticity accompanied by a well-defined hysteresis loop, consistent with stress-
induced martensitic transformation. As the temperature increases, both the nonlinearity and the 
hysteresis gradually decrease. When temperature above ~60 °C, the micropillars display fully linear 
elastic behavior with negligible hysteresis, indicating that the deformation mechanism transitions 
from transformation dominated to slip dominated.
This trend is consistent with the classical framework for deformation in shape memory alloys 
as shown in Fig. R2 [1-2], in which the deformation mechanism is governed by the competition 
between the critical stress for slip ( 𝜎𝑠 ) and the critical stress for transformation ( 𝜎𝑇𝑟 ). The 
transformation stress follows the Clausius–Clapeyron relation, 𝑑𝜎𝑇𝑟 𝑑𝑇⁄ = − 𝐿 𝜀𝑇𝑇⁄  , where 
L=HM-HA is the latent heat (typically negative), implying that 𝜎𝑇𝑟  increases with increasing 
temperature. As temperature approaches and exceeds the martensite deformation limit temperature 
Md, 𝜎𝑇𝑟 surpasses 𝜎𝑠, leading to the deformation mode shift from martensitic transformation to 
crystallographic slip. This mechanistic transition is fully consistent with the experimentally 
observed evolution from nonlinear superelasticity at low temperatures to linear elasticity at elevated 
temperatures in the Co22 alloy. We have incorporated this additional discussion and the 
supplementary figure in the revised manuscript and SI.

4. Strengthening the connection between atomic-scale strain and atomic-size mismatch. The authors 
have provided in-situ TEM observation of lattice strain through atomic-scale strain mapping. Later, 
the authors have claimed the complex interplay of atomic-size mismatch and chemical ordering to 
be the main factor in the fundamental mechanism, but the connection is weak. The authors are

Response: We thank the reviewer for the insightful comment. We would like to clarify that the 
atomic-scale strain in this work was obtained from high-resolution STEM images using geometric 
phase analysis rather than in-situ TEM, and we apologize if this point was not sufficiently clear. The 
connection between chemical fluctuation, chemical short-range order, and lattice distortion is well 
established in chemically complex alloys [1-5] and is directly supported by our experimental 
observations. In multi-principal-element systems, the large atomic-size mismatch among constituent

Response: We thank the reviewer for this question. In the geometric phase analysis (GPA), the 
reference lattice is mathematically defined following the standard procedure established by Hÿtch 
et al. [1] and implemented in the Strain++ algorithm [2]. Specifically, the reference state is defined 
in reciprocal space by selecting two non-collinear reciprocal lattice vectors ( g1 and g2) from the
