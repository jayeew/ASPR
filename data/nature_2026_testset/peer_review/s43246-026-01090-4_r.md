Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript presents a novel approach by integrating a dual-reference triboelectric sensor with deep learning to predict
material surface potential. However, the study requires significant revisions to address fundamental questions regarding the
research motivation, the underlying physical model, and the reconciliation of macro-scale measurements with micro-scale
properties before it can be considered for publication. 
1. The introduction states that predicting surface potential is a challenge but fails to compellingly argue why this specific
capability is so urgently needed. The practical applications are mentioned (wearables, robotics) but remain vague. The
authors should more clearly articulate the specific scenarios where this method is superior to existing techniques (like
KPFM), not just in cost but in functionality. For instance, is the goal real-time, in-situ monitoring of surface charge decay? Is it
for classifying unknown materials in a robotic hand? 
2. The authors attribute the triboelectric output solely to the surface potential difference, which is an oversimplification. The
charge transfer and stability are profoundly influenced by the charge trapping characteristics (trap depth, density,
distribution) of materials. A material with a high surface potential but shallow traps may generate a signal that decays too
rapidly for reliable measurement, unlike one with deep traps. The manuscript should acknowledge this fundamental aspect
of contact electrification. The discussion in Fig. 3d on humidity hints at this (charge screening), but a direct discussion on
how trap states might influence the signals is necessary. 
3. A central weakness is the challenge of inferring a microscopic and localized property (surface potential, which can be
highly non-uniform due to surface roughness, contaminants, or inhomogeneous charge distribution) from a macroscopic and
integrated electrical output (the Voc of a TENG). The authors assume a single, representative surface potential value for
each material, but in reality, the measured Voc is an average over the entire contact area. The authors should address this
discrepancy explicitly. How does the model perform when contacting a material with known, deliberate inhomogeneities?
Some discussion on the statistical nature of the prediction (i.e., it predicts an effective surface potential) is required. 
4. The authors use PDMS (both pristine and functionalized) as the dual-reference sensor material. This choice is
paradoxical and requires strong justification. PDMS is widely recognized in the triboelectric community as a material with
comparatively low charge trapping capability and weak triboelectric output (often cited as a "weakly negative" material). The
manuscript would be strengthened by explicitly addressing why a material with such inherent limitations was chosen as the
foundation for a high-accuracy sensing platform. A discussion comparing the charge retention properties (e.g., trap density
and depth) of PDMS against more triboelectrically strong materials (like PTFE or FEP) is necessary to validate this
fundamental design choice. 
5. The model is trained on only seven materials and tested on three. This is a very small dataset for a deep learning model
and raises concerns about overfitting and generalizability. The performance on Aluminum (Al, 10.1% MAPE) is notably
worse than on polymers, suggesting the model may struggle with material classes outside its training set. The authors
should include a discussion on this limitation and perform further validation, perhaps with a leave-one-out cross-validation
scheme on the training set, to better demonstrate the model's robustness and scope of applicability. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
Review comments: 
This manuscript presents a dual-reference triboelectric sensor combined with deep learning algorithms for surface potential
prediction. The authors proposed an alternative to Kelvin Probe Force Microscopy (KPFM) for surface potential
characterization by using both pristine PDMS and PEI(b)-functionalized PDMS references coupled with Temporal
Convolutional Networks (TCN). They reported improved prediction accuracy (~85% improvement over single-reference
methods), with errors below 8%. However, several aspects require attention to strengthen the manuscript. This study
demonstrated limited testing beyond controlled laboratory conditions and lacked a comprehensive discussion of model
generalizability to diverse environmental conditions. Some methodological details require clarification, and the statistical
analysis could be more rigorous. While the concept of combining triboelectric sensing with machine learning for surface
potential prediction has merit, the current presentation needs improvement. Therefore, minor revisions are necessary before
this manuscript can be considered for acceptance. 
1. Although the authors tested their system under two humidity conditions (20% and 50% RH), the generalizability of the
trained models to a wider range of environmental conditions remains unclear. Testing the model under a broader range of
humidity levels and varying temperature conditions and discussion of how the model might perform with materials not
included in the triboelectric series shown in Fig. 1d, would have a more positive impact on the paper. The authors should
also analyze the minimum dataset size required for reliable predictions. It is suggested to expand the environmental testing

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

conditions or providing a more detailed discussion of the model's limitations and expected performance boundaries. 
2. The manuscript used Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) as the primary performance metric, but additional
statistical analysis could strengthen the conclusions. The authors should report confidence intervals for the prediction
accuracy, perform statistical significance tests comparing single-reference vs. dual-reference approaches, and provide detail
information on the number of independent experiments and measurement replicates. Cross-validation results can also help
demonstrate the robustness of the model. Additional statistical significance tests and more comprehensive error analysis
and confidence intervals are recommended. 
3. While the authors compared three different deep learning architectures, the rationale for choosing these specific models
and their hyperparameter optimization needs further explanation. The authors should explain why they chose LSTM, CNN,
and TCN over other potential architectures, provide more details about the Bayesian optimization process for
hyperparameter tuning, and discuss the computational requirements and training time of each model. The analysis would be
strengthened if a simpler baseline model (e.g., linear regression) could be included for comparison. It is recommended that
the methodology section be expanded to better justify the model selection and optimization approach. 
4. The authors mentioned using "differences between adjacent elements" as input features, but this preprocessing step
requires more detailed explanation. The manuscript should provide a mathematical formulation of the data preprocessing
steps, explain the choice of differential signals rather than raw voltage measurements, and discuss alternative feature
engineering approaches considered. The impact of sampling frequency (500 Hz) on prediction accuracy should also be
analyzed. A more detailed description of the data preprocessing methodology and its impact on model performance is
recommended. 
5. The manuscript demonstrates proof-of-concept results but lacks a discussion of practical implementation challenges. The
authors should address the scalability of the dual-reference sensor fabrication, discuss potential integration challenges in
real-world applications, and analyze the cost-effectiveness compared to KPFM. It is suggested to add a discussion section
addressing practical implementation considerations and potential commercialization pathways. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
In this paper, the authors proposed a deep learning-based dual-reference triboelectric sensor for direct surface potential
prediction. However, theoretically speaking, it is impossible to predict the surface potential of different materials by using
triboelectric sensing arrays combined with machine learning algorithms. In this work, the two different material sensing
arrays developed by the author can only determine the type of the measured material through the signal difference between
the two electrodes when they are triboelectric with a third material, but cannot quantitatively analyze the specific surface
potential of the measured material. Overall, no effective measurement methods for surface units were found in this article; it
was merely a simple material identification. Material recognition based on triboelectric technology has been widely reported
in previous studies, and the recognition effect in this work is worse than that in previous reports. Therefore, this work does
not possess the innovation and advancement required by this journal. 
Meanwhile, the author himself also indicates in the article that environmental factors such as humidity and temperature have
a significant impact on the frictional electrical signal. Then, how can we ensure that for a material, its surface charge
(potential) remains the same under different environmental humidity conditions, but the measured values are different due to
the influence of humidity? In this case, there is no such thing as quantitative characterization of surface potential. 
Thus, I think this work is not suitable for publication in this journal.

Response: We agree that the introduction can better highlight the unique practical needs for 
rapid surface potential prediction. In the revision, we clarified why this capability is important 
and give concrete examples of use cases. In particular, we  emphasize that our method enables 
real-time, in-situ assessments of surface charge in situations where traditional Kelvin Probe 
Force Microscopy (KPFM) cannot be applied. For example, in wearable sensors or soft robotics, 
surfaces are constantly in contact with various objects and environments – a triboelectric-based 
system could continuously monitor the surface charge state at the macroscale without 
interrupting operation. One specif ic scenario is a robotic hand or gripper that can identify or

something not feasible with slow, laboratory -bound KPFM. Another scenario is real -time

where KPFM is impractical. We revise d the introduction to clearly state these points, thereby 
demonstrating the need for a triboelectric sensor approach to surface potential measurement in 
emerging applications. 
Comment 2: “The authors attribute the triboelectric output solely to the surface potential 
difference, which is an oversimplification. Charge transfer and stability are profoundly 
influenced by charge trapping characteristics (trap depth, density, distribution) of materials. A 
material with a high surface potential but shallow traps ma y generate a signal that decays too 
rapidly, unlike one with deep traps. The manuscript should acknowledge this fundamental 
aspect of contact electrification. The discussion in Fig. 3d on humidity hints at this (charge 
screening), but a direct discussion on how trap states might influence the signals is necessary.” 
Ans) We appreciate this important observation. We acknowledge that the triboelectric open -
circuit voltage (V oc) is indeed influenced not only by the surface potential difference (work 
function difference) but also by how charges are retained on each surface, which is governed 
by trap states (depth and density of charge traps in the material). In the current manuscript, we 
focused on correlating V oc with the effective surface potential difference, but we did not 
explicitly discuss charge trapping characteristics. We will add a discussion to acknowledge and 
explain this point in the revised manuscript. In particular, we will note that materials with 
deeper or more abundant trap states can retain trib oelectric charge for longer, yielding more 
stable and higher V oc outputs, whereas materials with shallow traps may see their charge 
dissipate or redistribute quickly1. For example, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) is known to act 
as a triboelectric electret with a large number of deep traps, enabling very stable charge storage2. 
In contrast, PDMS (polydimethylsiloxane) and certain other polymers have fewer or shallower 
trap states, so even if their initial charge transfer (and associated surface potential) is significant, 
the charge can be neutralized more rapidly1. This means the observed triboelectric signal (Voc)

can underrepresent the true surface potential if the charges cannot stably remain on the surface. 
We will incorporate this understanding int o our discussion of the results. For instance, when 
interpreting our sensor outputs, we will clarify that the deep learning model may be implicitly 
learning the effective outcome of both the surface potential difference and the charge retention 
characteristics of the materials. The humidity effect discussed (Fig. 3d) indeed partly relates to 
charge screening by water molecules, which is another way charges are neutralized. We will 
extend that discussion to explicitly mention trap state effects. By doing so,  we address the 
reviewer’s point: acknowledging that our current model correlates the net triboelectric output 
(which is influenced by surface potential as well as charge trapping/dissipation) to the reference 
surface potential measurement. We will add ref erences and reasoning to support how trap 
depths influence triboelectric outputs and ensure the manuscript does not oversimplify contact 
electrification. This additional discussion will strengthen the physical grounding of our 
approach and make clear its assumptions and limitations. 
Comment 3: “A central weakness is the challenge of inferring a microscopic and localized 
property (surface potential, which can be highly non -uniform due to surface roughness, 
contaminants, or inhomogeneous charge distribution) from a macroscopic and integrated 
electrical output (the V oc of a TENG). The authors assume a single, representative surface 
potential value for each material, but in reality, the measured Voc is an average over the entire 
contact area. The authors should address this discrepancy explicitly. How does the model 
perform when contacting a material with known, deliberate inhomogeneities? Some discussion 
on the statistical nature of the prediction (i.e., it predicts an effective surface potential) is 
required.” 
Ans) We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comments regarding the relationship between 
microscopic surface potential variations and the macroscopic triboelectric output of the dual -
reference sensor. We clarify that the aim of this work is not to predict highly localized or

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

nanoscopic surface potential values, but rather to infer an effective, material -level surface 
potential measured under well-defined reference conditions. In our study, the “surface potential” 
used for model training was determined by averaging multiple KPFM measurements performed 
at spatially separated points at 20% RH, and this averaged value—already representing an area-
integrated property—was used as the ground-truth label. Thus, the target value of the prediction 
task is inherently an area-averaged effective surface potential, not a point-specific microscopic 
potential. It is also important to emphasize that triboelectric measurements were conducted at 
both 20% and 50% RH, and our deep learning framework was explicitly designed to use  the 
triboelectric output obtained under these humidity conditions to predict the material’s effective 
surface potential at 20% RH. In other words, the goal of the model is not to estimate “the surface 
potential at any humidity,” but to infer a consistent reference value (20% RH surface potential) 
from triboelectric signals measured under varying humidity. Because the dual-reference signals 
(PDMS and PEI(b) -PDMS) respond differently to humidity -induced charge screening, the 
combined two -channel output conta ins humidity -dependent compensatory information that 
enables robust prediction of the 20%-RH surface potential even when measured at 50% RH. 
 Regarding surface roughness or natural spatial variation, all materials used in this study 
were intentionally prepared as triboelectrically homogeneous, smooth surfaces (spin -coated 
polymer films with RMS <10 nm, cleaned commercial films and foils with RMS <120 nm), 
thereby minimizing local heterogeneity. While surface roughness may modulate absolute 
output magnit ude, the dual -reference configuration preserves the relative polarity and 
magnitude relationship between the two outputs, which is the key information used by the 
model to infer the effective surface potential. Therefore, as long as the material exhibits uniform 
triboelectric behavior across the contact area, our method reliably predicts its averaged surface 
potential. We agree with the reviewer that a surface with deliberate or extreme inhomogeneity 
(e.g., patterned composite regions) would produce triboel ectric responses corresponding to a 
weighted average of the local domains. Although such structures were beyond the scope of the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

present study, our model would, by design, output an intermediate effective surface potential, 
consistent with both the area-averaged nature of TENG signals and the averaged KPFM labels 
used for training. We will explicitly state in the revised manuscript that our framework predicts 
an effective, area-averaged surface potential and that microscale variations are not resolved by 
the present sensor design. We also recognize the reviewer’s point that including patterned or 
composite surfaces could further extend the applicability of the approach, and we identify this 
as a promising direction for future work. 
Comment 4: “The authors use PDMS (pristine and functionalized) as the dual-reference sensor 
material. This choice is paradoxical and requires strong justification. PDMS is widely 
recognized in the triboelectric community as a material with comparatively low charge trapping 
capability and weak triboelectric output (often cited as a ‘weakly negative’ material). The 
manuscript would be strengthened by explicitly addressing why a material with such inherent 
limitations was chosen as the foundation for a high -accuracy sensing platform. A  discussion 
comparing the charge retention properties (e.g., trap density and depth) of PDMS against more 
triboelectrically strong materials (like PTFE or FEP) is necessary to validate this fundamental 
design choice.” 
Ans) We appreciate the reviewer’s insi ghtful comment regarding the use of PDMS (both 
pristine and functionalized) as the dual-reference material. Although PDMS is often described 
as a weakly negative triboelectric material with relatively shallow trap states, there were 
deliberate and practical reasons for selecting PDMS as the foundation of our sensing platform. 
 First, PDMS provides a surface for controlled polarity tuning. By grafting branched 
PEI—which contains abundant amine groups —onto one PDMS layer, we created a strongly 
positive reference, while pristine PDMS served as a stable negative reference. This allowed us 
to construct a positive –negative reference pair using the same polymer , ensuring consistent 
mechanical compliance, surface roughness, and viscoelastic properties between the tw o

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 13 -->

reference electrodes. Such uniformity minimizes mechanical artifacts and ensures that 
differences in the triboelectric signals arise primarily from the material -dependent charge -
transfer behavior rather than inconsistencies in the reference layers.  Second, while PTFE or 
FEP exhibit stronger triboelectric output, they also possess very deep trap states and strong 
charge-retention behavior, often leading to memory effects, hysteresis, and slow charge 
relaxation. These features are undesirable for a referenc e electrode, where stability and cycle -
to-cycle repeatability are more critical than maximizing the absolute output signal. In contrast, 
PDMS exhibits shallower traps and releases residual charges more readily, returning to a 
consistent baseline with minim al history dependence. This property makes PDMS more 
suitable for repeated triboelectric contact cycles, particularly when used as a reference surface. 
Third, the dual-reference model leverages the relative and differential changes between the two 
PDMS-based signals rather than relying on a large absolute voltage amplitude from a high -
output material. The stable polarity contrast between pristine and PEI -functionalized PDMS 
provides two reliable data channels that the deep learning model can fuse to extract the material-
dependent triboelectric characteristics. Thus, the effectiveness of the reference surfaces comes 
from their reproducibility and controllable polarity rather than their absolute triboelectric 
strength.

Ans) We appreciate the reviewer’s valuable comment regarding the limited number of target 
materials used in this study. The proposed framework addresses a multivariate time -series 
regression problem  rather than a conventional classification task. Specifically, the model 
predicts surface potential values directly from sequential noisy sensing signals. As shown in
