Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript presents an extensive experimental study of the low-cycle fatigue behaviour of an additively manufactured
(AM), dual-precipitate-strengthened high-entropy alloy, supported by strain-controlled fatigue testing, SEM and TEM,
dislocation analysis, and in-situ neutron diffraction. The scope of the experimental work is large, and it is clear that
significant effort has been invested since the original submission. Narrowing the focus to LCF and expanding the
microstructural characterisation are positive steps. However, in my view, the manuscript still falls short of conceptual clarity
and evidentiary rigour. I’ve highlighted these below in point form. 
1. The Results section contains numerous statements attributing fatigue behaviour to specific mechanisms, for example,
planar versus wavy slip, enhanced deformation reversibility, delayed crack initiation, precipitate shearing, or strain
compatibility between phases. These are often not directly / or weakly supported by evidence/results (but are often only
supported by citations). While these interpretations are often plausible and consistent with existing literature, they (at least to
me) are frequently presented as conclusions rather than as tested hypotheses. 
2. The role of porosity and defects is largely neglected. The work assumes high density based on “optimised” processing,
yet provides no quantitative assessment of porosity, defect size distributions, or relative density. To me, this is a significant
omission for a fatigue-focused study, even in the low-cycle regime, where crack initiation can still be governed by the largest
defect or surface imperfection. Considering that the samples were produced using AM, defects are inherent (as seen in the
fractographs in Figure 4). How were defect populations considered? If these were comparable between conditions, or if
initiation is not defect-controlled, it is difficult/impossible to isolate the role of precipitates and deformation mode in the
reported fatigue trends, especially since multiple initiation sites are argued to be a controlling mechanism. 
3. I find the introduction weakly framed. It relies heavily on qualitative language (“high strength”, “good ductility”, “enhanced
fatigue resistance”) without reporting actual strength and ductility values for the studied alloy or for relevant benchmark
materials. This makes it difficult to judge the absolute performance level and to contextualise the reported fatigue lives. 
4. I would strongly recommend that, from a processing and reporting standpoint, the use ISO/ASTM terminology (over legacy
terms such as “SLM” and “as-printed”). I would also encourage including key processing details, e.g., laser power, scan
speed, hatch spacing, layer thickness, and scan strategy, as well as post-processing steps, such as machining. 
5. Finally, the statistical robustness of the fatigue data remains a concern. The number of specimens per condition appears
limited, and some of the reported trends (e.g. changes in Coffin–Manson behaviour or crossovers between material states)
may fall within the expected scatter for AM materials. While the in-situ neutron diffraction data provide valuable insight into
microstructural evolution, they do not compensate for limited fatigue life statistics when strong conclusions are drawn.

Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The study investigates low-cycle fatigue behaviour in an L-PBF-processed precipitation-strengthened high-entropy alloy,
contrasting as-built and aged conditions. The central contribution lies in linking cyclic deformation, dislocation character, and
precipitate–matrix compatibility to fatigue performance, positioning dual-precipitate AM-HEAs as promising fatigue-resistant
materials. 
The authors have made a genuine effort to correct the most serious conceptual weakness raised. One of my main concerns
was the overstatement of mechanistic interpretations. Across the Results, Discussion, and Conclusions, deterministic
language has been softened and reframed. This helps with scientific credibility. 
I still feel that the treatment of porosity and defects remains the weakest aspect of the article, even after the revision. While
the authors now explicitly acknowledge AM defects and include a qualitative optical micrograph spanning 2–3 mm, this does
not fully resolve my concern. The argument that “optimised” processing and Archimedes density measurements suffice is not
fully convincing for a fatigue-focused study, even in the LCF regime. Ideally, XCT measurements are included to show pore-
size distributions and how they correlate across different sample groups. Similarly, the concern about limited fatigue
statistics remains. Testing 2–3 specimens per strain amplitude is minimal, and some reported cross-overs may indeed lie
within AM scatter – especially if porosity is at play. 
Overall, in its current form, the manuscript is scientifically defensible and suitable for publication, provided the editor is
satisfied with the explicit limitation statements regarding defects and statistics.
