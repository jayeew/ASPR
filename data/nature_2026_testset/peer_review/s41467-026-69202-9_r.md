Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study presents the fabrication of transparent glass ceramics that demonstrate dynamic light emission induced by both
photonic and thermal stimuli. It has been observed that lithium-ion doping facilitates phase separation, allowing for the
precise precipitation of defective non-stoichiometric nanocrystals within the glass matrix. The ability to achieve multilevel
stimuli-responsive and dynamically tunable persistent luminescence in glass holds significant implications for advanced
multi-dimensional information storage and display technologies. However, I have several inquiries and questions
concerning its originality for publication in the high-impact journal Nature Communications. 
Firstly, the title "multilevel stimulus-responsive dynamic tunable persistent luminescence" is, although intriguing, somewhat
misleading. The author refers to it as "photo-induced and thermal-induced dynamic tunable persistent luminescence."
However, persistent luminescence is characterized by sustained light emission at room temperature. If it relies on photo-
induced or thermal-induced mechanisms, it does not qualify as persistent luminescence. Emissions that are photo-induced
should be accurately termed photostimulated luminescence (PSL), while those that are thermally induced are typically
referred to as thermally stimulated luminescence (TSL). The employment of captivating terminology can result in
misinterpretation and confusion in this case. 
Secondly, phase separation in glass is a relatively frequent occurrence, often detrimental to the production of high-quality
glass. Nonetheless, this research considers phase separation as a viable approach for the precipitation of non-
stoichiometric nanocrystals. I cannot endorse this purported strategy, nor do I perceive it as a beneficial method. In reality,
such separation is poorly regulated and should be avoided to achieve optimal glass properties. Also, the PersL chromaticity
is influenced by the strategic distribution of Mn2+ activators within the amorphous glass matrix, which is also attributable to
the undesirable phase segregation. 
Finally, the mechanism involving the bandgap in glass is quite peculiar, considering that glass does not possess a well-
defined energy gap. The interplay between the amorphous structure of glass and the crystalline phase introduces
considerable complexity to the mechanism. 
I also noticed the mechanism discussion and application seem quite formulaic and templated. Due to the aforementioned
issues and lack of novelty, I cannot endorse it for the high-impact journal Nature Communications.

comprehensive characterizations indicate that Li ions are simultaneously present in the

1. Evidence for Li in the crystalline lattice 
First, based on the radius similarity principle  (Li⁺: 0.59 Å, Zn ²⁺: 0.60 Å, 
coordination number CN=4)  and the successful reported precedents (Laser Photonics

crystal interstitial sites. Further, an obvious blue shift of Mn²⁺ emission in green band 
(Fig. R1, b) is detected due to the weakened crystal field at increasing Li⁺ doping (Chem. 
Mater. 2019, 31, 10161), providing the additional evidence for Li incorporation into the 
crystalline lattice. 
2. Evidence for Li in the glass phase 
On the other hand, DSC results (Fig. R1, c) show that higher Li doping lowers the 
glass transition temperature (Tg) and crystallization temperature (Tc), and increases the 
crystallization enthalpy  (ΔH), demonstrating the facilitated crystallization process 
with a reduced crystallization kinetic barrier . Meanwhile, the corresponding XRD 
patterns (Fig. R1, d) show an increase in the intensity of the diffraction peaks. These 
observations indicate that lithium ions dissolve into the glassy phase, where they act as

network modifiers to increase the nonbridging oxygen (NBO) concentration due to its 
nonbonding oxygen coordination sphere. Finally, the reduced glass network 
connectivity enables the  easier atomic rearrangement during crystallization  process. 
(Chem. Rev. 2019, 119, 7848−7939; J. Non-Cryst. Solids 2023, 603, 122113).  
However, due to the light atomic weight of lithium ions, it is challenging to 
precisely determine its actual solid solubility in the crystalline lattice using current 
experimental techniques.

Response: 
Thank you for your valuable comment. 
The apparent discrepancy between the temperature -dependent PersL and the 3D-
TL spectra arises from the fundamental differences in their excitation conditions. In the 
3D-TL measurement, the sample is charged only once at room temperature prior to the 
heating readout of the afterglow . While temperature-dependent PersL is obtained by 
charging the sample at a series of separate temperatures.  Notably, t he charging 
temperatures define the critical trap depth required for stable trap filling (Laser 
Photonics Rev. 2020, 14, 2000060). 
In 3D-TL measurement, room temperature charging results in the stable trapping

of charge carriers  at defect energy levels with various depths. During the subsequent 
heating process, although the release of the carriers follows a stepwise mechanism from

identification of luminescence centers associated with deep -level defects. In contrast, 
charging at elevated temperatures  promotes a higher filling efficiency of carriers into 
deeper trap levels due to the thermal instability of shallow traps at those temperatures.

increase indicates that deep traps are more generated in crystal phases to facilitate the 
subsequent green afterglow emission at a higher temperature.
