Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study investigates the prediction of drug targets using an in silico approach by identifying common pathways across five
comparisons of glucotonic and non-glucotonic tissues or cells. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from these
comparisons were analyzed using the REMEDY platform, where similarities in up- and down-regulation patterns were
matched with known drug signature expression profiles. This led to the identification of PKC activation as a mechanism that
mimics the glucotonic effects induced by RYGB surgery. In a series of in vitro experiments, including hyperactivation and
knockout studies, the protein kinase aPKC was found to induce GLUT1-mediated glucotonicity. The study further identified
prostratin as a compound that activates aPKC and promotes GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake. 
- Single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed increased populations of enterocytes and higher expression of PKCζ in RYGB-
treated tissues. In intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) overexpressing aPKC, GLUT1 was found to increase in the plasma
membrane. At the same time, PKC knockouts helped to analyze prostratin-induced GLUT1 translocation, with ZIP
counteracting prostratin-induced glucose uptake. 
- Further experiments on human organoids showed that GLUT1 migrated to both the basolateral and apical membranes.
Prostratin and ZIP were found to have opposing effects on GLUT1 levels at the cell surface. Overexpression of aPKC and
prostratin treatment promoted glucose excretion in IECs, an effect that STF-31 abolished. 
- In a diabetic mouse model, vector-induced overexpression of PKCζ resulted in reduced weight gain, significantly enhanced
glucose tolerance, increased glucose uptake in the distal intestines, and higher glucose excretion, all independent of insulin
levels. There were positive correlations between PKCζ expression, GLUT1 expression, and glucose uptake in both the
colon and ileum. 
- Moreover, diabetic mice treated with prostratin showed reduced weight gain, improved glucose tolerance, and increased
glucose uptake and excretion compared to control mice. The sustained effects of prostratin treatment on mice with deficient
insulin signaling further confirmed that these effects are independent of insulin.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Noteworthy Results: 
• The study identifies aPKC as a key mediator of the glucotonic effect through GLUT1 expression, combining transcriptome
analysis of intestinal models with drug screen data. 
• Prostratin, an aPKC activator, is proposed as a potential anti-diabetic drug due to its ability to induce a glucotonic effect. 
Significance and Comparison to Literature: 
• While the link between aPKC and glucose uptake is known (Farese et al. 2009, Lee et al. 2015, not cited by the authors),
the connection to the glucotonic effect in the intestine and the use of Prostratin as an anti-diabetic agent are novel and
significant contributions to the field. 
Support for Conclusions and Claims: 
• The conclusions are generally supported by the experiments. 
• However, additional evidence is needed in the form of quantifications from western blots and immunohistochemistry across
multiple biological replicates (e.g., Fig. 2h-j, 3d, 4h-i). 
Flaws in Data Analysis, Interpretation, and Conclusions: 
• The main strategy of comparing RNA sequencing data with drug treatment expression data using REMEDY is vaguely
explained, and the results lack clarity. 
• The rationale for focusing on PKC or Prostratin seems arbitrary, particularly given the unclear presentation of the
"connectivity score" in Figure 1C. 
• This is a major flaw that requires clarification, as it undermines the foundation of the study's conclusions. 
Methodology and Experimental Standards: 
• The details of key experiments in Figure 1 are insufficiently explained, making interpretation difficult. 
• The methodology appears sound overall, but the lack of clarity in certain areas hinders a full assessment of its adherence
to expected standards. 
Overall Assessment: 
The study presents potentially significant findings regarding aPKC's role in the glucotonic effect and the potential of
Prostratin as an anti-diabetic drug. However, the manuscript requires revision to address the lack of clarity in the REMEDY
analysis and the rationale for focusing on PKC/Prostratin. Additionally, providing more detailed information about the key
experiments and including quantifications from multiple biological replicates would strengthen the study's conclusions. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors demonstrate that activation of atypical protein kinase C (aPKC) promotes glucose transporter 1
(GLUT1)-mediated glucose uptake and secretion through transcriptomic analyses of glucotonic models. The identification of
prostratin, a non-tumorigenic phorbol ester, as a potential activator of the aPKC/GLUT1 pathway is particularly noteworthy.
While the manuscript offers insights into the potential therapeutic implications of activating the aPKC/GLUT1 pathway for
diabetes treatment, some key conclusions require further exploration and clarification. Specifically, the mechanisms
underlying RYGB’s activation of aPKCs and the differences between the effects of prostratin and RYGB on this pathway are
insufficiently addressed. Additional experiments are needed to verify the specificity of aPKC activation in glucotonicity and
its role in glucose metabolism. The methods for data analysis also require more detailed descriptions. Below are several
specific comments: 
For single-cell data analysis: 
1. Line 129: It is mentioned that RYGB significantly increased enterocyte populations. Was this comparison made between
the RYGB and the sham group? The UMAP plot in Fig. 2a does not clearly indicate which groups were compared, nor does
it show if the differences in cell populations were statistically significant. 
2. Fig. 2c: Was the expression of PKCζ in RYGB distal enterocytes compared to sham distal enterocytes statistically
significant? If so, please label it in the figure and specify the statistical test used in the figure legend. Were there changes
observed in other PKCs? 
3. Fig. 2c: The Y-axis is labeled “Log2 fold,” but is this indicating log2 fold change? Please clarify the denominators in this
case, or if these values represent log2 read counts. 
4. Extended Fig. 5: Are the p-values adjusted for multiple testing? If so, please indicate this on the X-axes of the figures. 
Other Comments: 
5. Does prostratin, like RYGB, increase the expression of HB-EGF? In Extended Figs. 1b and 1c, EGFR ligands were tested
only at 100 ng/mL. Given the significant molecular weight differences between HB-EGF and EGF, it would be more
appropriate to use molarity rather than mass concentration. Testing at more than two concentrations would strengthen the
conclusion. 
6. Fig. 4c and Extended Fig. 1d: It would be more informative to show and compare the area under the curve (AUC) for each
group in the IPGTT experiments. 
7. Could RYGB also increase GLUT1 expression? Including this information in Extended Fig. 1a would be valuable. 
8. Prostratin is used as an atypical PKC activator, but its effect on other PKC families is unclear. Compounds like PMA and
Ingenol, which had higher connectivity scores in CMap, are known to activate both cPKCs and nPKCs. The manuscript
should explore correlations between RYGB and other PKCs. For example, the authors should provide evidence, such as
western blots, of the activation status of different PKCs in tissue extracts following RYGB. 
9. Total aPKC levels should be shown in Fig. 2i. Additionally, including immunoblots that display membrane GLUT1
expression in Fig. 2h would strengthen the findings. 
10. After PKCδ knockout, p-GLUT1 levels following prostratin treatment were significantly reduced. However, the internal
control signal for this group is notably higher than others, suggesting the difference may be due to sample loading or
exposure. Repeating the western blot experiment is necessary.

The authors state: “We orally administered stable isotope-labeled glucose (13C6-glucose, 2 g/kg body weight) to RYGB and

majority of this glucose is excreted into the portal circulation” is not justified by the data presented.

samples and quantified 13C6-glucose concentrations using liquid chromatography -mass

The theory or process of finding the drugs/targets through a connectivity map is well described 
in the reference “Lamb et al ., The Connectivity Map: using gene -expression signatures to

connect small molecules, genes, and disease. Science 313, 1929-1935 (2006),” which is cited

Briefly, the pipeline operates by comparing the molecular signature of interest (the “query,” 
representing a disease or therapeutic model) with a database of molecular signatures (the 
“reference”). Each reference signature consists of upregulated and downregulated DEGs 
resulting from the application of a specific perturbagen. The degree of similarity between the 
query and each reference signature is quantified using statistical pattern-matching algorithms. 
In this study, we applied a modified Kolmogorov –Smirnov (KS)-based algorithm, which was 
refined in-house to improve accuracy and reduce the influence of outliers. The output is a 
connectivity score , which numerically reflects the extent to which the query DEGs either 
positively or negatively align with the reference signatures. 
This connectivity score, together with other parameters such as the number of assays 
conducted per perturbagen (termed counts), enables the statistical prioritizat ion of 
perturbagens/MoAs through a ranking process. Importantly, although the specific DEGs varied 
across sets, the PKC-related gene ontology and signaling enrichment consistently emerged . 
Both the number and identity of PKC activator -associated DEGs varied across the sets, and 
this variation is captured by the connectivity scores and subsequent ranking.
