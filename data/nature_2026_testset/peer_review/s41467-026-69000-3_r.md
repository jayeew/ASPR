Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, Yuanyuan Huang et al., assessed the role of the p53-induced gene Ei24 in the homeostasis and function of
alveolar macrophages, AMs. They found that EI24 deficiency in AMs disrupted homeostasis but enhanced phagocytosis and
inflammatory responses, eventually leading to enhanced anti-viral and anti-tumor functions. The transfer of EI24-deficient 27
bone marrow-derived macrophages more effectively inhibited viral infection and tumor metastasis in the lung than wild-type
cells. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that both epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming pathways contributed to the
enhanced functional activities observed upon EI24 deficiency. Finally, they found that the microbiota increased Ei24
expression in AMs. The Authors concluded that deleting Ei24 offers a new approach to enhance macrophage-based
immunotherapy. 
This is an interesting study based on a huge number of solid and credible scientific data. However, there are some aspects
that need clarification. 
Comments 
The authors convincingly show that EI24 deficiency is associated with the acquisition of a novel phenotype in AMs.
However, the question is how? Ei24 is a DNA damage response gene involved in the basal autophagy pathway. Is the
impairment of the autophagic flux or the accumulation of protein aggregates causing the observed phenotype? 
Will
promoting autophagy reverse the phenotype? The authors may wish to address this question in order to close the circle of
their findings. 
Figure 1 shows that EI24 deficiency did not influence macrophage death in other tissues like liver and spleen. However,
mice deficient in Ei24 show severe liver injury, a finding suggesting that EI24 deficiency may also affect the function of
Kupffer cells. Do the Authors have evidence that this may indeed occur? It would help a better positioning of the findings of
this paper within the current knowledge in the field. 
Figure 2e shows that EI24 deficient Ams do produce inflammatory cytokines upon in vitro stimulation with LPS+ATP. This
finding is apparently in contrast with the normal lung architecture e no signs of pathology in the lung in condition of EI24
deficiency, as reported in Figure 2a. One would have expected to see some sort of inflammatory pathology, considering the
lung exposure to a variety of infectious and non-infectious stimuli. 
The authors showed that depleting CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells in vivo by administration of either anti-CD8 antibody or
anti-CD4 antibody did not impact the survival rate of PR8-infected Lyz2creEi24 fl/fl mice. If the protective effect against
influenza in these mice is independent of T cells, please provide the mechanisms behind the antiviral resistance. 
Along the same reasoning, if AM depletion but not T cell depletion increases tumor metastasis in Lyz2creEi24 196 fl/fl mice,
please provide a mechanism by which Ams and not T cells mediate resistance against tumor metastasis in lung.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Huang et al. attempt to describe the role of microbiota in inducing EI24 in improving alveolar
macrophages homeostasis while impeding their function via epigenetic and metabolic regulation. Despite the generation of
a logic and sophisticated mouse model, the study lacks focus and covers too many aspects of AM physiology. In fact, results
from elegant yet too diverse experiments lead to controversial results not always in line with the available literature. This is
not a problem per se, but the question on how lack of homeostasis in AM physiology could be paired with beneficial effects
during viral infection or metastatic processes remains unclear. Moreover, the use of pathogen specific free mice, especially
in this setting, has a limited value, since this itself can lead to an altered immune response. This also dampens the
translatability of their findings. Therefore, only speculative conclusions can be made of the enormous amount of data
presented, spanning from metabolic, to metagenomic, epigenetic and other analysis, reducing the impact that the results can
have in offering new approaches to enhance macrophage-based immunotherapy. The manuscript could benefit from a
reshape and a careful consideration of what could really be instrumental to draw their conclusions.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
In this paper, Yuanyuan Huang et al., assessed the role of the p53-induced gene Ei24 in the 
homeostasis and function of alveolar macrophages, AMs. They found that EI24 deficiency in AMs 
disrupted homeostasis but enhanced phagocytosis and inflammatory responses, eventually leading 
to enhanced anti-viral and anti-tumor functions. The transfer of EI24-deficient 27 bone marrow-
derived macrophages more effectively inhibited viral infection and tumor metastasis in the lung than 
wild-type cells. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that both epigenetic and metabolic 
reprogramming pathways contributed to the enhanced functional activities observed upon EI24 
deficiency. Finally, they found that the microbiota increased Ei24 expression in AMs. The Authors 
concluded that deleting Ei24 offers a new approach to enhance macrophage-based immunotherapy. 
This is an interesting study based on a huge number of solid and credible scientific data. However, 
there are some aspects that need clarification. 
Comments 
The authors convincingly show that EI24 deficiency is associated with the acquisition of a novel 
phenotype in AMs. However, the question is how? Ei24 is a DNA damage response gene involved 
in the basal autophagy pathway. Is the impairment of the autophagic flux or the accumulation of 
protein aggregates causing the observed phenotype? Will promoting autophagy reverse the 
phenotype? The authors may wish to address this question in order to close the circle of their 
findings. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for bringing up this issue. We understand the reviewer’s concern, 
since EI24 has been previously shown to regulate autophagy1, 2. According to reviewer’s suggestion, 
we analyzed autophagy in alveolar macrophages and found that EI24-deficient AMs exhibited 
impaired autophagy, as indicated by increased p62 accumulation and a reduced LC3-II/I ratio 
(Extended Data Fig. 5a). However, rapamycin administration in Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice promoted 
autophagy in EI24-deficient AMs but failed to reverse those phenotypes, including cell frequencies, 
active state, phagocytic capacity, and IL-6 production (Extended Data Fig. 5b-f). These results 
exclude the possibility that EI24 deficiency changes AM function and fate via downregulating 
autophagy. These data are shown below and are included in the revised manuscript.  
In Fig. 5, we showed that both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) were enhanced 
in EI24-deficient AMs, and with new data we demonstrated that the enhanced metabolic activity 
contributed to increased functions and Caspase-3 activation (Fig. 5i, j, n). Therefore, the altered 
metabolism explains the elevated cell function and apoptosis in EI24-deficient AMs.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

Figure 1 shows that EI24 deficiency did not influence macrophage death in other tissues like liver 
and spleen. However, mice deficient in Ei24 show severe liver injury, a finding suggesting that 
EI24 deficiency may also affect the function of Kupffer cells. Do the Authors have evidence that 
this may indeed occur? It would help a better positioning of the findings of this paper within the 
current knowledge in the field. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this issue. Previous work using hepatocyte-specific 
EI24 knockout mice (Ei24fl/fl mice were crossed with mice expressing Cre controlled by the albumin 
promoter) has shown that loss of EI24 in hepatocytes leads to hepatomegaly and structural 
abnormalities of the liver, including irregular lobules and swollen hepatocytes 1. In our study with 
Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice, EI24 was deficient in macrophages, and that showed no influence on 
frequencies of Kupffer cells in liver and red pulp macrophages in spleen (Fig. 1e, f), but reduced 
AM frequencies in lung (Fig. 1c). In support of the key role of EI24 in maintaining AM homeostasis, 
we found that AMs expressed high levels of EI24, whereas Kupffer cells and red pulp macrophages 
expressed low levels, as indicated by GFP level in those cells from Ei24-Gfp reporter mice. This 
data is shown in Fig.1a and shown below. Meanwhile, in Fig. 4e-h, we provide new evidence to 
show that EI24 in AMs is upregulated by microbiota through TLR2/4 signaling in lung to maintain 
AM survival. AMs in germ-free mice had low levels of Ei24, and in germ-free mice deficiency of 
EI24 did not reduce AM frequencies (Fig. 4b). It is likely that the “open” microenvironment in lung 
stresses AMs, and EI24 is required for their survival in this stress condition. This EI24-mediated 
regulatory pathway is absent/not required for red pulp macrophages and Kupffer cells due to their 
distinct tissue microenvironment. New data are included in manuscript, and we discuss this in the 
Discussion section.  
Additionally, we also performed histological analysis of liver sections. As shown below, there was 
no liver injury or inflammation in Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice.

Figure 2e shows that EI24 deficient Ams do produce inflammatory cytokines upon in vitro 
stimulation with LPS+ATP. This finding is apparently in contrast with the normal lung architecture 
e no signs of pathology in the lung in condition of EI24 deficiency, as reported in Figure 2a. One 
would have expected to see some sort of inflammatory pathology, considering the lung exposure to 
a variety of infectious and non-infectious stimuli. 
Response: We understand the reviewer’s concern. In line with the normal lung architecture and 
function in Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice, levels of inflammatory cytokines in their BALF were low and hardly 
differed from those in  Ei24 fl/fl mice (Fig. 3a, b, shown below) in physiological condition. These 
results demonstrate that lung exposure to non-infectious stimuli would not induce full activation of 
EI24 deficient AMs, and thus would not lead to obvious lung inflammation. On the other hand, in 
viral infectious condition, Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice cleared virus more efficiently than Ei24fl/fl mice and 
exhibited better survival (Fig. 3d, f).  
The authors showed that depleting CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells in vivo by administration of 
either anti-CD8 antibody or anti-CD4 antibody did not impact the survival rate of PR8-infected 
Lyz2creEi24 fl/fl mice. If the protective effect against influenza in these mice is independent of T 
cells, please provide the mechanisms behind the antiviral resistance. 
Along the same reasoning, if AM depletion but not T cell depletion increases tumor metastasis in 
Lyz2creEi24 196 fl/fl mice, please provide a mechanism by which Ams and not T cells mediate 
resistance against tumor metastasis in lung. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising these issues. Our data indicate that the enhanced 
antiviral and antitumor resistance observed in Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice is primarily mediated by AMs 
rather than adaptive T cells (Fig. 3g-j, m-q). Previous studies demonstrate that macrophages could 
directly clear tumor cells via phagocytosis 3, 4. In our study, we proved that EI24-deficient AMs

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

displayed significantly increased phagocytosis of mCherry-expressing tumor cells in vitro , 
confirming an enhanced capacity to directly eliminate tumor cells. This data is now shown in Fig. 
3r, s and shown below. 
In terms of viral clearance, AMs are the main source of type I interferons (IFNs) which are well-
known key molecules in viral control5, 6. Indeed, we observed elevated IFNα levels in BALF from 
PR8-infected Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice, in comparison with BALF from infected Ei24fl/fl mice. This 
finding explained the enhanced anti-viral function of EI24-deficient AMs. This data is now shown 
in Fig. 3k and shown below.

improving alveolar macrophages homeostasis while impeding their function via epigenetic and

the manuscript. We performed additional experiments according to reviewers’ suggestions and 
reorganized the data. Now we show 6 figures in manuscript. Epigenetic data are moved to extended 
figures to show the concordance between chromatin accessibility and gene transcription, and to 
support the enhanced activity of EI24-deficient AMs. Germ-free mice related data are used to prove 
the upregulation of EI24 expression in AMs via microbiota activated TLR signaling. Germ-free 
mice data with limited value are deleted. The major points of our findings are explained below.

apoptosis driven by caspase activation. New data are included to prove the involvement of caspase 
activation. Briefly, when Lyz2creEi24fl/fl mice were administrated with caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-
FMK, it reduced apoptosis and restored AM numbers, demonstrating that caspase-induced apoptosis 
is a key driver of AM loss upon EI24 deficiency. In Fig. 2, we showed that EI24 deficiency in AMs 
enhanced their functions including inflammatory cytokine production and phagocytosis. In Fig. 3, 
we proved that EI24 deficiency in AMs enhanced their anti-tumor and anti-viral effects. With

additional experiment, we show that EI24-deficient AMs cleared tumor cells via enhanced
