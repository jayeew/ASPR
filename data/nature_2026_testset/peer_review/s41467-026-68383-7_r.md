Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Heterogametic progeny of crosses between two different species are often absent or sterile. For example, crosses between
the nematodes Caenorhabditis nigoni XX females and C. briggsae X0 males produce F1 fertile females and sterile males,
which conforms to this Haldane law. Here the authors use elegant genetic experiments to 1) transform sexual identity and 2)
render animals tetraploid to test the cause for this pattern: Is the male sex development particularly sensitive? Or would
instead a male developing with two parental X chromosomes be fertile, indicating instead a X-autosome incompatibility? 
The authors show that it is the occurrence of a single X and not the developing male sexual state that matters, as the
paternal X rescues the male sterility in XX sex-transformed male animals and tetraploids. At least in this cross, sterility
derives from an incompatibility of the hemizygous maternal X with the paternal autosomes. The authors present a possible
model in the Discussion on how this could occur. 
Other exciting results from this work are: 1) the threshold of X:autosome ratio that determines sex is not the same in these
two species as in C. elegans; 2) tetraploids provide a way to easily obtain fertile hybrids of the two species, which can of
great use for further studies. 
The work is beautiful and important. My main problem with this manuscript is the lack of detailed information and controls. 
Specific comments in order of the text: 
1) The title is too general: this is just one instance in nematodes.

one is dominant. 
lines 24 -37: are the two hypotheses incompatible? It seems that one could have a maternal X - paternal autosome
incompatibility that matters in males.

whole brood? Please provide a proof that tra-1 males differ from males in panel A. What were the crosses of the next

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript with the title “Haldane’s law works through X:Autosome incompatibility in nematodes”, Hardin et al
propose that the critical feature underlying Haldane’s rule is incompatibility between a sex chromosome derived from only
one species, and autosome pairs derived from both. For their experiments, the authors use the Caenorhabditis species C.
briggsea and C. nigoni, which have previously been shown to produce hybrids, and they take advantage of a sex
determination mutant (tra-1 lf) and tetraploid animals to investigate the mechanistic basis of Haldane’s rule. 
Overall, the
experiments are well done and the data convincing. However, I disagree with the authors conclusion. Specifically, with the
experiments performed and the data obtained, they cannot rule out that the phenomenon is the result of a deleterious
recessive mutation on the X chromosome and a recessive suppressor on an autosome.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
In this manuscript with the title “Haldane’s law works through X:Autosome incompatibility in 
nematodes”, Hardin et al propose that the critical feature underlying Haldane’s rule is 
incompatibility between a sex chromosome derived from only one species, and autosome pairs 
derived from both. For their experiments, the authors use the Caenorhabditis species C. briggsea 
and C. nigoni, which have previously been shown to produce hybrids, and they take advantage of 
a sex determination mutant (tra-1 lf) and tetraploid animals to investigate the mechanistic basis 
of Haldane’s rule. Overall, the experiments are well done and the data convincing.  
However, I disagree with the authors conclusion. Specifically, with the experiments performed 
and the data obtained, they cannot rule out that the phenomenon is the result of a deleterious 
recessive mutation on the X chromosome and a recessive suppressor on an autosome. 
We agree that the scenario the reviewer describes could explain hybrid male defects. Indeed, 
in a formal sense, loss of “B” from the X chromosome could be viewed as a deleterious 
mutation on X, and a dosage compensated “B” on an autosome could be viewed as a recessive 
suppressor of that deleterious mutation. Examples involving two, three or more genes also 
work. 
Since the nature of the genes in our model is not specified, any movement of genes between 
chromosomes and subsequent changes in dosage compensation could create hybrid 
incompatibilities in XO animals. Some of these changes could indeed manifest as mutations 
in one gene that suppress those in another. 
 
Many thanks for your consideration,
