Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presented by Kirino et al. aims to understand the relationship between revSCs and CRCs in the intestinal
epithelium and hence compare the concepts they describe as spatial plasticity and fetal reversion. They utilise primarily
organoid cultures and lineage tracing to address these concepts. This is reinforced by some analysis in mouse models and
transplantation of tissue. 
Overall, although there are some results that add to the overall field, the majority of the work does not provide much
additional insight to what is already known. The manuscript also lacks clarity in some parts and many experimental
outcomes need to be verified by more quantitative data as outlined in the specific comments below. 
Specific comments. 
1. The authors claim they are comparing a “spatial plasticity” model to “fetal reversion” yet they primarily use organoid
assays to examine this. How can spatial cues be examined when the overall structure of the crypt villus axis is lost with
many cell types and components missing? 
2. The authors claim that the relationship between differentiating enterocytes, CBC stem cells and revSCs is not known
during intestinal regeneration. There have been numerous studies that have examined this in mouse models. Tetteh et al.
Cell Stem Cell (2016) used the same AlpiCRE line used in this study to demonstrate using lineage tracing that enterocytes
can reconstitute LGR5 CBCs in crypts. This original study was performed in vivo where all the tissue components were
present, making this a more physiologically relevant study to that completed by the authors in this study. 
Likewise, Ayyaz et all Nature 2019 showed that when CBC cells are lost following damage, RevSCs appear and again,
utilising lineage tracing within the mouse, show that RevSCs produce CBCs. Bi-direction regeneration has also been
previously shown. 
3. The authors compare collagen and Matrigel for the capability to support CBC versus RevSCs this has also be
demonstrated by Yui et al. Cell Stem Cell 2019. 
4. Figure 1 scRNA sequencing. This analysis was performed at one timepoint making it difficult to compare. Were the
organoids in collagen and Matrigel harvested at a similar timepoint of growth? This experiment is difficult to interpret with
one timepoint.

identify one population or do they have distinct signatures. Likewise with the CBC signature UMAP.

distinguish these populations? The authors should comment and clarify this. 
What do the revSC1 and 2 clusters represent? What does the CBC clusters 1 and 2 represent?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary: 
The study from Kirino and co-authors builds upon the group’s previous expertise on the phenomenon of fetal-like reversion,
a regenerative mechanism utilized by the intestinal epithelium to resolve various types of damage (Yui et al 2018). In the
present study, the authors initially utilize two types of 3D cultures to compare organoids grown in Matrigel (used to model
“homeostatic” adult epithelium) to fetal-like spheroids grown in Collagen I (used to model “fetal reversion” or regenerating
epithelium, often marked by expression of Sca1) with the main aim of dissecting the lineage relationship between Lgr5+
CBCs, Sca1+ revSCs and differentiated cells. Using these culture systems, the authors convincingly show that there is a
bidirectional conversion between CBCs and revSCs. Next, using Alpi-Cre labeled organoid and villus cultures, the authors
show that Alpi+ cells can also generate revSC both when labeled in vitro, and when cultured in collagen ex vivo. Finally, the
authors show that revSC cultures are more resistant to 5-FU mediated damage compared to organoids grown in Matrigel
and identify GST activity as a potential mediator of this process. The strengths of the paper include the villus cultures and in
vitro damage response experiments with 5-FU. The manuscript would benefit from a deeper characterization of what the
authors term “inflammatory resilience”. Additionally, certain conclusions are not strongly supported by the data presented
and should be tempered. 
Major points: 
1) The lineage relationships between enterocytes and revSCs inferred based on RNA velocity are not convincing. The
authors perform scRNA seq of organoids and show that in both mouse and human organoids, Matrigel cultures contain
diverse cells, while collagen cultures display a more homogenous revSC phenotype. Based on RNA velocity in both
species, the authors suggest that revSCs arise from both Lgr5 stem cells and Alpi+ enterocyte lineage, however, the
directionality, especially from the enterocyte clusters is difficult to visualize and therefore interpret. In human organoids, the
collagen cultures contain two clusters. Here the velocity relationship between the Lgr5 cluster and the 2 revSC clusters is
clearer, although the plot size should be increased to enable better visualization. Given the very small size of cluster 3, it is
again difficult to fully agree with the conclusion that absorptive enterocytes give rise to revSCs as stated in line 145-148. In
line 197-199, the claim regarding differentiated cells (i.e. “both progenitors and differentiated cells retain the capacity for fetal
reversion”) is based only RNA velocity and should be moderated. 
2) The authors show that collagen organoids might be more primed for inflammatory resilience based on gene expression of
unperturbed organoids. This is an interesting observation and it would be important to expand upon this finding to
understand how these distinct cell states respond once damage is induced. Can the authors profile both types of culture
(Matrigel and Collagen) after 5-FU treatment to understand the differences in damage response? 
3) In reference to their villus organoid cultures, the authors claim that Alpi+ villus cells re-acquire stemness in the absence of
oncogenic mutation. While the reviewer agrees that culture of cells in collagen likely does not involve oncogenic
transformation, the authors do not present any genetic data to support their claim. They should temper the language used in
line 277, and 391. 
4) For quantification of clonal ribbon analysis in 5FU treated mice (Figure 5E, Figure S3C) swiss rolls visualizing the full
length of the small intestine would better demonstrate the point and show how consistent this phenotype is across multiple
animals. The authors should display individual datapoints in figure S3C. From the data presented, it appears that this
phenomenon is quite rare- with ~12 ribbons per 5 cm of tissue- therefore the conclusion presented in line 315-317 should be
tempered, as plasticity in epithelial gene expression or changes in niche signals in damaged tissue may be contributing to
the observed lineage tracing. Similarly, the claim in line 387-388 that involvement of enterocytes represents a “robust
regenerative mechanism” seems overstated based on this evidence. 
5) Can the authors better characterize the human organoid data? Do the same pathways emerge in human organoids as
mouse organoids (Fig 6D-E)? Can the authors visualize glutathione metabolism-related genes in their dataset? Conversely,
are differences in GST activity (Figure 6F) also observed in mouse organoids cultures in collagen vs. matrigel?

6) In several IF images, it is difficult to evaluate whether staining is real or whether signal is arising from autofluorescence
(often seen in the center of organoids). This is a concern for Alpi staining in Figure 2K, 3E and Olfm4 staining in Figure 4E.
Higher resolution and single channel images could be included to help readers visualize the signal the authors are

the organoid, so the positive stain is clearer. 
Minor points: 
1) Do V-organoids grown in collagen differ from crypt-derived collagen organoids in cell composition or transcriptionally? 
2) For the human single cell data, can the authors show more genes besides only FABP1 or perform gene set enrichment

have high FDR values (<0.25) and do not clearly show gene set enrichment.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study from the Yui lab presents interesting new work on an emerging area of the regeneration field – the revival or
regenerative of fetal-like stem cell. The authors use organoid cultures and lineage tracing approaches to characterize the
ability of different cell populations to grow in collagen (pro-regeneration conditions) or Matrigel (homeostatic). They also use
in vivo experiments to look at organoid engraftment assays or in vivo lineage tracing assays to study the origins and inter-
conversion between homeostatic stem cells and regenerative stem cells. I think the work is interesting and will add to
knowledge of the field in this important area. There are some concerns that I would suggest to improve the rigor and clarity of
the work, which I detail below: 
Please make the omics data public to reviewers (at least) and indicate the number of replicates conducted for the omics
studies (scRNA in fig.1 and RNAseq in fig. 6, especially). 
The analysis of GST activity seems incomplete in Fig. 6 and I don’t think it adds enough to the current study to be included.
This idea seems to need more development for publication. For example, can increased antioxidants enhance viability to 5-
FU? Can genetic targeting of GST hamper survival against 5-FU? I recommend publishing without panels 6F-H and
developing the story more for a future publication where it can be completed more rigorously. 
RNA velocity can be over-interpreted, especially depending upon replicates and sequencing depth. It’s good that the
authors performed serial organoid assays and lineage tracing to verify the results, but I suggest softening the conclusions
about the RNAvelocity in the results section. 
I suggest plotting 2D on a log scale or on another axis for the GFPlow and GFPneg conditions. Also, please include
individual data points from each replicate for this graph. It is currently hard to evaluate. 
Lineage tracing from Alpi-Cre seems like a pretty rare event? Can the authors comment (or if possible quantify) on the
relative contribution of different intestinal cell types to the regenerative cells? 
In the methods, different organoid media formulations are used throughout the manuscript. I commend the authors for
declaring this carefully, but it’s such an important point that I suggest including these details in the figure legends as well,
otherwise readers might not fully interpret the experiments correctly. 
The GSEA results don’t look very robust in some cases (FDR < .25). It might help the reader to publish the full list of results
or publish a GO term analysis of the RNAseq in a supplement to provide a more complete picture of the data? 
What is the source of cWNRE? 
The text states that Ly6a was stained for in 5C but that doesn’t seem to be in the figure. 
Is the 0 centered on graph in 6D?
