Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper by Nunez et al. describes a novel chip-based approach that detects circulating mesenchymal cells shortly after
heterotopic ossification induction in a mouse model. The concept of circulating osteoprogenitors remains controversial,
specifically in terms of the physiologic significance of these cells in bone formation. However, the authors cleverly make use
of cMPCs as a biomarker for the initiation of heterotopic ossification, which if verified has significant diagnostic potential in
the clinic. Overall, this is a well-written and concise manuscript that addresses an important problem. There are some
weaknesses that should be addressed, and a more rigorous presentation of some data would enhance this manuscript. 
A few major points are: 
The authors verified that cMPCs are not detected at meaningful levels following multiple modes of injury without HO. This is
interesting, as the genes expressed by the cMPCs match a (likely activated) fibroblast phenotype and beg the question why
HO is more potent in terms of inducing the “shedding” of these cells into the bloodstream. Would circulating cells associated
with these other modes of injury (if any) at different timepoints higher? More importantly, would bone fracture also result in
cMPC shedding, and if it does, could the associated signal be distinguished from HO following a traumatic injury? 
Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure 3: 
Relevant to the previous point, a more rigorous characterization of cMPC transcriptome would be helpful. Several genes in
the violin plot in Supplementary Figure 3c are not detectable in many of the cMPCs. Is it possible that there are subclusters
here that could not be detected due to low cell quantity? More importantly, what do the Col1a2-negative cells express, if not
type I collagen, which is a principal marker for mesenchymal cells? How about Pdgfra? 
Page 3, line 21: 
The number of cells detected with this method are (although seemingly robust) very low (4 cells/ml at baseline), and I
presume the authors had to collect all the blood they could from mice immediately after euthanasia. How successful would
such a platform be when implemented to diagnostic use on human patients? 
Minor comments: 
Page 2, line 14: How about palovarotene? 
Page 3, line 25: Why do you think you observe an early increase in cMPCs followed by a reduction by 7 days? 
Figure 1d: A more rigorous, unbiased analysis of the RNA-seq data (for instance top differences and gene set enrichment
analysis, as well as larger supplementary tables) would be helpful here. 
Figure 1e-g, Page 4 line 16: “Thus, for the first time, we have demonstrated that rare circulating MPCs are present in the
blood after an HO-inducing injury and that these derive from the periosteum/peritenon.“ These data indicate that the cMPCs
are of the Gli1-lineage, however, there still remains the possibility that these circulating cells have originated from an

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Nunez et al. evaluate the performance of a method to capture circulating skeletal lineage cells and
performing bulk RNA-seq as a diagnostic to predict the development of heterotopic ossification. 
The work overall is seen as
conceptually appealing and creative, with the main appeal being the broader concept that liquid biopsy approaches can be
applied to musculoskeletal disorders. If substantiated in further work, this concept could ultimately have broad importance for
disorders outside of HO by providing a proof of concept for the ability of liquid biopsy to inform about a wider set of
musculoskeletal disorders. 
The technical approaches are seen as sound, though a few likely non-critical points are noted
below. 
The main weakness is that the current data are a bit thin overall to make a strong, high-impact point about the utility
and significance of the proposed cMPC liquid biopsy method, as the fairly limited demonstration of this in a mouse model is
not seen as quite convincing or rigorous enough for the present venue. Demonstrating a mouse diagnostic is not enough for
a high impact report. However, there would be a high degree of interest in a revised manuscript that could better substantiate
this proposed liquid biopsy method with additional data. 
Major points 1 and 2 below are the most important issues. 
Major points 
1. The concept of this study is creative and intriguing, but the validation performed only on mice here doesn't quite go far
enough to establish this concept. As the authors are aware, the homogeneity and tight control possible in a mouse system
make demonstrating robust diagnostic performance in this setting a very low bar to clear. Additionally, the claim of enhanced
time to detection versus radiograph is not strongly compelling when made in a mouse model. Given the proposed diagnostic
application here, validation of performance on human specimens is needed to substantiate the impact of this study. 
2. The effort in Sup Fig 2 to measure total cMPCs doesn’t go far enough to establish the specificity of the proposed
approach. Really, such studies are only strongly meaningful if the full cMPC isolation + bulk RNA seq approach is applied to
full models of skeletal or soft tissue injury that are relevant clinical diagnostic conundrums. Really, such a study would be
most meaningful if performed in human specimens when examining clinically relevant conditions that represent a diagnostic
dilemma as to whether HO will develop or not. 
3. The conclusion that Gli1-lineage labeling of the cMPCs indicates a long bone periosteal origin is perhaps premature. Gli1
is well known to label broadly, though the data that endosteal labeling was not observed under the specific conditions used
here is noted. What about Gli1 labeling calvarium? Ribs? Vertebrae? What about fibroadipose progenitors present in the
muscle interstitium? It is challenging with the current data to exclude the occurrence of Gli1-lineage labeling at sites other
than those examined here and that those other sites contribute to the labeled circulating cells. 
4. What is the purity of the cMPC preparations input for downstream analysis like bulk-seq? How many CD45+ cells are
present? How much does this purity vary from sample to sample (again this would be most meaningful if conducted in
humans). Does sample to sample variation in purity impact the downstream analysis? The implications of this purity
question for the broader analysis should be more thoroughly considered in the text. 
5. As an optional comment, it is possible that epigenetic profiling like ATAC-seq or methyl-seq of cMPCs will be more robust
than the RNA-seq performed here. There is precedent for this in the oncology liquid biopsy space. It is appreciated that this
is a somewhat different direction than the present report and therefore this is intended as an optional comment. However,
perhaps this or a similar direction to more comprehensively profile cMPCs and more strongly establish the mechanism of
their disease link would provide an alternative pathway to improve the scope of the work if the suggested human studies in
major comment 1 and 2 cannot be fully completed. 
6. Projecting the bulk seq cMPC profile here onto the authors' prior studies of HO scRNA-seq characterization is likely to be
useful in generating initial insights into the relationship of cMPCs to the cells directly present at the HO site. 
7. Was a separate validation cohort run in HO vs control mice that is distinct from the initial dataset used to nominate the HO-
associated cMPC markers? This was not totally clear, but would be important if not done. 
Minor points 
1. There is a long history of studying circulating skleetal-lineage cells that needs a brief summary in the intro for readers to
understand the present study context. 
This introduction should explicitly state that these circulating cells do not subsequently
engraft into skeletal tissue and are therefore of no direct relevance themselves for skeletal physiology as shown in
parabiosis experiments by Charles Chan, Ivo Kalajzic and others. However, this lack of engraftment does not preclude the
creative application of profiling these cells for diagnostic purposes as performed here. 
2. Why focus on CD90+PDGFRa+ cells when other immunophenotypes are known to circulate? In the Chan schema,
CD90+ cells are typically considered fairly far down the differentiation chain and this subset of cells is likely to contain a fair
percentage of osteocalcin+ mature osteoblasts. Is progenitor the right term for these cells? There is also some discussion int
he field that perhaps the term mesenchymal should be reserved for the embryonic mesenchyme. 
3. Line 36--adipoq+ cells are now considered as adipoCAR cells and should be labeled as such for clarity. 
The Qin lab eLife

report establishing the identity of these cells should be cited, likely alongside the Baccin et al. Nat Cell Bio paper
establishing the adipoCAR vs osteoCAR schema. 
4. Sup Fig 2. These additional controls are appreciated and helpful, but it is premature to claim that other disease processes
do not impact circulating skeletal cells on the basis of total cMPC levels alone. These processes could still impact cMPC
circulation without impacting total numbers. The language here should be qualified a bit more. 
5. Line 32 missing text "had a statistically significant expression" 
6. Line 31, "we expected HO-specific cMPC gene expression to be zero in HO-negative conditions." This doesn't seem like

7. There has been much consideration in the pathology literature about the practical and regulatory challenges of applying

ensured? What specimens are appropriate for quality control? How can proficiency testing be conducted and how would it
be judged if a given lab has passed or failed the proficiency testing? These issues are of practical importance for the
implementation of this and similar approaches and should be considered in brief in the discussion. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is an original manuscript by Nunez et al. that reports an identification of circulating mesenchymal progenitor cells
(cMPCs) in peripheral blood, which are positive for Pdgfra and CD90 and are derived from the heterotopic ossification (HO)
site in soft tissues, as an early marker of HO. Identified cMPCs expressed six unique genes, which were expressed in cells
at the HO site prior to and after injury. A prediction score for the expression of the 6 genes was 88% sensitive and 100%
specific to an HO injury compared to non-HO injuries. Furthermore, in a mouse model of HO, administration of beta-
aminopropionitril (BAPN), which suppresses HO, reduced the expression of the six genes in cMPC. Together, the authors
claimed that the identified cMPCs could be used in liquid biopsy to assess the risk of HO prior to the formation of HO. 
Major points 
1. The authors found that the expression of Pdgfra and CD90 on the surface in circulating cells was a marker for MPCs,
which are derived from the HO site in burn and tenotomy (B/T)-induced HO model mice. Cell numbers of cMPCs were low in
controls (burn and skin incision (B/S) mice). How were in burn-only or tenotomy-only mice? Please show such fundamental
control data in each experiment to confirm the specificity of data in B/T mice. 
2. The cMPCs showed similar phenotypes to periosteal cells at the HO site in B/T mice. However, both Pdgfra and CD90
are known to be widely expressed in various types of cells in many tissues. Therefore, there was the possibility that the
circulating cells originated from others, rather than the HO site, and that the circulating cells were independent cells from HO.
Please show additional data showing direct evidence of the origin of MPCs and HO by circulating MPCs, but not other local
MPCs. 
3. The identified cMPCs in B/T mice expressed various types of genes compared to cells in B/S control mice (Fig. 1d).
Expression of Pdgfra and CD90 is wide in many types of cells. What was the homogeneity or heterogeneity of the cMPCs?
Again, please show direct evidence showing that circulating MPCs were derived from the periosteum of the HO site. 
4. The authors are encouraged to examine the numbers and functions of cMPCs in other in vivo models of HO, such as
fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva and BMP-implants. These results may further support the authors' claim. 
5. BAPN administration reduced floating HO and minimal in total HO, but reduced expression of the six marker genes in
cMPC (Fig. 4). Which types of cells were the target(s) of BAPN at the HO site? Histological data related to this point may
help to confirm the origin of cMPCs. Other drugs for HO, such as bisphosphonate, may also be useful in examining the origin
of cMPCs.
