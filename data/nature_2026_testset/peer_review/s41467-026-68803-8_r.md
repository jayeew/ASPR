Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper describes several material features on regenerated cellulose xerogels formed via the coagulation of cellulose
solutions using ethanol and successive ambient pressure drying. Unfortunately, the production process of xerogels in this
work is not surprising considering the previous studies on cellulose xerogels including foam-type porous structures (for
example, Antonini et al. Nanomaterials, 2019, 9, 1142; Josset et al. Cellulose, 2017, 24, 3825; Cervin et al. ACS Appl.
Mater. Interfaces, 2016, 8, 11682; Sakuma et al. ACS Nano, 2021, 15, 1436), and their material properties shown in this
work also fall within the range of values previously reported in those papers. The other features described in this paper, such
as moldability, recyclability and biodegradability, are just referred to the inherent properties of conventional regenerated
cellulose. In addition, it is known that solvent-exchanging with organic solvents (even ethanol) is a time-consuming and
costly process and is not put into practical use in cellulose and paper industries. The authors should clearly show the
scientific novelty of this work in the field of cellulose science. 
Although the gradient structure existing in regenerated cellulose gels has already been reported (for example, Guo et al.
Carbohydr. Polym. 2021, 258, 117694), it should be significant to highlight the gradient porous structure formed in the
regenerated cellulose xerogels, along with supplemental experiments to systematically explain the structure formation, at
least at different solution concentrations of cellulose.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments: 
This manuscript describes the development of a recyclable and biodegradable all-cellulose foam featuring a vertically
graded porous architecture, realized through a mild solvent-induced molecular assembly process. The study provides
comprehensive characterizations and analyses covering structural morphology, mechanical robustness, thermal insulation,
flame retardancy, and environmental impact. The fabrication method avoids freeze-drying and toxic crosslinkers, and
enables shape adaptability and closed-loop recycling. The work is scientifically rigorous, methodologically sound, and of
broad interest to the field of sustainable materials. I support publication after minor revision. Here are some comments for the
authors: 
1. Clarification of the gradient structure formation mechanism would enhance understanding. The vertically graded porous
architecture is a central feature of the All-Cel foam and is well supported by SEM imaging. However, the current explanation,
which attributes the gradient to ethanol diffusion and differential cellulose relaxation, remains largely qualitative. To improve

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

mechanistic clarity, the authors are encouraged to elaborate on how solvent exchange influences hydrogen bonding
dynamics, molecular rearrangement, and progressive network densification along the vertical direction. Expanding the
related discussion in Fig. 2a would provide readers with a clearer picture of how the gradient structure emerges during
assembly. 
2. The flame-retardant mechanism could benefit from additional explanation. The enhanced flame resistance of the phytic-
acid-modified All-Cel foam is clearly demonstrated through cone calorimetry and LOI tests. However, the underlying
mechanism is only briefly discussed. It would strengthen the manuscript to include a short explanation of how phosphorus-
containing compounds in phytic acid promote thermal stability and char formation during combustion. 
3. Additional discussion of the mechanical performance changes in recycled foams would be valuable. The manuscript
notes a reduction in compressive modulus after recycling, attributed to a decrease in cellulose polymerization degree. While
this is a reasonable explanation, it may also be helpful to consider other contributing factors such as the loss of
supramolecular entanglement or irreversible structural rearrangements during re-dissolution. A brief discussion of these
possibilities, along with potential approaches to mitigate performance loss (e.g., mild crosslinking, reinforcing additives),
would provide a more balanced assessment of the material’s recyclability. 
4. A more explicit comparison with existing cellulose foams would strengthen the positioning of this work. Although several
related studies are referenced, the novelty of the All-Cel foam could be better appreciated through a clearer and more direct
comparison with existing cellulose-based foams and aerogels. Emphasizing distinctions in processing methods (e.g.,
ambient-pressure assembly), mechanical properties, and functional features (e.g., moldability and recyclability) would help
articulate the unique contributions of this system. Adding a concise comparative summary in the Discussion section would
enhance the contextual framing and more effectively position the All-Cel foam within the broader landscape of cellulose-
based porous materials. 
5. Figure schematics and captions could be improved for better clarity and self-contained interpretation. Several figures that
are central to the conceptual framework, particularly Fig. 2a, would benefit from clearer visual representation. Illustrating the
spatial progression of ethanol diffusion and the corresponding cellulose assembly using directional arrows, color gradients,
or labeled zones (such as “dense surface region” and “looser interior”) would help clarify the underlying process. In addition,
Figure 6 includes multiple subpanels showcasing shaping, recycling, and biodegradation, but the current caption lacks
sufficient explanatory detail. Providing a brief description for each panel would enhance clarity and improve the standalone
interpretability of the figure.

I appreciate the authors' detailed and affordable responses. However, to be honest, the comparisons with the previously
reported cellulose xerogels studies in Table R1 and Figure R4 are not fair. 
From a processing standpoint, the issue with this study is that the thick solvent of ZnCl2/FA/H2O mixture (1:2:2) is
impractical due to its toxicity and lack of recyclability (see, for example, DOI: 10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2024.132912). This point
should be justified because the authors argued that formylation is a key step to inducing the programmed self-assembly of
cellulose molecules and constructing honeycomb-like, gradient morphology in All-Cel foam (see page 6 of the manuscript). 
As I mentioned in a previous comment, the material properties shown in this work fall within the previously reported range of
values (Antonini et al., Nanomaterials, 2019, 9, 1142; Josset et al., Cellulose, 2017, 24, 3825; Cervin et al., ACS Appl. Mater.
Interfaces, 2016, 8, 11682; Sakuma et al., ACS Nano, 2021, 15, 1436). Although the mechanical properties of porous
materials exponentially scale with density and drastically change depending on the cross-linking structure, the comparison
in Figure R4 does not mention any structural profiles for xerogels. 
Furthermore, given my understanding of the hygroscopic nature of cellulose, the authors' assertion that All-Cel foam is a
promising alternative to traditional plastic foams seems unrealistic. The authors should present a strategy for maintaining the
compressive strength of All-Cell foam after immersion in water for at least 24 hours; see, for example, DOI:
10.1021/acs.biomac.0c00330, in which cellulose experts report best practices for reporting the wet mechanical properties of
cellulosic materials. Without such a demonstration of water resistance, the comparisons with plastic foams in Figures 3 and
4 are meaningless, and All-Cel foam is not distinct from cardboard papers with structural integrity, moldability, and thermal
insulation.

(1) Ethanol-induced molecular assembly with gradient pore formation mechanism (Our 
work) VS. additive-assisted network formation and external foaming agents. In our work, 
All-Cel foam is produced through ethanol-induced molecular assembly, as illustrated in Figure 
R1. The introduction of ethanol disrupts the coordination and hydrogen bonding between 
cellulose and ZnCl 2/FA solvent, enabling cellulose chains to re -establish intermolecular 
hydrogen bonds and form interconnected networks. At the ethanol–cellulose molecular system 
interface, this reassembly begins first, where cellulose chains rapidly associate to produce a 
dense surface layer (Figure R2a). This surface layer is the key factor to form the gradient porous 
of All-Cel foam. It slows the diffusion of ethanol into the cellulose system, leading to depth-
dependent assembly kinetics. Near the surface, the higher ethanol concentra tion leads to rapid 
solidification, producing a compact region with fine pores, while deeper regions experience a 
longer relaxation time that allows more extensive aggregation, resulting in progressively larger, 
honeycomb-like pores  (Figure R2b,c) . This is the first time that  simultaneously realizing 
cellulose molecular chains reorganization and in situ porous structure formation, eliminating 
the need for additional crosslinkers and foaming agents.

(3) Unique capability for arbitrary 3D molding and reshaping (Our work) VS. limited or 
irreversible shapeability. In our work, the cellulose molecular system can be readily cast into 
molds, and ethanol-induced assembly enables the formation of diverse 3D dimensional shapes 
(Figure R3a). Notably, the molecular-level assembly of All-Cel foam facilitates the formation 
of robust hydrogen bonding and supramolecular entanglements between cellulose chains, 
endowing the material with the unique ability to retain i ts structural integrity in water despite 
softening upon immersion.  As a result, All -Cel foam can be processed into customized 3D 
geometries through a compression-molding-like method, and  reshaped architectures are 
subsequently stabilized by ethanol exchange (Figure R3b). This process is repeatable, allowing 
All-Cel foam to be reprogrammed into different shapes multiple times (Figure R3c). 
 
In contrast, previously reported foams assembled from nanocellulose or microfibrillated  
cellulose are generally limited to simple  molding and lack reshapeability. As shown in Figure 
R4a and R4b , nanocellulose foam (CNF foam) tends to lose its structural integrity when 
reprocessed in water and is difficult to shape. Moreover, is readily collapses under compressive 
of 200 g. By comparison,  All-Cel foam not only maintains well-defined shapes  but also 
preserves its mechanical robustness, supporting a 3 kg weight without structural failure (Figure 
R4c).

Process advantages and robust material performance: Our research provides a new method 
to fabricate a cellulose  foam. The ethanol-induced process is not a mere solvent replacement 
step in our work , it controlled cellulose molecular reconstruction mechanism that enables in 
situ hydrogen bond network and gradient porous structure simultaneously. This method does 
not require complex pretreatment of cellulose, relies on neither foaming agents nor crosslinkers,

Moreover, to give a clearer picture of our innovative work according to the comments of 
Referee #1, we summarize the novelty of our work as follows: 
 
(1) Ethanol-induced molecular assembly for gradient porous structure formation 
Ethanol participate in competitive hydrogen bonding within the cellulose –solvent system, 
promoting the reorganization of cellulose chains and the formation of cellulose hydrogen 
bonding. During ethanol diffusion, the surface region undergoes rapid solidifi cation to form a 
dense skin layer, which subsequently acts as a diffusion barrier that slows down ethanol 
penetration into the interior. This creates differences in chain relaxation time along  the the 
thickness, leading to progressive pore enlargement and ultimately generating the characteristic 
gradient porous architecture of All-Cel foam. 
 
(2) Additive-free and ambient fabrication process without complex pretreatments 
The preparation of All -Cel foam rely on cellulose molecular self -assembly to construct 
interconnected networks and porosity at ambient environment. This process eliminates the need 
for energy -intensive and chemical -intensive pretreatments ( such as  TEMPO oxidation, 
mechanical fibrillation, or chemical modification) and  external foaming methods (such as ice 
templating, mechanical or chemical foaming) , highlighting a simplified, energy-efficient, and 
scalable fabrication pathway. 
 
(3) Robust cellulose molecular network delivering high performances and moldability 
without compromising sustainability 
The ethanol -induced assembly promotes the formation of strong hydrogen b onding and 
molecular entanglements among cellulose chains, resulting a robust molecular network.  This 
architecture enables All -Cel foam to achieve a high compressive modulus, superior thermal 
stability, and low thermal conductivity, while simultaneously offering  unique 3D shape 
moldability and reprocessability.  These advanced properties are achieved without 
compromising the natural recyclability and biodegradability of cellulos e, demonstrating the 
advantage of cellulose molecular design in achieving robust performance and sustainability.

DOI:10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2024.132912 with that by our ZnCl₂/FA/H₂O solvent. 
 
In the ZnCl 2/H2O system, cellulose retains most of its hydroxyl (–OH) groups, which drive 
strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding . Upon ethanol addition, chains rapidly reassociate, 
restricting pore formation and causing significant shrinkage during drying.  The resulting 
material is dense (Figure R2a, left). 
 
In the ZnCl 2/FA/H2O system, partial esterif ication converts some –OH groups into formate 
esters (–OCHO), reducing hydrogen -bonding capacity. Chain reassociation during ethanol -
induced assembly is slower and more controlled, permitting the progressive growth and 
interconnection of pores. Consequently, the foam adopts a gradient, honeycomb-like structure 
(Figure R2a, right). 
 
Quantitative and morphological comparisons further support these observations . As shown in 
Figure R2b, the ZnCl 2/H2O material has a bulk density of 0.69 ± 0.02 g/cm³, while the bulk 
density of the All-Cel foam is only 0.12 ± 0.01 g/cm³. SEM images of the ZnCl2/H2O material 
exhibit a dense, fissured cross -section due to rapid aggregation and contraction  (Figure R2c). 
In contrast, All-Cel foam shows a stable gradient and honeycomb morphology (Figure R2d), 
reflecting moderated chain association . These results confirm that formylation of cellulose is 
critical for moderating chain interactions and enabling the development and stabilization of the 
foam’s porous architecture.

Reply to the Referee: 
We thank the reviewer for emphasizing the need to demonstrate wet‐state mechanical stability. 
To confer water resistance on o ur All -Cel foam, we applied a surface silanization using 
methyltrimethoxysilane (MTMS), a well ‐established method for hydrophobizing cellulose 
materials. In practice, the All-Cel foam, MTMS, and a trace of water were sealed in a reactor 
and heated to 70 °C. Under these conditions, MTMS first hydrolyzes and then condenses with 
cellulose hydroxyls, forming covalent Si–O–cellulose bonds and yielding the hydrophobically 
modified “All-Cel-M” foam.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 34 -->

FTIR spectra (Figure R 6a) confirm successful grafting: new absor ption bands appear at 1273 
cm⁻¹ (Si–CH₃ stretching) and 778 cm ⁻¹ (Si–O vibrations). As a result, the static water contact 
angle increases from 34.9° to 118.0° (Figure R6b), and water droplets remain nearly spherical 
on both top and side surfaces (Figure R6c). 
 
When immersed in water for 24 h, the All-Cel-M foam exhibits no visible deformation (Figure 
R6d). Compression tests carried out under water at 0 h and after 24 h (Figures R 6e–f) show 
compressive moduli of 13.0 ± 1.3 MPa and 11.9 ± 0.8 MPa, respectively—retaining over 90 % 
of its initial stiffness. These results demonstrate that MTMS silanization effectively prevents 
water uptake and preserves the foam’s mechanical integrity.
