Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a compelling and important investigation into the climatic effects of extreme surface ocean
warming during marine heatwave (MHW) events. While the ecological consequences of MHWs have been extensively
studied, their influence on global precipitation, particularly in coastal regions, remains underexplored. The study’s main
contribution lies in demonstrating that MHWs can lead to significant increases in rainfall several days in advance on a global
scale, with a particularly pronounced impact on extreme rainfall over coastal land areas. 
The methodology used to identify the relationship between MHWs and precipitation, as well as the underlying mechanisms,
draws inspiration from approaches commonly applied in mesoscale eddy studies. However, its application in the context of
MHWs is novel and well-suited to the problem at hand. I find this methodological approach both innovative and effective in
capturing the climatic impacts of extreme marine events. It has the potential to advance our understanding of how MHWs
influence atmospheric processes and extreme weather. I recommend the manuscript for publication following appropriate
revisions. 
However, several issues merit attention before publication: 
Major Comments 
1. While the novelty of the method is acknowledged, one of the study’s key implications—the regional differences in rainfall
responses—is not fully explored. Rainfall formation mechanisms vary by region, influenced by processes such as tropical
convection, extratropical storms, western boundary current-induced adjustments, atmospheric rivers, .etc. Not all
mechanisms are directly linked to local sea surface temperature (SST) gradients, and some may even act as drivers of SST
anomalies at synoptic timescales. Given the spatial variability in precipitation and its diverse societal, ecological, and
economic impacts, the authors should consider stratifying their analysis by latitude bands or climate regimes. This would
allow for a more detailed examination and discussion of how MHW impacts differ across regions. Additionally, the potential
differences between short-duration and long-duration MHW events—despite having similar intensity—should be examined
and discussed. This could reveal that certain regions may experience more severe consequences than the global average,
which would be of significant value for policymakers and environmental planners. 
2. The physical interpretation, causality, and spatial/temporal scales of the large-scale patterns in the rotated and
composited fields require further clarification. If these large-scale patterns are independent of MHWs, could they be
considered a driver of MHW formation rather than a consequence? In this context, the residual—defined as the difference
between raw changes and the large-scale pattern—would represent the isolated effect of MHWs? The distinct spatial and
temporal characteristics of this residual should be clearly defined in both the abstract, results and the main text to avoid
potential misinterpretation. Additionally, the manuscript should clarify whether the timescales of vertical mixing and pressure
adjustment mechanisms differ. If the pressure adjustment is linked to the large-scale pattern, its removal in the residual may
lead to vertical mixing becoming the dominant mechanism. Furthermore, how to evaluate the regional differences in
precipitation regimes in the raw changes, large scale pattern and residual in the rotated fields? 
3. As ERA5 also provides precipitation data, it is advisable to examine ERA5 precipitation alongside its convergence and
wind speed fields. This consistency check would strengthen the robustness of the results and support the reliability of the
observational findings. 
Minor Comments 
1. The manuscript does not clearly present the transitioning from a global precipitation perspective to a focus on extreme
rainfall in coastal regions. While this focus emerges later in the results section, it should be introduced and motivated

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigates how marine heatwaves (MHWs), rather than their commonly studied biological impacts, may
influence extreme rainfall events over land. The central idea, that warm sea surface temperature anomalies (SSTAs) during
MHWs can enhance evaporation, moisture transport, and ultimately precipitation over nearby continental regions, is
scientifically reasonable and partly supported by the correlations presented. Given the complexity of coupled air–sea
processes, this work could provide a valuable perspective for linking coastal ocean variability to hydrological extremes and
for improving weather and climate forecasts. 
Overall, I find the topic timely and the story compelling enough to warrant publication. However, several analyses and
clarifications are needed to strengthen the evidence base and mechanistic interpretation. At present, reliance on reanalysis
and remote-sensing products without sufficient validation against in situ observations weakens the robustness of the
conclusions. In addition, the role of long-term climate change in shaping the climatology and extremes is not adequately
addressed. Therefore, I recommend major revision. My specific comments are as follows: 
1. The identification of extreme rainfall events is based on IMERG precipitation data. While I appreciate the authors’
sensitivity tests with multiple datasets, TRMM’s accuracy for quantifying precipitation, particularly extreme intensities, has
been questioned in past evaluations. Although the final product of IMERG precipitation data is often adjusted with rain
gauge data near coastlines, direct precipitation measurements would generally provide more reliable ground truth,
especially since many of the extreme events in Fig. 3 occur near coastal regions. The authors should discuss the limitations
of IMERG precipitation more explicitly, and, if feasible, validate their extreme rainfall identification against station-based
observations to strengthen confidence in the results. 
2. The manuscript employs ERA5 reanalysis fields to argue for enhanced upward moisture transport during MHWs. While
ERA5 is a valuable tool for examining large-scale circulation patterns, its skill at reproducing near-surface wind and
moisture variability over land, particularly in coastal regions, can be limited. Numerous weather stations existing along
coasts can provide observational support. Therefore, I strongly encourage the authors to conduct a statistical analysis using
station-based wind speed, direction, and humidity anomalies to the MHW events. This would help validate whether the
observed SSTA indeed triggers the relevant processes, thereby lending more credibility to the proposed mechanism. 
3. The manuscript gives little attention to how global warming may affect the definitions of both MHWs and extreme rainfall.
For example, the climatological SST baseline (1982–2022, line 343) implicitly includes a pronounced warming trend, which
may bias the definition of SST anomalies. Similarly, the identification of extreme rainfall events based on TRMM wet-day
climatology is also likely influenced by long-term changes in precipitation statistics. The statement at L104 seems unclear to
me. The details need to be provided for explaining the “background” and “large-scale” pattern. The authors should discuss
and, where possible, quantify the sensitivity of their results to these definitions. The values shown at L84 should vary
significantly worldwide. Can this value be supported by the previous studies for enhancing the wind speed over the warm
core? 
4. Please provide more information on Fig. 2. The results of vertical motion of the air are from the ERA5 product. Is there any
land mask on the ERA5 model results? Or do you simply use the results in the marine atmosphere boundary layer? Are all
the MHW events reliably reproduced in the ERA model results, including both the spatial coverage and magnitude? 
5. This issue is relatively minor. L41-52 of the introduction devotes significant space to ecological impacts of MHWs. While

informative, this section feels tangential to the manuscript’s meteorological focus and distracts from the central research
question. I recommend streamlining these passages and instead providing more background on known linkages between
SST anomalies, air–sea fluxes, and atmospheric circulation, which would better set the stage for the present study.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is my second review of the manuscript entitled “Extreme Rainfall Over Land Exacerbated by Marine Heatwaves.” I
appreciate the authors’ efforts in addressing my previous concerns, particularly regarding the validation using weather
station data along coastal regions. The revisions have significantly improved the robustness and credibility of the analysis.
The manuscript presents a compelling case for the link between marine heatwaves (MHWs) and extreme rainfall events over
land, supported by thorough observational and modeling evidence. The methodology is sound, and the discussion
effectively situates the findings within the broader context of climate variability and ocean-atmosphere interactions. While the
overall quality of the manuscript is commendable, as an physical oceanographer, I would encourage the authors to further
elaborate on the occurrence of MHWs in mesoscale eddy-rich regions. Specifically, incorporating statistical insights on eddy
size distributions and examining atmospheric precursors prior to MHW onset could enrich the discussion. Such an analysis
would provide a more nuanced understanding of the dynamical processes influencing MHW development and their potential
role in amplifying rainfall extremes. However, these suggestions are relatively minor and do not undermine the validity of the
conclusions presented. In its current form, the manuscript makes a valuable contribution to the literature on ocean-
atmosphere coupling and extreme weather phenomena. Therefore, I recommend it for publication.

Major comments  
LL 76-85: The first major comment concerns  the possibility of including warm-core

Robust rainfall response of MHW with/without eddies 
Mesoscale warm core eddies are one of the most important causes of MHWs (Bian et 
al., 2023; He et al., 2024; Holbrook et al ., 2019). We do not exclude MHWs that co-
existed with mesoscale eddies. Our computa tion confirms that about 24% of the local 
SST extreme points of MHW identified in our paper are located within one radius of a 
mesoscale eddy. The locations and radii of  mesoscale eddies are from the Mesoscale 
Eddy Trajectory Atlas Product (META ver3.2) provided by A VSIO+. The composited 
precipitation anomaly in rotated coordinates centered at the MHWs’ cores with and 
without eddies show a comparable am plitude and similar spatial patterns (Figure.R2-
1). The similarity indicates that our result is robust regardless whether a MHW’s core 
is influenced by a warm core eddy or not.
