Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Liao et al. presents a compelling and rigorously conducted study that challenges the long-standing belief
that high molecular weight polysaccharides cannot be absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract. The authors provide
strong in vitro and in vivo evidence that β-1,3- and α-1,4-linked glucans can cross intestinal epithelial barriers and reach
systemic circulation, with uptake mediated by clathrin-dependent endocytosis and specific cell surface receptors. 
The study employs a broad array of techniques—including fluorescent and radiolabeling, gene silencing, knockout models,
and subcellular localization—to provide a comprehensive view of polysaccharide uptake. These findings offer valuable
insight for the development of orally administered polysaccharide-based therapeutics and advance our understanding of
carbohydrate pharmacokinetics. 
While the manuscript is of high quality and scientific merit, several key issues remain that should be addressed to strengthen
the conclusions and enhance the manuscript's impact. 
Major points 
1. Mechanism of Entry into the Bloodstream 
While the authors convincingly show that polysaccharides can be absorbed by intestinal cells, the mechanism by which
these macromolecules subsequently enter the bloodstream remains unclear. This is a critical gap in the current work.
Investigating whether polysaccharides exit the basolateral side of intestinal epithelial cells via transcytosis, transporter-
mediated efflux, or paracellular leakage would provide essential pharmacokinetic context. Without addressing this, the
conclusion regarding systemic availability remains incomplete. 
2. Lack of Evidence for Direct Interaction with Clathrin 
The study identifies clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) as a major route of polysaccharide uptake, yet it does not
demonstrate a direct physical interaction between polysaccharides and clathrin. The current reliance on knockdown and
inhibition data is supportive but insufficient to confirm a mechanistic link. Incorporating direct binding assays, such as co-
immunoprecipitation, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), or pull-down assays, would substantiate the central claim and
significantly reinforce the mechanistic conclusions. 
3. Specificity of Polysaccharide–Receptor Interactions 
EGFR, BMPRIA, and Dectin-1 are identified as receptors involved in polysaccharide uptake. However, the specificity and
binding affinity of these interactions are not thoroughly characterized. To understand receptor selectivity and the structural
basis of recognition, further experiments such as isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), competition assays, or structural
modeling would be beneficial. Additionally, it remains unclear whether other receptors may be involved; a broader screening
of receptor–polysaccharide interactions would improve the generalizability of the findings. 
Minor points 
Reproducibility across cell types: The manuscript describes cell line-dependent differences in uptake dynamics but does not
explore the underlying causes (e.g., differential receptor expression, cell polarity). A more in-depth discussion or data on
receptor expression profiles could provide useful context. 
Exclusion of other endocytic pathways: The conclusion that polysaccharide uptake is independent of lipid raft- or caveolin-
mediated endocytosis is primarily based on pharmacological inhibition. Including genetic knockout models or more specific

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors attempted to elucidate polysaccharide absorption mechanisms in intestinal mucosa. The
authors showed that some polysaccharide samples can permeate intestinal cell layers in vitro, and found that the isotope- or
fluorescein-labeled polysaccharides are detectable in blood and liver after their oral administration to rodents. Using gene-
knockdown strategies, pharmacological inhibitors, and specific knockout mice, the authors demonstrated that the
polysaccharide absorption requires clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) machineries in both intestinal cells in vitro and
intestinal mucosa in vivo. Interestingly, the authors also showed the functional involvement of plasma membrane receptors
such as BMPRIA, Dectin-1, and EGFR in the cellular uptake of distinct polysaccharides. 
Experiments are carefully performed, and the information presented in this manuscript is invaluable as a foundation for oral
polysaccharide-based drag development. 
Major point #1 
In this study, in vitro polysaccharide uptake was evaluated by measuring fluorescent intensity of the polysaccharide samples
used. Considering the involvement of membrane receptors in the polysaccharide internalization, does the detected
fluorescence truly reflect those of the internalized polymers only? Can the influence of those bound to the cell surface be
excluded? Similar concerns exist in the results using tissue sections. The authors should clarify these points. 
Major point #2 
Unlike a beta-1,3-linked glucan GFPBW1 and an alpha-1,4-linked WGE, are there any polysaccharides that are not
absorbed in the authors' experimental systems? Such examples may reinforce the functional importance of cell membrane
receptors in polysaccharide absorption. 
Major point #3 
In most of experiments, the polysaccharide uptake was examined by functional inhibition of CME-related molecules and
some membrane receptors. Therefore, some complement results showing the increased polysaccharide uptake by
functional augmentation of the target molecules are required to strengthen the author’s conclusions. 
Major point #4 
The authors should also show whether the transport of orally administrated polysaccharides (GFPBW1 and WGE) to serum
and liver is actually restricted in mice deficient in CME-related molecules. 
Minor point #1 
This manuscript contains a large amount of data and many repetitive explanatory expressions, making it difficult to
understand the important points. It would be better to reconstruct the manuscript during the revision process. 
Minor point #2 
This manuscript contains many misspellings and citation errors for figures. Check them carefully. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The full article has a clear logic, a clear purpose, a good layout, and sufficient experimental data. It is a very valuable article .
