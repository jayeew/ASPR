Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript entitled "Double-dose Firmonertinib as First-line Treatment in Patients with Locally Advanced or
Metastatic Non-Small-Cell Lung Cancer harboring EGFR L858R Mutation: A Prospective, Multicenter, Phase II study
(FIRM)" Shen, et al describe data from a double dose of EGFR inhibitor firmonertinib as a first-line treatment option for
EGFR L858R mutated NSCLC. Historically, patients with EGFR L858R derive less benefit from EGFR TKIs than those
harboring EGFR ex19del. In this prospective phase II trial of 33 patients treated with double-dose firmonertinib, the median
progression-free survival was 21.1 months - notably longer than the 14.4 months observed with osimertinib in the phase 3
FLAURA trial (NEJM 2018), which included 104 osimertinib-treated patients with EGFR L858R. This suggests a potentially
meaningful improvement for this subgroup. In addition, correlative analyses also suggest that longitudinal ctDNA clearance
may be associated with outcomes. However, there are several key questions arise from the manuscript that warrant
clarification: 
1. Statistical rigor and generalizability 
This was a single-arm, multicenter study with no formal statistical testing. While the authors acknowledge this as one of the
limitations of the study, this absence of rigorous statistical testing raises concerns about generalizability. The primary
endpoint was investigator-assess PFS. Additionally, the original planned sample size was 25 patients, yet, the trial
ultimately enrolled 33 patients, and the rationale for this increase is not explained 
2. Dose selection 
The authors justify using a daily dose of 160mg by referencing that firmonertinib demonstrates a "dose-dependent antitumor
activity" and "may improve disease control", including CNS efficacy. However, in the randomized, double-blind, phase 3
study (FURLONG) conducted in China of firmonertinib versus 1st-generation EGFR inhibitor, firmonertinib at 80 mg/day
demonstrated a median CNS PFS of 20.8 months and 18.1 months in EGFR L858R patients (Shi, JTO 2022). In the present
study, Shen et al report a median CNS PFS of 27.3 months in patients with brain metastases, but it is unclear whether this
(or the overall PFS endpoint) reached statistical significance (see #1). 
Moreover, a pooled post-hoc analysis of two phase 2 studies of firmonertinib reported CNS objective response rates (ORR)
and disease control rates (DCR) that were similar between 80 mg and 160 mg: ORR 65% (22/34) and DCR 97% (33/34)
with 80 mg versus ORR 85% (11/13) and DCR 100% (13/13) with 160 mg (Hu, BMC Med 2023). Given the small sample
sizes, these differences may be due to chance alone. While 160 mg was generally tolerable, a higher daily dose could still
carry increased toxicity risk compared with 80 mg. The authors compare, in cross-trial comparisons, that the double-dose
firmonertinib toxicity appears more favorable than with combination regimens (e.g., osimertinib plus chemotherapy, or
amivantamab plus lazertinib). Would similar results have been achieved at 80mg dose of firmonertinib? In general, I suspect
that it is not advisable to report on subgroup analyses that inlcude only 6 patients. 
3. Efficacy 
The median PFS in this study with double-dose firmonertinib was 21.1 months and curiously enrolled exclusively patients
with ECOG PS of 1. In the FLAURA2 trial (NEJM 2023), the median PFS of osimertinib + chemotherapy in EGFR L858R
was 24.7 months. The current therapeutic landscape in newly diagnosed metastatic EGFR+ NSCLC in patients with good
performance status would warrant a discussion of escalation of therapy from single agent EGFR inhibitor alone to
combination therapies which have shown to improve overall survival. Therefore, the authors claims that the data supports

double-dose firmonertinib as a potential first-line option for EGFR L858R-mutated advanced NSCLC would warrant further
prospective testing in a larger clinical trial with appropriate statistical rigor. 
Overall, the study provides meaningful results demonstrating that double-dose firmonertinib is efficacious and tolerable in
EGFR L858R lung cancer, but the use of this regimen would warrant further testing in a larger, randomized trial. 
A minor comment includes that when the authors note that the drug is approved, they should be specific about where this is
currently an approved agent. 
One additional finding is that there was baseline ctDNA found in over 90% of the patients. That is pretty unusual for an
EGFR mutation positive patient population. Is there any reason from their selection criteria that they would have had such a
high frequency of patients with ctDNA positivity at baseline?

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This prospective phase II trial (FIRM) is well-conducted and addresses a key unmet need in advanced EGFR L858R-mutant
NSCLC—a population with suboptimal outcomes on standard third-generation EGFR-TKI monotherapy. As the first study to
evaluate first-line double-dose firmonertinib (160 mg), it offers an innovative and scientifically-grounded strategy to enhance
efficacy without resorting to complex combination regimens. The findings are promising and support this approach as a
practical therapeutic alternative. I endorse publication and await further validation from phase III randomized trials. 
Here are some specific examples of problem area: 
1.Regarding resistance mechanisms, please clarify if molecular data at progression were collected from any patients. Initial
observations on whether the double-dose regimen alters the acquired resistance pattern (e.g., C797S rate) would be
important to discuss. 
2.The incidence of Grade ≥3 treatment-emergent adverse events was only 6.1%, with no AEs leading to treatment
discontinuation or death—an outstanding safety profile. Given that the double-dose regimen theoretically carries a higher
risk of toxicity, did the study predefine specific toxicity monitoring and management protocols? 
3.Please specify the reasons one patients failed to meet eligibility criteria in the paper. 
4. The baseline characteristics table (Table 1) should include statistical tests to assess the initial comparability of the groups.
Currently, the table only presents descriptive statistics. To strengthen the manuscript, I recommend adding a column with p-
values derived from appropriate tests.

and would like to provide clarification on the specific issues you raised as follows:  
Regarding the single-arm design and the absence of formal statistical testing  
We agree that the absence of formal statistical testing represents one of the limitations of our study, as 
we acknowledged in the manuscript. However, we would like to clarify that: 
1) This was an exploratory phase II study, and the use of a single-arm design without formal hypothesis 
testing is widely accepted in early-phase oncology trials. 1,2 As recognized in the oncology drug 
development pathway, such hypothesis-generating phase II single-arm studies represent an essential 
step bridging early clinical observations and subsequent confirmatory randomized trials.  
2) Specifically, the design of this study was inspired by real-world clinical observations. In clinical 
practice, patients who developed resistance to standard-dose third-generation EGFR TKIs (such as 
osimertinib 80 mg) and subsequently received higher doses of firmonertinib often achieved

transient disease control, particularly those with brain or leptomeningeal metastases. This effect 
may be related to increased intracranial drug exposure at higher doses with maintained tolerability. 
Together with early phase I/II data showing a clear dose-response relationship, these findings 
motivated our prospective, single-arm exploratory study to evaluate the feasibility of the 160 mg 
firmonertinib regimen in patients with advanced NSCLC harboring the EGFR L858R mutation. 
However, at the time the study was conceived (approximately three years ago), third-generation 
EGFR TKI monotherapy remained the standard first-line treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC. 
There were no established data or comparative evidence available to support a robust statistical 
hypothesis regarding dose intensification or combination strategies. 
3) Given the unmet clinical need for treatment strategies that can delay resistance and reduce 
chemotherapy exposure, we believe this exploratory study holds meaningful clinical value. It 
provides essential feasibility evidence and efficacy signals to inform subsequent confirmatory 
randomized controlled trials. Accordingly, we have explicitly acknowledged the inherent 
limitations (Page 9, Lines 239-240) of this study design in the manuscript and tempered any 
potentially overoptimistic interpretation of the results, as follows: 
“Second, this exploratory single-arm design, without a control group or formal sample size 
calculation, may affect generalizability of the findings.” 
Regarding the use of investigator-assessed PFS as the primary endpoint 
Regarding the concern that investigator-assessed PFS may introduce bias, we would like to clarify that 
investigator assessment of PFS is a common and acceptable practice in phase II exploratory single-arm 
studies, particularly in investigator-initiated trials. To minimize potential bias, all radiologic evaluations 
in this study strictly followed the predefined RECIST v1.1 criteria. Each assessment was jointly reviewed 
by at least two experienced imaging specialists or oncologists at each study center, all of whom were 
trained under Good Clinical Practice principles and the standardized study protocol, to reach a consensus 
and ensure consistency across evaluations. 
Explanation for the increase in sample size 
This study was exploratory in nature, and the initial target enrollment of 25 patients was determined 
based on feasibility and clinical accessibility rather than a formal statistical hypothesis or predefined 
power calculation. Without a rigorous hypothesis-testing framework, the study primarily aimed to 
provide descriptive estimates of efficacy and safety outcomes and to generate parameters to inform 
subsequent studies. During study implementation, we observed that the number of progression events 
among the early enrolled patients was lower than initially expected. In the absence of a formal hypothesis, 
this raised concerns that the sample size might be insufficient to support a meaningful descriptive analysis 
of the primary endpoint. 
Based on this consideration, the study team determined that a modest increase in sample size was justified. 
In addition, as emerging real-world clinical experience began to suggest that higher-dose firmonertinib 
could confer modest efficacy benefits with acceptable tolerability, patient recruitment proceeded faster 
and more smoothly than anticipated. To allow more eligible patients to benefit from the exploratory 
regimen—while maintaining data quality and ethical compliance—the final enrollment was expanded to 
33 patients. This adjustment was made solely to enhance the reliability of the descriptive findings and

descriptive estimates in the absence of a formal hypothesis-testing framework.”

Regarding the inclusion of only patients with ECOG PS of 1 
Indeed, our study enrolled only patients with ECOG PS of 1. We acknowledge that this differs from some 
previous studies, such as FLAURA2, in which approximately 40% of patients had PS 0.1 The ECOG PS 
score is a relatively subjective assessment, and the distinction between 0 and 1 is often subtle. In most 
clinical studies, these two categories are considered to have minimal impact on efficacy outcomes, 
whereas patients with ECOG PS ≥2 are typically analyzed as a separate group. In our study, all enrolled 
patients were those routinely treated in oncology or respiratory departments, rather than asymptomatic

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

individuals identified through health screening. Most had mild symptoms but remained fully ambulatory 
and capable of self-care, and were thus consistently assessed by their physicians as ECOG PS of 1—a 
reflection of clinical reality rather than a deliberate selection. We believe this characteristic aligns with 
the real-world profile of advanced NSCLC patients eligible for systemic therapy. From a prognostic 
perspective, patients with ECOG PS of 1 generally have poorer outcomes and face greater therapeutic 
challenges. Therefore, the favorable efficacy signals observed with double-dose firmonertinib in this 
ECOG PS of 1 population may actually underscore its potential clinical value for patients representing a 
slightly less favorable real-world subset. 
Regarding the need for further prospective validation in larger clinical trials  
We completely agree with the reviewer’s opinion. The findings of our study are exploratory in nature, 
and their clinical relevance requires confirmation in larger, well-designed prospective trials. We also 
acknowledge that, at the time of manuscript preparation, the final OS results of the FLAURA2 study had 
not yet been reported.1,2 Consequently, our discussion was based on the available efficacy and safety data, 
suggesting that double-dose firmonertinib might have first-line potential. We fully understand and concur 
that for newly diagnosed, systemically fit patients with metastatic EGFR-positive NSCLC, treatment 
strategies should evolve from monotherapy with EGFR TKIs to combination regimens, which have now 
demonstrated overall survival benefits. In response to the reviewer’s suggestion, we have revised the 
Introduction (Page 3, Lines 55-58) to explicitly note that the FLAURA2 trial has established EGFR TKI 
plus chemotherapy as the new first-line standard of care, as follows: 
“Although combining EGFR-TKI with chemotherapy or bispecific antibody (e.g., amivantamab) has 
yielded promising survival benefits in the L858R-mutated population compared with monotherapy, 
widespread application in routine practice is limited by the overlapping toxicities and cumbersome 
dosing schedules.3-6” 
In the Discussion (Page 8, Lines 194-201), we have included additional discussion, as follows: 
“While it remains unclear whether double-dose firmonertinib can provide long-term OS benefit, the 
systemic efficacy, manageable toxicity, and the availability of subsequent treatment options post-
progression observed with double-dose firmonertinib are similar to the key factors that contributed to the 
FLAURA2 success, suggesting a potentially positive trend for long-term outcomes as follow-up 
continues.6 However, these findings warrant further evaluation in phase III randomized trials comparing 
this regimen with current standards of care, including standard-dose firmonertinib and osimertinib plus 
chemotherapy, to clarify its role in L858R-mutated NSCLC.” 
Within this evolving landscape where combination therapy has become the new standard of care, we 
believe our study still carries scientific value. As an exploratory investigation, it provides theoretical 
rationale and preliminary data to support future studies evaluating the efficacy of double-dose 
monotherapy. Consistent with the reviewer’s advice, we have softened the statement (Page 8, Lines 205-

Regarding the issue of dose selection, we are pleased to provide additional clarification. As illustrated in 
Figure 4 of the referenced study (Cross et al, Cancer Discovery 2014), dose escalation of osimertinib 
from 2.5 mg/kg/day to 5 mg/kg/day in the H1975 (L858R/T790M) xenograft model following 14 days 
of daily treatment resulted in a degree of tumor regression comparable to that achieved with 2.5 
mg/kg/day osimertinib in the PC-9 (exon 19 deletion) xenograft model in vivo. 1 These findings suggest 
that dose intensification of third-generation EGFR TKIs is biologically feasible and further indicated that 
dose intensification may be a viable strategy to achieve efficacy in patients with EGFR L858R mutations 
comparable to that observed in those with EGFR exon 19 deletions. Beyond this preclinical evidence, 
the rationale for dose intensification of third-generation EGFR TKIs in our study is further supported by 
emerging clinical studies in EGFR-mutant populations exhibiting suboptimal responses to standard-dose 
EGFR TKI therapy. For example, the FURTHER and FURMO-003 trials demonstrated that tripling the 
dose of firmonertinib significantly improved clinical outcomes in patients harboring EGFR exon 20 
insertion and PACC mutations.3,4
