Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports an omnidirectional strain-invariant, fully MXene-based microgrid system (sos-MXetronics) that
seamlessly integrates energy harvesting, storage, sensing, and wireless communication within a single architecture. This
work represents a substantial advance beyond all previously reported MXene-based integrated devices, both in terms of
functional complexity and mechanical robustness, highlighting the critical role of multifunctional unit integration in advancing
next-generation wearable electronics. The research is comprehensive, the integration strategy is highly innovative, and the
overall presentation is clear and compelling. Therefore, the manuscript could be published in Nature Communications after
minor revision. My specific comments are as follows: 
1.MXene is prone to oxidation in humid or oxygen-rich environments. To more comprehensively understand its performance,
it is necessary to determine whether the fully integrated device can operate reliably over the long term, especially given that
wearable devices often function in sweat-rich environments. 
2.For MXene obtained via molten salt etching, is it possible to directly produce MXene nanosheets through ultrasonication to
avoid organic intercalation, or to use alkali metal salts for direct intercalation? 
3.The authors mention that the modulus of MXene is close to the GPa range and cite previous literature for support. Given
that different preparation methods can greatly influence the modulus of MXene, it is recommended that the authors measure
and present the tensile stress–strain curves of their MXene to substantiate this claim. 
4.The authors propose unifying all components with MXene and have demonstrated its feasibility. Could this approach also
be applicable to other materials? 
5.The authors discuss the sensitivity range of the MXene pressure sensor; however, there is a lack of discussion on the
sensor’s consistency and long-term cyclic stability, which are crucial for stable pulse measurement.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

6.In Figures 4i and 4j, the MXene coil charges wirelessly to 2.8 V. Why is this specific voltage chosen? Could it be higher or
lower? In this regard, what considerations are involved in the matching design between the coil and the capacitor? 
7.The current system radius is 3.3 cm. For broader adoption, especially in wearable health monitoring, integration into
smaller form factors may be desired. A brief discussion on possible scaling strategies or design modifications for
miniaturization would strengthen the impact. 
8.It is recommended to compare this work with all previously reported MXene fully integrated devices and highlight an in-
depth comparative analysis with other integrated systems, which would help enhance the depth of the paper and its
relevance from a forward-looking perspective. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors fabricate strain-invariant omnidirectional stretchable MXetronics (sos-MXetronics) through
hierarchical stiffness engineering by introducing patterned elastiff regions and achieving strong bonding with an elastomeric
substrate. The authors demonstrate the potential of sos-MXetronics for human physiological signal monitoring using fully
MXene-based near-field communication (NFC) antennas, micro-supercapacitors (MSCs), and resistive-type pressure and
temperature sensors. Specifically, the sos-MXetronics successfully acquire pulse and body-temperature signals while
minimizing the influence of wrist deformation, thereby improving data reliability. Overall, I think that the authors have
effectively demonstrated the potential of sos-MXetronics for human physiological signal monitoring with convincing
experimental results. However, the manuscript lacks sufficient explanation regarding the operating mechanism of the
pressure sensor (P-sensor) and leaves some questions about the omnidirectional stability of fabricated electrode. Therefore,
I recommend that the authors address the following issues, as detailed below. 
1. More detailed specifications of the pressure sensor’s stability and properties should be analyzed. 
1.1. As mentioned in Figure 1, conventional stretchable devices without a hierarchical structure composed of elastiff and
rigid layers often lose their electrical properties upon deformation. From this conclusion, the MXene spin-coated PDMS
(PDMS–MXene) can be inferred to be unstable under compressive deformation. Therefore, the stability of the PDMS–
MXene should be verified through cyclic loading tests. 
1.2. The observation that the intermittent pattern exhibits high pressure sensitivity but low strain sensitivity is an interesting
and unique characteristic. However, the underlying mechanism for the enhanced sensitivity of the intermittent pattern
compared to the homogeneous pattern should be clarified for better reader understanding, which is likely related to changes
in contact area under applied pressure. In addition, presenting the sensor’s performance under strain would help elucidate
its strain-insensitive behavior. Moreover, in Supplementary Figure 24, the authors state that the intermittent pattern exhibits
good hysteresis behavior as reported in previous studies; however, the relevant reference is not provided and should be
cited. 
2. Additional analysis of line-pattern stability and deformation behavior is required. 
2.1. The interdigitated MXene patterns used in the pressure sensor and MSCs consist of line electrodes that are likely to be
significantly influenced by the direction of applied strain. However, the manuscript only analyzes unidirectional deformation.
To validate the omni-directional stability of sos-MXetronics, additional tests under multi-directional deformation should be
conducted. 
2.2. In Figure 2h, the relative resistance change of the straight-line structure with rigid isolation should also be evaluated to
further support the stability of the line-patterned electrodes. Moreover, resistance variation under different strain directions
should be examined to confirm the claimed omni-directional mechanical stability. 
3. The effect of the serpentine electrode pattern is not clearly demonstrated in Figure 2h. Presenting a larger-scale graph
would help clarify its role in mechanical stability, and the inset image should be described in greater detail for better reader
understanding. 
4. The authors successfully demonstrate the advantage of a fully MXene-based platform by employing MXene electrodes as
NFC antennas, micro-supercapacitors (MSCs), and multifunctional sensors. However, it raises concerns about the long-term
stability of the device, since MXene materials are prone to oxidation, which can significantly reduce their electrical
conductivity over time. Therefore, a discussion or experimental verification of the device’s durability under ambient or
operational conditions would be beneficial.

2 
 
been devoted to structural design and interfacial engineering within the field of stretchable 
electronics. However, the primary novelty of our work lies in realizing a fully integrated, 
system-level MXene-based microgrid platform rather than isolated structural optimization. We 
respectfully clarify that the innovation of this study does not lie solely in structural design or 
interfacial modification, but rather in achieving a system -level breakthrough that has not been 
realized in previous MXene-based electronics.  
It is important to note that the single island -bridge structure is effective for certain materials, 
such as liquid metals, which are inherently stretchable. These materials can endure large strains 
without compromising performance, making the single island-bridge design suitable for them. 
However, this approach is not applicable to MXene, which is inherently non -stretchable and 
brittle under high strain. When MXene is directly patterned onto a soft elastomeric substrate, it 
experiences localized strain concentration, leading to cracking and failure. Therefore, a single 
island-bridge structure alone would be insufficient to maintain both the mechanical integrity 
and electrical conductivity of the system. 
To address this limitation, we introduced a hierarchical stiffness microgrid architecture. In this 
design, MXene is initially deposited on PET islands, which have a modulus significantly higher 
than the elastomeric layers (Ecoflex/PDMS). This configuration  forms the first island -bridge 
layer, where MXene is supported and stabilized by the rigid PET islands. Next, based on the 
patterned geometry of the PET islands, MXene is placed onto PDMS/Ecoflex islands, with the 
Ecoflex /PDMS layers acting as soft, flexible bridges. This second layer forms another island-
bridge structure, where the MXene is supported by the stiffer PDMS under regions requiring 
less flexibility (e.g., straight-line interdigital electrodes), while the softer Ecoflex supports more 
flexible r egions (e.g., serpentine patterns).  As a result, the system achieves omnidirectional 
strain-invariant performance rather than mere unidirectional stretchability.  In addition, the 
strong interfacial coupling among the gradient elastomer layers (PDMS/Ecoflex ) and the PET 
interconnects ensures mechanical integrity and stable electrical coupling at high strain, which 
distinguishes our sos -MXetronics from prior MXene -based devices that often suffer from 
delamination or mismatch at heterogeneous interfaces.

Response: 
We sincerely apprecia te your thoughtful question about the microscopic interactions that 
stabilize the electrical resistance of the MXene conductors during stretching. In our architecture, 
the strain-invariant behavior arises primarily from the combination of strong interfacial bonding 
between MXene and the rigid PET island, and the strain -shielding effect provided by the 
hierarchical stiffness design, rather than from the MXene flakes needing to withstand large 
tensile deformation directly. 
First, the MXene film is supported o n a PET island whose modulus is orders of magnitude 
higher than that of the surrounding Ecoflex matrix. During tensile deformation, the global strain 
is preferentially absorbed by the soft Ecoflex, while the PET island acts as a rigid load-shielding 
platform. Consequently, the MXene region experiences only a very small fraction under applied 
strain. Second, the residual micro-strain that does reach the MXene film is dissipated through 
reversible interlayer sliding. Surface terminations ( -O, -OH, -F) provide  weak hydrogen -
bonding and van der Waals interactions that can break and reform during deformation. This 
mechanism accommodates slight lateral displacement between nanosheets without producing 
long, through-thickness cracks, thereby preserving electrical continuity. 
As designed, the MXene films are patterned on serpentine -shaped PET islands, which act as 
rigid backbones embedded within a soft Ecoflex matrix. Under tensile deformation, the 
majority of the applied strain is absorbed by the Ecoflex, while the serpentine PET confines the 
MXene layers to regions of minimal deformation.  In our previous in situ mechanical -optical 
tests, we directly observed the evolution of microcracks in the MXene films during stretching  
(Supplementary Fig. 14, The first draft). The results show that visible cracks begin to appear 
only when the global strain approaches 40%, yet the MXene networks remain electrically 
connected without complete rupture. It is important to emphasize that the aim of our design is 
not to induce strain in the MXene itself, but to maintain its electrical integrity by minimizing 
internal displacement  within the MXene network. Within this architecture, the hierarchical 
stiffness gradient (Ecoflex →PDMS→PET → MXene) creates an efficient strain -isolation 
mechanism, allowing the device to sustain stable conductivity without requiring MXene

(2) Environmental stability under temperature–humidity aging 
MXene oxidation is known to occur only when both water and oxygen are present, and the 
degradation rate follows Arrhenius-type temperature dependence. For this reason, we selected 
two representative environments that together cap ture the practically relevant oxidation 
envelope: 
• 60°C, 100% RH – an accelerated aging condition widely used in MXene stability studies 
• 25°C, 50% RH – typical ambient conditions for wearable electronics 
This pair of conditions is sufficient to evaluate deg radation without considering low-
temperature environments, as MXene is typically stored only under refrigerated conditions to 
ensure long-term aqueous dispersion stability, and actual device s operate under room 
temperature conditions. Under 60°C and 100% RH, unencapsulated MXene rapidly oxidized, 
with its resistance increasing by more than seven orders of magnitude within 10 days, 
eventually exceeding the 200 M Ω limit of our multimeter and becoming unmeasurable. In 
contrast, Ecoflex -encapsulated MXene showed only a ~10 3-fold increase after 15 days 
(Supplementary Fig. 54), demonstrating that the elastomer layers significantly retard moisture 
ingress. Under 25°C and 50% RH, unencapsulated MXene exhibited only ~1 5-fold resistance 
increases over 30 days, whereas the encapsulated MXene changed by merely ~1 -fold. This 
suggests minimal degradation in normal wearable conditions, consistent with the fact that 
MXene oxidation is strongly humidity- and temperature-dependent.

Response: 
We sincerely thank you for raising this important question regarding breathability. We fully 
agree that breathability is a key consideration for certain categories of long -term wearable 
electronics, particularly those based on textiles or porous membranes. 
However, in our system, the multilayer substrate is designed primarily for mechanical strain 
redistribution and protection of the MXene conductive network, rather than for facilitating air 
or moisture transport. The skin-facing layers consist of Ecoflex and PDMS, both of which are 
widely used in soft bioelectronics and medical-grade epidermal devices. These elastomers offer 
excellent elasticity, conformability, and biocompatibility, which are essential for achieving the

Molten-salt-etched MXenes are known to exhibit densely packed, accordion -like multilayers 
with much stronger interlayer couplin g than MXenes produced by traditional HCl/LiF routes 
(Chemistry of Materials 29.18 (2017): 7633-7644). Consequently, direct ultrasonication of these 
multilayer MXenes results in extremely low yields, because their interlayer bonding is 
significantly stronger than that in graphite or bulk MoS ₂, making sonication alone insufficient 
for effective delamination.  To overcome this limitation, polar organic molecules such as 
hydrazine, urea, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), isopropylamine, and larger organic bases (e.g.,

tetrabutylammonium hydroxide, choline hydroxide, or n -butylamine) are commonly used as 
intercalants. Intercalation with these species followed by mechanical shaking or ultrasonication 
in water effectively expands the interlayer spacing, enabling the forma tion of single - or few-
layer colloidal MXene dispersions.  Furthermore, some well -studied MXenes such as Ti 3C2Tx 
can also be delaminated through metal -cation intercalation using aqueous solutions of ionic 
compounds (e.g., halide salts or metal hydroxides). In fluoride-containing etching systems (e.g., 
HCl/LiF), additional organic intercalants are not strictly necess ary because metal cations are 
introduced in situ during synthesis, allowing delamination simply by increasing the pH to near-
neutral and applying mild mechanical agitation.

small inorganic cations further hinder effective intercalation. 
(ii) Direct ultrasonication: 
For 1 g of MXene obtained from the LTMS etching process, direct ultrasonication in 100 mL 
of deionized water yielded a colloidal concentration of approximately 0.8 mg /mL, 
corresponding to a monolayer yield below 10 %. Most of the product remained in a multilayer 
state because the molten -salt environment produces tightly stacked MXene with strong 
interlayer coupling, which resists delamination by sonication alone. This is consistent with 
previous reports that molten-salt-etched MXenes possess much stronger interlayer interactions 
than van-der-Waals solids such as graphite or MoS2.
