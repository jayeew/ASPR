Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled “Multiple Hydrogen Bonding Enables Large-Area Phosphorescent Glasses with Robust Stability
and High-Temperature Afterglow” by Yan and co-workers ingeniously selected tetracarboxylic acid with multiple carboxyl
groups as the host component to construct doped materials. A large number of carboxyl groups can form multiple hydrogen
bonds, making the doped materials have excellent room temperature phosphorescence (RTP). More interestingly, strong
hydrogen bonds enable doped materials to exhibit RTP activity in both ordered and disordered (i.e. glassy) states. What is
even more impressive is that these doped materials exhibit the phenomenon of solvent corrosion resistance that is generally
only present in non-polar metal luminescent materials, which is very rare for organic compounds. Compared to the common
hosts used to construct doped phosphorescent systems (such as benzophenone, triphenylamine, PVA, PMMA, etc.), the use
of compounds containing multiple carboxyl groups such as butanetetracarboxylic acid as the host is still relatively limited. In
summary, the glass based RTP in this work is highly interesting particularly for the fields of functional materials, chemistry
and information science. Therefore, I believe that the manuscript should be published in Nat. Commun. pending some minor
revision as below. 
1. Butane tetracarboxylic acid has been confirmed to have excellent glass forming ability through various characterization
techniques such as differential DSC and XRD. Its unique molecular structure and intermolecular interaction mode provide a
basis for the stable existence of the glass state. However, can other polycarboxylic acid molecules with similar structural
features, such as citric acid and malic acid, also be used to construct glass phosphorescence materials? 
2. Based on the delayed emission spectrum of TFH/BTA glass shown in Fig. 5 (4d) in the temperature range of 20-200
℃
,
the phosphorescence intensity gradually decreases with increasing temperature. Does the intensity of its fluorescence
emission (~410 nm) also undergo systematic changes during the temperature variation process? Please provide the steady-
state spectrum of TFH/BTA glass in this temperature range. 
3. In the practical application of organic photofunctional materials, photostability is an important indicator. Given that
tetracarboxylic acid is a new host, it is necessary for the authors to investigate the material's photobleaching, and fatigue
resistance under continuous UV-light irradiation. 
4. Although the authors prepared the doped materials in a nitrogen atmosphere, considering the strong antioxidant
properties of the host and the curing protection effect of multiple hydrogen bonds. Can the doped materials also exhibit
excellent phosphorescence activity when prepared in air? 
5. The authors demonstrate that this supramolecular glass system exhibits excellent thermal stability. However, there is a
lack of a comparison of the performance of this new system with those of the previously reported high-temperature
phosphorescent materials. Including such a comparison, perhaps in a table format, would certainly enhance the presentation
of the research. 
6. The recent related works in this field can be enriched as references. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a novel approach for developing organic room-temperature phosphorescent glasses through the
incorporation of multiple hydrogen bonds within a non-conjugated molecular host system. The authors utilize star-shaped

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

1,2,3,4-butane tetracarboxylic acid as a host matrix, which exhibits both excellent glass-forming ability and robust
phosphorescent properties. The incorporation of hydrogen-bonding interactions stabilizes the disordered structure, enabling
an ultralong afterglow with a phosphorescence quantum yield of 56.8% and an ultralong lifetime of up to 3.8 seconds. The
work further demonstrates remarkable material properties, such as high-temperature afterglow stability, excellent solvent
resistance, and unparalleled processability for large-area, transparent films and complex 3D shapes. Additionally, it is worth
noting that the authors have addressed the issue that the phosphorescence performance of small-molecule-doped materials
is highly dependent on the crystalline state of the host. This work offers a new perspective on selecting the host matrix,
which involves combining multiple interactions (hydrogen bonds formed by carboxyl groups) and flexible groups, as a
promising strategy for developing excellent room-temperature phosphorescence materials. The findings represent an
important contribution to the field of organic phosphorescent materials, as they effectively address the traditional trade-off
between high performance and good processability in glassy systems. Therefore, I strongly recommend accepting this
manuscript with the following suggestions for further clarification: 
1. Please provide the photophysical data of all guest molecules, such as quantum yield and lifetime, since the luminescence
properties of guest molecules also have a significant influence on the photophysical properties of the doped materials. 
2. The authors have performed photophysical tests of the TFH/BTA glass under different atmospheric conditions. However,
additional tests on crystalline counterparts or other reference materials are needed. Such comparisons would provide more
insights into the role of multiple disordered hydrogen bonds in stabilizing triplet excitons and enhancing RTP performance. 
3. While the manuscript reports good thermal and environmental stability, it would be beneficial to include more data on the
long-term stability of the materials, particularly under prolonged exposure to high temperatures and other environmental
conditions, e.g., UV light. This will further substantiate the claim of high stability for practical applications. 
4. Following up on the previous question, in practical applications, it is also essential to conduct cyclic durability tests
(evaluating phosphorescence properties) on these phosphorescent materials. 
5. The demonstration of a 25 cm × 25 cm panel is impressive. To underscore the practicality and scalability, the authors
should provide a brief statement or data in the Supplementary Information regarding the uniformity of the phosphorescence
performance, e.g., lifetime or intensity, across different regions of the large-area panel. This would reassure readers about
the reproducibility and homogeneity of their fabrication method on a large scale. 
6. Please give more details about " The emission quantum yields were collected by using FluoroMax-4 (Horiba Jobin Yvon)
fluorimeter equipped with integrated sphere." How did the authors differentiate the emission between RTP and fluorescence
when measuring the quantum yield? 
7. Text Refinements: The last sentence in the Introduction section is somewhat forward-looking and reads more like a
conclusion. Consider rephrasing it to better set up the study's objectives. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports the generation of a glass generated from 1,2,3,4-butane tetracarboxylic acid (BTA) and the
composites formed between it and several aromatic anhydrides to realise phosphorescent glasses. The results are
interesting and merit publication, whether this is the correct journal for this article I am not totally convinced. 
The authors should make more transparent in the title and abstract that these are composite materials – the 4th sentence in
the abstract is misleading. 
I think the reader should be allowed to establish if a piece of work is ‘a new record among state of the art’ so this text should
be removed. The abstract does not make clear what BTA was doped with, this is crucial information that has been omitted. 
The authors have also been very selective in their choice of references – the work by Baker et al. (Eur. Polym. J. 2022, 162,
110889 & Yuan et al. (Nat. Nanotechnol., 2024, 19, 184) on low molecular weight glasses should be cited. 
Page 3, line 83 – more detail about the aromatic anhydrides used should be provided. 
IR spectroscopic data should be reported on the assembly of BTA and the composite glasses – VTIR spectroscopy could
provide valuable insight into the way in which these composites assemble in the bulk. 
SI Fig. 7 – the photo of the glass is not convincing – the authors should provide a better image to enable the reader to see
that a glass has been produced – at present it looks like a liquid droplet. 
Synthesis of TFH – IR spectroscopic data and the melting point of this solid should be reported.

Reply from authors:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

We thank the reviewer  for the insightful suggestion. Exploring other polycarboxylic acids is 
indeed an interesting direction.  Based on the reviewer ’s suggestion, we  have conducted 
additional experiments to investigate whether other polycarboxylic acids, such as citric acid  
(CA) and malic acid  (MA), could serve as potential candidates for constructing glass 
phosphorescent materials. Using the same evaporation -induced self-assembly procedure, we 
prepared doped materials with TFH as the guest in CA and MA host matrices, respectively. As 
shown in Figure R1, the differential scanning calorimetr y (DSC) curves and powder X-ray 
diffraction (PXRD) analysis confirm the formation of amorphous supramolecular glasses (SGs) 
in these systems. However, due to the reduced hydrogen-bonding sites in these host molecules, 
which hinder the formation of the more compact hydrogen -bonding network that effectively 
suppresses non -radiative decay, the phosphorescence quantum yield ( ΦP) and lifetime are 
significantly lower compared to the BTA -based SGs, with values of 5.90% and 3.99%, and 
lifetimes of 0.74 s and 0.72 s, respectively  (Figure R2 and Table R1). Moreover, these SGs 
exhibit instability and rapidly crystallize after 6 h and 2  h under ambient conditions  (Figures 
R1c and d). This could be attributed to the absence of the star-shaped, non-planar geometry of 
BTA, which is critical for forming a stable disordered glass state. The unique combination of 
four carboxyl groups in the flexible aliphatic chains of BTA enables the formation of a dense, 
multi-directional hydrogen -bonding network that effectively inhibits crystallization. While 
citric acid and malic acid are promising, their strong crystallization tendency makes them less 
suitable for forming stable glasses via our simple evaporation-induced method.
