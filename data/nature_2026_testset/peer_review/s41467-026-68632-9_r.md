Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors developed a diffusive N-cadherin functionalized hydrogel system that effectively modulates intercellular
communications by providing cell-cell adhesion cues, thereby significantly enhancing the formation of active neural
networks. Both in vitro (cellular) and in vivo (rat traumatic brain injury models) studies provide compelling evidence for the
therapeutic efficacy of this hydrogel system, demonstrating its advantages and underlying mechanisms. These findings hold
significant implications, particularly for the development of engineered biomaterials designed to regulate cell fate in the
regeneration of diverse tissues. While the study demonstrates methodological rigor, certain clarifications and minor
refinements would further enhance the manuscript's quality. Therefore, I recommend acceptance after minor revisions.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this interesting manuscript, Tang and colleagues describe the development of a tuneable, biocompatible material that
recapitulates cell-cell adhesion cues to enhance neural cell network formation. The authors highlight that most materials
employed for similar applications focus on mimicking cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) cues, whereas N-cadherin-mediated
cell-cell signalling provides an alternative to enhance neural network formation. This is investigated in vitro with PC12 and
primary E16 cortical neurons from rat pups as well as in vivo. Overall, the article demonstrates that NL@SFMA hydrogels
are a promising tool for promoting neural growth. 
However, several concerns should be addressed prior to accepting the study for publication:

1. The introduction emphasizes the role of cell–cell adhesion cues in central nervous system processes and the need to
recapitulate these in regenerative materials. The final paragraph introduces the hydrogel system and briefly mentions THBS-
1–mediated neural communication. This section would benefit from a more comprehensive summary of the study's key
findings, including a brief mention of the experimental approaches—such as RNA-seq or pharmacological inhibition—that
link N-cadherin signaling to THBS-1–mediated effects. 
2. Furthermore, the novelty and broader applicability of dynamic lipid-diffusive hydrogels for tissue regeneration is only
discussed in the outlook. Introducing this potential earlier in the manuscript would help the reader better contextualize the
significance of this platform. 
3. Authors mention the limited application of lipid bilayers with cell-cell adhesion cues as they are limited to 2D applications,
yet it remains unclear if the NL@SFMA or similar platform could be employed in 3D. It is confusing that while the scheme in
Figure 1 suggests that the lipid-driven functionalisation of the underlying silk fibroin happens in 3D, then all the in vitro
studies are conducted in 2D 
4. Further, the 
NL@SFMA hydrogel (or even the L@SFMA hydrogel) consists of lipid bilayers on polymer chains (i.e. silk
fibroin). There is no mention to the stability of this coating. This is particularly relevant in biological environments and in the
presence of cells. It’d be important to gather some data in this sense. 
5. Similarly, in vitro experiments employ primary rat pup and PC12cells and in vivo studies used a rat TBI model. Therefore,
why were mouse fibroblasts used for cytocompatibility studies? Were similar cytocompatibility assays conducted using the
rat primary neurons or PC12 cells? 
6. The authors use Gabapentin to test THBS-1 involvement, describing it as a THBS-1 inhibitor. Please provide a reference
supporting this claim. Given Gabapentin’s known off-target effects (e.g., on calcium channels and neurotransmitter release),
we question whether it sufficiently specific to draw conclusions about THBS-1 signaling. Authors are encouraged to use a
more selective inhibitor to strengthen their mechanistic claims. 
7. The authors claim that NL@SFMA enhances intercellular communication (Fig. 2L), yet no direct recordings of synaptic
activity are presented. Including functional assays—such as calcium imaging, synaptic marker immunostaining, or
electrophysiological recordings— are needed to demonstrate for enhanced neuronal communication. 
8. The study highlights the importance of cell–cell adhesion cues in promoting neural network regeneration. However, it
remains unclear how the proposed approach compares to existing strategies addressing the same biological challenge. The
discussion should include a brief discussion on how this platform performs relative to current state-of-the-art materials would
help contextualize its advantages and potential impact.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript describes an N-cadherin-functionalized hydrogel (NL@SFMA) that mimics membrane-like cell-cell
adhesion to promote synaptogenesis and neurological recovery after TBI. I have several major concerns: 
1.Similar hydrogel systems diffusible N-cadherin or mimicking cell-cell adhesion have already been reported to enhance
neural connectivity and repair after CNS injury. The presented work does not clearly distinguish itself in design or

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

mechanism. 
2.Mature cortical neurons in adult rats lack intrinsic regenerative ability. The described effects likely reflect synaptic
remodeling or neuroprotection, not true regeneration. The authors overstate the regenerative claims. 
3.Despite proposing anti-apoptosis as a mechanism, the study lacks apoptosis validation in TBI brain tissue. 
4.The treated brains show unusually well-preserved structures without glial scar, which raises concerns about data
reliability. 
5.Multiple group comparisons were analyzed using student’s t-tests instead of ANOVA, which is statistically inappropriate. 
Overall, the study lacks sufficient novelty and biological rigor to meet the publication standards of Nature Communications.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Tang and colleagues have performed revisions and clarifications of their manuscript that describes a diffusive N-Cadherin
functionalized hydrogel that mimics cell-cell adhesion cues to promote neural cell network formation. The new version of the
manuscript incorporates several additions, including schematic illustrations, expanded discussions of rationale and
additional functional assays. However, the study still lacks analytical depth and experimental rigor to substantiate its claims. 
1. Authors claim that the novelty of their work lies within the diffusivity of N-Cadherin in their system as well as describing a
potential mechanism for neurological function recovery post injury. Meanwhile, authors have cultured primary cells from the
cerebral cortex of E16 for 24h on the compared hydrogel systems, which is a limited in-vitro timepoint that can’t support
claims. Authors should include more time points to follow the evolution of the network formation on the hydrogels.
Additionally, from the images provided and the described methodology, it is also unclear on how neurites were identified,
analyzed and quantified. As other studies report slower neurite growth in 24h 
i.e. ~ 40um at 24h in cortical neurons
(Riquelme et al., 2024) or ~20um in SVZ neurons (Khodosevich and Monyer, 2010). 
2. We appreciate the authors performing functional assays to sustain their claims, providing new experimental data on
spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs). 
While NL@SFMA hydrogels increased the frequency of sEPSCs,
results reported in Fig 3, H (amplitude) are never discussed, which weakens the interpretation of enhanced neuronal
communication. 
3. The new scheme added to the introduction and the additional paragraph have made it easier for the reader to
contextualize the work. Still, the description of the scheme should be improved so that the reader can better interpret the
image. 
4. Sample size and statistical analysis remain elusive, how many cells were used to get neurite tracings, what statistical test
was employed to obtain the reported p values? Even if a statement was added to respond to Reviewer 5, comment 6, it is
still unclear whether authors have used t-tests to perform pairwise comparisons between group conditions. 
5. We appreciate that the authors incorporated a new inhibitor for THBS-1 (LSKL peptide) and provided supporting
references for the use of Gabapentin, which appropriately justify its use. However, the results of these additional
experiments should be more fully discussed to clarify how they strengthen the proposed mechanism. Our original concern
aimed to question the specificity of the pharmacological inhibition—whether the reduction in neurite extension observed
upon inhibitor treatment truly reflects THBS-1 blockade or could instead arise from off-target effects of the compounds used. 
6. In lines 405-408: ‘All TBI rats were divided into four groups: the control (…) Fig 5A’ The animal groups are not shown in
figure 5A, as it describes the in vivo experimental timeline. 
Overall, while the study is ambitious and has improved in response to reviewer’s feedback, it remains descriptive rather than
mechanistically rigorous. The range of data is impressive, with a significant part of the manuscript dedicated to the
implantation of the hydrogel in vivo which is practically useful but adds little to the conceptual novelty and mechanistic
understanding of the new hydrogel. In summary, in our opinion the narrative remains diffuse, and key experiments such as
neurite quantification, statistical validation, inhibitor specificity are not developed enough to support some of the author’s
conclusions.
