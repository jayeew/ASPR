Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
It still remains largely unclear about the mechanisms preventing immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) from effectively
reactivating antitumor immunity in SCLC. In this manuscript, Chakraborty et al. proposed an important role for
nonhomologous DNA end joining (NHEJ) signaling in regulating immune evasion of SCLC. The authors found that the
NHEJ regulator PRKDC, also known as DNAPKcs, was frequently overexpressed in SCLC and associated with
unfavorable patient outcomes. They further showed that PRKDC expression was negatively correlated with the expression
of key immune-related genes, and predictive of poor responses to immunotherapy in SCLC. Through functional studies, they
demonstrated that inhibition of DNAPKCs by NU7441, a DNAPKcs inhibitor currently undergoing clinical trials, decreased
tumor growth, induced immune cell infiltration, and enhanced the response to ICB therapy in two immunocompetent SCLC
models. Mechanistically, DNAPKCs inhibition triggered GSK3β-mediated MYC degradation and subsequent activation of
the STING pathway, which led to increased cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage population, and
upregulation of MHC-I, thus conversing immune ‘cold’ tumors into immune ‘hot’ tumors. Collectively, these findings highlight
DNAPKcs as a potential therapeutic target to enhance the treatment response of ICB in SCLC. This work is interesting,
aligning well with their previous studies showing that targeting DNA damage response pathways is an effective strategy to
improve the anti-tumor immune response of ICB in SCLC preclinical models. Below, I list some concerns for the authors to
further improve the manuscript. 
1. Fig. 1D and 1G show that high PRKDC expression predicted shorter OS in SCLC patients, both with or without
chemotherapy. However, the criteria used to classify high and low PRKDC expression in these two panels appear to be
different (11:10 in Fig. 1D, 164: 62 in Fig. 1G). Could the authors clarify the rationale for choosing different cut-off values in
these cases? Additionally, the authors mentioned in Fig. 1G that a total of 423 patients treated with cisplatin were included in
the survival analysis (Line 240 on page 8), yet the figure shows only 224 patients at the starting point. Could the authors
address this discrepancy? 
2. The authors stated that “IFNγ response, IFNα response were found to be enriched in SCLC clinical samples with low
baseline expression of PRKDC (Fig. 1E). Whereas E2F target genes, G2M checkpoint and MYC target genes were
observed to be enriched in SCLC patient samples with high expression of PRKDC (Fig. 1F)” (Lines 201-204 on page 7).
However, the direction of the enrichment profile curves in Fig. 1F appears unusual as they are in the opposite direction
compared to the curves in Fig. 1E. 
3. Results from Fig. 3C and 3E show that NU7441 treatment significantly reduced the protein levels of total STING while
simultaneously inducing STING signaling activation in DMS114 and H446 cells. However, the data in Fig. 3D indicate that
PRKDC knockdown did not affect the protein levels of total STING in these cell lines. These data suggest that the
mechanisms of action underlying pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown of DNAPKcs might be different. Did
NU7441 treatment also inhibit the transcriptional levels of STING or affect its protein stability? 
4. From the results in Fig. 4E, the authors concluded that “NU7441 treatment led to an appreciable increase in the cGAS-
STING pathway markers, p-TBK1 (Ser 172) and p-IRF3 (Ser396) in NTC cells which was relatively abrogated upon MYC
knockdown (Fig. 4E)” (Lines 472-474 on page 15). Nevertheless, if take a close look at the result, it’s hard to conclude that
MYC knockdown abrogated the effect of NU7441 on cGAS-STING activation. To confirm the role of MYC, the authors should
perform MYC overexpression experiment in this setting to determine if MYC overexpression can reverse NU7441-mediated
cGAS-STING activation. 
5. The proposed working model of this study suggests that DNAPKcs inhibition leads to MYC degradation and subsequent
STING activation, which induces upregulation of interferon pathway and MHC-I expression. However, the data in Fig. 4L
seem to contradict this model, as the combined effect of NU7441 treatment and MYC knockdown on the upregulation of

MHC-I expression appears much less than the effect of either NU7441 treatment alone or MYC knockdown alone. 
6. Similarly, the data from Fig. 4P and 4Q show that MYC knockdown significantly reversed the NU7441-induced
upregulation of CCL5 and B2M, suggesting that the presence, rather than the absence, of MYC is essential for the NU7441-
induced upregulation of these genes. This finding also seems somewhat contradictory to the proposed working model of this

7. In Fig. 7A-B, why it was the NU7441 monotherapy group (Orange dotted line) but not the combination treatment group

γ-H2AX in H446 cells. In Fig. 4E, NU7441 treatment did not induce an appreciable upregulation of p-STING. The protein
levels of MYC in Fig. 4E-G should be included to better support the findings. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Chakraborty et al. investigate the role of nonhomologous DNA end-joining (NHEJ), specifically
PRKDC/DNAPKcs, in small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and discover the potential of clinically targeting this molecule to
improve both chemotherapy and immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) immunotherapy. They show that PRKDC is
overexpressed in SCLC and the patients with the highest PRKDC experience worse clinical outcomes. In addition, lower
PRKDC expression predicts better responses to both chemotherapy alone and combined immunotherapy–chemotherapy
treatments. They demonstrate that PRKDC inhibition with a small molecule inhibitor (NU7441), in addition to another
inhibitor as well as siRNA-mediated knock-down, leads to increased cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and micronuclei formation.
Through a series of clever experiments, they show that PRKDC inhibition causes MYC degradation (through the AKT-GSK-
beta signaling axis), which in turn activates the cGAS-STING pathway to upregulate several genes, including IFN family
members, antigen presentation, and PD-L1. Using well-established mouse models, they show that NU7441 treatment alone,
as well as in combination with anti-PD-L1 ICB, significantly decreases tumor growth, which is accompanied by several
alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment towards a more active anti-tumor state. 
This manuscript explores a relatively understudied NHEJ in a very aggressive cancer type, SCLC, and identifies PRKDC
targeting as a novel treatment strategy to sensitize these tumors to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy. The authors
employed multiple cell lines, mouse models, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models that cover various molecular
subtypes of the heterogeneous SCLC to test their hypothesis. Their finding is significant to the field as both chemotherapy
and ICB work relatively poorly (i.e., the effects are rather short-lived) for SCLC, and there is an immediate clinical need to
improve these treatments through combination therapies. The manuscript is very well-written, and experiments were
performed thoroughly with complementary approaches and various models. I firmly believe their findings will be valuable to
the cancer community. 
Please see below my comments that aim to suggest minor improvements: 
1) The authors show elevated PRKDC in SCLC and its predictive power for patient outcomes. Can you please also
comment on mutations in this gene? How frequent are PRKDC mutations in SCLC, and do they show any correlations with
clinical outcomes? In addition, is it possible to survey whether NHEJ as a pathway is enriched in tumors with high PRKDC
levels? 
2) Does any of the key lineage transcription factors (i.e., ASCL1, NEUROD1, POU2F3, and YAP1) directly regulate the
expression of the PRKDC gene? 
3) Figure 1D. The survival plot data is based on only 21 patients. Could this analysis be extended to larger cohorts, such as
TCGA? 
4) Figure S1C. Is DDL3 correlation with PRKDC levels due to PRKDC being enriched in SCLC-A (as shown in Figure 1C)? 
5) Line 244. Please write the full drug name and its target; i.e. Atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1). Also, please clarify in the text that
this clinical trial tests Atezolizumab in combination with chemotherapy. 
6) Figure 1I. The graph shows that tumors with lower PRKDC expression benefit ICB better. Since the authors also show
that PRKDC can regulate both MYC and IFN, which are known to regulate PD-L1, it would be useful to know whether the
PRKDC levels show any correlation with PD-L1 levels in this cohort. In other words, can PRKDC levels predict ICB
response independent of PD-L1 levels? 
7) Figures 2C-E. Do PRKDC levels or MYC levels in these PDXs predict response to NU7441? 
8) Figures 3J-K. In line 409, the authors mention the importance of an intact immune system in PRKDC-mediated anti-tumor
response but proceed to test the treatment in only athymic nude mice (immunocompromised). Would it not be more useful to
test the drug on RPP and RPM transplants in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised mice side by side to
address this question? Given that they later perform CD8 T cell depletion (Figure 7), the question is partially answered. So,
additional experimentation would only be marginally useful, but it would be beneficial to revise the relevant text to better
explain that there are both cancer cell-intrinsic (as NU7441 is effective even in nude mice) and extrinsic (and immune-
mediated) effects (as CD8 T cell depletion decreases the effectiveness of the drug) of the drug.

10) Figure 4E. The drug treatment (i.e., PRKDC inhibition) does not seem to induce p-STING or p-IRF3 but only p-TBK1 on

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors report that PRKDC is highly expressed in SCLC tumor tissues and cell lines. The authors
showed that PRKDC expression correlates with lower expression of immune activation signaling and predicts a poor
response to multiple therapies. DNA-PKcs depletion leads to cytoplasmic DNA accumulation and micronuclei formation,
thereby activating cGAS-STING signaling. Moreover, DNA-PKcs depletion promotes the proteasomal degradation of MYC,
which seems to further enhance cGAS-STING signaling activation. DNA-PK inhibitors show a synergistic effect when
combined with PD-L1 inhibitors, and this effect is dependent on CD8+ T cells. While this study provides insights into the role
of DNA-PKcs in SCLC, substantial issues need to be addressed to consolidate the study. 
General Comments: 
1.The role of the DNA-PK complex in cGAS-STING signaling has been extensively explored in the literature, with some
studies presenting conflicting results. It would be beneficial for the authors to reference these studies and engage with the
existing literature. 
2.The accumulation of cytoplasmic DNA and micronuclei following DNA damage or genome instability is widely accepted
as a common mechanism for cGAS activation. However, DNA-PKcs inhibition or depletion typically results in a relatively
weak DNA damage signal, and the accumulation of damage signaling takes a long time frame. This seems at odds with the
treatment time frame in the study. The authors may clarify this point. 
3.MYC is a well-known oncogene and its role in suppressing cGAS-STING signaling has been explored. However, the
mechanisms by which MYC suppresses cGAS-STING signaling in SCLC are not elaborated in this study. Moreover, the
connection between MYC, DNA-PKcs, and cGAS/STING signaling shall be further strengthened. 
4.The study relies heavily on the use of inhibitors, but the potential off-target effects of these inhibitors are a critical
consideration. When gene loss-of-function experiments were conducted, only one siRNA or shRNA was used. It would be
more convincing if at least two independent siRNAs/shRNAs/sgRNAs were employed to confirm the phenotypes. 
5.Many immunoblot images presented lack sufficient quality, including uneven internal controls and high backgrounds. 
Specific Comments: 
1.Following treatment with NU7441, the presence of micronuclei was confirmed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 2K and 2L).
However, a time-dependent increase in micronuclei after NU7441 treatment was not observed. 
2.For Figure 2, protein levels are generally more reliable than mRNA expression for evaluating IFN-α, IFN-β, IFN-γ, and
CCL5. Therefore, ELISA quantification is recommended to provide more strong evidence. 
3.In lines 450-453, the authors suggest that the baseline mRNA and protein levels of MYC correlate with the anti-tumor
efficacy of DNA-PKcs inhibition. However, in the cases of LX1322 and LX33, the MYC mRNA levels do not fully correspond
with its protein levels. The authors may provide explanation. 
4.In lines 513-515, the authors stated that NU7441 treatment did not further affect the surface expression of MHC-I in MYC
knockdown cells. However, the flow cytometry data (Fig. 4K) show that MHC-I expression is further reduced in the MYC
knockdown + NU7441 treatment group compared to MYC knockdown alone. This needs to be clarified. 
5.In Figure 4M-4P, some of the genes, such as IFNB1, showed enhanced transcription upon MYC knockdown+NU7441
treatment, while other genes do not display the effect. The authors may provide explanation. Additionally, the authors should
use gene names (e.g., IFNB1) rather than protein names (e.g., IFNβ) in this context. 
6.In Figures 7A and 7C, the depletion of CD8+ T cells does not seem to promote SCLC tumor growth. This is unexpected,
and the role of CD8+ T cells in SCLC could be further evaluated or at least discussed.

Response: We thank the reviewer for the question.  
 
Figure 1D represents data from a publicly available GEO dataset consisting of 21 small cell lung cancer patient 
samples. This dataset was not stratified by any treatment status, and given the small sample size, we categorized 
patients into high and low PRKDC expression groups using a median split to maximize statistical power within

Figure 1G uses an independent, real-world clinical dataset (from Caris Life Sciences) comprising 423 patients 
with small cell lung cancer who were treated with cisplatin-based chemotherapy. For this larger dataset, we 
stratified patients by PRKDC expression into quartiles. We then compared patient prognosis between the highest 
and lowest quartiles. As a result, Figure 1G reflects survival data from the top and bottom quartiles only, totaling 
224 patients (112 per group), which explains the apparent discrepancy with the full cohort size of 423 patients 
mentioned in the manuscript text.

Response: We thank the reviewer for the comment.  
 
The enrichment curves in Figure 1F were originally shown curving toward the right because they reflected gene 
set enrichment in the high PRKDC expression group. In contrast, Figure 1E showed enrichment in the low 
PRKDC group, with curves curving to the left. This difference in the direction of the curves was due to how the 
data were plotted.

To improve clarity and consistency, we have revised the presentation of Figure 1F to match the orientation of

These figures continue to support our conclusion that low PRKDC expression is associated with interferon

3. Results from Fig. 3C and 3E show that NU7441 treatment significantly reduced the protein levels of total 
STING while simultaneously inducing STING signaling activation in DMS114 and H446 cells. However, the data 
in Fig. 3D indicate that PRKDC knockdown did not affect the protein levels of total STING in these cell lines.

of DNAPKcs might be different. Did NU7441 treatment also inhibit the transcriptional levels of STING or affect

Response: We thank the reviewer for the question.  
 
To clarify the apparent difference between pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown of DNAPKcs, we 
have repeated the STING western blots and confirmed that NU7441 treatment consistently reduces total STING 
protein levels compared to vehicle-treated DMS114 and H446 cells, as shown in Figure 3C and Figure 3E (also 
pasted below; R1A). To further investigate, we examined RNA sequencing data following NU7441 treatment. 
We observed a trend toward reduced STING (TMEM173) RNA expression in four of five SCLC cell lines (figure 
below R1B), although the suppression was not statistically significant. This suggests that NU7441 may also 
influence STING at the transcriptional level to a limited extent. 
 
One of the discrepancies found in this study in respect to pharmacological and genetic inhibition of DNAPKcs 
was protein expression of total STING. Given the dynamics signaling proteins, stability of STING not only 
depends on transcriptional and translational induction of STING but also degradation mechanism. ESCRT 
mediated micro-autophagy has been reported to be a significant contributor to STING degradation pathway 
[PMID:36918692, PMID:36739287]. Both mTOR and PI3K have been known to play a crucial role in initiating 
autophagy and lysosome mediated degradation of long lived / impaired proteins. Thus, it is possible that upon 
NU7441 mediated pharmacological inhibition of DNAPKcs mTOR or PI3K signaling may play a role in decreasing 
STING expression which is not found in case of siRNA mediated genetic inhibition of DNAPKcs.  
 
However, PRKDC knockdown by siRNA (Figure 3D) did not significantly reduce total STING protein levels in 
these cell lines. These findings support the notion that pharmacological inhibition with NU7441 and genetic 
knockdown of PRKDC may elicit partially distinct cellular responses, potentially due to differences in DNAPKcs 
complex dynamics or drug-induced protein destabilization. Pharmacological inhibition may affect STING through 
mechanisms involving protein stability or post-translational modification. 
 
We have included the RNA sequencing results in the rebuttal and are happy to add them as a supplementary 
figure in the revised manuscript upon request. We have now included this apparent difference between genetic 
and pharmacological DNAPKcs inhibition in the discussion of the revised manuscript, highlighting it as an 
important observation that encourages further mechanistic validation in future studies. (page 27-28).
