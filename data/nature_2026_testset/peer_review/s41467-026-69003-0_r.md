Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Condakes et al., describes a novel class of covalent G12C KRAS inhibitors capable of targeting both the
inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound conformations of KRAS. 
A series of biochemical, cellular, and in vivo
experiments were performed to compare the dual inhibitors with divarasib (sotorasib), a covalent G12C KRAS inhibitor
selective for the inactive GDP-bound conformation of G12C KRAS, and the non-covalent tri-complex inhibitor, RMC-6291,
that targets the active, GTP-bound form of G12C KRAS. The authors show faster kinetics of RAS-RAF inhibition with two
lead dual inhibitors, compounds 6 and 8, in biochemical assays and potent growth inhibitory activity in G12C KRAS mutant
pancreatic cancer cell lines. 
Compound 8 showed strong in vivo antitumor activity resulting in tumor regression. 
However,
both in vitro and in vivo experiments evaluating anticancer activity show no apparent advantages of the dual inhibitors
compared with inactive-state or active-state inhibitors. The authors also show evidence that growth factor (e.g. EGF)
activation of wild-type NRAS and HRAS can cause resistance to G12C KRAS inhibitors regardless of the mechanism of
binding. 
The manuscript is well-written and contains important results that support the author's conclusions. 
The following are
suggestions to improve the overall quality: 
1) Why is there such a large discrepancy in treatment time for the dual inhibitors to block RAS-RAF binding (9-56 mins) vs.
inhibition of MAPK signaling (24-48 hrs)? 
2) The evidence that activation of wild-type NRAS and HRAS cause resistance to mutant-specific KRAS inhibitors is
important. 
However, the authors should provide evidence confirming growth factor activation of wild-type RAS isozymes
using isozyme-specific antibodies as recently described by Foote et al, Cancer Research, March 2025). The authors should
also discuss the advantages of pan-RAS inhibitors over mutant-specific KRAS and other RAS inhibitors in development, as
described in this publication, for their ability to simultaneously block both mutant KRAS and wild-type NRAS and HRAS
isozymes.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Condakes et al. describes a new set of KRAS G12C inhibitors that covalently engage both the GDP-bound,
inactive state and the GTP-bound, active state of the KRAS G12C oncoprotein protein. Most KRAS G12C inhibitors to date

disclosed dual-state KRAS G12C inhibitor BBO-8520, have identified new modality of KRAS G12C engagement.

cancer community, and it will be of particular interest to the KRAS field.

pERK dose-response curve of Compound 8, and this right shift is attenuated in the H/NRAS double KO cells.

RAF disruption. Compounds 6 and 8 inhibit phosphorylation of ERK1/2, a proximal

addition, compounds 6 and 8 inhibit the transcription DUSP6, a validated biomarker 
downstream of ERK phosphorylation, at 24 hours post-treatment (Extended Table 3).

robust suppression of ERK phosphorylation within 30 min.

We thank the reviewer for the opportunity to sharpen our original response: 
 
The totality of data presented is consistent with the novel warhead not showing 
promiscuous reactivity with nucleophiles. Kinetic evaluation of compound 8 (Figure 3a) 
showed single adduct formation at all concentrations and time points, and data for an 
exemplary concentration & time point was added in Extended Figure 4. Crystal 
structures of KRAS G12C bound to inhibitor (PDB: 9NZM, 9NZN) indicate the continued 
presence of multiple solvent-exposed nucleophilic residues (e.g., Cys80, Lys88, etc.). A 
chemical reactivity experiment with glutathione corroborated non-promiscuous levels of 
cysteine reactivity with the novel warhead and has been added in Extended Table 1. 
Global cysteine profiling likewise is consistent with a lack of warhead promiscuity. In this 
experiment, over 22,000 unique cysteines within 6,900 protein groups were covered, 
and 5 cysteines were identified as significantly decreased upon compound treatment (< 
0.02%). We have updated the manuscript to specify this coverage and remove 
subjective language. As additional context, this experiment evaluates whether a 
cysteine is more or less accessible to desthiobiotin iodoacetamide labeling. For

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

instance, the two cysteines of DUSP4 that were identified as being significantly less 
accessible following compound treatment (Cys141 and Cys159) are not less accessible 
due to compound binding at those positions; rather, DUSP4 – a known MAPK biomarker

flow-through data. Accordingly, (biased) non-cysteine reactivity is passively assessed in 
this workflow. A compound that irreversibly modifies any residue on the cysteine-
containing peptidic fragment, or otherwise prevents the identification of that peptide 
(e.g., by blocking trypsin digestion through covalent modification of the lysine or arginine 
at the cleavage site), would be expected to be identified. As an example, we include the 
specific peptide sequences for Cys80 of KRAS and NRAS as well as the raw fold-
change data and the protein level data in Extended Figure 5, which show no significant
