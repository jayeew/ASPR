Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I have read the manuscript carefully and find it interesting and relevant. The idea of facilitating the determination of
calibration parameters from experimental results using Neural Monte Carlo is very promising. The paper is clearly written,
with abundant information and detailed explanations of both the methodology and the results. The methods employed are
appropriate for the task, and the statistical validation appears sound. I particularly appreciate the clarity of the presentation
and the thoroughness of the analysis. 
That said, I have one major criticism concerning the scope of the results. The study relies exclusively on Monte Carlo
simulations, which significantly limits the generality of the conclusions. In realistic experimental conditions, additional
degrees of freedom in the fit could modify the outcomes—for example, the dependence on the location within the detector
where the radioactive disintegration occurs. The work convincingly demonstrates that the proposed method can reproduce
with remarkable precision the values introduced in the model, but it remains difficult to assess the robustness of these results
in practice. 
It is indeed correct to state that the system is able to disentangle the strong correlations among the three input parameters
(f_c, K_b, and Y), which is an indication of the analysing power of the proposed technique. 
However, an important open
question is how the method would perform in real conditions, where additional hidden dependencies are likely to appear.
Addressing this issue, at least through a discussion or preliminary validation with experimental data, would considerably
strengthen the manuscript. 
In addition, I have a few specific comments on the wording and presentation: 
L. 370–373: I find the sentence confusing and even contradictory. 
L. 485–489: How can we argue that the method is actually capturing the correlations? Is this conclusion solely drawn from
the results presented in Table II? 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The submitted paper is motivated by next-generation neutrino experiments, such as JUNO, which require sub-percent
precision in modeling the detector's energy response. Traditional Monte Carlo calibration data methods are infeasible due to
complexity, strong parameter correlations, and computational cost. The authors proposed two neural network density
estimator techniques, one based on normalization flow and the other based on the transformer architecture. Both models
accurately capture complex spectral features, maintain interpretability, and directly recover physical detector parameters.
However, the method appears to be limited by the specifics of the JUNO experiment and the properties of the data analyzed.
The authors are restricted to 1D distributions, and the method relies on massive MC-generated and uniformly distributed
data.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

The paper deserves publication; however, it would benefit from a more thorough discussion of the approach's limitations. In
particular, the successful models were obtained using uniformly distributed data across the parameter space. It is important
and interesting (for the generalization of the study's method) to consider how the method performs when the data is
fragmented or not uniformly distributed. For instance, how would the results change if the data were obtained through
random sampling or if the training data were not uniformly distributed? 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Dear editor and authors, 
I congratulate with the authors for the excellent work described in this paper. It presents a novel application of Simulation-
based Inference for tuning the simulation of the JUNO photomultiplier tubes based on calibration runs with a set of
calibration sources. The methods proposed here have can simplify the MC tuning process, thus potentially leading to
reduction both on systematic uncertainties and computing resources required to produce the related MC samples. As far as I
know, this represents the first application of these techniques to neutrino experiments thus opening the way for similar work
in other detectors. The paper describes the methods developed clearly and with sufficient details, so that other researchers
should be able to replicate them. 
I have a few comments and questions below, aiming at improving the clarity of the paper and at strengthening its claims.
Once these are addressed I do not anticipate issues with recommending the paper for publication. 
General comments: 
A direct comparison with parameter estimation using a traditional method would be useful to show the improvement from
using SBI. I understand this might be too much work to do this from scratch, but maybe a comparison can be made with
respect to previous work in JUNO or to requirements outlined in the technical design. 
The structure of the paper follows the journal guidelines, discussing the results before the methods are detailed, which
allows the reader to be exposed to the main takeaways upfront, without getting lost in technical aspects. However, the level
of details presented in each section does always not seem to be appropriate for the reader’s understanding. There are many
concepts introduced early on that are clarified only much later in the methods section; some examples are found in my
detailed comments below. As a matter of fact, in Sec. II I count 10 forward references to either Sec. IV or the Appendices. I
think a different balance of details may help, possibly providing some key details early on (I personally would not mind some
repetition in this case). 
Prospect for actually applying the methods described here to real calibration data are not discussed. Some relevant
questions are the following. Will data taking with all calibration sources described in the paper be easily performed by
JUNO, both in terms of availability of the sources and duration of the data taking runs? If not, which are minimum
configurations required for sufficient precision in the parameter estimation for the experiment’s physics goals? Are there
other uncertainties or possible mis-modelings in the simulation that may limit or bias the application of the methods
described (e.g. uncertainties in GEANT4, simplifications in the geometry or material description, possible limitation of the
Birk’s model)? 
Line by line comments: 
Eq. 1 presents the PDF. While formally S is one of the input parameters, and you state that you create a PDF for each set of
parameters, it is not clear at this stage how formally the different sources will be used downstream, i.e. if they will be fit
simultaneously or not. 
Eq. 2 presents the NFDE probability density. Two aspects that are not clear at this stage are how many transformations are
used in the sequence, and how the Jacobian is accessed (i.e. whether analytically or if approximated through some
functional form). These are later explained in Section IV, but some information at this stage may help the reader. 
Line 276: the parameter grid is not clear at this stage (explained in Sec. IV) 
Line 278: at this stage it is unclear how much the PE distributions changes with the different possible parameter
combinations. Again this information is presented in Sec. IV but it may be useful to give an idea of it at this stage. 
Line 308: is this representative point from testing dataset 1 or 2? (I see the information is present only in the header of Figure
2) 
Line 407: “Section A”, should this be “Appendix A”? 
Line 465: why Ns are free parameters? Aren’t them known already? You should know how many events are taken from each
calibration source. 
Line 477: the ability to interpolate depends on the distance of the estimated value from the grid points and needs to be
