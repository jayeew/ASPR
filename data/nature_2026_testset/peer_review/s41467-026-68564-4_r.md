Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this article, Vestergaard et al. explore the complex relationships between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and other
immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs) using a range of complementary approaches. From the title onwards, the
study captures the reader’s attention and lives up to expectations by addressing a topic of increasing relevance in IBD
management. 
The article is clearly written and well-structured. The data originate from an extensive dataset based on the Danish national
health registries, covering a long time span from 1910 to 2010. This timeframe appears appropriate, considering the study’s
aims and methodology. The results are robust and intriguing, offering a novel contribution to IBD research and enhancing
our understanding of the broader spectrum of IMIDs beyond conventional diagnostic classifications. 
GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS: 
• The study integrates genealogical, genetic, and microbiota data from Danish national sources. However, while the
genealogical data encompass a substantial portion of the Danish population, the genetic and microbiota datasets are
significantly smaller. This discrepancy could introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. These
limitations should be explicitly acknowledged. 
• For certain IMIDs, the sample size may be insufficient to allow reliable analyses across all three domains. In particular,
associations between IBD and 17, 10, and 7 IMIDs were analysed within the genealogical, genetic, and microbiota datasets,
respectively. This limitation should also be clearly discussed. 
• It would be informative to specify which genetic loci and microbial taxa were associated with either positive or negative
correlations across Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), and the various IMIDs. 
• The discussion could be strengthened by expanding on the clinical relevance of the observed associations. This is
especially important given that multidisciplinary care for patients with IBD and concomitant IMIDs remains suboptimal in
clinical practice. Furthermore, the section on future directions (lines 270–284) is overly generic and should be more directly
linked to the study’s findings and limitations. 
SPECIFIC COMMENTS: 
Abstract 
• The phrase “despite an estimated negative genetic overlap” is conceptually dense and unclear; consider rephrasing for
greater clarity. 
• Line 44 contains a typo: “systemic erythematosus” should be corrected to “systemic lupus erythematosus”. 
Main Text 
• A brief summary of the environmental factors potentially associated with different IMIDs would enhance reader
comprehension. 
• The statement about “pharmacological factors” (line 63) is unclear. Are the authors referring to paradoxical reactions, or to
the direct effects of medications in triggering IMIDs? Clarification is needed. 
• The term “comorbidity” likely refers to co-occurring IMIDs; this should be explicitly explained. 
• The mention of epigenomic and transcriptomic data (lines 78–79) is misleading unless such data were actually analysed.
Otherwise, the statement should be clarified or removed. 
• The methodology for constructing microbiota disease profiles (lines 92–96) is insufficiently detailed and should be moved

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents a comprehensive, multi-omics investigation of the relationships between IBD (CD/UC) and other IMIDs
using Danish national registries, GWAS data, and fecal microbiota profiling. The integration of familial, genetic, and
microbial dimensions provides novel insights into shared and distinct mechanisms underlying IMID comorbidities. The work
is methodologically rigorous, leverages unique population-level resources, and addresses a clinically gap in understanding
immune disease heterogeneity. The manuscript is suitable for publication pending minor revisions. 
Major Comments 
1.Limitations in GWAS Data for Rare Variants and Sample Heterogeneity. 
a)SNP-based heritability estimates are notably lower than genealogical heritability, indicating that GWAS inadequately
captures rare variants. This discrepancy, which may obscure key genetic contributions to disease associations, warrants
critical discussion. 
b)GWAS summary statistics are aggregated from diverse cohorts in Table S3, but the source of samples (e.g., blood or fecal)
for these GWAS remains unexplained. 
2.Restricting familial analyses to full siblings ignores extended kinships available in the Danish pedigree. A sensitivity
analysis incorporating all relatives to estimate heritability and compare results with sibling-based findings would strengthen
conclusions about familial aggregation. 
3.The microbiota analyses, while comprehensive, have two key limitations that should be more explicitly addressed: 
a)The cross-sectional design inherently precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of observed associations
(e.g., whether IBD drives microbial changes that subsequently trigger MS, or vice versa). 
b)Potential confounding by unmeasured variables - particularly medication use (e.g., biologics), diet, and antibiotics - may
influence microbial signatures, as these factors were not fully accounted for despite adjustments for geography and
sequencing batch effects. 
4.The study design should explicitly address how participants diagnosed with multiple concurrent IMIDs were handled in the
analyses. Given that immune-mediated diseases frequently co-occur, this population may represent a distinct subgroup with
unique genetic and microbial profiles. 
Minor Comments  
1.The Methods section references "Table 2," which is absent from both main and supplementary materials, requiring
correction. 
2.Discrepancy in Microbiota Disease number. The Methods section states that 8 diseases were analyzed for microbiota
profiles, yet the Results report only 7. This inconsistency should be resolved to ensure clarity. 
3.The manuscript states that analyses were restricted to diseases with ≥3,000 affected full-sibling pairs to ensure adequate
statistical power for heritability and familial correlation estimates (lines 119–122). However, the rationale for choosing this
specific threshold is not explicitly justified. A brief reason is needed.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Overall Assessment 
This manuscript represents an ambitious and well-executed attempt to integrate nationwide genealogical data, genome-
wide association summary statistics, and microbiome datasets to dissect the shared and distinct etiological pathways linking
IBD (Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis) with a range of immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs). The multi-
modal framework and scale of the data (spanning millions of individuals) are impressive and timely, given the increasing
interest in systems-level understanding of autoimmunity. The study is rich in novel insights, including divergent genetic and
microbial correlations between IBD subtypes and specific IMIDs (e.g., MS, RA, SLE), and provides a compelling case for
refining disease nosology beyond organ-centric labels. 
However, there are some areas where the manuscript would benefit from clarification, additional validation, or
methodological rigor, as outlined below. 
1. Validation of the Microbial Correlation Method 
The method for generating "microbiota disease profiles" and estimating pairwise disease correlations based on t-values of
ASVs is novel but not previously validated. While sensitivity analysis was conducted, the authors acknowledge that the
method itself has not been benchmarked. The authors might want to consider applying the microbial correlation method to
diseases with well-established microbiota signatures (e.g., colorectal cancer, IBS) as positive controls to demonstrate face
validity. 
2. Causality and Temporal Resolution 
The microbiota data is entirely cross-sectional and drawn from heterogeneous studies. The authors should more explicitly
discuss how temporal ambiguity in the microbiome data affects interpretation. E.g., could microbial similarity be a
consequence rather than a cause of disease clustering? 
3. Clinical Relevance 
While the results are statistically robust, their clinical implications could be more clearly articulated. The authors might want
to expand discussion on how findings could guide surveillance, risk stratification, or therapeutic decision-making for IBD
patients with familial or genetic susceptibility to specific IMIDs. 
4. Sex-Based Confounding or stratification 
Stratified analyses by sex and age (even descriptively) would strengthen the findings, especially since IMIDs often have sex-
specific prevalence (e.g., SLE in females, AS in males). 
5. Generalizability Beyond European Ancestry. The study is based entirely on Danish registry data and European-ancestry
GWAS. Authors should acknowledge the limitation that findings may not generalize to non-European populations and
discuss how future work might extend these insights globally. 
Minor Comments 
Terminology: The manuscript alternates between “ankylosing spondylitis” and “spondylarthritis” inconsistently—please
standardize for clarity. 
Figure 5: The visualization is informative but could be more intuitive if grouped by disease category or strength of correlation.

Overall Assessment 
This manuscript represents an ambitious and well-executed attempt to integrate nationwide 
genealogical data, genome-wide association summary statistics, and microbiome datasets to 
dissect the shared and distinct etiological pathways linking IBD (Crohn’s disease and 
ulcerative colitis) with a range of immune-mediated inﬂammatory diseases (IMIDs). The multi-
modal framework and scale of the data (spanning millions of individuals) are impressive and 
timely, given the increasing interest in systems-level understanding of autoimmunity. The 
study is rich in novel insights, including divergent genetic and microbial correlations between 
IBD subtypes and speciﬁc IMIDs (e.g., MS, RA, SLE), and provides a compelling case for 
reﬁning disease nosology beyond organ-centric labels. 
However, there are some areas where the manuscript would beneﬁt from clariﬁcation, 
additional validation, or methodological rigor, as outlined below. 
We appreciate and welcome the reviewers’ valuable comments and feedback for 
improvement of our manuscript, and for that we thank.

The method for generating "microbiota disease proﬁles" and estimating pairwise disease

analysis was conducted, the authors acknowledge that the method itself has not been 
benchmarked. The authors might want to consider applying the microbial correlation method

positive controls to demonstrate face validity.
