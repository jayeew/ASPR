Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, the authors report a high 99% conversion of PET to p-xylene using a CuCo/CoOx catalyst. While the catalyst
design is conceptually interesting, I believe the study is not suitable for publication in Nature Communications. A major
limitation lies in the absence of well-defined active sites, which complicates any detailed mechanistic investigation.
Furthermore, the transformation primarily involves C–O bond cleavage—a reaction pathway that is relatively facile and well-
documented. The reaction was also demonstrated on a tiny scale, using a 1:1 catalyst-to-polymer ratio, which raises
concerns about the scalability and economic feasibility of the process. Although the authors compare the production cost of
p-xylene from this PET upcycling route to the conventional naphtha-based process and claim a nearly 50% cost reduction,
this conclusion does not take into account the high catalyst loading, thereby limiting the validity of the economic analysis
and the practical viability of the approach. 
Also, the 1H-NMR contains impurities; use of HPLC-grade solvent is advisable. Also, the 1H-NMR peak for p-xylene is
unclear, and the integration for the two types of protons is not shown. The 1H-NMR spectra appears quite blurry, making it
difficult to assess data quality. Also, how is the impurity peak in Dioxane solvent not visible in GC-MS spectra? 
It is quite intriguing that the catalyst can cleave the C-O bond of PET, but no solvent decomposition is observed. How?
Solvent also has a C-O bond. Please clarify. 
The author provides a detailed explanation suggesting that copper doesn’t actively participate in the reaction but instead
plays a synergistic role, evidenced by the red shift of the A1g mode from 661 to 651 cm
⁻
¹, indicating lattice distortion and the
creation of oxygen vacancies. This claim is further supported by XPS and TPR studies. However, one question still remains
why copper? Have the authors tested other metals for comparison? Also, the copper XPS data is missing; most of the
characterization focuses only on cobalt. This raises the possibility that copper might be directly involved in the mechanistic
pathway, which hasn't been thoroughly ruled out.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present an interesting catalyst system for the hydrogenolysis of PET to paraxylene with high selectivity. The

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

significance of this reaction is clear. It produces pure para xylene without contamination from ring hydrogenation, ring
cleavage, or over-reduction, which is an advantage over previously reported precious metal system. The catalysts are well
characterized, and the role of copper in promoting the reduction of Co3O4 is supported by both experimental data and DFT.
The authors also show that para xylene desorbs more easily from CuCo/CoOx than from Co/CoOx, which improves turnover
and selectivity. However, i believe that major revisions are required, outlined below: 
the paper focuses mainly on catalyst characterization, while the actual catalytic work is rather limited, actually only one
reaction was studied. Since the authors have already developed and characterized this catalyst in detail and understand
why it works well, it would be more convincing if they tested it on additional substrates. For example, they could try
polybutylene terephthalate (PBT) or polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT, now commonly used in China to replace
LDPE). 
further, it would also be interesting how aliphatic polyesters such as PLA would react with their system. 
Although the authors show that the catalyst works with post-consumer PET containing additives and dyes, it would also be
useful to test it with mixed plastic streams. Real plastic waste often contains HDPE, LDPE, and PP from bottle caps, labels,
and cups. Understanding how these plastics affect the catalyst’s performance would help assess its practicality. It would
also be interesting to test a mixture of polyesters, such as PET and PLA. One major hurdle in plastics recycling is the
difficulty of sorting plastics, especially those used for similar applications (for example, PET and PLA are both used for
disposable cups). If the catalytic system can produce xylene and light alkanes from PET and 
PLA in a mixed-polyester
stream, it would strongly support its potential for real-world application. Also, PLA is commonly mixed with PBAT in straws
used in most large coffee brands in china. 
More concerning is the supporting data for the environmental and economic claims. The information provided for product
quantification, process modeling, LCA, and TEA is seriously lacking. These issues make it impossible to evaluate the
impact and reproducibility of the work. Since environmental and economic factors are a key motivation for converting plastic
waste into chemicals, these sections are critical to the significance of the paper. Major revisions are required to provide
adequate data to support the TEA and LCA, or these sections should be removed, and the paper should be reevaluated
based only on the catalysis. 
The authors claim that their process reduces the cost and global warming potential of producing p-xylene compared to the
conventional naphtha-based route. However, the supporting data are missing, and the analysis cannot be published in its
current form. 
The life cycle assessment is not reproducible or verifiable. There is no life cycle inventory provided in the main text or the
supplementary information. Key assumptions such as hydrogen source, electricity mix and transport distances are not
described. The emission factors of the materials are not given, and no data sources are cited. the authors also do not cite
where the GWP values for p-xylene from naptha come from. No sensitivity analysis or uncertainty assessment is provided.
Since hydrogen production and energy mix can strongly affect the LCA outcome, this lack of detail makes the LCA results
unreliable. 
The techno-economic analysis also lacks key details. The authors report a PX production cost of $413 per ton, but no cost
breakdown is given. We do not know the assumed plant scale, catalyst lifetime, utility costs, or operating schedule. No
performance indicators such as payback time, internal rate of return, or capital investment are reported. Therefore, the claim
that this route is cheaper than the conventional process is unsupported. 
A process flow diagram is included but is purely schematic. No data are included in the diagram or in any table in the
supplementary information. It is unclear whether mass and energy balances were done, and solvent or heat recovery is not
discussed. As it stands, the process diagram does not help readers understand the system or evaluate the LCA and TEA. 
Minor comment: 
1: In Figure 2e, the FTIR spectra show two flat lines. It is unclear what the purpose of this figure is. The authors state that the
spectra show no accumulation of organic materials and no structural changes to the catalyst. However, a flat FTIR does not
provide useful information about the structure. The claim that the catalyst maintains structural integrity is not supported by
this data. it world make more sense to show TEM before and after reaction. 
2: The NMR figure could possibly be larger, and the ppm range is a bit too broad. it would make more sense to focus on the
range between 0-10 ppm, and possibly include integrals and peak values.

Comment 1-1: A major limitation lies in the absence of well-defined active sites, which complicates 
any detailed mechanistic investigation.  
Response 1-1: Thanks for your comments. Although the original manuscript identified the oxygen 
vacancy–rich Co⁰/CoOₓ interface as the true active site for PET hydrogenolysis, the explanation 
provided may have lacked sufficient clarity. In the revised version, we offer a more thorough 
discussion and present extensive additional experimental evidence to reinforce and clarify this 
conclusion. A detailed analysis of the well -defined active sites and the catalytic mechanism is 
presented below, with newly added content highlighted in yellow: 
As comprehensively demonstrated in the original manuscript, the stepwise hydrogen spillover–
engineered CuCo/CoOₓ catalyst features low crystallinity, a partial phase transformation from hcp -
Co to fcc-Co, and abundant oxygen vacancy –rich Co/CoOₓ interfaces, with fcc -Co/CoOₓ interfaces 
predominating. The hydrogenolysis of PET to PX on CuCo/CoOₓ primarily proceeds through three 
steps: (1) activation of hydrogen on Co⁰ sites, (2) adsorption and activation of the substrate on CoOₓ, 
and (3) spillover of dissociated hydrogen species from Co⁰ to CoOₓ sites at Co/CoOₓ interfaces. 
Regarding hydrogen activation, zero-valent metal species typically serve as the active sites. H₂-
TPD profiles (Fig. R1) indicate that CuCo/CoOₓ exhibits superior hydrogen adsorption and 
dissociation performance compared to Co/CoOₓ. Furthermore, DFT calculations (Fig. R2) reveal that 
the energy barriers for H₂ dissociation on Co⁰, particularly the fcc -Co phase, are significantly lower 
than those on Cu⁰. Therefore, Co⁰ species, especially fcc -Co at the interface, are identified as the 
primary active sites responsible for H₂ activation during PET hydrogenolysis, rather than Cu⁰.

Fig. R2 (Fig. 4c in the manuscript) Calculated adsorption energies of H2 and dissociation energy of H2 on Cu (111),

Regarding substrate activation, we conducted TPD experiments using ethyl p-methylbenzoate as a 
probe molecule on a series of catalysts enrich ed in CuOₓ, Cu⁰ and Co⁰ sites, respectively ( Fig. R3). 
No evident desorption peaks were observed for these CuOₓ, Cu⁰ and Co⁰ -rich catalysts, whereas 
distinct desorption peaks appeared for both Co/CoOₓ and CuCo/CoOₓ catalysts. These observations 
indicate that CoOₓ is the primary active site for substrate adsorption. Notab ly, CuCo/CoOₓ exhibits 
significantly higher adsorption capacity for the probe molecule compared to Co/CoOₓ. Oxygen 
vacancies in Lewis acid catalysts (CuCo/CoOₓ and Co/CoOₓ) could play a critical role in the 
adsorption and activation of C-O bonds due to their enhanced oxophilicity1, 2. To further elucidate the 
role of oxygen vacancies, a series of characterizations including Raman, EPR, pyridine -IR, XPS, 
XAS and UV -Vis spectroscopy were performed to compare the oxygen vacancy concentrations of 
Co/CoOₓ and CuCo/CoOₓ (Fig. R4a-f). These results consistently revealed a higher oxygen vacancy 
concentration in CuCo/CoOₓ, confirming that Cu incorporation significantly increases the oxygen 
vacancy density in CoOₓ at the Co/CoOₓ interfaces. This enhancement is attribu ted to a decrease in 
the formation energy of oxygen vacancies at the interface, which is induced by the high interfacial 
strain. Therefore, we propose that the oxygen vacancy –rich CoOₓ species at the Co/CoOₓ interfaces 
serve as the primary active sites for the adsorption and activation of substrates.

catalysts, where the limited synergistic effect between metal sites for H2 dissociation with support for 
C-O activation constrains catalytic performance. Although Co/CoOₓ systems exhibit a certain degree 
of synergistic behavior, their activity remains suboptimal due to increased crystallinity and the 
significant loss of coordinatively unsaturated CoOₓ species during high-temperature reduction, which 
hampers the balance between effici ent H₂ dissociation and substrate activation . To address this 
limitation, the stepwise hydrogen spillover strategy we developed can construct oxygen vacancy-rich 
Co/CoOₓ interfaces active sites featuring an optimal balance between low-coordinated CoOₓ species 
and partially phase-transformed Co⁰ species, significantly enhancing the synergistic effect between 
C-O activation and H₂ dissociation. Comprehensive investigation based on DFT calculations, WO₃ 
color change experiments, H2-TPR, XRD, Raman, XPS, and sXAS (Fig. R2, R11, R12, R13, R4a, d, 
e) elucidate that the overall active-site formation follows a stepwise hydrogen spillover mechanism: 
Initially, CuOx species are preferentially reduced to metallic Cu0 sites capable of dissociating H2 into 
active H* species. These H* species subsequently migrate to adjacent CoOₓ regions, promoting the 
reduction of a small amount of CoOx to Co0. The newly formed Co0 sites, particularly those with fcc-
structure, then act as secondary spillover centers, f urther facilitating hydrogen activation and

migration for the reduction of more CoOₓ species. In summary, the unique stepwise hydrogen 
spillover process not only facilitates the partial phase transformation of hcp-Co into fcc-Co, but also

interfaces are dominant. Such unique active sites achieve an ideal synergy between H₂ dissociation 
and C-O bond activation, thereby enabling near -quantitative conversion of PET to PX. Considering

strategy, which enables the effective construction of synergistic active sites in the C–O bond cleavage

activation within the catalyst.

“To enhance the credibility of the TEA/LCA results, we have included detailed flow data, mass 
balances, and energy balances (Table S6 –S77) from the Aspen simulation process, which strictly 
adhere to the balance of mass and energy. The following section presents a comprehensive discussion 
of the treatment processes for solvents, hydrogen, and by -products. The naphtha -to-PX route uses 
naphtha as feedstock without additional solvents, and hydrogen is recycled except for consumption 
in the removal reaction. Nota bly, this process generates numerous by -products, resulting in 
substantial utility losses due to multiple separation steps. All by -products (including hydrocarbon 
pool Cn≤5, C6, C7, C8, PX, heavy oil and cyclohexane mixture) are considered in both LCA and TEA 
analysis, with carbon emissions and economic costs allocated proportionally based on the mass of 
each product (Table S78, S79, S82). The PET -to-PX route employs only 1,4 -dioxane as the solvent. 
After the reaction, the solution consists of 1,4 -dioxane and PX, with ben zene introduced as an 
azeotropic agent to accelerate separation. The recovered 1,4-dioxane is reused in subsequent cycles. 
The remaining benzene and PX are further separ ated to yield high -purity PX, while the separated 
benzene is also recycled. Likewise, the component of natural gas is captured as a valuable gaseous 
by-product and shares the carbon emissions and economic costs associated with the production 
process for th e target product PX. Specific yield and value assessments are detailed in Table S80, 
S81 and S83. Of course, our final LCA and TEA results are presented per kilogram of PX to precisely 
highlight the significant differences between the two routes when PX is  the target product. Utility 
consumption data from the simulation (Table S84, S85) are imported into Simpra for carbon 
emissions analysis and economic evaluation. In summary, the Aspen simulation provides 
comprehensive computational results, fully accounti ng for the handling of solvents, hydrogen, and 
by-products, and serves as a robust theoretical foundation for carbon emissions and economic 
assessments.” (See page 8 of the Support Information).
