Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Overall 
This manuscript presents an ultrafast optical imaging method that probes exciton transport in a monolayer WSe2 sensor
near twisted WS2 moiré heterostructures. By integrating femtosecond transient absorption microscopy, the authors visualize
spatial and temporal exciton dynamics with high resolution. They observe distinct modulation of exciton diffusion and
lifetime correlated with fractional electronic filling states in twisted WS2, suggesting that WSe2 1s exciton dynamics are
strongly affected by generalized Wigner crystal phases in adjacent layers. The experimental approach is innovative, and the
main findings are of interest for the research communities of both exciton transport and moiré-correlated systems. However,
additional supporting data needs to be provided in order for the data interpretation to be convincing. In the current state,
alternative explanations are just as viable to explain the results in this manuscript. Furthermore, additional devices and a
deeper understanding of the fractional correlated states should be provided. If these questions can be fully addressed by the
authors, I can recommend publication.

Major Comments 
1. Can the authors expand on the statement, “Typically, in regions with higher dielectric functions, Coulomb scattering is
suppressed, and dielectric disorder is reduced, leading to enhanced exciton transport”? Typically, higher dielectric functions
increase the number of charges, screening the exciton binding which increases exciton dissociation rates (shorter lifetimes).
The authors’ argument in this manuscript is counterintuitive to this. 
2. Related to the above, do the authors account for the doping in WSe2 due to the change in dielectric screening of the top
gate for correlated insulating states? Another explanation for the authors’ observations is that when the twisted WS2 forms
an insulating state, the top gate is no longer screened and can dope the WSe2 sensor layer. It is known that the existence of
charges in TMD layers will reduce lifetime and diffusion in the sample. Have the authors confirmed that the WSe2 layer
remains undoped in the Wigner crystal states and that the exciton diffusion is from the claimed dielectric disorder effect? At
high temperature when the correlated insulating states no longer exist, the diffusion seems to be unchanged regardless of
doping. This contradicts the authors’ claims that the diffusion changes for the metallic and non-metallic state. Can the
authors perform these measurements purely as a function of the bottom gate or in a monolayer device to isolate the doping
behavior? 
3. The authors do not provide any spectroscopic data on the WS2 layers, the WSe2 exciton or their behavior across various
fillings. Can the authors show this data so readers can relate the pump/probe wavelengths with the excitonic resonances in
the sample? Supplementary information on how they extract ΔR from this spectrum should also be included. 
4. The authors also claim their change in dielectric screening changes the binding energy. Do they observe this in the WSe2
A-exciton absorption/reflection spectrum as a function of the gate voltage? It will also be important for confirming that there
aren’t any trivial doping effects in the second question. 
5. From Fig. 2d, the exciton diffusion coefficient is strongly suppressed at filling factors 1/4, –1/4, 3/4, –3/4, 2/5, –2/5, 3/5, and
–3/5. Fig. 3 shows corresponding reductions in lifetime and diffusion length, and Fig. 4 indicates that 1/4, –1/4, 3/4, and –3/4
exhibit particularly high critical temperatures, implying unusual stability. Do the authors have a microscopic explanation for
why triangular lattice configurations could be stabilized at these commensurate fillings? 
6. On the electron-doped side, additional fractional fillings of 1/7 and 6/7 are observed, which are absent on the hole-doped
side. It would be important to comment on the possible origin of this electron–hole asymmetry. 
7. The discussion focuses on fractional fillings, but the trends at large |Vg| are not fully addressed. Given that the hBN gate
dielectric is ~48 nm thick, could higher gate voltages be applied to reach larger filling factors? If so, might additional
insulating states or other correlated phenomena be observed? 
8. Do the authors consider the path length difference when scanning the probe beam relative to the pump beam? Does this
affect the delay times measured? 
9. Reproducibility is always a concern in 2D materials studies. While I acknowledge the difficulty in creating these devices,
can the authors show consistent behavior in multiple devices?

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The work from Huan Liu et al. investigates the modulation of 1s exciton transport in a WSe2 monolayer leveraging on the
gate-tunable modifications of the dielectric environment, induced by the correlated electronic states in the neighboring moiré
superlattice of a twisted WS2 bilayer. This work delves into the current knowledge of Wigner crystals in moiré systems to
achieve an unconventional transport modulation, investigated by a pump-probe spectroscopy technique with spatial
resolution based on a galvo-scanning system. The device and the experimental technique are well designed, and the
conceptual advancements are intriguing, representing a timely feat within the current research on van der Waals
heterostructures. 
Nonetheless, there are technical points of relevance that are mentioned without a thorough explanation. Furthermore, the
scope of the text fades in several passages, drowning the impact of its advancements. At the current state, this work could be
considered for publication in Nature Communications only after major revisions. Hereafter, the authors can find a list of
points to be addressed in this regards: 
1)[Title] The terminology “ultrafast exciton transport” in the context of this work is misleading. We understand that an ultrafast
measurement method is used to probe exciton transport in the system under study. Nonetheless, 1s exciton transport per se
is not ultrafast, as all exciton diffusion processes are intrinsically not ultrafast phenomena. Any mention of “ultrafast transport”
throughout the text should be rephrased. 
2)[Results, Device design] “[…] Coulomb scattering is suppressed and dielectric disorder is reduced, leading to enhanced

exciton transport”. This is true only in the low density regime, and it does not stand as a general statement for all excitation
densities. This is detailed in ref. 49 and other works, such as [Zipfel, Jonas, et al. Physical Review B 101.11 (2020):
115430]. Even though the authors clearly state that they are working at low excitonic densities, they do so only after the
aforementioned sentence, in the following subsection of the Results. The authors should specify that they are working in the
low density regime before introducing the effect disorder has on exciton transport. 
3)[Results, Exciton transport in Wigner crystals] The title of this section is misleading, since the authors are not monitoring
exciton transport in Wigner crystals. Rather, as correctly described in other points of the main texts, the authors are
measuring the transport modifications of 1s excitons in the WSe2 sensor layer based on the changes in the dielectric
environment given by the tunable neighboring Wigner crystal. The same goes for the title of the caption of Figure 2.
Furthermore, the first sentence of this section (“We next investigate how exciton transport responds to varying electric fields
[…]”) should also be changed, since exciton transport is not responding to varying electric fields in general, but rather to field
responses to the modifications in the neighboring dielectric environment. Otherwise, the readers might think that even an
applied vertical dual-gate electric field could induce changes in the transport of 1s excitons in monolayer TMDCs − which is
not the case. A similar change should be made to the first sentence in the caption of Figure 2 (“a, Exciton transport under
varying electric fields […]”) and to the Conclusions section ([Conclusions] “In summary, we have visualized exciton transport
in generalized Wigner crystals […]”). 
4)[Results, Exciton transport in Wigner crystals] “In contrast to conventional electrical transport measurements, which often
suffer from large contact resistance at TMDC–metal interfaces, our approach offers a non-invasive a means to directly probe
correlated electronic states.” The formation of Wigner crystals probed via exciton sensing approaches has been widely
investigated in the literature, with several of such papers referenced by the authors themselves. Photoluminescence and
reflectance spectroscopy techniques are intrinsically non-invasive, and have been widely employed to probe correlated
electronic states. Thus, it would be preferable to avoid stressing the non-invasiveness of the method developed by the
authors with respect to electrical transport measurements, since this is not a prerogative of the authors’ study. The above
sentences should be rephrased accordingly, and the same should be done to other parts of the text following a similar
reasoning ([Exciton lifetime and diffusion length] “This enhanced sensitivity demonstrates the ability of our optical approach
to probe correlated electronic states with high precision.”; [Conclusions] “…a non-invasive, high-resolution, and highly
sensitive platform that bridges optical and transport measurements”). Furthermore, mentioning sensitivity as a metric without
quantitative estimates or quantitative comparisons should be avoided. 
5)[Results, Exciton lifetime and diffusion length] “[…] thereby reducing the dielectric screening of excitons in WSe2.
Consequently, the exciton binding energy increases, leading to a greater electron-hole wavefunction overlap and a shorter
exciton lifetime.” While this reasoning is sound and physically correct, the authors should present estimates to go along with
their arguments. Previous works, such as ref. 29, have detailed their modeling efforts regarding the effect of the changes
induced by the dielectric tensor from the moiré system on the binding energy of the excitonic sensor. While part of this
reasoning is mentioned in the Methods section of the present work, the authors should also show a model presenting the
relationship between the 1s exciton lifetime and the dielectric tensor modification. 
6)[Figure 3] In Figure 3c, two data bars should be present around Vg=-1V, according to their respective measurements in
Figure 3b and Figure 2d. Two bars should also be present close to Vg=-3.7 V. In other words, one data bar around each of
these gate voltages seems to be missing from Figure 3c. 
7)[Results, Temperature dependence] A detailed and clear explanation on how the critical temperature Tc is defined and
extracted from the experiments should be provided in the main text. Furthermore, temperature-dependent line plots at
specific filling factors of interest (as those in Figure 4b) should be displayed, as main Figures or Supplementary Figures, to
highlight the extraction of Tc itself. 
8)[Results, Temperature dependence] “Both effects contribute to enhance exciton transport […]” could be phrased more
accurately. In fact, rather than enhancing exciton transport, both factors help restoring the intrinsic diffusivity of the 1s exciton
in the WSe2 sensor, suppressing the effect of the neighboring moiré system. 
9)[Figure 4] What does the shaded area around the lines of Figure 4a represent? There are no details about this within the
respective caption. 
10)[Conclusions] “The technique achieves a spatial resolution of 50 nm, well below the diffraction limit, making it well-suited
for detecting spatial inhomogeneity.” This is true, but it does not constitute a point of novelty of the present work. There are
different scanning methods (as scanning APD, and any galvo-scanning system) that can reach low spatial resolution, and

Physical mechanism: dielectric screening and exciton energies Because the exciton electric field lines in monolayer WSe2 extend outside the layer, the sensor’s excitons are highly sensitive to the surrounding dielectric screening. For example, the response of the exciton resonance energy is explained by renormalization of the exciton binding energy Eb and the quasiparticle bandgap Eg by screening of the Coulomb interactions, with the exciton energy Eopt=Eg − Eb. When the moiré system evolves from a metal to a correlated insulating state, its electronic compressibility decreases and the effective dielectric permittivity experiences by WSe2 is reduced. The weaker screening simultaneously increases Eb (stronger electron–hole attraction) and partly restores Eg (weakened metallic gap-renormalization), thereby modifying Eopt. 
   Differential spectral responses of 1s and 2s excitons The net shift is set by the competition between ΔEg (bandgap recovery) and ΔEb (binding-energy increase). For the 1s exciton, these two contributions nearly cancel, leaving the 1s energy almost unchanged. In contrast, the energy of the 2s exciton is sensitive to the dielectric environment: although Eb increases when screening weakens, the recovery of Eg typically dominates such that Eopt=Eg − Eb >0. This yields a clear blueshift of the 2s resonance. Conversely, when the neighboring layer becomes metallic states, the 2s resonance redshifts. Therefore, the blueshift of the 2s exciton energy is employed as a probe of correlated electronic states (Nature 2020, 587, 214). These static spectra supply the baseline optical information requested by the reviewer and provide an internal cross-check of our interpretation: insulating states of the moiré bilayer appear as abrupt 2s blueshifts in the sensor, while the 1s energy changes only weakly, in line with prior exciton-sensing studies (Nature 2020, 587, 214; Nature Materials 2021, 20, 645; Nature Physics 2022, 18, 395). Our principal advance is complementary to static optics: by tracking 1s exciton diffusion in WSe2, we obtain a spatiotemporal probe of how the dielectric environment produced by the moiré bilayers evolves with filling. This observable transport remains highly sensitive to correlated insulating states even when the 1s resonance energy in static reflection shows only minimal shifts.

4. In page 4, paragraph 3, ‘This linear behavior reflects the role of the hBN spacer, which effectively suppresses the moiré potential in WSe2.’, the meaning of this sentence is unclear. Author reply: We thank the reviewer for pointing out this unclear statement. By “linear behavior” we mean that the variance (mean‑squared width) of the exciton transport profile in WSe2 increases linearly with the pump–probe delay, i.e., ∆𝜎!(𝑡)=4𝐷𝑡, which is the hallmark of ordinary (Fickian) diffusion. The key point is whether excitons in WSe2 feel the moiré potential of the twisted WS2 bilayers. In our experiment, the ~1 nm hBN spacer electrostatically screens the WS2 moiré potential from reaching the WSe2 layer. 3) Temperature dependence of the correlated insulating states In earlier versions, our wording may have caused readers to mistakenly interpret “metallic” and “non‑metallic” as referring to whether the WSe2 sensor is doped. To clarify, these terms describe the electronic phase of the WS₂ sample layer, not the doping level of the WSe2 sensor. As confirmed by the control experiments above, only the twisted WS2 sample is gated and doped, while the WSe2 sensor remains neutral. At low temperature, the WS2 moiré bilayer develops correlated insulating states at fractional fillings. In this regime, the electronic compressibility (∂n/∂μ) is strongly suppressed, causing the static dielectric screening of the WS2 layer to decrease. The reduced screening, together with the spatially varying local potential, produces stronger dielectric disorder. These effects lead to the observed suppression of exciton diffusion.  When the temperature increases, the correlated insulating states melt, and the WS2 bilayer becomes a compressible metallic phase across all gate voltages. In this metallic regime, the Thomas–Fermi compressibility (∂n/∂μ) remains nearly constant because ∂μ/∂n is constant for two-dimensional massive carriers (Nature 2020, 587, 214). This explains why exciton diffusion shows little dependence on gate voltage at high temperature. This temperature evolution is consistent with our model: the diffusion suppression arises from correlation‑driven dielectric screening and dielectric disorder in the WS2 layer, rather than any electrostatic doping of the WSe2 sensor. Author reply: We thank the reviewer for this insightful question. In moiré superlattices of two-dimensional materials, the reduced dielectric screening makes both nearest-neighbor and next-nearest-neighbor Coulomb interactions significant. These inter-site repulsive interactions drive electrons to arrange themselves into patterns that minimize the total Coulomb energy. As a result, at certain fractional fillings, the electrons spontaneously form periodic charge-ordered configurations that break the translational symmetry of the underlying moiré lattice, forming generalized Wigner-crystal states, as illustrated in Fig. R6 (for ν = 1/4, 3/4, 2/5, and 3/5). As temperature increases, electrons become thermally activated out of moiré potential minima, progressively randomizing the charge distribution. Because each fractional filling possesses a different correlation-energy scale, their ordered states melt at different temperatures, explaining the observed variation in critical temperature and exciton-transport suppression.

Author reply: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. We carefully examined whether scanning the probe relative to the fixed pump introduces a path‑length change that could shift the pump–probe delay time.
