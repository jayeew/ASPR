Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Zhang et al. describes the base-catalyzed polymerization of elemental sulfur with dithiols and diacrylates
to yield poly(ester disulfide)s. While the chemistry is potentially of interest, the work in its current form does not meet the
standards required for Nature Communications. Several central claims appear overstated, the analytical characterization
remains incomplete, and inconsistencies in the reporting of stoichiometry significantly undermine reproducibility. 
Given these limitations, I cannot recommend publication in Nature Communications. Instead, I suggest that the authors
consider a transfer to a more polymer-focused journal after substantial revision, which are mentioned below: 
Crucially, the description of atom economy is misleading. Only one sulfur atom from S
₈
 is incorporated into the repeat unit,
while the other seven are lost. By IUPAC definition, the process cannot be described as atom economical. A proper
calculation and a sulfur mass balance should be included, and the sustainability narrative must be tempered. More
generally, the frequent references to “green,” “environmentally benign,” and “closed-loop recycling” are not supported by
quantitative evidence such as E-factor, PMI, or energy/solvent consumption data. The use of MTBD, a costly and specialized
base, further undermines the sustainability and scalability argument. The synthesis of asymmetric disulfides is synthetically
valuable but relies on column chromatography, which is solvent-intensive and not aligned with green chemistry. 
The stoichiometry presentation is inconsistent between the main text and the experimental section. In the main text,
monomer ratios are given as 400:200:200, which clearly references sulfur atoms, while in the methods section 0.25 equiv of
S
₈
 relative to dithiol is reported. This discrepancy is confusing and must be resolved. The authors should explicitly state
whether equivalents are referenced to S atoms, S
₈
 molecules, or functional groups, and present the repeat-unit formula and
molar mass. DP
ₙ
 should be calculated accordingly and validated by absolute SEC methods and end-group analysis. 
The molecular-weight data are insufficiently supported. Mn values are reported based on SEC calibrated with polystyrene
standards, with dispersities up to 2.6. This cannot be considered reliable for absolute values. SEC-MALS or triple detection
is needed to substantiate chain lengths. Full SEC traces for each polymer (P1–P6) should be provided in the Supporting
Information, ideally with replicates to demonstrate reproducibility. End-group analysis would also strengthen the argument. 
Evidence for exclusive disulfide linkages remains incomplete. While ¹³C NMR, Raman, and MALDI data are consistent with
disulfides, they do not exclude polysulfide incorporation or unreacted sulfur. Elemental analysis (CHNS) and XPS (S 2p with
mapping) are required to confirm sulfur content and chemical state. Raman spectra should be compared with polysulfide
standards, and if possible, solid-state NMR or MS/MS fragmentation should be performed. What bothers me also that the
manuscript lacks sufficient specification of the Raman analysis. Experimental details (e.g., excitation wavelength, resolution,
acquisition parameters, and spectral assignment) are not provided, which makes it difficult to evaluate the quality and
reliability of the data presented. This omission further limits the reproducibility and robustness of the reported findings. 
The mechanistic role of PPNCl is attributed to accelerated sulfur cleavage, but no direct evidence is provided. Control
experiments with varying PPNCl loadings and alternative salts should be performed to confirm this effect and to demonstrate
that the approach is robust. 
The thermal and mechanical property data require more careful analysis. The reported Td,5% values of 248–281 °C indicate
only moderate stability, not “high thermal stability.” Td,5% and Td,10% should be reported alongside char yields at 600 °C in

a tabular form. The DSC data are also insufficiently reported. In the main text, it is not clear whether the traces correspond to
first or second heating cycles, and the displayed heating ranges differ between polymers, which complicates comparison.
This is particularly problematic because the first heating scan is critical for identifying residual sulfur. Without showing first-
heating traces, the claim of clean sulfur incorporation cannot be out-ruled. All scans must be provided, and Tg and Tm
values should be clearly assigned as taken from second heating. Consistency across polymers is necessary, and the
authors should justify the variable temperature ranges used. Mechanical properties are very modest, with tensile strengths
below 3 MPa and elongations varying widely. Averaged values with error bars (n ≥ 3) are required, as well as strain-rate
dependence and quantified cyclic hysteresis energy and recovery. Comparisons to known sulfur-containing elastomers
should be included to contextualize performance. 
The degradation experiments rely exclusively on DTT treatment of P2. This raises several questions. Why was P2 chosen
rather than demonstrating the effect across the series? Why was DTT selected as the reductant, when other reducing agents
such as glutathione or TCEP would be more relevant for biomedical or environmental claims? The products of degradation
are not characterized beyond SEC traces. CHNS, NMR, or XPS of the degraded material are necessary to confirm disulfide
cleavage and to exclude uncontrolled scission or polysulfide formation. Without re-polymerization of degradation products,
claims of “closed-loop recycling” are overstated. Kinetic fits (Mn vs. time) should be provided to quantify the degradation
process. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Zhang et al. reports a highly efficient strategy for the synthesis of poly(ester disulfide)s using elemental
sulfur as a key monomer. The work represents a significant conceptual and practical advance in the field of sustainable
polymer science. The authors develop an organobase-catalyzed, three-component step-growth polymerization at ambient
temperature. This methodology is not only novel but also remarkably scalable and atom-economical. The ability to achieve
high molecular weights (up to 42.0 kDa) with excellent yield under such mild conditions is a notable improvement over
existing harsh and limited synthetic routes for dynamic polymers. 
The work is comprehensive and compelling. The combination of mechanistic studies (including DFT calculations), precise
structural elucidation (MALDI-TOF, NMR), and thorough materials characterization (tunable thermal/mechanical properties,
programmable degradation) leaves little doubt about the validity and significance of the findings. The demonstration of
materials ranging from super-elastomers to high-strength semicrystalline polymers from a single versatile platform is
particularly impressive. 
This work transcends typical polymer chemistry studies by effectively bridging sustainable chemistry (valorization of S8
waste), mechanistic insight, and advanced material design with clear applications in biomedicine and recyclable materials. It
meets the high impact and broad interest standards of Nature Communications and should be published after minor
revisions. 
(1) On the wording regarding byproduct detection: The text states that possible byproducts “were not found from the crude
products by NMR analysis”. For greater scientific precision, please consider rephrasing this to state they “were not detected”
or “were below the detection limit of NMR spectroscopy”. 
(2) On the DFT calculation model: As the DFT calculations utilize a small-molecule model reaction, please add a brief
clarifying sentence in the text, such as: “The DFT calculations employed small-molecule analogues to model the key
mechanistic steps of the polymerization.” 
(3) Regarding the MALDI-TOF analysis: The manuscript states the MALDI-TOF results confirm the structure with n ≈ m. For
clarity, could you briefly specify in the text what the variables n and m represent in this context (e.g., n for symmetric disulfide
units, m for asymmetric units)? 
(4) On the long-term stability of elastic recovery: The exceptional elastic recovery is demonstrated over 10 cycles. Please
consider adding a short phrase like “without significant fatigue” or “with consistent performance” to succinctly describe the
stability observed over these cycles, as this is a critical performance metric. 
(5) The molecular weights of the synthesized polymers are relatively low. The authors should provide a detailed discussion
on this issue. 
(6) "How stable are polymers in air? Are they easily oxidized?" 
(7) Supporting Information: Given the substantial amount of valuable information provided in the Supporting Information, it is
recommended to include a Table of Contents at the beginning of the SI document. This will enhance navigability and allow
readers to quickly locate specific data, spectra, or methodological details, thereby improving the overall accessibility and
utility of the supplementary materials. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors report a very nice study on an elegant methodology for the selective utilization of sulfur in polymer synthesis. It
is highly topical and well executed. I suggest publication in Nature Communications after some revisions. 
My primary concern is the proposed mechanism shown in Figure 3. I find it highly unlikely that disulfide anions selectively
form and then undergo polycondensation. It seems much more plausible that the sulfur–sulfur bonds in the polymer can also
be attacked by sulfur-based anions, and that the number of sulfur centers therefore first forms a kinetic statistical distribution,
which then equilibrates to a thermodynamic minimum. One way to test this hypothesis—which the authors should include as

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

part of the revisions—is to perform the polymerization at higher sulfur loadings. Do oligosulfide linkages form? This could be
investigated by Raman spectroscopy. After polymer purification, addition of dithiol, acrylate, and catalyst (without additional
sulfur) should then yield polymers richer in disulfide linkages. If this is the case, the authors should revise the proposed
mechanism accordingly. 
As part of the above experiments, the authors should also perform reactions at higher sulfur loadings and comment on how
this affects the polymer properties. 
The authors should provide some mechanistic suggestion as to why PPNCl enhances molecular weights (rather than
selectivity or rate). 
The authors comment on the biodegradability of the ester units. It would be interesting to add degradation experiments of
these units. Is it possible to hydrolyze the esters under basic conditions while leaving the disulfide linkages intact? 
Also out of curiosity, the authors have now reported a series of beautiful polycondensation methodologies. Elsewhere, it is
well known that thiolate anions insert into sulfur faster than into other electrophilic reactants (e.g., CS
₂
; see, for example, Ref.
36 of the submission). What happens when another monomer is added that can insert into thiolates, forming an intermediate
which then inserts into acrylates? Do block polymers form?
