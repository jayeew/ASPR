Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Nagatsugi and co-workers investigated singlet oxygen-mediated photocatalytic oxidation of guanine in DNA duplex by
PAGE and MALDI-TOFMS analyses. They used simple self-complementary DNA (DD-DNA) having GC pair at the termini,
which was applied to the photo-oxidative lesions in the presence of photocatalyst. PAGE analyses revealed disappearance
of original DD-DNA and appearance of two groups of oxidative lesions, and Rose Bengal was the most active photo-
catalyst. TOFMS analyses could identify nearly all the photo-oxidative lesions, and generation of abasic (AP) site was
confirmed and quantitatively analyzed. Hot piperidine treatment revealed that AP sites were mainly generated at the terminal
G, and plausible pathway of AP site generation was predicted from DNA substituted with 8-oxoG. By varying the sequence
of DNA, importance of terminal G for AP site generation was further confirmed. They finally examined photocatalytic reaction
to G4 in the presence of K+ and Na+ and found that notable AP site generation was found in K+. 
The authors thoroughly examined photo-catalytic generation of AP site by MALDI-TOFMS. Since direct identification and
quantification of AP site by TOFMS may be reliable compared with other methods using enzymes, this manuscript is worth
publishing after addressing following concerns.

examined only after enzymatic digestion ... Consequently, this kind of AP site generation may have been overlooked in
previous studies”. This statement is much more important to motivate the investigation of oxidative damage. The authors
should move this part to the introduction, elaborate on it as a issue of previous works, and clearly position the significance of
their own study.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports a discovery in the field of DNA photochemistry, demonstrating that photocatalytic reactions can
generate AP sites as major lesions through guanine oxidation mediated by singlet oxygen. Using the well-characterized
Dickerson-Drew dodecamer DNA as a model system, the authors employ MALDI-TOF MS and denaturing PAGE to
quantitatively analyze the photocatalytic damage. The work reveals that highly solvent-accessible guanine residues,
particularly terminal guanines, are hotspots for AP site formation. Importantly, it suggests that this process occurs primarily
through an 8-oxoG-independent mechanism involving early oxidation intermediates. The study extends its findings beyond
duplex DNA, demonstrating AP site formation in RNA, single-stranded DNA, and G-quadruplex structures, suggesting the
broad applicability of this phenomenon. While guanine photooxidation has been extensively studied, the identification of AP
sites as major photocatalytic products represents a notable advancement that challenges the conventional focus on 8-oxoG,
Sp, and Gh lesions. 
The research addresses an important gap in our understanding of photocatalytic DNA damage mechanisms and has
significant implications for the design of photoresponsive nucleic acid probes and for photodynamic applications. The
experimental approach is methodologically sound, combining multiple photocatalysts, appropriate controls with ROS
scavengers, and mechanistic studies using 8-oxoG-modified oligonucleotides. The quantitative MALDI-TOF MS analysis
provides a novel and valuable approach for studying oxidative DNA lesions without the need for enzymatic digestion.
However, while the work presents clear evidence for AP site formation and establishes singlet oxygen as the primary
mediator, the proposed mechanism remains somewhat speculative, particularly regarding the exact intermediates involved
in the depurination process. The study's impact extends beyond fundamental understanding to practical applications in
nucleic acid biotechnology, where unintended AP site formation could affect the performance of photocatalyst-modified
probes. 
Overall, this work makes a valuable contribution to the field and merits publication, pending minor revisions that address the
following points. 
1. While the authors provide indirect evidence for the involvement of oxygen through experiments with singlet oxygen
scavengers and an endoperoxide reagent, direct anaerobic control experiments are notably absent. If AP site formation is
truly dependent on singlet oxygen (derived from O
₂
), then light irradiation under strictly oxygen-free conditions should
abrogate the generation of AP sites. This is a critical missing control that would definitively prove the oxygen-dependent
nature of the mechanism. The authors are strongly encouraged to include this experiment. 
2. The authors' investigation focuses exclusively on guanine oxidation. However, adenine is also susceptible to oxidative
damage. Given that this study establishes a novel singlet oxygen-mediated pathway for AP site formation from guanine, a
critical question arises: does this mechanism also apply to adenine residues? It is recommended that the authors investigate
whether adenine undergoes a similar photocatalytic depurination under these conditions, for instance, by studying
sequences that are devoid of guanine but contain adenine. 
3. A central and intriguing aspect of this work is the proposed pathway involving direct depurination from intermediates 1 or
2. The authors acknowledged that the precise chemical details of this mechanism, particularly the roles of these
intermediates and the specifics of N-glycosidic bond cleavage, remain to be fully resolved and will require future
investigation. However, the manuscript's conclusions would be substantially strengthened and rendered more definitive if
supported by initial corroborating evidence. Providing such evidence, for instance, through intermediate trapping
experiments or theoretical calculations to support the proposed depurination step, would elevate this key mechanistic insight
from a well-reasoned hypothesis to a more grounded conclusion, thereby greatly enhancing the impact of the present study.

strongly suggest that such abasic sites are produced through a yet unidentified type II mechanism involving singlet. 
The presented data are of interest for the scientific community since up to now only little attention has been focused on the
possible formation of abasic sites through type II photosensitization mechanism.

sites. The identification of the nature of the lesion is based on Maldi-tof analysis, and also indirectly, through hot piperidine

As Referee1 pointed out, our study focuses on the photocatalytic oxidative generation of AP

oligonucleotide, allowing for the direct and unambiguous identification of AP sites regardless 
of their optical properties. Therefore, the ab sence of prior reports is not due to the lack of 
reaction, but rather the limitations of the detection methods previously employed.

[Answer-2-3] We sincerely appreciate the Referee 2’s insightful suggestion regarding the 
mechanistic details of the depurination step from intermediates 1 or 2. 
While we agree that direct intermediate trapping or theoretical calculations would provide 
definitive evidence, obtaining such data is currently challenging. Instead, we would like to 
refine our hypothesis by drawing mechanistic insights from the established reactivity of singlet 
oxygen ( 1O2) analogues, such as 4 -methyl-1,2,4-triazoline-3,5-dione (MTAD) and 4 -phenyl-
1,2,4-triazoline-3,5-dione (PTAD), as reported by C. S. Foote et al. (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2002, 
124, 3905–3913). 
MTAD and PTAD are known to react with the imidazole ring of guanine to form [4+2] 
cycloaddition adducts—structural analogues to intermediate 1 —as transient species, which 
then undergo rearrangement to form stable products. Crucially, these reactions procee d 
without significant glycosidic bond cleavage or AP site formation. This lack of depurination in 
systems that mimic intermediate 1 provides a compelling chemical basis for our mechanism: 
if the [4+2] endoperoxide structure (intermediate 1) were the primar y cause of instability, 
similar depurination should be observed in the MTAD/PTAD systems. The absence of such 
cleavage suggests that intermediate 1 is likely not the branching point. Consequently, it is 
mechanistically more plausible that depurination orig inates from intermediate 2 (the 
hydroperoxide species) or its overoxidized derivatives. 
While this is an indirect observation, we believe this mechanistic analogy strengthens our 
proposal by logically narrowing down the potential reactive intermediates. We have added a 
discussion on this point to the revised manuscript. 
 
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION - Possible mechanisms for AP site generation under the 
photocatalytic reaction, 3rd paragraph 
To further refine this hypothesis, we compared our findings with the reported reactions of 1O2 
analogues, 4 -methyl-1,2,4-triazoline-3,5-dione (MTAD) and 4 -phenyl-1,2,4-triazoline-3,5-
dione (PTAD), with G derivatives.68 These dienophiles react with guanine to form transient 
[4+2] cycloaddition adducts equivalent to intermediate 1, which subsequently rearrange into 
stable 8-adducts. Importantly, no glycosidic bond cleavage or AP site formation has been 
reported in these reactions with  1O2 analogues. If depurination primarily originated from 
intermediate 1, similar glycosidic bond cleavage  would be expected in the MTAD/PTAD 
systems as well. The absence of such side products suggests that the depurination pathway 
in our system likely branches from intermediate 2—a highly reactive hydroperoxide species—
or its subsequent overoxidized species. Although the precise chemical details of this 
mechanism require further investigation, which is currently underway, this mechanistic 
analogy provides supportive evidence for our proposed pathway.

[Answer-3-3] We thank Referee3 for these important questions regarding our experimental 
conditions. We would like to clarify the rationale and the effects of 10% DMSO as follows: 
 
1. Rationale for using 10% DMSO: The 10% DMSO was utilized as a co-solvent to ensure 
the complete solubility of the organic photocatalysts and the various hydrophobic reagents 
used in this study (such as the specific ROS scavengers and the endoperoxide reagent). 
Maintaining this consisten t solvent system across the entire series of experiments was 
essential to accurately compare the results and mechanistic evaluations under uniform 
conditions. 
 
2. Structural integrity of the DNA duplex: We fully understand Referee3’s critical concern 
regarding whether the 10% DMSO might disrupt the duplex structure. To definitively 
address this point, we experimentally measured the UV melting temperature ( Tm) of the 
DD-DNA in our reaction buffer. The measured Tm of the DD-DNA (10 μM) in the absence 
of DMSO is 59 °C. We also attempted to measure the Tm in the presence of 10% DMSO; 
however, it could not be accurately determined under these exact conditions, probably due 
to physical artifacts such as microbubble formation in the mixed solv ent upon heating at 
elevated temperatures.  
Nevertheless, we can confidently assert that the oligonucleotides stably form a full duplex 
prior to the photoirradiation. Comprehensive reviews and recent single -molecule 
biophysical studies explicitly demonstrate that low concentrations of organic co -solvents, 
including 10% DMSO, do not significantly disrupt standard Watson -Crick base pairing or 
DNA mechanics (S. Nakano & N. Sugimoto, Biophys. Rev. 2016, 8, 11–23; K. R. Storm, 
et al. , Biophys. J.  2025, 124, 2639 -2654.). Furthermore, empirical thermodynamic 
evaluations have established that each 1% of DMSO decreases the Tm of an 
oligonucleotide by only 0.75 °C (N. von Ahsen, et al., Clin. Chem. 2001, 47, 1956-1961.). 
Even when accounting for this expected Tm reduction of ~7.5 °C caused by the 10% DMSO, 
the estimated Tm of our DD-DNA remains above 50 °C. This is overwhelmingly higher than 
the reaction temperature (0 °C). Thus, it is thermodynamically certain that the DD -DNA 
has already formed a stable double -stranded structure before being subjected to the 
photoreaction. 
 
3. Potential impact of DMSO on AP site generation: From a mechanistic perspective, it is 
well established in the literature that DMSO is chemically inert toward singlet oxygen (1O2). 
In fact, the lifetime of 1O2 in pure DMSO (~5.5 µs) is known to be slightly longer than that 
in pure water (~3.5 µs) (e.g., J. Varchola et al., J. Lumin. 2016, 177, 17–21; R. L. Jensen 
et al., J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2010, 132, 8098–8105). Thus, the presence of 10% DMSO does 
not shorten the lifetime of 1O2 or negatively impact the oxidation efficiency. Furthermore, 
owing to this chemical inertness toward 1O2, DMSO is often employed as a common co -
solvent in aqueous systems when investigating the reactions between singlet oxygen and 
nucleic acids. 
Because our experimental data (using NaN3 and argon purging) clearly demonstrate that 
the AP site generation in this system is a 1O2-dependent (Type II) process, there is no 
chemical basis to suspect that DMSO acts as a promoter or secondary sensitizer for this
