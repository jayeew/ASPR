Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Choung, Kang, and Kim et al. integrate biomimetic chemical synthesis, single cell RNA-seq, and
biochemical enzyme characterization to elucidate several key steps in the biosynthesis of medicinal securinega alkaloids
(SeAs), which are produced by Flueggea species and derive from the amino acids lysine and tyrosine. Prior to this work,
there was limited prior knowledge of biosynthetic pathway intermediates and only two SeA biosynthetic enzymes had been
characterized. To overcome this, and the lack of differential expression of biosynthetic genes across tissues, the authors
employ single-cell transcriptomics and uncover a cell cluster that seems to be enriched for the two known SeA biosynthetic
genes (FsPS and FsBBE) and lysine biosynthetic genes, suggesting that these cells may be specific to SeA biosynthesis.
Guided by the logic of chemical synthesis of SeAs and in vitro biochemical assays with lysate from Flueggea leaves, the
authors identify a ketoreductase enzyme (FsMS) that acts to produce a key precursor to scaffold formation (menisdaurilide),
as well as a pair of homologous sulfotransferases (FsNSST1/2) that appear to install sulfates to precursor molecules that
subsequently rearrange to form the characteristic scaffolds of the SeAs. 
The insights from this paper are novel and of broad importance to both natural product chemistry and biosynthesis, and the
paper is very well-written and easy to follow. The synergy of chemical synthesis, single cell RNA-seq, and traditional
biochemistry was particularly compelling. In general, the results are presented rigorously with proper controls and the figures
are very high quality. As described below, my main critiques are about the characterization of the sulfotransferases and the
discussion surrounding this, particularly regarding whether or not this enzyme has non-canonical function or if it actually
catalyzes scaffold rearrangement rather than it occurring spontaneously. My opinion is that minor edits are required prior to
publication. 
Major items 
• Despite the sulfotransferases (FsNSST1/2) being a central discovery of the manuscript and this reaction being the “first
report of a sulfotransferase involved in alkaloid scaffold remodeling”, not much biochemical characterization has been done
on these enzymes beyond in vitro assay and heterologous expression in N. benthamiana. Additional insight on the
biochemistry of these enzymes (described in detail below) is important for clarifying their role in biosynthesis, as well as their
proposed novelty. 
o The wording of the manuscript (e.g. in the Abstract and the Results, line 282-283, line 380-384, line 405-407) makes it
seem as though the sulfotransferase enzymes are responsible for the scaffold rearrangement, but the evidence that is
presented doesn’t necessarily demonstrate this. Rather, at first glance it would seem that NSST1/2 are canonical in their
catalytic activity and that the addition of a sulfate leads to the spontaneous (non-enzymatic) 1,2 amine shift, as demonstrated
by the biomimetic chemical synthesis. The low level (presumably non-specific) activity from g21405 to form allosecurinine
from virosine B would seem to support this – that is, if the enzyme can install a sulfate on the appropriate hydroxyl of virosine
B, then scaffold rearrangement will occur (a side note; in Fig 5g it looks like g21405 has very low activity on virosine A as
well?). Also, the fact that both NSST1 and NSST2 can act on either virosine A or virosine B – with presumably equal
efficiency - would suggest that they are just adding a sulfate; though it’s possible that the same NSST can catalyze scaffold
rearrangement on both products, this seems unlikely. More definitive experiments would need to be performed to show that
NSST1/2 catalyze more than just the addition of a sulfate. In the absence of more experiments, I don’t feel that these

enzymes should be labeled as “non-canonical” and the authors should be specific about the catalytic role of these enzymes
in SeA biosynthesis. 
o There is no identification of a sulfated intermediate, which would presumably be produced prior to the spontaneous 1,2
amine shift to form the SeA scaffolds over time. The fact that there is relatively low yield (34%) of securinine from chemical
sulfation of virosine B would suggest that some sulfated product is still present in the chemical reaction, and thus one would
expect there to be some amount of this after the presumed installation of the sulfate by NSST1/2. Can the authors expand on
the observation of 34% securinine yield from the O-sulfated intermediate (29) in the chemical synthesis highlighted in Fig.
5b? What percentage yield still exists as the O-sulfated intermediate (29)? In my opinion, it is important to try and detect this
molecule from the enzyme assay and/or tobacco expression (e.g. in ref 68, the authors are able to detect a sulfated
intermediate in withanolide biosynthesis, despite the spontaneous loss of this functional group). If this product is detectable,
it would be good to include structural information (e.g. MS/MS fragmentation; or can it be produced in sufficient quantities
synthetically to provide a standard?) to support the biosynthetic proposal. Though the canonical (sulfate-installing) activity of
NSST1/2 is likely, the only evidence of this is the requirement of PAPS as a cofactor, so the detection of a sulfated
intermediate would be an important experiment to verify the role of these enzymes. 
o Similarly, can NSST1/2 act directly on the sulfated intermediate (29)? That is, do they significantly accelerate the
spontaneous decay of this molecule (supporting a non-canonical function)? 
o Are NSST1 & NSST2 specific to virosine A/B as substrate or could they act on other hydroxyl-containing molecules – e.g.
menisdaurilide or the “diol” premenisdaurilide (4)? 
o Considering the difference in Spearman correlation value of FsNSST1 and FsNSST2 to FsPS, these two
sulfotransferases seem to have different expression profiles. Could the authors discuss the duplicated function of these
sulfotransferases? Are they redundant, or is there some substrate (or cell-type) specificity for these enzymes? 
o Are NSST1 and NSST2 homologs? How similar are they (%identify)? Tandem duplicates on the genome, different
alleles? A bit more info on these enzymes – particularly given their novelty would be useful. 
o Could the authors comment about the inconsistency of result between Fig. 5g and Fig. 5h? NSST1 has higher activity for
converting (–)-virosine A to allosecurinine compared to NSST2 in vitro, but lower when expressed in N. benthamiana.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Choung et al. elucidates the biosynthetic pathway of securinine alkaloids (SeAs) in Flueggea suffruticosa
through an innovative integration of chemically guided approaches and single-cell transcriptomics (scRNA-seq). A key
achievement is the identification of a vasculature-associated cell cluster (Cluster 7) exhibiting enriched expression of SeA
biosynthetic genes (FsPS, FsMS, FsNSST1/2), sulfate assimilation pathways, and jasmonate signaling components. This
high-resolution approach successfully overcomes the limitations of bulk transcriptomics in resolving spatially restricted
metabolic pathways. The work underscores the power of interdisciplinary strategies to decipher complex alkaloid
biosynthesis and reveals the noncanonical role of sulfotransferases in scaffold remodeling. 
However, I have several concerns regarding the scRNA-seq analysis: 
1. The reported mapping rates (34.9–45.6% overall alignment, 32.8–42.9% exonic reads) are suboptimal for plant scRNA-
seq, where higher rates (>80% genome alignment and >60% exonic) are typically expected. This could stem from
incomplete genome annotation or high ribosomal RNA contamination, potentially omitting critical biosynthetic genes. The
authors should address whether low alignment rates biased cluster identification or marker gene detection. 
2. The Spearman correlation-based ranking of genes (using FsPS as bait) lacks robustness. A more rigorous approach (e.g.,
hdWGCNA or SCENIC) could identify co-expressed gene modules and regulatory networks, distinguishing true biosynthetic
partners from spurious correlations. Additionally, the top 80 correlated genes should be validated for Cluster 7-specific
expression, as co-expression alone does not guarantee spatial colocalization. 
3. While the manuscript references "a reference set of cell type marker genes," the methods lack transparency. The
supplement should provide: 1) A full list of marker genes used for annotation (e.g., orthologs from Arabidopsis or other
models). 2) Evidence supporting their specificity. 3) Metrics for cross-species marker gene transfer reliability (e.g., sequence
similarity thresholds). 
4. Replicate 1 (6,313 cells) and Replicate 2 (7,146 cells) show disparities in median genes/cell (1,425 vs. 1,782) and
reads/cell (19,482 vs. 17,590). Were batch correction methods (e.g., Harmony or Seurat’s integration) applied?
Discrepancies may confound cluster interpretation. 
5. The study presents UMAP projections of F. suffruticosa leaf cells in both Figure 1b (schematic overview) and Figure 2b
(genome assembly context), but these plots appear discordant in cluster distribution and cell density. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Choung et al. adopted a highly innovative strategy that integrates biomimetic synthesis, stable isotope labeling, single-cell
transcriptomics, and functional enzymatic assays to systematically elucidate the biosynthetic pathway of Securinega
alkaloids (SeAs) in Flueggea suffruticosa. The authors successfully identified the key intermediate menisdaurilide and its
upstream reductase FsMS. More notably, they discovered for the first time that plant sulfotransferases (FsNSST1/2) mediate
a noncanonical scaffold remodeling step, converting neosecurinane to securinane through a 1,2-amine shift. This represents
the first reported involvement of sulfotransferases in core scaffold rearrangement during plant alkaloid biosynthesis. The
study is well-designed, methodologically rigorous, and supported by extensive in vitro and in planta evidence. The proposed
approach of “chemically guided single-cell transcriptomics” offers a promising paradigm for decoding complex specialized
metabolism in non-model plants. 
However, several aspects require further clarification or improvement: 
1. The authors demonstrated that menisdaurilide and 1-piperideine could spontaneously form neosecurinane-type
intermediates under non-enzymatic conditions. However, it remains unclear whether enzymes participate in this process in
planta. It is recommended that the authors expand the discussion to consider whether enzyme-mediated or enzyme-
facilitated versions of this coupling might exist. 
2. What are the endogenous levels of allosecurinine and securinine in F. suffruticosa? Since NSST1 and NSST2 show

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

enzymatic activity toward both precursors, and the formation of (–)-virosine A/B is thought to be non-enzymatic, how does the
plant regulate the relative production and distribution of these two alkaloids in vivo? 
3. Inspired by chemical synthesis studies, the authors hypothesize that securinine biosynthesis proceeds through a sulfated
intermediate. Their chemical experiment shows that the O-sulfate intermediate yields 34% conversion to securinine under
pH 8. Have the authors evaluated whether pH affects the conversion efficiency? Exploring a range of pH values could
provide further insight into the biochemical plausibility of this step. 
4. Are the amino acid sequences of NSST1 and NSST2 highly similar? It would be informative to compare their conserved
domains, or to use AlphaFold or homology modeling to explain why they exhibit similar catalytic activity toward structurally
distinct substrates. Additionally, are there any functional differences between NSST1 and NSST2? Among the NSST
candidates, gene g21405 showed activity in converting (–)-virosine B to securinine but not (–)-virosine A to allosecurinine.
How does the protein structure of g21405 differ from NSST1/2? Is this due to lower catalytic efficiency, or does it indicate
stronger substrate specificity? 
5. In Figure 5g, NSST2 produced a higher yield of allosecurinine, yet in Figure 5h, NSST1 shows higher production. What
accounts for this discrepancy? Additionally, in Figures 5j and 5k, the peak areas for securinine differ by approximately
threefold between in vitro and in planta assays. Could the authors explain this large variation? 
6. In the enzyme assays of FsMS, both NADH and NADPH were tested as reducing cofactors. Figure 4c shows that FsMS
performs similarly with both cofactors, which is uncommon for plant reductases. It is recommended that the authors analyze
the protein structure of FsMS to investigate possible reasons for this cofactor promiscuity. 
7. The authors excluded 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate (4-HPP) as an upstream precursor via isotope tracing but did not propose
alternative routes. It is encouraged that the authors discuss possible biosynthetic scenarios from L-tyrosine to
premenisdaurilide and provide hypotheses or future directions to explore this unknown segment.

(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors combine biomimetic synthesis with single-cell transcriptomics in Flueggea suffruticosa to dissect
securinega alkaloid (SeA) biosynthesis. Stable isotope–feeding experiments help identify precursors and corresponding
enzyme classes. They propose that neosecurinanes, (–)-virosine A and (–)-virosine B, form via conjugation of 1-piperideine
with menisdaurilide. A key finding is that sulfotransferases, rather than acting only as tailoring enzymes, catalyze a scaffold-

identify a precursor and biosynthetic gene for menisdaurilide. Overall, the work showcases a strategy of integrating chemical
logic with single-cell omics to reveal cryptic steps in complex SeA alkaloid pathways, leading to the discovery of a new
ketoreductase and a sulfotranferase involved in SeA pathway. 
The paper is well executed, supported by robust data, especially for the novel enzymatic chemistry, and will make a
significant contribution to the field of alkaloid biosynthesis. A final experiment to reconstruct the whole pathway to securinine
from premenisdaurilide and 1-piperdeine using FsMs and FsNSST to show that the whole pathway to
allosecurine/securinine is possible in planta, including the two non-enzymatic steps scaffold formation to make virosine A
and B and the spontaneous 1,2 amine shift.

Our response: We thank the reviewer for this careful and important comment. We agree that, without further 
evidence, it is not proper to label these enzymes as ‘non -canonical’. We also agree that our original wording 
could be interpreted as implying that FsNSST1/2 directly catalyze the scaffold rearrangement step. To address

see be low). Thus, we believe it is inconclusive to argue Fs NSST1/2 act as non -canonical sulfotransferases 
catalyzing 1,2-amine shift. 
   Based on these results, we have revised the text to clarify the catalytic role of NSSTs: Fs NSST1/2 catalyze 
sulfate transfer to (–)-virosine A and B, and this sulfation step in turn enables the subsequent 1,2- amine shift.

plant single cell experiment. Specifically, we  
(1) re-evaluated genome annotation quality;  
(2) reprocessed the data using an alternative alignment pipeline;  
(3) quantified the contribution of rRNA and organelle-derived RNA contamination to the reduced mapping

(1) Re-evaluation of genome annotation quality 
   First, to determine whether the low mapping rates originated from suboptimal genome annotation quality,

the same genome and annotation used for the single-cell analysis have been mapped using published bulk RNA-
seq data (Jiao et al., 2022; PRJCA007529). This analysis yielded a mapping rate of 90%, indicating that the

a standardized workflow involving aligning bulk RNA-seq data from various tissues to the soft-masked genome.

(2) Reprocessing the data using an alternative alignment pipeline  
   Secondly, the choice of alignment tool was examined. In a notable study of plant metabolism at the single -
cell level (Li et al., 2023), STARsolo was used, whereas our original pipeline employed Cell Ranger. Following 
the approach of that study, we reprocessed our data with STARsolo using the same parameter settings. This led

(3) Quantification of rRNA and organelle-derived RNA contamination 
  Thirdly, we assessed whether rRNA and organelle -derived RNA contamination could account for the 
suboptimal genome alignment rates. Guided by the QC framework proposed by the Bert De Rybel group, we 
quantified contamination by mapping raw reads to (i) nuc lear rRNA loci predicted from the F. suffruticosa 
genome, (ii) chloroplast rRNA and the full chloroplast genome, and (iii) mitochondrial rRNA and the full 
mitochondrial genome, and then estimating a non-redundant union fraction. Across the two 10x datasets, nuclear 
rRNA comprised ~10–13% of reads, chloroplast rRNA ~8–10%, and total chloroplast-derived reads ~12–13%, 
whereas mitochondrial-derived reads were minor (<0.4% with the closest available reference); the redundancy-
removed union contamination therefore reached ~18–21% (Supplementary Table 6). These levels are 
higher than those typically reported for optimized Arabidopsis scRNA -seq and indicate that rRNA/organellar 
carryover is a major plant -specific bottleneck in our datasets, providing a direct explanation for the reduced 
genome alignment despite adequate annotation.
