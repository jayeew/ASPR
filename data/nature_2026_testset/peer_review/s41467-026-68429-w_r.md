Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, Zhang et al. reported light-driven restructuring generated nano-island alloy catalyst NiIr/TiO2-LNi, which
demonstrated excellent catalytic activity during photothermal methane dry reforming reaction. The authors carefully studied
the catalytic reaction process, the role of each component, and the reaction mechanism by combining in situ
characterization, theoretical calculations and controlled experiments under different conditions. However, some of the key
conclusions lacks of solid evidence, and the necessary experiment must be supplemented before accepted. Here are
several issues that should be concerned: 
1. The authors provides the completely opposite conclusion in the Supplementary information and main text. In the
manuscript, the conclusion was that “the reactant gases are non-essential for NiIr alloying”, while the Supplementary
information in Note 6 demonstrated that “reactant gases are dispensable for alloy formation”. From the author's argument, it
seems that the conclusion of SI might have been written wrongly. If the increase in activity is caused by light-induced
structural reorganization, then will the material excited by light be able to enhance its activity when used in a thermal
catalytic reaction? 
2. In the conclusion that "Under light excitation, Ox-NiIr/TiO2 undergoes interfacial charge oscillations…This triggers the
upward migration of Ir atoms, leading to the formation of NiIr nano-island alloys", the key evidence is lost. The author merely
discovered through XPS and XAS that there was an electron transfer process of Ni-ir under light conditions, but this does not
necessarily imply the formation of a nano-island structure. Furthermore, before the illumination, what is the state of the Ir
atoms? And how does the illumination cause the migration of the Ir atoms? 
3. The results of PL and EIS tests show that Ir/TiO2 exhibits the best ligh-induced charge separation ability, while
NiIr0.5/TiO2 shows the strongest current intensity in i-t curves. This seems to be contradictory, and the more detailed
explanation should be included. 
4. When the Ni/Ir ratio of NiIr/TiO2 is larger than 0.5, why does the H2/CO ratio increase while the total yield of the product
decreases? 
5. The evidence of the direction of electron transfer is insufficient, the XPS results of Ti and O orbits should be included to
prove the electron transfer pathway of O-Ni-Ir. Moreover, it is generally considered that the appearance of completely
overlapping peaks in XPS spectra is incorrect. In this manuscript, many such cases occur in the NAP-XPS spectra. 
6. The fonts in all charts are too small, compromising readability. We recommend increasing the font size. Additionally, some
figures present excessive content, making them challenging to interpret; for instance, Figure 1d could be simplified. Finally,
Figure 4g appears to lack necessary annotations, which should be added for clarity. 
7. Why does the photoelectric effect disappear when the light intensity larger than 3.89 W/cm2, and how to distinguish the
contribution of photoelectric effect and photothermal effect? And how is the TAVG (average surface temperature of the
catalyst) in Figure 1d calculated? 
8. There are some conclusions without evidence or reference in the manuscript, such as “Activity surged above a threshold
light intensity of 2 W cm-2, marking the onset of photoelectric-photothermal synergy (Fig. 1d)”, and “Beyond 4.27 W cm-2,
photon absorption saturation redirected excess energy into thermal pathways, diminishing the per-unit-light efficiency
despite continued temperature increases (TCenter > 600 °C).” There is no reference or experimental evidence to support
these conclusions. 
9. The light intensity and temperature in the monometallic control experiment should be included. Under the same

10. The explanation for Figure 1 f is too simplistic. Under the full-spectrum condition, both the Ir and Ni surfaces can be
excited by light to generate hot electrons. Moreover, Fig 1f shows Ir has a stronger light absorption ability to than Ni. Will the
LSPR effect on Ir trigger the transfer of hot electrons to Ni?

excited state exhibits a smaller Sr index, directly indicating superior charge separation", it seems that "Sr" here might be
written wrongly. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper investigates the structural dynamic evolution of NiIr/TiO2 under light irradiation, which represents a critical
challenge in light-driven catalysis. Furthermore, the NiIr/TiO2 system demonstrates comprehensively favorable efficiency in
the dry reforming of methane reaction, with its anti-coking mechanism thoroughly explained. The authors’ elegant
experimental design using condensation equipment and light intensity thresholds to deconvolute photoelectric and thermal
effects is methodologically sound and mechanistically informative. Overall, this work is innovative, and the experimental
results sufficiently support the conclusions. Therefore, I recommend this paper for acceptance in Nature Communications
after the following revisions. 
1. In the catalyst preparation section, the authors state: "By conducting the adsorption process in an aprotic solvent and
introducing an anionic Ir precursor complexed with quaternary ammonium, we minimized competitive adsorption of Ni and Ir
and effectively reduced metal leaching." Please clarify the specific role of the quaternary ammonium in this process. Explain
why IrCl3 could not be used directly for targeted adsorption instead of the complexed precursor. If this strategy effectively
overcomes the drawbacks of conventional co-impregnation methods, its clear explanation will provide valuable guidance for
future synthesis. 
2. The authors provide a detailed discussion of the morphology and structure of TiO2-supported partially oxidized NiIr
nanoclusters (Ox-NiIr/TiO2) in the Supplementary Information, demonstrating Ni-Ir interactions while maintaining their
oxidized states via the cationic-anionic pair adsorption strategy. This information is fundamental for understanding the
subsequent structural dynamic transformations. Please consider moving this discussion to the main text if space permits. 
3. The accuracy of temperature measurement is critical for understanding photothermal effects. The authors used a
thermocouple to monitor the catalyst temperature, but significant thermal resistance between the gas phase and the
thermocouple may lead to inaccurate readings, as noted. It is necessary to clarify the detailed setup of temperature
measurement (e.g., thermocouple position, calibration methods) or explain how potential thermal resistance effects were
mitigated. Additionally, whether complementary methods such as infrared thermography were used to verify the measured
temperatures should be addressed. 
4. In Fig. 1f, the UV-VIS-NIR spectrum shows an absorption intensity of ~1.35 in the ultraviolet region, which theoretically
should be below 1 for diffuse reflectance spectra. The authors need to explain this discrepancy, including details of the
measurement parameters (e.g., baseline correction, sample preparation) that might lead to this result. Furthermore, the
experimental setup and operational details of the monochromatic light activity tests (marked by red dots) should be
elaborated. 
5. The oxidation state of the catalyst under light-off conditions is a concern. The authors mention that the catalyst contacts air
when the light is turned off, and Ni and Ir are prone to oxidation. However, the in situ XAS results (Fig. 2a and b) indicate
non-negligible Ni-O and Ir-O signals even under illumination, suggesting that oxidized states coexist with metallic states. It is
necessary to discuss whether the reaction mechanism changes when the catalyst is in an oxidized state, especially during
light-off periods. 
6. The caption for Supplementary Fig. 9 requires verification to ensure it accurately corresponds to the labels within the
figure itself, facilitating reader comprehension. Fig. 4g is missing its caption. The authors need to perform a thorough check
of all figure caption information.

Reply: We sincerely thank the reviewer for this valuable comment, which has helped us further 
clarify and improve the manuscript. 
(1) Regarding the state of Ir and Ni atoms on the TiO2 surface before illumination: 
During sample preparation, our directional adsorption strategy ensured preferential Ir anchoring 
onto pre-adsorbed Ni sites, guaranteeing a certain degree of mutual contact between Ni and Ir atoms 
in the initial state. This has been confirmed by filtered and color-coded HAADF-STEM images (Fig. 
1c-d, Supplementary Fig. 5b -c in the revised version), 3D surface plots (Fig. 1f in the revised 
version), EDS line scans (Fig. 1g in the revised version), and elemental mapping (Fig. 1h and 
Supplementary Fig. 5d -i in the revised version). However, despite this contact, a complete alloy 
structure between Ni and Ir was not fully formed initially. Instead, the NiIr nanoclusters exhibited 
a partially oxidized state, with electronic modulation occurring between Ni and Ir at oms (as 
evidenced by EXAFS and CO -probe DRIFTS spectra in Fig. 1i -j and Supplementary Fig. 7 in the 
revised version). This indicates that both Ni and Ir atoms had strong interactions with the oxygen 
atoms on the TiO2 support surface. Therefore, before illumination, the state of both Ir and Ni atoms 
was predominantly oxidized. While there was some interaction between Ni and Ir, this mutual 
interaction was weaker than their respective interactions with the TiO2 support. 
(2) Concerning the evidence for nano-island alloy formation under light conditions: 
In situ X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) is a powerful technique that directly probes the 
local atomic structure and coordination environment around specific elements. By analyzing the 
EXAFS region, we can directly identify the types and numbers of neighboring atoms and their bond 
distances, providing direct evidence for structural changes, including alloy formation. In situ Near-
Ambient Pressure XPS (NAP-XPS) analysis is a surface-sensitive technique that monitors changes 
in the chemical states and elemental composition of surfaces under realistic reaction conditions. It 
serves as a complementary technique to in situ XAS by providing evidence of electronic transfer 
and chemical state evolution.

The section of "Light-induced dynamic restructuring to form NiIr nano -island alloy on TiO2" in 
the manuscript provides a detailed comparative analysis of the structural evolution of NiIr/TiO 2 
under both light irradiation and heating.  Under light irradiation, Ir L3-edge EXAFS spectra  of 
NiIr0.5/TiO2 (Fig. 3a in the revised version ) show a dominant Ir-Ni scattering peak at 2.18 Å , 
replacing the initial Ir -O coordination observed in the dark. Concurrently, Ni K -edge EXAFS of 
NiIr0.5/TiO2 (Fig. 3b in the revised version ) retains Ni -O (1.60 Å) and Ni -Ni (2.09 Å) scattering 
alongside emerging Ni-Ir (2.18 Å) coordination, indicating alloy formation without complete Ni 
reduction. Quantitative EXAFS fitting analysis (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 19-20, Supplementary 
Tables 4 -5 in the revised version) quantifies this structural evolution. Under light irradiation , 
NiIr0.5/TiO2 exhibits nearly 100% Ir-Ni coordination at the Ir L 3-edge and 32% Ir-Ni coordination 
at the Ni K -edge, alongside 37% Ni-O interfacial bonds. This confirms that Ir in the alloy (red 
ball in above figure) phase does not directly interact with TiO2, while Ni maintains interfacial 
Ni-O bonds  (green ball in above figure) , forming a nano -island architecture . The TiO 2 
substrate serves as the “sea” with Ni atoms bonded to TiO2 forming the “island” base (green 
ball), while the Ni-Ir alloy located on top of the island does not come into direct contact with 
the “sea”. In other words, the absence of Ir -O bonds confirms that  Ir does not come into 
contact with the “sea” (TiO2) and therefore remains confined to the “island.” Only such a 
nano-island alloy configuration ensures nearly 100% Ir-Ni coordination together with partial 
Ni-O coordination corresponding to oxidized Ni species. This specific coordination environment 
is direct evidence for the formation of NiIr nano -island architecture under light irradiation.  In 
contrast, under dark thermal conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2 8 in the revised version), thermal 
catalysis induces limited Ni or Ir reduction (Ir0/Irδ⁺=0.61 at 500 ℃ vs. 1.54 under unfocused light) 
and fails to form obvious Ni-Ir bonds. This intuitively demonstrates that light irradiation is essential 
for promoting the formation of the NiIr nano-island alloy. Furthermore, re-oxidation in post-EXAFS 
(Supplementary Fig. 27 and Note 4 ) and reversible NAP -XPS trends confirm that NiIr/TiO2-LNi 
exists transiently only under light irradiation. These analyses provide factual evidence that Ox -
NiIr/TiO2 undergoes a structural transformation under light irradiation, leading to the 
formation of a NiIr nano-island alloy. 
(3) Regarding the mechanism of how illumination causes Ir atom migration and the feasibility of 
NiIr nano-island alloy formation: 
Firstly, we have performed DFT calculations to rationalize the formation of the NiIr nano-island 
alloy. As discussed in the manuscript: “We further constructed DFT structures (Supplementary Fig. 
33) for a series of samples based on the above analysis and actual Ni/Ir atomic  ratios. Fig. 3g 
confirms NiIr/TiO2-LNi’s stability with a lower formation energy than the hypothetical Ir -linked 
model (NiIr/TiO 2-LIr), where Ir -O bonds elongate ( black arrow indication, >3.0 Å), 
destabilizing the interface.” Electron-density distributions (Supplementary Fig. 40a-d and Note 8) 
indicate Ni-mediated charge polarization enriches electrons at Ir sites . Simultaneously, density-of-
states profiles (Supplementary Fig. 40e-h) reveal that NiIr alloy-induced d-band center modulation 
optimizes the trade -off between adsorption strength and intermediate desorption kinetics, 
suppressing both carbon deposition and parasitic reactions. Time-dependent DFT simulations (Fig. 
4i) further identified excitation-dependent charge redistribution on NiIr/TiO2-LNi. 
Secondly, we added relevant literature in the revised version to strengthen the theoretical 
foundation. Numerous works have studied the structural features of bimetallic nanoclusters via 
theoretical and/or experimental approaches. For instance, according to DFT, Au atoms in bimetallic

AumAgn clusters (2≤m+n≤8) tend to occupy the surface sites because of the charge transfer from 
Ag to Au, which is consistent with the experimental observation with bimetallic Au mAgn (m+n = 
34) nanoclusters. (J. Chem. Phys. 125, 014303 (2006); Nat. Commun. 11, 2229 (2020); Chem. Rev. 
123, 4855 -4933 (2023) ) These references demonstrate that wh en electronic transfer 
interactions exist in bimetallic nanoclusters, one metal would preferentially occupy more 
surface sites.  
More importantly, under light irradiation, prior studies have demonstrated that photoexcited 
electrons can weaken the intrinsic  metal–oxygen (M–O) bonds at metal/oxide interfaces, thereby 
altering surface structural sites (Nat. Catal. 6, 519–530 (2023)). For example, Wang et al. reported 
that the introduction of Mn induces charge transfer from La to Mn; the Mn centers capture a fraction 
of the photoexcited electrons, lowering the Mn valence state and reorganizing the perovskite 
electronic structure, which in turn weakens the La –O–Mn linkages ( J. Am. Chem. Soc. 147(17), 
14705–14714 (2025)). More recently, Prof. Xiong described a l ight-induced dissolution –
reprecipitation process using high-entropy oxides (HEOs) as supports (Adv. Mater. 37(21), 2500928 
(2025)), wherein illumination weakens metal–O bonds in the HEO, leading to Co and Ni leaching 
and subsequent alloy formation. Taken together, these results strongly corroborate our finding that 
light-induced charge transfer weakens metal -O bonding. In our work, before illumination both Ir 
and Ni interact strongly with the TiO2 support, exhibiting partial Ir–O and Ni–O coordination. Upon 
irradiation, electron transfer from the support to the metals similarly weakens these metal–O bonds, 
as confirmed by our original characterizations and the additional computations below , thereby 
facilitating the formation of the IrNi alloy.  
Moreover, Gong et al.  utilizing XAFS, XPS, and DFT analysis, explained that introducing Sn 
into a PtCu alloy can extract Pt atoms to the alloy surface. Addition of Sn to the alloy led to the 
formation of surface Pt 1Sn dimers. The larger atomic radius of Sn compare d to Cu drove it to the 
surface, and its stronger interactions with Pt abstracted it from the bulk, ultimately achieving 
efficient surface exposure of Pt atoms  to reduce the total energy of system ( Science, eadw3053 
(2025). DOI:10.1126/science.adw3053). In our system, the atomic radius of I r is also larger than 
that of Ni. Under light -induced electron transfer from Ni to Ir, the radius disparity between Ir and 
Ni is exacerbated (atomic radius: Ir0 > Irδ⁺ > Niδ⁺), thereby promoting the surface exposure of Ir and 
consequently reducing the overall energy of the system . This thermodynamic stability induced by 
the atomic radius difference facilitates the extraction of the larger Ir atoms to the surface during 
alloy formation following metal –O bond cleavage. Consequ ently, Ir atoms are preferentially 
distributed on the surface of the nanoislands rather than at their bottom. This observation is 
consistent with our experimental results, where the absence of Ir –O bonds under illumination 
confirms that Ir does not reside at the bottom of the Ir–Ni alloy nanoislands. 
Finally, to further elucidate the structural rearrangements induced by photoexcitation, we 
performed comparative calculations of chemical bond strength variations between the excited and 
ground states using our  original DFT model. It is worth noting that the recently published article 
(Science, eadw3053 (2025). DOI:10.1126/science.adw3053 ), the authors used the differences in 
chemical bond energies as key evidence to demonstrate bond reconstruction, thereby validating the 
atomic extraction process and the accompanying structural transformation. As shown in 
Supplementary Table 6 of the revised manuscript, the bond energies of Ir-Ni, Ir-O, and Ni-O in the 
excited state are all lower than those in the ground state. This arises from bond weakening caused 
by photoinduced charge transfer, consistent with the aforementioned literatures. In concert with

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

thermal effects, the photoexcited state thus facilitates atomic rearrangement toward a more stable 
structural configurat ion. A detailed comparison reveals that in the O x-NiIr/TiO2 structure, the 
difference in Ir-O bond energy between the excited state and the ground state is larger  (-28.06 kJ 
mol-1), while the difference for the Ni-O (-9.36 kJ mol-1) bond is smaller. This indicates that under 
photoexcitation, the Ir-O bond becomes more unstable and is weakened more significantly, 
thus it preferentially breaks, facilitating Ir migration.  In addition , simulations under 
photoexcitation show that the Ni -Ir bond strength (92.35 k J mol -1) in the NiIr/TiO2-LNi alloy 
structure in the excited state is more stable than the Ir-O bond strength (69.64 kJ mol-1) in the excited 
state of the Ox-NiIr/TiO2 structure. This provides supporting evidence for the formation of the 
Ni-Ir alloy under light irradiation.
