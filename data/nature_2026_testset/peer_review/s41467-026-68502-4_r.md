Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The work describes a new kind of a cageable click to release system using cyclooctynes that react with caged
hydroxylamines. In general, bioorthogonal chemistry can suffer from off target reactions, and so mechanisms to control
where such reactions occur are needed. The work is thorough and should be of interest to the wider community. However, I
found several issues that need to be addressed before being reconsidered for publication. 
A major issue is the lack of certain controls. The mechanism of the release strategy relies initially on a nucleophilic attack of
the COT by the hydroxylamine. This necessarily makes one question the biorthogonality and whether COT or the many COT
derivatives react with biological nucleophiles like amines and especially thiols. Its actually well known that cyclooctynes can
react with thiols and this leads to side reactions. Thus, it would be important for the authors to rigorously show how their
caged COT derivatives 
respond to high concentrations of thiols and amines in physiologically relevant buffer conditions. 
The imaging in Figure 5a and 5d can be of higher quality and could be magnified to make the details clearer. As it is now, its
extremely unclear and blurry. 
In the conclusions the authors should discuss the drawbacks and potential future direction for improvements of their
technology. One drawback is the rate of reaction, which is lower than the faster tetrazine/TCO click to release reactions.
Also, the use of very strong nucleophiles like hydroxylamines might have drawbacks in vivo as would the use of
cyclooctynes which are known to undergo side reactions with thiols. Some more discussion here would be good. There are
likely other areas for improvement that the authors may wish to discuss. 
Minor points: 
Regarding the statement “along with the methods reported by Devaraj and Pu that employ photocontrolled or enzymatic
deprotection of dihydrotetrazine (DHTz) precursors to generate Tz—have begun to surmount these challenges.” The
Devaraj lab has also described enzymatic uncaging in a preprint that was published before the work of Pu. I suggest the
authors cite this work as well. See: https://chemrxiv.org/engage/chemrxiv/article-details/65b6ca2e9138d23161ee5b4e 
Also, I’m not sure this is a fair comment: However, the necessity for the challenging synthesis and modification of tetrazines,
coupled with the inherent instability of tetrazines or DHTz themselves. . . clearly the author’s system will suffer from the same
challenges and unless the authors do a head to head comparison of stability, this statement be removed. I also will point out
that the references cited don’t necessarily support the authors claim, particularly that of the instability of DHTz’s which are
actually very stable structures in comparison to tetrazines.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
• What are the noteworthy results? 
In this manuscript, Guo, Zheng and coworkers describe a click-to-release reaction involving mono-alkyl-hydroxylamines and
cyclooctynes. The method builds on retro-Cope Elimination work from the labs of Kim, with modification of using a
secondary (rather than tertiary) hydroxylamine in combination with a cyclooctyne bearing a propargylic leaving group. 
The
cycloaddition is efficient provided sufficient concentrations of reactants, and release kinetics are rapid. The authors
characterize the kinetics (rates in the range k2 ~10E1), and demonstrate the release of varied payloads. Reactions are
demonstrated to be successful in vivo and under cell culture conditions. 
• Will the work be of significance to the field and related fields? How does it compare to the established literature? If the work
is not original, please provide relevant references. 
While the reaction chemistry is related to prior art, the addition of release chemistry to the retro-Cope reaction is an intriguing
addition that will be appreciated by the chemical biology community. 
Kinetics are a challenge for the described work, as they lag significantly behind the click-to-release methodologies involving
TCO and tetrazine, and we are left wondering what niche this new method will find for the field. The cellular experiments in
Figure 5 are described as “confirming intracellular activation”, but there is no evidence to whether the reactants are even cell
permeable, as reactions could take place extracellularly and deliver payload which subsequently becomes internalized.
Such “on cell” demonstrations are of limited value relative to demonstrations where one reactant is clearly localized to a
cellular target (e.g. via a covalent bond). In Figure 6, while it can be concluded that disulfide reduction takes place
intracellularly, there is no way to conclude that the cycloaddition takes place inside a cell. 
For the in vivo experiment
described in Fig 6c, there is no evidence that uncaging takes place with spatial control. 
The uncaging could take place
anywhere within the animal, followed by enhanced retention in cancerous tissue. 
This is a known effect for other dyes used
in NIR imaging (review: PMC10827512). 
In vivo studies appear to indicate that the combination of 6a + 5c is more effective than doxirubicin itself. This potentially
significant finding warrants additional study to higher statistical significance. 
The concluding statement from this section that
“These in vivo findings confirm that our GSH-triggered bioorthogonal click–release strategy effectively activates the prodrug
within the tumor microenvironment, leading to targeted cancer therapy with enhanced efficacy and minimal systemic toxicity”
needs to be reconsidered, as there is no evidence that the click reaction takes place at the tumor. 
Finally, the authors use light to uncage a hydroxylamine (6b), which can then be used to uncage a fluorophore in vitro, or
tetracaine on the plantar surface of rat paws. These uncaging experiments are successful, but it is unclear why the authors
pursue a double uncaging experiment, when tetracaine could have just been as easily have been directly caged and
photouncaged. 
Summary: I find this to be interesting chemistry. 
Applications are limited by modest reaction rates that are going to limit in
vivo and cellular applications. 
Biological experiments were performed “on cells” and in vivo at high concentrations without
hard evidence for spatial control. 
• Does the work support the conclusions and claims, or is additional evidence needed? 
yes 
• Are there any flaws in the data analysis, interpretation and conclusions? Do these prohibit publication or require revision? 
The major flaw, described above, relates to the interpretation of spatial control. 
• Is the methodology sound? Does the work meet the expected standards in your field? 
yes 
• Is there enough detail provided in the methods for the work to be reproduced? 
yes

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In a prior evaluation of this manuscript, it was noted that the manuscript describes interesting chemistry, but applications are
limited by modest reaction rates that will hinder in vivo and cellular applications. It was further noted that some biological
experiments were performed “on cells” while others were conducted in vivo at high concentrations without hard evidence for
spatial control. In this revised manuscript, the authors have partially addressed some of the concerns raised previously but
some questions remain. 
In their response, they do provide a brief comment on the rate limitation of the rate constant, but further opine that reaction
kinetics alone do not dictate the utility of a bioorthogonal reaction, especially in in vivo contexts where factors such as
stability, biocompatibility, and controllability are equally, if not more, critical.” 
While it is of course true that rate constant is not the only important parameter, it is hard to argue that it is not a critical
parameter, as bioorthogonal chemistry requiring millimolar concentrations of reagents introduces a host of limitations
(toxicity, background, washout in cellular experiments, clearance in animal experiments, etc). 
In response to a comment that it was not shown that reactions are taking place intracellularly, the authors used a
triphenylphosphonium-conjugated hydroxylamine derivative (compound 2j) to target the group to the mitochondria. 
While
this experiment suggests that the reaction occurs intracellularly, the authors should note that TPP targeting is non-covalent
and reversible, allowing for an extracellular reaction to be possible. In general, the use of covalent intracellular ligands (e.g.,
SnapTag or HaloTag) is necessary to prove intracellular reactivity. 
The images provided in the supporting information to
support this experiment are of low resolution; higher-resolution images should be provided. 
In the prior review, it was noted that there was insufficient evidence to demonstrate that the click reaction uncages the
prodrug at the tumor. 
In response, the authors carried out biodistribution comparing the prodrug system (6a + 5c) versus free
doxorubicin. 
The authors describe the findings as “strong pharmacokinetic evidence that the bioorthogonal click–release
reaction predominantly occurs at the tumor site”, but the data require further interpretation. 
In Fig S21, slightly higher
accumulation is actually observed for the Dox control at 2 hours, and there is no statistical difference at 24 h. At 4 and 8
hours, a statistical difference is observed (p value *), although the difference in tumor uptake is relatively modest. My view of
these data is that Dox accumulation is comparable for the uncaging system vs the control, with the benefit of lower
background in the release system. 
Finally, it was noted that photochemical uncaging experiments were successful; however, it is unclear why the authors
pursued a double uncaging experiment in animals when tetracaine could have been directly photouncaged more easily. 
The
authors discuss the need to “(1) Decouple activation from payload structure and (2) provide spatiotemporal hierarchy and
amplification... This separation between trigger and release enables precise spatial targeting and repeatable, on-demand
drug activation, as demonstrated in our local anesthesia model.” I do not find either of these responses especially
compelling. Coupling photochemistry to a kinetically slow seems disadvantaged relative to a simple photochemical
uncaging, and more reagents need to be synthesized in the dual uncaging approach. 
The authors point to signal
amplification, as they have described in Angew. Chem. for dihydrotetrazine/TCO chemistry, but it is not clear how that will
apply here, and the aforementioned kinetic limitations would seem to counter any advantages for the present system. 
As before, I find this to be an interesting study, but one that is ultimately more limited than current art in the field.

1. Intrinsically Triggerable Click –Release Without Need for Exogenous Reducing 
Agents.

Unlike N-oxide-based systems that rely on exogenous chemical triggers (e.g., diboron 
reagents or reducing environments), our click –release platform employs N -alkyl-
hydroxyl-enamine intermediates that undergo spontaneous β-elimination of the payload 
at the propargylic position under physiological conditions —no additional chemical 
activation is required. This property simplifies in vivo applications by eliminating 
reliance on systemically administered triggers, which may have limited bioavailability 
or cause undesired systemic effects. 
2. Modular Spatiotemporal Activation via Caging the Mono-Alkyl-Hydroxylamine. 
As described in(ACS Cent. Sci. 2021, 7, 631–640), hypoxia has been utilized within 
tumors to enable activation of hypoxia-responsive prodrugs and imaging agents. 
However, our approach expands upon this by allowing for spatiotemporally controlled 
reaction through the modular modification of the nitrogen atom of the hydroxylamine 
with a variety of stimuli-responsive groups (e.g., disulfide for redox-responsiveness, o-
nitrobenzyl for photocaging), thus broadening the scope of controllable conditions. This 
strategic design decouples the click reaction and subsequent release, ensuring the 
active process remains dormant unti l specifically triggered by a selected endogenous 
(e.g., high GSH) or exogenous (e.g., light) stimulus. This enables high adaptability 
across a wide range of biological environment. 
This dual-layered control (bioorthogonality + on -demand activation) is not  easily 
achievable with the N -oxide/diboron approach, where the reducing reagent is 
administered systemically and lacks spatial specificity. 
3. In Vivo Biocompatibility and Application Readiness. 
Our system exhibits excellent biological stability and safety: Mono-alkyl-
hydroxylamine derivatives exhibited no cytotoxicity even at 1 mM concentration  in 
cellular environments . Co-administration in a local anesthesia model revealed no 
observable toxicity or inflammation ; The click partners (COT derivatives) were 
demonstrated to be exceptionally stable toward biological nucleophiles (GSH, Cys), 
unlike conventional strained alkynes such as BCN, which undergo thiol addition side 
reactions. 
These features support its safe and selective use in complex in vivo envi ronments,
