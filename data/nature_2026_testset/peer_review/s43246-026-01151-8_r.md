Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors have created a conductive polymer based on PBTTT with a maximum thermal conductivity over 2-fold better
than cited literature. Moreover, electrochemical transistors were made in which said conductivity could be reversibly
modified by ~25%. This goes hand-in-hand with an ON/OFF ratio of 2 orders of magnitude in terms of electrical conductivity.
This presents a fundamental work which may open the field of thermal logical devices to conductive polymers. After minor
revisions the manuscript may be accepted for publication. 
I would like the authors to address the following points before suggesting the manuscript for publication: 
1. The authors show tremendous advances, yet tell very little on the mechanism of how this works. I would suggest XRD or
GIWAXS measurements to elucidate any structural effects. Alternatively, the diffraction pattern in TEM may be used to
visualize the change before and after doping. 
2. Table S2 shows relevant data to the reader- would it not be better to move it to the main text body? 
3. What is the reason TFSI was used as the doping agent and not another doping agent, such as sulphate or hydrogen
sulphate? (10.14314/polimery.2020.9.6 or 10.1039/d5ta01145k) 
4. How does the repetition of the doping process affect crystallinity? XRD-based techniques such as GIWAXS may
significantly improve the manuscript. This may further elucidate if the hypothesized edge-on orientation is preserved. 
5. Since a transistor geometry was used, it should be possible to recalculate the mobility of the charge-carriers (and
subsequently the charge-carrier density). This would help the reader to understand the reason why these improvements
were achieved and what might present the way forward. Likewise, this may explain the difference in thermal conduction
when doping is performed with TFSI vs. 4F-TCNQ. 
6. The work measures charge-carrier and thermal transport, two features I would expect to see in a material with applications
in a Pelltier element. Would it not be reasonable to measure and report the Seeback coefficient? 
7. Since orientation seems to be a key feature have you considered the use of self-assembled monolayers? Either on the
gold electrodes (thiols, i.e.: 10.1021/acsanm.1c03096) or on glass (silanes, i.e.: 10.1039/C6CS00509H). 
8. Regarding the calculations, the charged nature of the doping agent was avoided in an elegant way by using a non-
charged molecule of similar size. This is done, as the authors argue to “accurately identifying their positions” Since the
polymer contains plenty of sulphur-atoms, there may arise relatively strong interactions such as S-O....S-polymer bridges,
significantly affecting the polymer and how it interacts with the doping agent (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ccr.2020.213243 or
10.1021/acs.jpca.2c08965). Have the authors considered to replace the BTA-model system by, say, H2SO4, DMSO, or
dimethylsulfate? 
Once these few questions are answered, I strongly recommend the manuscript to be published. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors conducted a quite interesting and rigorous study for the properties of these materials. I would recommend
publishing this work under the certain, following conditions : 
1. Even though the authors conducted a rigorous and extensive characterization of their materials - which itself is heavily
contributing to the already limited knowledge on the field and it is admirable - the two properties that they are comparing are
not directly relevant. The clear correlation would come by comparing electrical conductivity and thermal conductivity in the
same directions along the film substrate (perpenticular and along the substrate). For that reason, what I am missing in the
manuscript, it is 
a clear explanation and description of why the authors chose to compare the two different properties in the
two different directions. I understand that measuring the electrical conductivity perpenticular to the substrate and the thermal
conductivity along the substrate are extremely challenging and I don't request any additional experiments from the authors.
However, I request that they elaborate and discuss extensively the scientific value that we acquire by comparing in these
dissimilar directions. 
2. Following my first comments, the authors should make a clarification on the relevant application of their results and work
in a industrial level i.e. thermoelectrics, transistors or smart windows ? 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript by Ito and colleagues presents a very detailed study of the change in thermal conductivity in the polymer
PBTTT upon electrochemical doping. While there is previous literature for inorganic systems and CNTs, in the field of
organic semiconductors I found very original the idea of reversibly modulating the thermal conductivity in semiconducting
polymers electrochemically, being this the first time that it is reported as far as I am aware. While the changes in the thermal
conductivity out of plane are moderate, the measurements are accurate and being the first publication on the topic, there is
plenty of room for improvement in the future. The rigor of the (very complex) thermal experiments should be highlighted. The
honest attempt to understand the origin of the changes by theoretical means is also noted. The manuscript is very well
structure, follows a concise and logical narrative and there are not overclaims. I strongly support the publication of this piece

Below a few comments for the optional consideration of the authors. 
1) Introduction: the authors have made a very comprehensive review of the state of the art. Perhaps consider only a small
note: while 62 W/mK would correspond to thermal conductivity of some metals (M. F. Ashby, Acta Metallurgica, 37,1273
(1989) this value might be moderate for typical metals (e.g. cupper or silver), perhaps it would be more appropriate to
compare it with typical inorganic semiconductors? Or mention the publication in which k of PE along the chain orientation
reaches around 120W/mK (Shen et al., Nat. Nanotech. 5, 251 (2010)) 
2) Is the doping homogeneous in the configuration of the TDTR experiment? Typically, doping in transistors is accumulated
at the semiconductor dielectric interface within the channel, so I wonder if the level of doping varies on top of the Au
electrode? Clearly, the mechanism may differ in electrolyte gated transistors compared to standard OFETs. Perhaps the
observed change is modest because doping is relatively small on top of the electrode compared to the channel? Perhaps a
simple way of looking at this is by the change in colour typical of PBTTT, is it observed on top of the electrode too?
Alternatively, if the laser spot is sufficiently small, one could always look at the thermal conductivity at positions closer or
further from the channel. 
3) The comparison between in plane electrical conductivity and out of plane thermal conductivity is very intriguing. Although
I understand that it goes beyond the scope of this paper, it would really be interesting to look at the change in the in-plane
thermal conductivity as it has been noted that doping does change this component too (e.g. in T. Degousée, et al. J Mater
Chem A 2021, 9 (29), 16065–16075; and in D. Rosas-Villalva, et al Materials Horizons, 2022, 9, 500-508). 
4) To further understand the correlation between doping level and thermal conductivity, could the authors plot the out of
plane thermal conductivity as a function of electrical conductivity for the two sets of experiments (as a function of doping
level and the cycling experiment)? The electrical conductivity could be used as a proxy for the charge carrier density and/or
mobility. Is it a linear relationship or more of a saturating or percolative behaviour? Do the two experiments follow the same
trend, in which case the reversibility might be compromised by trap dopant molecules within the polymer film? 
5) It is interesting to note that both, the magnitude and the decrease in thermal conductivity out of plane upon doping
deduced in the theoretical approach has been observed experimentally for PBTTT doped through ion exchange doping in a
recent publication (Guo et al, Advanced Functional Materials 2025). In the same publication it is shown that an increasing or
decreasing thermal conductivity out of plane may be related to the degree of order of the as cast film. For instance, the out of
plane thermal conductivity of regio random P3HT increases upon doping. I believe that this may be the origin of the
discrepancy in the values and trends between the experiments and theory in the current paper under review. 
I conclude by congratulating the authors again and repeating that the comments below are only for their consideration but
should not preclude publication of the paper as is.

We appreciate the reviewer’s comment. As pointed out, understanding the structural origin of the observed

Grazing-incidence small-angle X-ray scattering measurements 
Grazing-incidence small-angle X-ray scattering (GI-SAXS) measurements were performed to investigate 
structural changes in PBTTT thin films induced by electrochemical doping. PBTTT films were prepared on Au 
electrodes under the same conditions as those used for the TDTR measurements, ensuring consistency between the 
structural and thermal analyses. GI-SAXS experiments were carried out using a Xeuss Pro system (Xenocs) at the 
Materials Characterization Support Team, RIKEN Center for Emergent Matter Science, with a Cu Kα radiation 
source (λ = 1.5418 Å). Electrochemical doping and de-doping were performed ex situ by applying a gate voltage in 
an electrolyte environment identical to that used in the TDTR experiments. After reaching the desired doping state, 
the electrolyte was removed, and the samples were transferred to the GI-SAXS measurement. Figure S6 shows the 
GI-SAXS results. The (h00) diffraction peaks correspond to the lamellar stacking direction along the out-of-plane 
direction of the film. In the pristine state, the lamellar spacing was 21.2 Å, which expanded to 24.4 Å upon 
electrochemical doping. Furthermore, when a positive reverse gate voltage was applied to induce de-doping, the 
lamellar spacing decreased to 21.9 Å. These results are consistent with previous reports on TFSI intercalation into 
PBTTT via electrochemical doping and anion-exchange doping7,8.
