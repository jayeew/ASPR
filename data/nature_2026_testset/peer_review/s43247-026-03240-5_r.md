REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Overview and general recommendation:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

This manuscript presents a comprehensive and methodologically rigorous assessment of infrastructure risks due to
permafrost degradation across northern Alaska. By coupling a physics-based ground thermal model with geomechanics-
informed projections of thaw settlement and long-term bearing capacity, the authors develop a high-resolution (30
 
m)
modeling framework capable of quantifying the nonlinear and accelerating hazards that Arctic communities may face
throughout the 21st century. The study provides valuable insight into the accelerating nature of thaw settlement and bearing
capacity loss across both local and regional scales. The identification of a tipping point in the 2060s and the use of long-term
strength for analysis are particularly novel. I think the manuscript will make a valuable and citable contribution and should be
published. However, several issues should be addressed before the manuscript can be accepted. I recommend major
revisions before publication. 
Major comments: 
The main innovation of this manuscript lies in the development of a new framework that integrates physics-constrained
geotechnical models with a process-based ground thermal model to assess 21st-century changes in thaw settlement and
the bearing capacity of civil infrastructure foundations at a 30-meter spatial resolution. A particularly notable aspect is the
consideration of additional loads, and the use of long-term strength to quantify foundation bearing capacity in permafrost
regions. However, the validation of the methodology needs to be strengthened. Currently, validation is only performed for
undisturbed ground without additional loads form civil infrastructure. Is it possible to validate the impact of additional loads
as well? 
In addition, the study is limited to northern Alaska. Expanding the analysis to cover the entire state of Alaska would
significantly increase its broader relevance and impact. 
Specific comments: 
(1) Figure 1b and 1c: Would it be possible to show both the observations and simulations together for better comparison?
Also, would it be more appropriate to use centimeters as the unit? 
(2) Page 4 Line 86: 
Is this referring to InSAR measurements? Please specify the data source and measurement method
more clearly. 
(3) Page 5-6 Line 106-109: The content overlaps with the description in Figure 1 and can be integrated into the description
in Figure 1. 
(4) Page 11 Line 225: It is necessary to provide a comparison of the differences in four community driving factors as a
comparison on a local scale. Using only a regional scale graph (e.g., Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3) is insufficient. 
(5) Page 18, Lines 345 and 357: The concept of a "tipping point" is introduced somewhat abruptly. Please provide a clear
definition of what is meant by "tipping point" in the context of this study. Additionally, please elaborate on why the identified
threshold qualifies as a critical tipping point—specifically, what physical, geotechnical, or system-level changes occur
beyond this threshold that justify its classification as a tipping point? 
(7) Page 19 Line 375: The threshold bearing capacity used to classify infrastructure as “at risk” are reasonable but somewhat
arbitrary. Please provide a short justification or sensitivity discussion for these choices. 
(8) Method: The framework that integrates physics-constrained geotechnical models needs more detailed description and
illustration. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
Wang et al. present a high-resolution (30 m) community-scale model to project geohazards and their implications for civil
infrastructure on the Alaskan Arctic Coastal Plain (ACP) throughout the 21st century. Using this model, they provide
spatiotemporal patterns of thaw subsidence, derive thaw settlement rates for the ACP with and without accounting for the
loading effect of infrastructure, and quantify the projected loss of bearing capacity. In addition, they introduce a risk index to
assess which types of civil infrastructure (e.g., buildings, roads, pipelines) are most at risk due to these geohazards (thaw
settlement and loss of bearing capacity). 
While there are models that quantify thaw settlement and loss of bearing capacity as a consequence of permafrost
degradation (e.g., Streletskiy et al., 2023, DOI: 10.1088/1748-9326/acab18), to the best of my knowledge – and
acknowledging that this is not my primary field – no 
study has applied a community-level model at such high spatial
resolution. This approach not only provides communities with valuable information for decision-making, but also advances
the state of the art by accounting for the loading effect of infrastructure on thaw settlement – an important factor, as additional
stress from infrastructure accelerates settlement. 
Overall, I find the study innovative and valuable. At the same time, certain sections of the manuscript could benefit from
further clarification and additional contextual information. Providing these details would make the work more accessible to
readers from different disciplinary backgrounds, including those without a strong engineering perspective, and thereby

For the physical and geotechnical rationale of this transition, three processes contribute to 
the sharp increase in the at-risk infrastructure fraction (𝐼𝑟𝑖𝑠𝑘) during the 2060s-2080s: 
1. Thermal and settlement accumulation: The long-term effects of climate warming drive 
a continuous increase in ground temperature, active layer thickness, and talik 
development. Within the 2060s -2080s timeframe, the cumulative thaw settlement or 
loss of bearing capacity of most infrastructure first crosses the threshold values, 
marking the sharp increase in the at-risk fraction. 
2. Nonlinear permafrost table response: The decline of permafrost table due to increasing 
air temperature is highly nonlinear. Assuming the soil properties are homogeneous and 
independent of temperature, the active layer thickness can be estimated using the Stefan 
relation, which relates it to the square root of the thawing in dex. However, as air 
temperature continues to rise, the annual freezing index can no longer offset the annual 
thawing index. Subsequent summers then begin from a warmer baseline (Farquharson 
et al. 2022) . The combined thickness (ALT and TT) integrates the net annual heat 
surplus and produces an accelerating trajectory of thaw settlement. This acceleration 
results in most infrastructure crossing the threshold during the 2060s-2080s. 
3. Strength degradation with temperature: The decrease in the long -term strengt h of 
permafrost with increasing temperature is highly nonlinear  (Wang et al. 202 6). As 
subfreezing temperatures rise toward the melting point, a large amount of unfrozen 
water appears. The creep rate of frozen soil increases sharply with temperature (Glen’s 
flow law), resulting in a drastic decrease in bearing capacity. Consequently, most 
infrastructure crosses the threshold during the 2060s-2080s.

General comment 
Wang et al. present a high -resolution (30 m) community -scale model to project geohazards and 
their implications for civil infrastructure on the Alaskan Arctic Coastal Plain (ACP) throughout 
the 21st century. Using this model, they provide spatiotemporal patterns of thaw subsidence, derive 
thaw settlement rates for the ACP with and without accounting for the loading effect of 
infrastructure, and quantify the projected loss of bearing capacity. In addition, they introduce a 
risk index to assess which types of civil infra structure (e.g., buildings, roads, pipelines) are most 
at risk due to these geohazards (thaw settlement and loss of bearing capacity).

consequence of permafrost degradat ion (e.g., Streletskiy et al., 2023, DOI: 10.1088/1748 -
9326/acab18), to the best of my knowledge – and acknowledging that this is not my primary field 
– no study has applied a community-level model at such high spatial resolution. This approach not 
only provides communities with valuable information for decision-making, but also advances the 
state of the art by accounting for the loading effect of infrastructure on thaw settlement – an 
important factor, as additional stress from infrastructure accelerates settlement. 
Overall, I find the study innovative and valuable. At the same time, certain sections of the 
manuscript could benefit from further clarification and additional contextual information. 
Providing these details would make the work more accessible to readers from different disciplinary 
backgrounds, including those without a strong engineering perspective, and thereby broaden its 
impact.

Response: Thank you for comments and suggestions for improvement of this manuscript . We 
addressed your concerns as follows: 
(1) Representativeness of the validation site for infrastructure loading 
We appreciate the reviewer’s concern about the representativeness of the selected validation site. 
However, our goal in this validation step is to test the model response to vertical loads on 
permafrost, rather than to reproduce the full community layouts. The chosen location south of

Prudhoe Bay includes two of the most common civil infrastructure types on the North Slope —
roads and pipeline—which exert vertical stress on the ground and are known to induce pronounced 
thaw settlement. In this sense, the site captures the key mechanical process we s eek to validate: 
the additional thaw settlement caused by infrastructure loading. 
 We agree that the footprint of infrastructure at this site differs from those in communities 
dominated by residential buildings, and we now acknowledge this limitation. However, the validity 
of the thaw settlement framework itself depends on its ability to reproduce deformation under an 
additional vertical stress, not on whether the validation site lies within a town or not. 
(2) Validation of thaw subsidence without additional vertical stress 
The purpose of the newly added validation section is specifically to test the model response to 
additional infrastructure loading, i.e., thaw settlement under ∆𝑞 > 0. For this reason, we focused 
on an urban-disturbed area with roads and pipelines that impose additional vertical stress on the 
ground. Validation of thaw subsidence in the absence of additional vertical loading on the tundra 
is already provided earlier in the manuscript (Fig. 1), where we compare modeled thaw subsidence 
with both in-situ (Streletskiy et al. 2017) and InSAR measurements (Liu et al. 2010). Thus, the 
two validation steps are complementary: Fig. 1 validates thaw subsidence without added load, and 
the new section (Fig. 10) validates thaw settlement under infrastructure loading. 
(3) Inclusion of pixels near, but not directly on the road and pipeline 
The InSAR deformation product used for validation has a ground spatial resolution of 15 m (Liu 
et al. 2015). We have also added a scale bar to Fig. 10.  We agree that some validation pixels are 
not centered exactly on the road or pipeline but also include adjacent tundra. Because vertical 
stress from the infrastructure decays laterally away from the load (as described by elastic stress 
distribution solutions), these pi xels are expected to experience lower ground stress and therefore

smaller thaw settlement than pixels located directly beneath the infrastructure. We now clarify this 
in the revised manuscript and note that the inclusion of pixels near, but not directly on the road 
and pipeline may lead to underestimation of local thaw settlement. 
 Importantly, Liu et al. (2015) reported a mean deformation rate of 2.7 ± 0.7 cm/yr for 2006-
2010 using the identical study region. Our validation window is chosen to be consisten t with the 
validation sites so that our modeled thaw settlement is compared against the same spatial average 
as the InSAR data. 
(4) Discussions of the limitations of the validation strategy 
At present, to the best of our knowledge, the south of Prudhoe Bay site is the only location where 
spatially long-term InSAR-derived vertical deformation rates are available in North Slope and civil 
infrastructures are clearly characterized. As discussed above, this site is therefore the only one that 
presently allows a quantitative validation of thaw settlement under additional vertical stress.  In 
response, we have added the following discussions to clearly acknowledge the limitations of this 
validation approach: 
 In the lines 550-554, we added: “ The validation site contains road s and pipelines rather 
than the residential buildings that dominate our four study communities and therefore does not 
capture full community layouts. However, the site represents a typical infrastructure loading area 
and captures the key mechanical process we seek to validate —thaw settlement under additional 
vertical stress from infrastructure.” 
 In the lines 558-560, we added: “To ensure consistency with the spatially averaged InSAR 
measurements (Liu et al. 2015) , the comparison is carried out over the similar spatial polygon. 
Some pixels near, but not directly over the infrastructure likely experience lower ground stress and 
smaller thaw settlement.”
