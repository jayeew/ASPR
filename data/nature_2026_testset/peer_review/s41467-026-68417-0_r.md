Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a review of NCOMMS-25-67092. In this study, the authors use a previously described biophysics approach for
measuring single molecule DNA synapsis by core factors in the non-homologous end joining pathway. The experiments are
straightforward with appropriate controls. The main finding is that adding compatible DNA overhangs enhances synapsis via
KU/XRCC4/LIG4 and reduces the requirement for XLF. My concern is that the relative requirements for NHEJ factors for
distinct end configurations have been relatively well established, and as such these findings are not very surprising. One
example is this paper from 2016 (PMID: 27703001). Much of the research on end synapsis, including from this group,
focuses on blunt DNA ends merely because that end configuration needs to be stabilized without annealing. Other studies
have also addressed the impact of non-homologous tails too. The authors mention that other end configurations are likely
found in cells, such as damaged/oxidized bases, but such end configurations were not tested here, but rather focus on
microhomology substrates. Perhaps some experiments with other such substrates would improve the scope of the study. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Zhang et al. present an elegant and well-executed study investigating the role of DNA end configurations
in NHEJ-mediated synapsis. Using a combination of biochemical and single-molecule FRET analyses, the authors
convincingly demonstrate that DNA end configurations—including terminal sequences and 5′ phosphate groups—play
critical roles in determining the sub-pathway choice of NHEJ synapsis. Given that broken DNA ends generated during
chemo-/radiotherapy, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing, V(D)J recombination, and class-switch recombination exhibit
diverse end structures, understanding how these configurations influence NHEJ synapsis has significant implications for
genome stability, gene editing, and antibody maturation. This makes the work highly relevant to the readership of Nature
Communications. I am generally supportive of this study, but several concerns should be addressed before acceptance. 
Major concerns: 
1. The authors should provide gel images or Western blots to demonstrate the quality of the recombinant Ku70–Ku80,
XRCC4–LIG4, and XLF proteins. Additionally, activity assays of the purified proteins should be included, as the purity and
functional integrity of these proteins are crucial for supporting the conclusions drawn in this study. 
2. In Figure 5, the authors examine the effects of 5′ P on NHEJ synapsis using duplexes with 5′ phosphorylated ends on both
strands. The authors should also test the effect of a single 5′ P group on synapsis efficiency, as the number of phosphate
groups present at the DNA ends has been reported to influence synapsis (see PMID: 27924007). 
3. In the Discussion (lines 350–352), the authors mention the processing of incompatible sequences by Artemis. They
should provide a more detailed explanation of how Artemis processes these sequences and how this activity relates to
NHEJ synapsis. 
4. The authors should include statistical analyses for all quantitative plots, specifying the number of single molecules
analyzed and the number of experimental replicates performed.

5. In Figures S2f and S3a, the authors should include schematic diagrams illustrating the configurations of the positive-

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Zhang et al. presents a meticulous mechanistic study that fundamentally advances our understanding of
non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). By employing single-molecule FRET with a series of well-designed DNA substrates,
the authors systematically dissect how DNA end configurations regulate the formation and architecture of synaptic
complexes. The work yields several key and novel findings: first, it conclusively demonstrates that the presence of
microhomology (≥3 nt) at DNA ends can efficiently drive the formation of stable, ligation-competent close synaptic (CS)
complexes through distinct structural pathways, even in the absence of the critical facilitator XLF. Second, it establishes a
clear hierarchy where end complementarity can functionally substitute for XLF, thereby defining a microhomology-
dependent bypass pathway for NHEJ synapsis. Third, it reveals that DNA end chemistry, specifically 5' phosphorylation,
acts cooperatively with base pairing to promote synapsis. Overall, this study provides a new paradigm for understanding the
flexibility and substrate-driven nature of the NHEJ machinery. I recommend publication after addressing the comments
outlined below. 
Major Comments 
1. In Figure 2, the assignment of the four distinct FRET states to specific synaptic complex conformations is central to the
manuscript's conclusions. While references are made to pre-ligated controls, the logical flow from the control FRET values to
the assignment of states like the "junction-contacting" (E4) and "end-pairing" (E3) complexes in the experimental data could
be more explicitly detailed in the main text or figure legends for the general reader. 
2. The claim that observed FRET signals represent protein-mediated synapsis requires solidification by including negative
control data. Quantification of the background frequency of co-localized Cy3/Cy5 spots (i.e., apparent FRET pairs) in
experiments performed with DNA substrates alone (in the absence of all proteins) should be provided in the supplement.
This is essential to rule out the contribution of any rare, non-specific DNA-DNA interactions to the reported synapsis events. 
3. The ensemble ligation assay in Fig. 1a uses 5' P-terminated DNA, whereas the smFRET assays in Figs. 1-4 use 5' OH-
terminated DNA to isolate synapsis. Given that 5' P is itself a synapsis-promoting factor (Fig. 5), this discrepancy should be
explicitly acknowledged. 
4. The study focuses exclusively on perfectly complementary overhangs. To better contextualize the findings within the
physiological reality of diverse DSB ends, the authors should discuss the potential implications of their model for partially
complementary or non-complementary ends. For instance, how would a 4-nt overhang with only 2-nt of microhomology
behave? Would it exhibit dynamic flickering, and how would XLF influence this? 
5. The primary overhangs used are G/C-rich, with ACTG/CAGT as a control. To generalize the conclusion that the effect
depends on "complementarity rather than base composition," it would be strengthening to include data or a discussion
addressing whether A/T-rich overhangs of equivalent length exhibit similar synapsis efficiency and CS complex stability. 
6. The data clearly show that XLF is dispensable for CS formation with overhangs ≥3 nt. However, the manuscript would
benefit from a deeper discussion on the potential role(s) of XLF under these conditions. 
Minor Comments 
1. There appears to be a discrepancy in the relative ligation efficiency of blunt ends versus overhangs when comparing Fig.
1a and Fig. S6. Additionally, in Fig. S6, what explains the doublet band for the GGG/CCC substrate, and why might the G/C
overhang exhibit lower efficiency than the blunt end. 
2. The legend for Fig. 4f notes that data for the critical GGG/CCC condition (which defines the 3-nt threshold) are from
duplicate experiments (n=2). A sample size of n=2 is insufficient for reliable calculation of standard deviation. These data

are not explicitly defined in the legends. Providing a clear color-to-state mapping directly in the figure legends would

4. Page 5, line 135-136, The phrasing "with the XLF present" seems awkward and grammatically imprecise. For better

We appreciate the Reviewer’s thoughtful comments regarding the novelty of our work. It is true that 
previous studies have examined how distinct end configurations influence NHEJ factor requirements 
and the repair (e.g., PMID: 27703001, PMID: 27924007, PMID: 28930678, and PMID: 24989324), 
and our Introduction also acknowledges these contributions  (Page 4). However, despite extensive 
biochemical characterizations, how different DNA ends determine synapsis architecture and 
factor dependence at a mechanistic and structural level has remained unclear . As Reviewer 3 
also noted, our study provides the first systematic single -molecule dissection of how end 
configurations govern the formation, stability, and architecture of NHEJ synaptic complexes.  
Importantly, the synapsis model that emerges from our work has direct implications for 
understanding how NHEJ synapsis coordinates with Artemis -mediated end processing —a major 
focus of ongoing research in our laboratory. 
 As suggested, we have now tested overhangs containing one or two 8-oxoguanine(s) located 
at different positions. We find that a single 8 -oxoguanine in the 4 -nt 3’ overhang does not impair 
synapsis efficiency, whereas two 8 -oxoguanines produce a modest redu ction in synapsis mediated 
by Ku and X4L4 (Fig. S3c). Additionally, neither one nor two 8 -oxoguanines disrupt the formation or 
stability of CS complexes compared with undamaged GGGG/CCCC overhangs (Fig. S3d–S3f). These 
data indicate that NHEJ can tolerate 8 -oxoguanine lesions, consistent w ith previous reports (PMID: 
28930678 and PMID: 24989324). Lesions retained after NHEJ may subsequently be repaired by other 
pathways.  
 Finally, the mechanistic insight gained here into how such changes (and others discussed in 
the manuscript) destabilize or modulate synapsis is essential for understanding the extent to which 
the synapsis step does—or does not—restrict progression to the final ligation stage. In our view, these 
advances underscore why our study merits publication in Nature Communications.

We appreciate the Reviewer’s concerns regarding the effects of base composition on CS formation. 
Based on our observations of CS formation and the FS -to-CS transition mediated by Ku plus X4L4

plus X4L4 promote C S formation primarily depending on overhang compatibility rather than base

have lower pairing stability compared to  other compatible overhangs. These results reinforce that 
complementarity, rather than base composition, is the primary determinant of synapsis, although base 
composition can modulate its efficiency.
