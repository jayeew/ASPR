Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports the development of an Acoustic Shape-Morphing Micromachine (ASM) capable of ultrafast and
reversible morphological reconfiguration under acoustic-field actuation. The ASM architecture consists of a pair of
isodiametric microbubbles interconnected by a compliant microhinge, wherein shape transformation is driven by secondary
acoustic radiation forces and acoustic streaming flows generated in the inter-bubble region. The authors present a
comprehensive design framework that enables precise modulation of deformation kinetics, angular displacement, and
structural stability, and introduce a modular design methodology applicable to both forward and inverse kinematic modeling.
This framework supports the fabrication of ASMs with fully programmable 2D and 3D morphologies, as well as integrated
information-encoding functionalities. The study offers potentially significant implications for the future design of shape-
morphing micromachines in domains such as soft robotics, microfluidics, and adaptive microstructures. I recommend the
manuscript for publication, contingent upon the authors’ satisfactory response to the following technical comments. 
Comments: 
1. While acoustic streaming generated by oscillating microbubbles typically propagates radially away from the bubble
surface, prior studies have demonstrated that even subtle variations in excitation frequency or bubble volume can induce a
reversal in streaming direction. A more detailed and mechanistically rigorous exposition is warranted to clarify how such
streaming flows, in conjunction with secondary acoustic radiation forces, effectuate morphological reconfiguration of
microscale structures. Although the present work effectively demonstrates the practical feasibility of employing acoustically
driven microbubbles for microscale structural deformation, the fundamental physico-mechanical underpinnings of these
transformations—rooted in bubble oscillatory dynamics—are not yet sufficiently elucidated. 
2. Related to the above, in cases where the bubble volume is sufficiently small, transient shifts in its resonance frequency
could undermine the reproducibility or fidelity of the intended deformation response. Further clarification is requested on the
protocols employed for precise generation of such small-volume bubbles, and on the temporal stability of their volume during
operation. In addition, previous investigations have shown that both the oscillation amplitude of microbubbles and the
magnitude of the resultant acoustic streaming and secondary radiation forces vary with the spatial offset from the
piezoelectric transducer (PZT). It would be of interest to determine whether similar spatially dependent phenomena were
observed in the present experiments, and if so, how they were accounted for in the design and analysis. 
3. The originality and conceptual ingenuity of the reported research are noteworthy. To facilitate a more rigorous and
objective evaluation of the work’s novelty, it would be advantageous for the authors to provide a concise synopsis of their
prior related studies and to delineate explicitly the principal advancements and distinctive contributions of the present
investigation relative to those earlier efforts.

Response: We thank the reviewer for their insightful remarks regarding the mechanical basis 
of the ASM deformation and the underlying microbubble dynamics. In response, we have 
expanded our discussion to elucidate how the combined action of acoustic streaming and 
secondary acoustic radiation forces effectuates the morphological reconfiguration of the 
microstructures. 
We have noted the phenomenon raised by the reviewer, wherein variations in excitation 
frequency or microbubble volume can lead to a reversal in the direction of acoustic streaming. 
For instance, Doinikov observed that acoustic scattering between microbubbles of different 
sizes can cause an increase in their natural frequencies, which may in turn reverse the sign of 
the secondary acoustic radiation force[R1]. Specifically, when the driving frequency exceeds the 
natural frequencies of both microbubbles, the natural frequency of the smaller microbubble in 
an approaching pair may rise above the driving frequency as the inter-microbubble distance 
decreases, thereby changing the force from attractive to repulsive. Conversely, when the driving 
frequency lies between the natural frequencies of the two microbubbles, the natural frequencies 
of both microbubbles may increase as they approach each other. If the natural frequency of the 
larger microbubble surpasses the driving frequency, the force may switch from repulsive to 
attractive. It is important to emphasize that such sign reversals are more likely to occur when 
the driving frequency is sufficiently close to the natural frequencies of the microbubbles. 
Furthermore, under high-intensity acoustic fields, strong driving amplifies the 
microbubbles' radiation fields, causing multiple scattering effects—which are typically 
negligible—to become dominant. This shift significantly alters the phase relationship between 
the microbubbles, which can also result in a reversal in the sign of the secondary acoustic 
radiation force[R2]. 
The distances between microbubbles are significantly smaller than the acoustic 
wavelength in all our prototypes. The secondary forces that act on the ASM are thrust (𝐹𝑆), drag 
force exerted on a microbubble by the flow generated by the neighboring microbubbles ( 𝐹𝑅), 
and secondary acoustic radiation force (𝐹𝑈) (also known as secondary acoustic radiation force 
or secondary Bjerknes force). Consequently, the secondary acoustic radiation force is 
proportional to the time-averaged product of the volume velocities of the two microbubbles 
under the standard monopole approximation [R3, R4]. The secondary acoustic radiation force 𝐹𝑈
