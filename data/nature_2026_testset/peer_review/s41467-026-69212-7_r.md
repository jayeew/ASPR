Title: An Interpretable AI System Reduces False-positive MRI Diagnoses by Stratifying High-risk Breast Lesions

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

The manuscript presents BL4AS (BI-RADS 4 Lesions Analysis System), a deep learning-based AI model designed to
stratify BI-RADS 4 breast lesions using dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI). Trained on a substantial dataset of
17,149 MRI volumes, BL4AS demonstrates high diagnostic performance across multiple external and prospective test sets,
achieving AUCs ranging from 0.892 to 0.930. Notably, the system significantly reduces false-positive rates and inter-reader
variability and enhances radiologists' specificity and efficiency when used as a decision-support tool. 
A prospective test with 88 cases demonstrated improved diagnostic consistency among radiologists when aided by the
system. The model also provides interpretable Grad-CAM visualizations to support clinical decision-making. Despite
encouraging results, the study's limitations include a small prospective sample and lack of formal adherence to reporting
standards like CLAIM 2 (Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging) guidelines [Radiology, doi:
10.1148/ryai.240300]. 
Significance and Comparison with Established Literature 
This work addresses a relevant clinical problem in breast imaging: the overdiagnosis and low specificity of MRI in BI-RADS
4 lesions. While similar efforts have been reported (e.g., radiomics or CNN-based classifiers for breast MRI), few have
employed foundation models with prospective testing. However, key works such as Daimiel Naranjo et al. (Cancers, 2022,
doi: 10.3390/cancers14071743) and Sun et al. (J. Transl. Med., 2021, doi: 10.1186/s12967-021-03117-5) have tackled
similar goals. 
Support for Conclusions and Need for Additional Evidence 
The conclusions are generally supported by the results, particularly in terms of improved specificity and inter-reader
agreement. However, the small prospective sample size (n=88) undermines the strength of the claims related to clinical
impact. Furthermore, there is no sample size or power justification in the statistical methods, which is essential for validating
the adequacy of the test cohort. A major omission is the lack of a detailed analysis of false negatives, which are critical in
oncologic diagnostics. 
Flaws in Data Analysis, Interpretation, and Conclusions 
- The limited prospective cohort size and single-institution origin introduce concerns of overfitting and limit generalizability. 
- Absence of formal sample size calculations and interaction testing in subgroup analysis weakens the methodological rigor.
- Grad-CAM is a reasonable starting point but provides only limited insight into decision boundaries. 
Another major limitation of the manuscript is the lack of direct comparison with existing AI models. While BL4AS
demonstrates strong performance within its own dataset, the absence of a quantitative comparison against previously
published models limits the ability to assess its true innovation and clinical added value. 
In the methods section, two critical design choices are reported without sufficient justification and should be clarified by the
authors. First, the decision to select only the largest lesion in cases of multifocal disease may introduce bias, particularly if
smaller lesions have different imaging characteristics or histopathological profiles. The rationale for excluding additional
lesions should be explained, along with its potential impact on model generalizability and performance. Second, the division
of patients from Center 1 into training and internal test sets using an 8:2 ratio, and the assignment of patients from Centers 2
and 3 to external test sets A and B, respectively, is described without justification. The authors should clarify whether
stratification or balancing was applied during the split and explain why this center-specific allocation was chosen, especially
given the possible influence of institutional imaging protocols and patient populations on model performance. Additionally,
these omissions result in the lack of compliance with CLAIM items 19–21, which require a detailed explanation of data
partitioning, the rationale for dataset construction, and potential sources of bias or confounding. 
The manuscript does not report whether any analysis was performed to assess differences in imaging data derived from
1.5T versus 3T scanners. Given that image quality, signal-to-noise ratio, and enhancement characteristics can vary
significantly between field strengths, this may influence both feature extraction and model performance. Could the authors
clarify whether such a comparison was conducted, and if not, provide justification for its omission? 
Data and Methodology 
The prospective validation cohort includes only 88 cases, which is, in my opinion, insufficient to draw strong or
generalizable clinical conclusions. This limited sample size significantly reduces the statistical power of the study and raises
concerns about potential selection bias. Moreover, the manuscript does not include any formal justification or power analysis
to validate the adequacy of the sample size for prospective evaluation. Furthermore, the prospective cohort used for real-
world testing (Table 1) includes a disproportionate prevalence of malignant cases 65/88 (70.7%), which does not reflect the
true distribution typically encountered in clinical practice for BI-RADS 4 lesions. This artificially high prevalence may bias
performance metrics, especially positive predictive value, and limits the generalizability of the findings. 
The manuscript does not provide a comprehensive analysis of false negative cases, which is a critical omission. Given the
high clinical risk associated with missed malignancies in breast imaging, detailed examination of these errors is essential.
Understanding where and why the model fails would be necessary to assess its safety and clinical reliability. 
Analytical Approach 
The analytical approach is sound, employing appropriate performance metrics (AUC, specificity, sensitivity) and
comparative analyses with radiologists' assessments. To enhance this section: 
- Discuss the rationale behind chosen thresholds for classification and their clinical implications. 
- Consider performing subgroup analyses based on lesion characteristics or patient demographics to explore model
performance across different scenarios.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Dear Colleagues, 
The study presents an innovative and timely contribution to breast imaging by addressing one of the most critical limitations
of MRI: the high number of false positives leading to an excessive number of unnecessary biopsies. These biopsies, often
performed under MRI guidance when feasible, significantly increase healthcare costs, prolong diagnostic timelines, and
utilize vital radiological resources that could otherwise be allocated to more pressing clinical needs. In this context, the
development of AI systems capable of refining diagnostic specificity, particularly for BI-RADS 4 lesions, is both relevant and
commendable. 
The manuscript introduces the BI-RADS 4 Lesions Analysis System (BL4AS), an AI-based tool leveraging multiphase DCE-
MRI and a pretrained foundation model to segment and classify breast lesions. The system demonstrates high diagnostic
accuracy and promising potential to improve radiologists' performance in both retrospective and prospective cohorts. 
While the topic and approach are noteworthy, several critical concerns necessitate a thorough revision: 
Abstract 
-The abstract effectively summarizes the study's aim, methods, and key findings 
-you provided a good general introduction to the topic and well explained the results’ implications. 
Methodological Rigor: 
- Overall, the "Methods" section is detailed and well-structured but needs some refinement in terms of clarity, accessibility,
and correction of typographical errors. 
-The manuscript does not provide specific details about the MRI machines used, including vendors, models, or coils, which
are important given the variability in image quality across platforms. It is unclear whether subgroup analyses were performed
to examine performance differences between different scanners, or if such differences were considered negligible. 
-Protocol standardization across centers is not sufficiently addressed. There is no discussion of harmonization of dynamic
phase timing, contrast agent type and dosage, or consistency in patient preparation and acquisition sequences. 
-Greater detail is needed regarding preprocessing steps across different centers 
-the washout period may be insufficient to eliminate recall bias, especially given the small prospective dataset. Consider
discussing this limitation, or justifying the chosen interval based on existing standards or prior literature. 
-Analysis is limited to the largest lesion in multifocal cases. Consider methods to analyze multiple lesions per patient in
future developments to better reflect multifocal cases and clinical practice. 
- It would be beneficial to have a clear distinction between primary and secondary outcomes in the analysis and results
presentation. 
Comparison to Established Literature: 
-The paper would benefit from a more rigorous and transparent comparison with other models. 
-You should clarify why the use of a foundation model substantively improves over traditional pretrained CNNs or radiomics-
based methods. Additionally, the term 'foundation model' should be clearly defined within the manuscript, especially for
readers unfamiliar with the concept. This clarification would enhance reader understanding. 
Discussion 
- The whole section is quite well structured however you should better elaborate on the limitations 
-The manuscript addresses a highly relevant clinical issue. Nevertheless, its utility is currently confined to BI-RADS 4 cases.
Broader implications, such as integration into comprehensive AI-aided diagnostic pathways, should be more thoroughly
explored in the discussion section. 
The manuscript could further elaborate on how such systems might support clinical practice in resource-limited settings,
where variability in radiologist expertise could affect diagnostic consistency. 
-I would suggest elaborating, in this section, on the recommendations for future research 
Support for Conclusions and Claims: 
-While the results are encouraging and indicate substantial potential, certain assertions regarding clinical readiness and
generalizability may be premature. These should be more cautiously framed in light of the limited scope of the prospective
validation and the need for broader multi-center real-world testing. 
Best Regards.

Response to R1.1: We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comment regarding our focus on BI -
RADS 4 lesions. The main reason is that BI-RADS 4 lesions represe nt the greatest diagnostic 
challenge in breast imaging. BI-RADS 5 lesions have >95% malignancy probability, and BI -RADS 
1-3 lesions have ≤2% malignancy rate 1 — both relatively straightforward to diagnose. In contrast, 
BI-RADS 4 lesions exhibit significant overlap in imaging characteristics between benign and 
malignant lesions, accounting for the majority of false -positive biopsies (MRI-guided biopsy yields 
only 20.5%-30.3% positive rates)2. Therefore, AI assistance is most critically needed for this category 
where diagnostic uncertainty is highest. Although BI-RADS 1-3 and BI-RADS 5 lesions are generally 
more straightforward to diagnose in routine practice, their inclusion was not the primary focus of this 
study, as our work aimed to address the most pressing diagnostic gap. We plan to expand our analysis 
to include these categories in future research to provide a more comprehensive assessment.

Regarding the clinical utility of A/B/C subcategorization, while current BI -RADS guidelines 
recommend biopsy for all category 4 lesions, emerging evidence suggests more nuanced management 
strategies may be appropriate. Flowers et al. 3 demonstrated that experienced radiologists could 
provide highly accurate risk assessment, showing that low -risk 4A lesions could be safel y managed 
with clinical assessment and follow -up rather than immediate biopsy. This approach safely reduced 
false-positive biopsies by 22%-56% while missing only 0%-2% of invasive cancers. Furthermore, the 
latest BI-RADS 6th edition now formally recommends A/B/C subcategorization for MRI BI-RADS 
4 lesions. Our BL4AS system may reduce the burden on radiologists in this assessment process while 
providing more accurate risk evaluation and reducing inter-reader variability, as demonstrated in our 
manuscript. 
Regarding your specific question about biopsy avoidance, we analyzed different operational points 
of BL4AS based on training set to evaluate potential management strategies. Our BL4AS system 
demonstrates significant clinical impact by avoiding 123 unnecessar y biopsies per 1,000 patients 
while maintaining 100% sensitivity in the external test set at a high NPV operating point.  
While including all BI-RADS categories represents a reasonable future direction, our study aimed 
to address the diagnostic challenge where AI assistance is most critically needed. Additionally, we 
have added this concern to our revised manuscript’s limitations section, where we discuss how 
BL4AS could be integrated into a comprehensive AI-assisted diagnostic workflow that encompasses 
all breast lesions.
