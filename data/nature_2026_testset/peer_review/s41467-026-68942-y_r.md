Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors of the paper presented interesting observations showing that only integrin β5 can be targeted to curved
membranes made of artificial nanostructures created by nanobars. The targeting of integrin to the curved membrane was
possible due to the β5 Y766 phosphorylation, which reduces talin binding and allows its interaction with the curvature-
sensing protein FCHo2. Since β5 is targeted to the curved membrane in an FCHo2-dependent manner and interacts with its
ligand, the authors suggest that FCHo2 binding activates integrin αvβ5 at curved membranes. Furthermore, they showed
that the β5 transmembrane domain is also important for curved membrane targeting, which requires further investigation.
That study would give a helpful insight into the field, if some issues below can be addressed. 
1. The authors used nanobars with a height of 1 µm. However, cellular thickness, especially at the peripheral regions, can
be less than 1 µm, leading to both inward positive curvature at the basal surface and outward negative curvature at the
apical surface from the same nanobar, as shown by the authors in their previous study (PMID: 31591250, Fig 1I). This raises
question about whether the fluorescence signals shown throughout the manuscript come solely from positive curvature, as
the authors suggest (Fig. 1B), or also from negative curvature positioned above the positive curvature, or from the space
between these different curvature types. This ambiguity remains unless the cell thickness in the regions of interest is
considered, e.g. by adopting some z-section study. 
2. In most experiments, integrin β subunits fused to GFP were transiently transfected, potentially leading to its accumulation
in the ER. This makes it unclear whether the fluorescence near the nanobars represents integrins at the cell surface or within
the ER or other intracellular organelles. Similarly, it is not clear whether integrin β mutants, especially ITGβ5-3-3(1-755/769)
used in Fig. 5J, can actually form integrin αvβ5 complexes on the cell surface and near the nanobars as well. 
3. The cell images were taken after 24 hours of plating on vitronectin-coated nanobars, even with the presence of 10% FBS
(line 677). To observe the intended interactions between αvβ5 and vitronectin at the nanobars, an incubation of 30 minutes
to 1 hour in the absence of FBS would be enough. A 24-hour incubation with FBS could introduce unwanted interactions
due to ECM components in the serum or from cells adhering to the surface, allowing various endogenous integrins to
engage in cell-substrate interactions. An experiment under a 30-minute to 1-hour FBS-free incubation should be considered,
to confirm the results obtained by their 24 hour/FBS method. 
4. The main conclusion of the extensive mutation studies in Figs. 1 and 2 is that the phosphorylation state of Y766
determines the localization of β5. Without phosphorylation, β5 binds to talin and is recruited to focal adhesions. When
phosphorylated, β5 does not bind talin (or its affinity is reduced) and is recruited to curved membranes. However, there is no
direct demonstration of the phosphorylation status of integrin β5. For example, in the sentence on line 252, “Among all the
mutants, only the variants with Y766 can form both focal adhesions and curved adhesions, likely due to its dual
phosphorylation states,” it is critical to show whether β5 indeed possesses dual phosphorylation states, e.g. by using pY
specific antibody of β5 immunoprecipitates or by mass spectroscopy. 
5. If the Y766 phosphorylation hypothesis is correct, could the talin-binding-defective β5 Y774A mutant be phosphorylated
more than the wild type? Or, could talin overexpression inhibit β5 Y766 phosphorylation? Is there any evidence that
“phosphorylation of β5Y766 would abolish the cation-π interaction and drastically alter the interaction” (Line 194)? In
addition, could talin knockout lead to the preferential localization of β3 at the curved membrane?

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

6. The authors suggest that the unique Tyrosine residue in β5 (Y766) is responsible for targeting it to curved membranes,
thereby facilitating cell adhesion in 3D ECM fibers or during endocytosis. In contrast, other β integrins, containing tryptophan
at the corresponding position, cannot be targeted to curved membranes. Then the question is, do cells not form focal
adhesions but only curvature adhesions in 3D ECM fibers? Moreover, other integrins play roles in binding fibrous ECM (e.g.,
β1 integrins to collagen fibers) and endocytosis (e.g., β2 integrins during phagocytosis), even with the tryptophan residue.
This raises questions about the physiological relevance of β5 targeting to curved membranes. 
7. Biochemical evidence is required for the hypothesis that talin binding to the MP site prevents FCHo2 binding (line 257).
For example, an in vitro experiment using purified integrin β tails (wild type vs. Y766E vs. HDRRE) and FCHo2 interaction in
the presence of increasing amounts of purified talin should be performed. Only cell biology experiments is not sufficient to
prove the hypothesis. 
8. Although the authors concluded that FCHo2 is essential for inside-out activation of ITGβ5 at curved membranes (lines 411
and 452), no mechanistic explanation for the activation is provided. Rather, it seems to me that ITGβ5 binds to the ligand on
the curved membrane first for some reasons, e.g. by the curvature itself (PMID: 32634408), and that FCHo2 is recruited to
the region due to both the curvature and its binding site in the integrin, which can stabilize the integrin complex as suggested
by the authors (line 444). To support the argument that FCHo2 is essential for ITGβ5 activation at curved membranes, it
would be important to demonstrate whether FCHo2-bound ITGβ5 (on curved membranes, in the absence of ligand) adopts
an active conformation or increases ligand binding, as ligands themselves can also activate integrins (PMID: 38772370). 
9. Some minor comments: 
- Line 143: Is the end-to-side ratio of ~1.55 significant enough compared to enrichment in focal adhesions? 
- Line 144: Focal adhesions formed along the nanobar sidewall are not visible. 
- Line 330: “Bridget” -> “bridge” 
- Including the exact amino acid sequences near the transmembrane domain of the chimeric integrins (β5-5-3, β5-3-3, β5-3-
5, β3-3-5, β3-5-5) shown in Figure 5 would provide more informative detail. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments to Lu and co-workers (FCHo2-manuscript) : 
This manuscript is a continuation of the initial description of curved adhesion formed by the integrin avb5 on surfaces with
positive nanostructures, or fibrous extracellular material (Zhang et al., 2023). In the initial paper, curved adhesion recruited
talin and FCHo2, and now the current manuscript aims to better understand the relative recruitment of these two adapter
proteins. Thus, the authors analyze how the interplay of talin and FCHo2 is modifying the formation of avb5-containing focal
adhesion versus curved adhesions. On flat surfaces, the authors propose that FCHo2 is potentially excluded by Talin-
binding to the juxtamembrane (JM) region of b5-integrin, as long as a critical tyrosine residue in the vicinity of the membrane
distal NPLY-motif is not-phosphorylated. Upon phosphorylation of this tyrosine, talin binding is potentially reduced, leading
to the preferred recruitment of avb5 to curved adhesions. Further mutational analysis of the JM-region in different b5/b3-
integrin chimeras proposes that curvature sensing and FCHo2-binding requires a conserved 
membrane-proximal HDRRE-
motif. By performing divalent cation restriction and FCHo2-siRNA treatment, the authors claim that FCHo2 is binding only to
activated integrins and is itself involved in inside-out activation of avb5, at these sites. Ultimately, with transmembrane
domain swapping experiments the authors propose that the b5-transmembrane domain is also regulating the ability of the
avb5 integrin to localize to curved adhesions. The manuscript appears technically sound, describing a potentially new type
of focal adhesions formed by avb5 integrins, and its regulation by a new type of curvature-sensing adapter protein. 
Nevertheless, I would like to propose a few additional experiments, to make sure that the claim by the authors of differential,
and competitive binding of talin and FCHo2 to the JM domain of avb5 can be affirmed. Alternatively, a model in which the
synergy between talin and FCHo2 leads to the formation of very stable adhesions could be a mechanisms how to explain
the extraordinary stability of these curved adhesion (80 min). It would also be very good if the latter proposal of adhesion
stability would be confirmed by more quantitative measurements involving fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, or
fluorescence activation measurements. 
Major issues. 
Throughout the manuscript, the authors are comparing talin1 association with that of FCHo2 for the JM domain of avb5. In
previous studies in U2OS-cells, talin-1 was excluded from avb5 integrins that participated in reticular adhesions. Instead
tensin-3 and talin-2 showed strong co-localization with this particular adhesion type in these cells. It is not yet clear why
talin-2 and or tensin-3 show a preference for avb5 in reticular adhesions, and do not co-localize with avb3, the main other
integrin used in this study. For completion, it would be really important to inform the readers, whether curved adhesions can
recruit both or either one of these adapter proteins. Thus transfection with talin-2 RFP, or tensin-3 GFP would be helpful to
identify if these proteins can be recruited to curved adhesions as well. 
Furthermore in figure 1, b5-5-3 constructs are used to eliminate talin-1 binding by Y774A-mutation, which then still resulted
in curved adhesion recruitment of the latter integrin. In the past it has been shown that equivalent avb3-Y774A mutants can
still recruit and co-cluster with the talin-1-head domain when activated with Mn2+ (Saltel et al., 2009, cited). Because of the
possible recruitment of talin to the JM-region independent of Y774A, it would be relevant to exclude that not talin-2 is
occupying the place vacated by talin-1. As only complete double talin-depletion results in the loss of adhesion, it might well

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

be possible that FCHo2 is still recruited to a JM/talin-head complex in a membrane curvature-dependent manner, but
independent of the Y774-residue. 
To make sure the reason for FCHo2 exclusion from focal adhesions is not only due to talin competition, but rather a lack of
membrane-recruitment, it would be important to exchange the lipid-sensing domain of FCHo2 (F-bar-domain), with that of a
relevant PIP2-sensing domain. This would be the appropriate control, in order to affirm that the missing localization of
FCHo2 at focal adhesions is due to lack of membrane interaction, as compared to a lack of competition with talin-binding to
integrins. 
Figure 3: the c-terminal truncation analysis is helpful to see the adhesion phenotype in Fig3 B, and C. However when the
point mutations in the HDRRE region are analyzed, this is done in the context of the FL-construct of b5-integrin (panels I-L),
favoring the overall talin binding to the cytoplasmic tail. Therefore the sensitivity of the two recruitment assays are not
equivalent, the analysis represented in panels I-L should be performed with a truncation mutant (e.g. 1-759), which forms the
minimal FCHo2 binding site, with neglectable interference by talin-recruitment. 
In the past JM-domain mutations have been performed to characterize the linkage between b3-integrin and talin1-head
domain. While certain studies showed relevance of the di-phe-motif for talin/b3-binding, other studies showed an
electrostatic interaction between K316 in talin-1 and b3-E726 (E753 in this study), which could be swapped between the two
partners (Saltel et al., 2009). While the E753K mutation perturbs curved adhesion formation, and FCHo2 recruitment, the
authors could co-express talin-1 (K316E), to rescue the talin-1 binding defect. If this complementation by talin (K316E) is
unable to rescue the curved adhesion defect, the authors can clearly claim the independent association of talin-1 and
FCHo2. At the current state of the mutagenesis study of the HDRRE-motif, it is not clear whether and how the point-
mutations are directly or indirectly affecting the talin-1 interaction in the JM-region (as for example shown for the D750A
mutant). Furthermore, as the talin/JM interaction involves only one side of the helical JM-domain, it might well be possible
that the other side (e.g. R751 and R752) can be contacted by the adapter protein FCHo2, in a synergistic manner. 
Previously, the activation and association of talin with integrin avb3 has been probed with talin-head fragments only, and
also modulated by the addition of Mn2+ to keep avb3 in an open conformation. Since avb5 has always been detected in
more central cellular locations, (compared to avb3 localized to peripheral adhesions), its basal activation state is likely
increased, when compared to avb3. Based on the authors analysis with the chimeric integrins, it appears that the
transmembrane domain of b5, has the capacity to shift the conformational equilibrium of the ectodomain to the open
conformation, allowing for enhanced outside-in activation of avb5 over avb3. Have the authors attempted to reveal the
curved adhesions recruitment of avb3, with Mn2+ treatment (and on vitronectin surfaces)? 
When expressed as a full-length protein, talin1 is autoinhibited, and part of its activation mechanisms requires binding to
acidic lipids. Truncating the basic residues in the head-domain will actually not fully reveal their potential importance, in the
recruitment or activity of integrins in curved adhesions, as the talin protein remains inactiv and hence integrin binding is
obscured. In the past, talin1/b3-interaction studies have therefore been performed with the truncated talin head domain, or
more recently with a full-length version of talin-1 mutated in the R9-bundle. It would be interesting to see whether dominant
active forms of talin-1 or talin-2 (e.g. activate at the autoinhibitory site in the R9-bundle) would be able to perturb recruitment
of avb5 to curved adhesions. 
Minor issues: 
Figure 3: It would be good to indicate the position and sequence of the peptide linkers used for the GFP-fusion constructs in
figure 3A. 
Figure 5 (but also figure 1). It is not fair to use fibronectin as a ligand for avb3, as this FN-binding requires force for avb3
activation (Bachmann et al., JCS 2020; induction of ligand promiscuity of avb3 by force…). Therefore, the authors should
also use vitronectin coating for the analysis of avb3 integrin recruitment to curved adhesion and could then also use
relatively easy ways to modulate the activation state of the integrin (e.g. +/- Mn2+). 
Figure 5: the b3-5-3 mutant is missing in the analysis, is it equivalent to the b3-5-5? please explain. 
Line 556: “cured” should probably read “curved”.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The present study is based on a previous work of the same lab reporting « curvature-dependent integrin-mediated
adhesions called curved adhesions » published in Nature Cell Biology in 2022. In this previous paper the authors showed
that the curved adhesions recruit integrin 
ɑ
vβ5 and talin (but not vinculin and pFAK), bear mechanical forces (but lower than
focal adhesions) and are molecularly distinct from focal adhesions and clathrin lattices by involving a a curvature-sensing
protein, FCHo2 interacting with integrin β5. 
Herein, using the same kind of nanostructure platform to induce well-defined membrane curvatures in live cells, the authors
proposes a molecular basis for discriminating focal adhesions from curved adhesions and the exclusive formation of curved
adhesions by integrin 
ɑ
vβ5. Having previously shown that curved adhesions require the juxtamembrane region of ITGβ5

cytoplasmic tail, the authors designed truncations and mutations at the level of ITGβ5 cytoplasmic tail. They have identified
the tyrosine (Y766) as specific residues in β5 tail. This tyrosine is replaced by a tryptophan (W) which is conserved in all
homologous integrin β isoforms. The presence of tyrosine Y766 and its phosphorylated state are crucial for the localisation
of β5 integrin in curved adhesions while its replacement by the tryptophan residue does not impair β5 integrin localization in
focal adhesions. Disrupting the 
ɑ
β salt bridge is not sufficient for the formation of curved adhesions, while FCHo2 binding to
the HDRRE region is crucial. Talin is recruited in both focal adhesions and curved adhesions. Talin binding is crucial for
integrin activations in focal adhesions but it is dispensable in curved adhesions. In curved adhesions, talin would with
HDRRE motif to consequently activate 
ɑ
vβ5 integrin independently on talin. The experiments are well designed. However
the biological or physiological relevance is missing and should be analyzed a little be further. 
As already reported by the same lab, curved adhesions bear mechanical forces. 
-Using vertical nanopillars arrays from fig.3D is somehow puzzling. Does it mean that some conditions are not working with
previous nanobars? 
-Is the engagement of talin to only MD site sufficient to induce force generation (even low) in curved adhesions ? How are
these forces generated ? If FCHo2 is an alternative to talin, capable of inducing inside-out integrin activations selectively at
curved membranes, what are the consequences on myosin and actin in the model of curved adhesions ? Do we expect any
change of actin organization, myosin distribution or even force dysregulation ? All the mutants designed in this study are
available to perform experiments related to actin and force regulation and give an answer about actin/myosin based force in
curved adhesion. 
-In case of non-involvement of actin, one can consider septin giving the septin-bundling activity of FCHo2 (PMID:
36476870). 
-In addition, Tensin shares the same binding site as talin includind W residues (see Legate and Fassler, JCS PMID:
19118211) and they can be mutually exclusive. Moreover, keratin network is as an integral part of force-sensing elements
recruiting distinct proteins like tensin (PMID: 31527270; PMCID). Is tensin (and possibly keratin) an alternative to talin (and
actin) by working in concert with FCHo2 ? This would greatly complete this study to better discriminate curved adhesions
from focal adhesions and to identify an alternative for force generation involving FCHo2 and not talin. 
Knowing that curved adhesions facilitate cell migration in 3D ECMs through the involvement of integrin β5 and FCHo2 as
already reported by Cui’s lab, the manuscript should integrate the effect of the mutants of both FCHo2 and ITGβ5
cytoplasmic tail in cell migration in 3D in order to correlate their molecular findings with biological process and in particular
in cancer cell invasion. The phosphomutants FCHo2 might be tested in comparison with β5 integrin mutants (comparison
between mutants able or unable to interact with FCHo2 or talin or both). Moreover, considering the role of FCHo2 in soft
matrix the state of phosphorylation of FCHo2 might be tested in condition of soft matrix versus stiff matrix. In other terms, the
phosphorylation state of Y766 might control cell migration between stiff and soft environnement where focal adhesions and
curved adhesions were respectively occuring. It would be interesting to check.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This largely revised manuscript has clarified major points of criticisms and questions to which extent talin1 is contributing to
the avb5 integrin activation by FCHo2 in curved adhesion. The authors took a lot of care to distinguish the talin-mediated
activation mechanisms of avb5 on flat surfaces from that of the curved adhesions. The current model is compelling although
some questions are still outstanding such as the particular role of the transmembrane domain of b5-integrin, or investigations
on the lipid-binding preference of the F-BAR domain of FCHo2. However, this reviewer is of the opinion that such analysis
would be beyond the scope of this manuscript. 
At the current state the manuscript offers a new vision and function of avb5 integrin that is not due to classical set of adapter
proteins found for other integrin-dependent adhesions. The role for Y766 of b5 to serve as a phosphorylation-mediated
switch in adapter proteins is a potentially very important finding considering its potential regulation by intracellular kinase
signaling. The current hypothesis provided by the authors clearly opens the field for more structural and regulatory work on
this specific potentially highly relevant switch for the behavior of avb5. 
By reading the manuscript I identified a typographic error in line 439: “viculin” should likely read “vinculin”

I would like to thank the authors for their efforts in responding to the experts' requests. The authors have significantly
improved their manuscript by testing hypotheses and incorporating new experiments. I am entirely satisfied with the

The authors evidence different molecular interactions involved in curved adhesions and focal adhesions. FCHo2 is an
alternative to talin, capable of inside-out integrin activation selectively at curved membranes which allows to identify the
specificity of b5 integrin for curved adhesions as compared to b3 integrins.
