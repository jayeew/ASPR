Rydberg gas at room temperature. By applying a radio-frequency (RF) electric field near the system's intrinsic oscillation

locking, exhibiting canonical nonlinear synchronization . The main findings of this paper include: (1) The observation of
injection pulling (IP), the critical locking thresholds and synchronization (inject locking, IL) in spectrograms. (2) Entrainment
of higher-order harmonics, confirming control over the full nonlinear temporal structure;

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled “Injection Locking of Rydberg Dissipative Time Crystals” by Darmindra Arumugam presents an
observation of phenomena analogous to injection locking in optical systems, or more broadly, the phase-locking 
of
synchronized coupled oscillators. Phase locking occurs when a nonlinear system is subjected to an external drive with a
frequency near its intrinsic natural frequency. In this study, the intrinsic frequency arises from strong Rydberg interactions,
leading to competition among Rydberg sublevels and consequently supporting self-sustained oscillations in the Rydberg
density signal. In the literature, this is often referred to as a dissipative time crystal (DTC) in Rydberg gas systems. 
The originality of this manuscript lies in its investigation of the frequency pulling effect induced by an externally injected
radio-frequency (RF) electric field. The authors thoroughly examine various features of the injection locking effect, such as
frequency pulling and locking, the dependence of locking bandwidth on field power, and the locking of higher-order
harmonics, all of which clearly support their claims. Overall, I found the experimental and theoretical techniques employed
robust and appropriate, however, the observation of injection-locked effects in a nonlinear Rydberg system should perhaps
not be considered entirely unexpected. 
Please find the following questions and comments: 
1. A more comprehensive discussion of related works would benefit the reader and provide the manuscript with a stronger
foundation. In particular, the authors may consider addressing the following points: 
i. It would be valuable to elaborate on the relationship between the injection locking phenomenon described in this work and
the origin of self-sustained oscillations from optical bistability observed in Rydberg vapor system, as reported in [R1] and
[R2] (ref. 4 of the manuscript). 
ii. 
In addition, a recent study on external RF field-induced dissipative time crystals, presented in [R3], merits mention and
discussion in the context of the current findings. 
2. Although injection locking is a phenomenon commonly observed in classical nonlinear systems, as the authors have
noted, the Rydberg system studied here is inherently quantum. Therefore, in addition to the mean-field approach, it would be
valuable to discuss the role of intrinsic quantum noise as well as the impact of technical noise in the experimental realization
of phase-locking phenomena in this system. 
3. Could the authors clarify the meaning of the term “temporal quantum order” as mentioned in the abstract? Is it appropriate
to classify temporal order in this manner? 
4. One characteristic of the entrainment effect is that the injected frequency can be close to a higher harmonic of the
oscillator’s natural frequency. Have the authors investigated the scenario where the RF frequency is, for example, near 2 ×
11.2 kHz? According to [R4], such a configuration could also result in frequency locking. 
Minor comments: 
1. The abbreviation "OSC" is used inconsistently throughout the manuscript. In the Introduction, it refers to "self-sustained
oscillations," while in the Results section it denotes "spontaneously generated oscillations," "system’s intrinsic oscillations,"
and possibly other meanings in various parts of the manuscript. 
2. The formatting style of the reference list is inconsistent. 
References cited in this report:

87, 080502 (2024).

Comment: 1. What does the empirical factor of 0.11 mentioned in the bandwidth relationship 
mean? What is the meaning of the alpha=1 curve in Fig. 4(b)?

Response: The empirical factor α=0.11 enters the expression Δω=κE, κ=α(Kdind/ℏ), where dind is 
the induced dipole moment of the Rydberg transition and K is the coupling coefficient relating 
the applied RF field to the optical phase dynamics. 
In the simplest mean-field derivation of the V-type phase equation, one obtains a 
proportionality constant of order unity. In other words, at the level of the reduced phase model, 
the natural prediction corresponds to α = 1. That curve is plotted in Fig. 4(b) as the “unscaled” 
theoretical bandwidth, and it illustrates the raw prediction of the minimal analytical model. 
However, this unscaled curve overestimates the measured locking bandwidth by nearly an order 
of magnitude. This difference reflects the fact that the reduced phase equation ignores several 
experimentally relevant effects, including inhomogeneous broadening of the atomic response, 
finite optical coherence time in the warm vapor, competition among multiple coupled optical 
pathways, imperfect mapping between the applied RF field and the internal V-type phase 
variable, and reduction in effective dipole response due to Doppler averaging. 
To account for these experimentally observed reductions in effective coupling strength, the 
factor α=0.11 is introduced through empirical fit. It represents the net reduction in coupling 
efficiency between the applied RF field and the internal oscillator phase relative to the idealized 
theory. When this empirically determined factor is applied, the predicted locking bandwidth 
matches the measured values across the full range of injected fields. Thus: α = 1 gives the 
unscaled prediction from the simplest analytical limit derived from the mean-field model, 
whereas α = 0.11 gives the experimentally determined effective coupling factor that 
incorporates real-system reductions in phase-response strength. 
This empirical factor therefore does not introduce a new physical mechanism; it simply 
quantifies how strongly the experimental system couples compared to the idealized theoretical 
limit.

Comment: 3. The bandwidth relationship in this paper is consistent with Adler's injection 
locking for classical oscillators because the author used the mean-field approximation to obtain 
a system of nonlinear differential equations, which is mathematically similar to the classical 
case. However, as a quantum system, what are the unique characteristics compared with the

Response: Although the reduced mean-field phase equation has the same mathematical 
structure as Adler’s equation for a classical nonlinear oscillator, the underlying physical system 
here is fundamentally different, and several uniquely quantum features play an essential role in 
the formation and control of the dissipative time-crystal (DTC) state. 
(1) Origin of the oscillation: quantum optical Bloch dynamics, not classical circuit nonlinearities 
In this work the self-sustained oscillation arises from population–coherence feedback within a 
three-level V-type Rydberg system driven by two coherent optical fields. The oscillation 
frequency and limit cycle are set by the light-shifted Rydberg energy levels, the induced dipole 
moment, the optical pumping rates, and the decoherence of quantum superpositions. This is 
fundamentally different from classical oscillators, whose dynamics originate from macroscopic 
LC, mechanical, or electronic nonlinearities. The nonlinear phase evolution here is rooted in 
quantum coherence and optical dressing, not in classical signal propagation. 
(2) The forcing mechanism is the response of a quantum coherence to an external RF field 
The injected RF field modulates the Rydberg coherence and the AC Stark energy splitting, not a 
classical state variable. The phase variable in the reduced model is therefore the phase of a 
quantum superposition, not the phase of a classical voltage or displacement. Thus, even though

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

the mean-field reduction yields a classical-looking equation, the “oscillator” being synchronized 
is intrinsically quantum. 
(3) Dissipation and dephasing act on the quantum state, not on a classical trajectory 
The DTC limit cycle exists due to a balance between optical pumping, decoherence, Rydberg–
Rydberg interaction–induced feedback, and induced dipole response to the RF field. All of these 
act on density-matrix elements, not classical amplitudes. The oscillation amplitude and locking 
threshold are therefore set by quantum decay rates (γ), not classical loss coefficients. 
(4) The time-crystal regime itself is uniquely quantum 
A classical nonlinear oscillator can lock, but it cannot exhibit a dissipative time-crystal phase, 
which requires spontaneous breaking of continuous time-translation symmetry, stabilization of 
a subharmonic response, sustained oscillations emerging from driven–dissipative quantum 
feedback. Classical injection locking describes the synchronous behavior once oscillations exist, 
but it does not explain the origin of the oscillation itself, which in this system is quantum in 
nature. 
(5) Many-body Rydberg interactions are quantum and have no classical analog 
The induced dipole response involves Rydberg-Rydberg interactions, dressing of the excited 
states, and collective response of strongly interacting atoms. These change the effective 
nonlinearity of the phase equation in ways that do not have classical equivalent models. 
Even though the reduced phase equation matches the Adler form, the system remains uniquely 
quantum because: 
1. The oscillation originates from quantum coherence and optical dressing, not classical 
nonlinearities. 
2. The injected RF field perturbs a quantum superposition, not a classical state variable. 
3. Dissipation, dephasing, and feedback operate on density-matrix dynamics, not classical 
loss terms. 
4. The DTC phase is a purely quantum driven–dissipative phenomenon. 
5. Rydberg interactions modify the nonlinearity in a way that has no direct classical analog. 
These features make the injection-locked DTC fundamentally distinct from classical Adler 
locking, even though the mathematical form of the reduced phase equation is similar. 
 
Comment: 4. The author repeatedly mentions that injection locking is an "abrupt" change, but 
from the experimental data graphs  
(e.g., Fig. 1e), this change does not appear very sudden. Can the author explain the reason in 
detail?

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 13 -->

Response: In the revised manuscript I clarify that the abruptness refers to the underlying phase 
dynamics described by the reduced phase equation, rather than to the appearance of the 
spectral peak in a slow sweep measurement. In Adler-type phase equations (including the 
reduced mean-field V-type model derived here), the transition from frequency pulling to phase 
locking occurs through a bifurcation in the phase slip rate ⟨𝜙̇ ⟩. At the locking threshold, this rate 
collapses discontinuously to zero. In this theoretical sense, the transition is “abrupt.” 
Experimentally, however, the system is observed through the time-averaged frequency content 
of a continuously swept or stepped measurement. Several effects therefore smooth the 
appearance of the transition: 
1. Finite sweep rate and averaging time. 
In Fig. 1(f), for example, the injected frequency is stepped through resonance in discrete 
increments, and each spectrum is integrated over a finite time window. If the sweep is 
slower than the intrinsic phase-dynamics timescale, the sharp theoretical change is 
broadened into a gradual spectral shift. 
2. Spectral resolution limits and FFT windowing. 
The oscillation frequency is extracted from FFT slices with finite spectral resolution. The 
discrete frequency binning naturally broadens the transition region, making an 
intrinsically sharp change look gradual. 
3. Noise and decoherence in the warm vapor. 
Near the locking boundary, fluctuations in coherence (due to Doppler averaging, 
dephasing, and transit-time noise) cause the pulled peak to jitter, which smooths the 
transition when spectra are averaged. 
4. Competition between injected and free-running components. 
Just below the locking threshold both the natural oscillation and the injection-shifted 
component can coexist with comparable power. This coexistence region gives the visual

Response: The author appreciates the reviewer’s request for a deeper discussion of noise in the

1. Intrinsic quantum noise. 
The dominant quantum noise channels in the three-level Rydberg system are 
spontaneous emission, optical coherence decay, and quantum phase diffusion of the 
excited-state superposition. These processes appear in the optical Bloch equations as 
decay/dephasing rates (Γ, γ) and set the fundamental coherence time and minimal 
linewidth of the limit cycle. In a room-temperature vapor, however, quantum noise is 
typically weaker than classical dephasing mechanisms (Doppler broadening, transit-time 
averaging, collisional dephasing). As a result, the limit-cycle oscillation is well described 
by a mean-field phase equation, and quantum shot noise does not dominate the 
observed phase stability. 
2. Technical noise. 
Technical noise arises from experimental and environmental sources, including 
probe/coupler intensity noise, temperature-dependent atomic-density fluctuations, 
magnetic-field drift, and beam-alignment fluctuations. These act primarily as low-
frequency phase-noise perturbations by modulating optical depth, detuning, and the 
effective Rydberg interaction strength. Prior work on warm-vapor DTCs (e.g., Wu et al. 
Nat. Phys. 2024; Liu et al. Nat. Commun. 2025) has demonstrated that the limit cycle 
possesses nonlinear rigidity: weak technical perturbations do not destroy time-
crystalline order, although strong noise perturbations (e.g., Wu et al. Nat. Phys. 2024) 
can reduce the crystalline fraction. This establishes the stability of the DTC against 
realistic technical noise.
