Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript provides a comprehensive and interdisciplinary review of single-photon emitters (SPEs) based on organic
color centers (OCCs) in single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs). By integrating perspectives from synthetic chemistry,
computational modeling, and quantum optics, the authors convincingly argue that SWCNT OCCs are a promising platform
for room-temperature, telecom-wavelength single-photon generation. The review is exceptionally well-referenced, timely,
and accessible to researchers across quantum photonics, materials science, and nanotechnology. 
Before supporting the publication, I have a few questions and suggestions for the improvement of the presentation of the
work. 
1. While the advantages are clear, a more direct, quantitative comparison table contrasting key metrics (e.g., brightness,
indistinguishability, operating temperature, spectral stability) of SWCNT SPEs with other solid-state SPEs (quantum dots,
diamond NV centers, 2D material emitters) would be immensely helpful for readers to assess the competitive landscape. 
2. Although open questions are mentioned, a dedicated section on scalability, reproducibility, and long-term stability under
continuous excitation would provide a more balanced view. In particular, the heterogeneity in emission properties remains a
hurdle for large-scale device integration. 
3. There is a lack of images in Figure 1.

Reviewer #4 (Remarks to the Author): 
Chang et al. provide a comprehensive and timely review of recent progress on single-photon emission (SPE) from
functionalized SWCNTs, also known as organic color centers. The authors systematically discuss the chemical
functionalization strategies, excitonic mechanisms, theoretical modeling and photonic integration approaches that underpin
the development of CNT based SPS. Particulaly, the review successfully positions SWCNT-OCC systems as promising
candidates for room-temperature and telecom-band SPS which is an area where few solid-state SPS platforms satisfy both
criteria. 
The review is well organized, and the scope across synthesis, computation, and photonic integration strategies is
impressive. It will serve as a valuable reference for researchers in quantum photonics community. However, several
important aspects should be clarified or expanded to strengthen the paper’s impact and to make the discussion more
quantitative and balanced. If the authors can address the points below, I would recommend this manuscript for publication in
Communications Materials. 
Detailed comments are as follows. 
1) While the review highlights the advantages of CNT-based emitters, it lacks a systematic comparison with other major
single-photon source materials such as NV/SiV centers in diamond, V1/V2 centers in SiC, hBN defects, and III–V quantum
dots. A concise benchmarking table comparing key performance metrics such as emission wavelength range, g²(0),
linewidth, coherence time (T
₂
), HOM visibility, brightness, operation temperature, and fabrication scalability would greatly
improve the reader’s understanding of where SWCNT-OCCs stand in the current landscape. The discussion should also
clarify which aspects (e.g., telecom compatibility, chemical tunability, or room-temperature operation) are quantitatively
superior, and which remain limited (e.g., uniformity, coherence, deterministic placement). 
2) The paper’s tone is highly optimistic but underplays the remaining bottlenecks that prevent CNT-based SPEs from
becoming mainstream. The authors should explicitly discuss issues such as spectral inhomogeneity due to multiple bonding
configurations, low deterministic yield of single emitters, environmental instability (adsorption, dielectric fluctuation), and
challenges in achieving high photon indistinguishability at room temperature. A critical evaluation of these limitations,
supported by representative quantitative data (e.g., linewidth distribution, emission yield statistics), would make the review
more balanced and instructive. 
3) The section on integrated photonic devices and electrically driven CNT-based SPEs remains descriptive. The authors
should provide more detailed discussion on actual device architectures, coupling efficiencies, and fabrication routes (e.g.,
waveguide integration, fiber-coupled cavities, or plasmonic antennas).

6) Is it possible to use plasma treated CNT as single-photon sources? 
Author Response: We appreciate the Reviewer’s insightful question. Plasma treatment 
can indeed introduce sp3 defects; however, the resulting defect density and bonding 
configurations are typically poorly controlled. As a result, plasma-treated CNTs have not 
yet demonstrated the deterministic, isolated emissive sites required for reliable single-
photon sources.  
 
7) What are the real-life difficulties that experimentators faced in making single-source 
emitters and their measurements? 
Author Response: We thank the Reviewer for raising this important practical question. 
In the revised manuscript, we have added a discussion highlighting key experimental 
challenges in realizing and chacterizing single photon source emitters, including: 1. 
spectral inhomogeneity arising from multiple defect bonding geometries and local 
structural variability; 2. low deterministic yield of isolated OCC emitters; 3. spectral 
instability such as adsorbate-induced wandering and blinking; 4. limited room-
temperature indistinguishability due to strong exciton–phonon dephasing; and 5. 
intrinsically low quantum yield in 1D excitonic systems. 
These considerations and their implications for the practical implementation of single-
photon sources are now discussed in the experimental sections of the revised 
manuscript.

Author Response: We thank the Reviewer for this helpful suggestion. Alignment and 
bundling are indeed critical issues for CNT network-based SPEs. Poor alignment can 
limit efficient optical coupling to photonic structures, while bundling can suppress 
emission due to intertube exciton quenching. In the revised manuscript, we have added 
a brief discussion highlighting how these morphological factors affect the performance 
and scalability of CNT-network devices in section IV.

11) What are the possible rotes towards the mass production of such devices? 
Author Response: We thank the Reviewer for this forward-looking question. While a 
fully established route to mass production of SWCNT–OCC single-photon emitter 
devices is not yet available, several promising approaches point toward scalable 
implementation. Deterministic transfer and cavity-coupling strategies have enabled the 
integration of individual SWCNT emitters with photonic cavities and waveguides, 
significantly enhancing emission rates and collection efficiency, as recently 
demonstrated for OCC emitters by Hüsel et al. (Nat. Commun. 2024). In parallel, 
photochemical methods that create a single OCC site per nanotube offer a potential 
solution to the challenge of multiple-emitter formation (Nat. Commun. 13, 2814 (2022)). 
 
Importantly, recent demonstrations of electrically driven single-photon emission from 
OCCs represent a critical step toward device-compatible architectures (ACS Nano 18, 
9525–9534 (2024)). Looking ahead, scalable lithographic approaches, potentially 
extending established nanotube patterning and integration strategies demonstrated by 
Huang et al. (Adv. Mater. 2020), could enable the parallel fabrication and deterministic 
positioning of OCC-based emitters on photonic chips. In addition, advances in the 
growth and assembly of aligned SWCNT arrays may further facilitate large-area 
integration (Nano Lett. 8, 700–705 (2008)). Together, these developments outline 
complementary and realistic routes toward the scalable production of SWCNT-based 
single-photon emitter devices.

In terms of spectral linewidth, SWCNT OCCs exhibit values in the range of 2.5 –20 meV at 
cryogenic temperatures 108,144, which are significantly broader than the µeV -scale linewidths 
observed in NV centers in diamond, V⁻ centers in SiC, and h-BN defects145,155,171. Their linewidths 
are nevertheless narrower than, or comparable to, those of perovskite quantum dots (35 –65 meV 
at room temperature; 1 –5 meV at cryogenic temperatures) 160,161 and defect -bound excitons in 
monolayer TMDs (~33 meV at room temperature; ~4 meV at cryogenic temperatures) 165. These 
linewidths reflect both strong exciton –phonon coupling in 1D materials and local structural 
variability introduced by covalent functionalization.  
The most significant limitation of SWCNT OCCs remains their relatively low quantum yield 
(~16%)25, which is substantially lower than that of NV centers, SiC defects, perovskite quantum
