Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, the authors present a reaction network that uses competing displacement reactions and a DNA
polymerization/nicking module to create an "arbitrarily" multi-stable system. They build up their system step-by-step: 
1. Starting with a simple equilibrium between two competing displacement reactions, balancing two states (AA' + BB' and
AB' + BA'). 
2. Adding a nicking/polymerase sub-system to one of the two states, creating a far-from-equilibrium bi-stable system. 
3. Implementing the nicking/polymerase sub-system for both states, leading to the desired multi-stable system. The system
self-regulates to create a steady state at the initially present concentrations of the two states, whatever this may be. 
4. Adding a submodule that uses complementary RNA strands to stabilize either state, which can be removed using RNase
H, effectively allowing the shift of the relative amount of each state. 
The authors also show a more complex system with the same sub-systems that has three competing states instead of two.
Furthermore, the authors implement two functional downstream reactions that do not influence the multi-stable system itself. 
The work is novel, showing a new path to multi-stability which does not require increasingly complex reaction networks to
add additional states, and instead uses a more straightforward and modular approach to achieving multi-stability through
competing displacement reactions and DNA polymerization/nicking modules. 
However, I am somewhat concerned about the fundamental thesis of this manuscript: is "arbitrary multi-stability" truly useful?
Many of the interesting properties of bi- and multi-stability are caused by the discrete nature of the stable states: switching
behavior, hysteresis, oscillations, stabilization under perturbations, memory, etc. More stable points are interesting, sure, but
when transitions are continuous, like in this system, have we not lost much of the interesting behavior of these systems?
What exactly is the added benefit of this system compared to mixing two DNA strands directly without using a complex
dissipative system? In my opinion, this point must 
be properly addressed in the manuscript. 
On top of that I have some other major points: 
I would like to see at least one steady state for the three-module network that is not at the edge of the triangle in Fig. 5d. 
In all cases I see here, the modules (let's say AA' + BB') start out balanced: there is an equal amount of AA' and BB'. Is this
required for the system to work? What happens when we create an imbalanced stoichiometry, keeping the total
stoichiometry correct by applying an inverse perturbation on the AB'/BA' module? 
Possibly related to that: the data shows that the two components in a module do not always have the same concentrations,
sometimes showing quite a big difference, see for instance Fig. 4e. What is the origin of this? 
Many plots showing the development of the steady-states do not seem to be truly steady and show drift of a few percent per
hour even after 12 hours (Fig. 3d S1-1 for instance). Is this a question of waiting longer for true stability, are these effects
from consumption of dNTPs, aging reagents, or is something else going on?

The authors use a kinetic model to predict the behavior of the system, and mention it "aligns closely" with the experimental
system. However, in several plots it is clear that the model deviates and does not fit even qualitatively (Fig. 3c-v, Fig. 3d-S1-
5, Sup. Fig. 9c&d, Sup. Fig. 8d, Sup. Fig. 12a, Sup. Fig. 17f). I realize the deviations are not too big, but can the authors
formulate a reason why this is the case? 
Is there a reason why the rTaa/rTab & RNase H reactions are not investigated with the kinetic model? The relevant rate
constants seem to be determined in Sup. Fig. 20. 
I wonder about the robustness of the system. The authors convincingly show that a steady state is found at or around the
initial composition of the system, but how robust is this when concentrations of the various catalytic components, dNTPs,
etc., are varied/consumed? A small discussion in the SI would be appreciated. I realize some data is included (Sup. Fig. 13-
18), but some written discussion would be helpful. 
Fig. 3d, bottom row, second from the left as well as Fig. 5d (and their equivalent plots in Fig. 1a&b): These plots do not add
anything and suggest a higher dimensional system than is actually presented. For Fig. 3, it is really not clear what the
message of the plot is supposed to be anyway. In Fig. 5, a more appropriate way of showing this would be a ternary plot,
which correctly shows 2 orthogonal dimensions instead of 3 - which the authors do actually use in Fig. 1b.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, the authors present some observations about a network based on polymerization nicking reaction on two
templates competing for a single trigger (where the stoichiometry is balanced by introduction of a non-extendable pseudo-
trigger). The polymerization/nickase cycles slow down the trigger exchange reaction, but does not abolishes it on longtime
scales, a phenomenon that the author wrongly interpret as a new form of multistability. However, continuous multistablity
does not exist mathematically. Slow relaxation does. In addition it is inappropriate to discuss steady states (and hence their
stability) in a system where some of the variables (here compounds) can only increase, which is the case here for strand P,
which is continuously produced and accumulates. Furthermore mathematical support for multistability may not come from
kinetic ODE fitting but from Linear Stability Analysis (LSA), which I encourage the author to try (once they have corrected
their kinetic model). If the authors prefer an experimental test, they can also attempt 
to adjust the balance between
polymerization and nicking activities, upon which they will probably find that the system does relax to a single AA’/AB’ ratio,
which a time scale that decrease with pol/nick ratio, but this does not mean the system is multistable (the situation presented
in Fig 3a being the limit pol/nick->0). 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Zhong et al. introduces a novel strategy for constructing multistable biochemical networks using
reversible DNA strand displacement combined with polymerization/nicking modules. While the authors propose an
ambitious framework for achieving arbitrarily programmable multistability, several critical aspects require clarification to fully
support the claims. Notably, the extent and scalability of this "arbitrarily programmable" capability—specifically, how many
distinct steady states can be robustly engineered and maintained—remain unclear. Regrettably, the current evidence does
not sufficiently validate the authors' central thesis. Below are my specific concerns: 
1. The current schematic is challenging to interpret. To improve accessibility, the authors should explicitly label the 3′ and 5′
ends of DNA strands to indicate polymerase extension directions. Additional molecular details in the diagram would help
readers visualize the design principles. 
2. The domain-level labels on DNA complexes are insufficiently clear. Revising the figures to highlight domain names with
distinct colors or fonts would aid reader comprehension. 
3. The description leaves uncertainty about whether the four duplexes (AA', BB', BA', AB') are pre-annealed or formed
spontaneously from single strands. Clarifying this hierarchy in the text is essential for reconstructing the system’s operational
logic. 
4. The distinction between AA'-P1/AB'-P2 and AA'/AB' remains unclear. If these complexes are functionally equivalent,
consistent naming would reduce confusion. If not, a side-by-side comparison of their structural differences should be
provided. 
5. The manuscript does not sufficiently explain the driving forces behind the reversible transitions (AA'+BB' -- BA'+AB').

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

Specifically: What thermodynamic or kinetic factors enable bidirectional conversion? How does AA'P1 formation selectively
inhibit the forward reaction? A mechanistic diagram or kinetic model would help justify this design. 
6. The assertion that dNTP hydrolysis suppresses spontaneous interconversion lacks experimental or theoretical support in
the "Design" section. The authors should explicitly describe how dNTP consumption is coupled to module stabilization. 
7. The roles of strands A (BHQ2-labeled), A' (FAM), and B' (ROX) are ambiguously described. A dedicated subsection or
table mapping strands to their modifications would prevent misinterpretation of the monitoring strategy. 
8. The significant difference in equilibration kinetics between the two initial conditions (100% AA'+BB' vs. BA'+AB') appears
inconsistent with the proposed thermodynamic framework. The authors should address whether this asymmetry arises from
kinetic traps or unintended design biases. (Fig. 3a) 
9. The claim that rTAA' hybridization upregulates AA'+BB' requires mechanistic elaboration. How does AA'-rTAA' formation
preferentially stabilize specific modules? A diagram illustrating the energy landscape or binding competition would
strengthen this argument. 
10. The manuscript would benefit from exploring scenarios involving simultaneous or sequential RNA inputs: How would
competing RNA triggers at varying concentrations influence state selection? Is the system fully reversible if a second RNA is
introduced after rTAA'-induced stabilization? 
11. The observed convergence near initial concentrations raises questions about true multistability. To substantiate claims,
the authors should: Define quantitative criteria for distinguishing steady states. Compare experimental results with
theoretical predictions of bistable/multistable systems 
12. The gel lanes for DNAzyme 2 products lack sufficient contrast (Fig. 6b). Repeating the experiment with adjusted
exposure times or alternative visualization methods (e.g., SYBR Gold staining) would improve credibility.

Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for raising this fundamental and thought-

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

provoking question regarding the conceptual significance and practical utility of 
arbitrary multistability. We fully agree that classical bistable and multistable systems—
characterized by discrete attractor states—play a central role in both natural and 
synthetic biochemical circuits. These systems enable a wide range of behaviors, 
including hysteresis, memory, switch-like transitions, and robustness to perturbations. 
Such properties have been instrumental in enabling binary decisions and nonlinear 
information processing in diverse molecular and cellular contexts (e.g., genetic toggle 
switches and cell fate determination, ref. 50—Proc. Natl Acad. Sci. USA 106, 6638–
6643 (2009) and ref. 51—Science 375, eabg9765 (2022)). 
In contrast, our study explores a fundamentally different and complementary 
paradigm: continuous arbitrary multistability, where the system stabilizes not to a 
finite set of discrete states, but to any compositional states along a continuous spectrum. 
While discrete multistability is optimal for binary or categorical functions, many 
biological processes require the maintenance or encoding of graded, analog information. 
A salient example is the neural control of eye position, where the oculomotor integrator 
sustains arbitrary eye angles across a continuum (ref. 68—Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 
93, 13339–13344 (1996), ref. 69—Nat. Neurosci. 5, 775–782 (2002) and ref. 70—Curr. 
Opin. Neurobiol. 13, 204–211 (2003)). This function is achieved via neural circuits with 
continuous attractor dynamics, enabling persistent memory of analog inputs and precise 
motor control.  
Our AMN aims to mimic this mode of operation at the molecular level. Specifically, 
it allows the network to stabilize at any desired compositional state within a continuous 
range through programmable chemical interactions, allowing reversible and continuous 
transitions between steady states. Unlike traditional multistable systems with fixed 
switching thresholds, the AMN architecture supports smooth, analog modulation and 
memory, thereby endowing it with new functional modalities such as graded signal 
processing, proportional memory, and biomimetic analog computation—
capabilities not accessible with traditional discrete-state architectures. 
Notably, although the transitions in our system are continuous, the AMN retains 
several key dynamic features often associated with bistability. For example, hysteresis 
(refers to the failure of a system to return to its starting point when an external condition 
or control parameter is changed and then changed back, ref. 52—Nature 537, 656–660 
(2016) and ref. 70—Curr. Opin. Neurobiol. 13, 204–211 (2003)) is observed upon the 
modulation of the input species (e.g., rTAA'): once the system transitions to a new state 
upon rTAA' addition, the subsequent degradation of rTAA' (e.g., by RNase H) does not 
restore the original state (Fig. 4d). This path-dependent irreversibility reflects 
molecular memory, wherein the current state encodes the history of prior stimuli, a 
hallmark behavior of multistable systems. 
With respect to perturbation handling, classical discrete multistable systems tend to 
reject small perturbations by remaining within their attractor basins. In contrast, our 
AMN offers graded adaptability, dynamically adjusting its state in proportion to the 
magnitude of the input. As shown in Fig. 4f, the system can resolve a range of input 
concentrations into distinct steady states. We refer to this behavior as concentration-
adaptive molecular memory, wherein the system encodes and preserves the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

quantitative features of its environment. 
Another key strength of the AMN architecture lies in its modularity and its ability to 
connect downstream reactions without interference. In many conventional multistable 
networks, interfacing with downstream processes risks perturbing the core dynamics, 
as key intermediates must be rerouted. In contrast, our AMN system operates via a 
dNTP-fueled polymerization/nicking reaction cycle, which consumes dNTPs to 
generate single-stranded DNA waste products (e.g., P1, P2 in Fig. 6a). These ssDNA 
outputs are functionally decoupled from the multistable core and can thus be 
independently harnessed to drive downstream reaction modules without perturbing the 
system’s multistability. This waste-to-signal conversion strategy improves the 
modularity and functionality of synthetic molecular networks. 
Finally, we emphasize that the observed multistability is fundamentally dependent 
on the dissipative, far-from-equilibrium nature of the AMN. A thermodynamically 
passive mixture of DNA duplexes, lacking the energy-driven polymerase/nicking cycle, 
would invariably relax to a single thermodynamically equilibrated state, regardless of 
the initial composition (Fig. 3b and 3c and new Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, the 
continuous energy input in our system sustains multiple, programmable steady states 
(new Supplementary Figs. 13−15, see Comment 6) — an essential feature for 
mimicking the far-from-equilibrium behaviors observed in biological counterparts. 
In response to this insightful comment, we have clarified these conceptual and 
functional distinctions between discrete and continuous multistability in the revised 
manuscript (pages 4, 21, and 22). Also, we have included a discussion of the dNTP-
driven operation of the system in the revised manuscript (pages 5, 6, and 11) and in the 
new Supplementary Section 5. Furthermore, we have expanded the discussion to clarify 
the unique advantages of the AMN framework in the revised manuscript (page 18).

To evaluate the impact of imbalanced initial stoichiometries, we investigated a

global balance of the network. Consequently, a perfect restoration of overall 
stoichiometry via inverse perturbation is not achievable.

Comment 5: “Possibly related to that: the data shows that the two components in a 
module do not always have the same concentrations, sometimes showing quite a big 
difference, see for instance Fig. 4e. What is the origin of this?” 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful observation. As noted in Fig. 4e, 
the concentrations of the two DNA duplexes within a given module (e.g., AA' and BB'

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

in module AA'+BB') are initially comparable. However, upon addition of an RNA 
trigger, a marked divergence in their concentrations emerges. Below, we provide a 
mechanistic explanation, supported by thermodynamic analysis and NUPACK 
simulations, to elucidate the origin of this asymmetry.  
In AMN-1, each module consists of two DNA duplexes (e.g., AA' and BB'), which 
coexist in dynamic equilibrium with their corresponding single-stranded DNA 
components (A/A' and B/B'). We have used NUPACK to simulate the impact of RNA 
triggers on the network composition in the absence of DNA polymerase, nicking 
endonuclease, and dNTPs. NUPACK simulation (new Supplementary Fig. 3a) shows 
that, in the absence of RNA triggers, the network exists in a formation-dissociation 
equilibrium. Specifically, the concentrations of the DNA duplexes are approximately 
equal, and the concentrations of their associated single strands are approximately equal. 
Upon introduction of an RNA trigger (e.g., rTAA', new Supplementary Fig. 26b), two 
interrelated processes are selectively perturbed: (i) the reversible strand displacement 
between modules (e.g., AA'+BB' ⇌ BA'+AB'), and (ii) the intramodular equilibria 
between duplexes and their single-stranded components. RNA trigger rTAA' binds 
preferentially to its complementary sequences (either single strands A and A' or duplex 
AA'), forming a more stable hybrid complex (rTAA'AA'). This preferential binding 
effectively sequesters A and A', thereby shifting the module equilibrium to favor the 
formation of rTAA'AA' through the dissociation of BA' and AB'. Concurrently, the 
dissociation of BA' and AB' releases additional B and B' for the formation of BB'.  
Thermodynamic simulations (new Supplementary Fig. 25a) reveal that the free 
energy of rTAA'AA' (ΔG ≈ −40.88 kcal·mol−1) is more negative (i.e., more favorable) 
than that of the BB' duplex (ΔG ≈ −16.41 kcal·mol−1). This difference leads to 
preferential stabilization and accumulation of rTAA'AA', while BB' continues to 
participate in dynamic equilibria with its corresponding single strands. As a result, a 
concentration asymmetry between the duplexes gradually emerges. 
This asymmetry is further amplified over time due to a memory effect inherent to 
AMN-1: although the RNA trigger is degraded by RNase H, it can be re-added across 
multiple triggering cycles. This recursive reinforcement progressively biases the system 
toward accumulation of specific species (AA'), which is clearly observed in Fig. 4e (left 
panel). Importantly, this phenomenon is not unique to the rTAA'/AA'+BB' system. 
Similar asymmetric behaviors are observed in response to other RNA triggers, such as 
rTAB', as shown in Fig. 4d and Fig. 4e (right panel). 
To clarify this phenomenon, we have revised the manuscript accordingly (page 14).
