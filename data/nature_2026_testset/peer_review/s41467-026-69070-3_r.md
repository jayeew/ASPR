Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
report on Bell correlations between ... by Athreya et al. 
This paper reports on a two particle interferometer inspired by an optical experiment described in Ref. 17. The authors
observe a strong correlation which violates a classical locality condition. Unlike most Bell inequality experiments, this work
as well as that of Ref. 17 does not use a spin or polarization degree of freedom in the observations, rather the particles are
always in the same internal state and in a momentum entangled state. Some important differences exist between Ref. 17
and this work, having to do with how the relative phase is manipulated. These differences prevent the testing of a CHSH
inequality, but the authors show in an appendix that a related inequality is violated. The work is thus very interesting and
represents another important step in our study of quantum mechanics and its potential limits. I recommend publication, but
give a few comments below: 
For the most part the experiment, though complicated, is well described, and the data looks convincing. One question that
occurred to me was about the relationship of this work to the same group's earlier work described in Ref. 23. It seems to me
that that work used a very similar configuration. The chief difference I noticed was that the fringe contrast was much smaller,
too small to permit the kind of non-locality test described here. If I am correct, one would like to know what modifications led
to the improved fringe contrast. Were some kind of phase fluctuations eliminated? Perhaps the experiments are not as
closely identical as they look and in this case it would be useful if the authors could elaborate a bit on the relationship
between the two experiments. 
Minor points: 
In the inline equations below Eq. 1, it does not seem to me that the momentum labels are consistent with the labeling of
Fig.1. According to the diagram the equations should read p+p'= (-2k_0) and q+q'= (-6k_0). The equations in the text look
like they correspond to a different choice of reference frame. 
In their results, for example in Fig.3 the authors single out the special points phi = 13 pi/20 and 33 pi/20. As I understand it,
these values are the result of a fit to their fringes which in contain a phase offset particular to technical details in their
experimental. Thus giving them as rational fractions of pi, seems slightly misleading, even when the rational fraction is a little
weird. Of course the exact value of the offset is unimportant as long as its stable. They could just refer to it as delta and then
give the numerical value of delta as they do in the caption.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript claims the experimental observation of Bell correlations between the motional degrees of freedom of
massive particles (metastable He4 atoms) prepared in an entangled state by colliding two Bose-Einstein condensates,
which are in turn obtained by splitting a condensate via a Bragg pulse. Entanglement in general, and Bell correlations in
particular, between motional degrees of freedom are a long-sought goal of several experimental platforms, and claims in this
direction should not be taken lightly. 
Let me state from the start that the experimental procedure and its results shown in this paper are certainly solid in my
opinion, and they are based on the state of the art in the coherent manipulation of the motional degrees of freedom of
ultracold atoms. In particular the current setup allows for the preparation of an entangled pair of atoms occupying each two
momentum modes ((p,q) for atom 1, and (p',q') for atom 2), and the measurement of the state of each atom either in the basis
(p,q);(p',q'), or in a rotated basis, but with both atoms rotated in the same way. This means that the current setup does not
allow for a crucial ingredient required to make a successful Bell test of an entangled state, namely the possibility of
measuring the atoms in the entangled pair on two independent bases. Yet the authors clearly demonstrate joint Rabi
oscillations of the probability of occupancy of e.g., the modes p and p' upon rotating the measurement bases; and this
reveals the coherent nature of the superposition state which has been prepared in the experiment. This is beautifully
illustrated in Fig. 3B, which, in my opinion, is the central result of the paper. And they argue that the amount of coherence
they observe -- expressed in terms of the contrast A of the joint Rabi oscillations -- would be sufficient to lead to the violation
of a CHSH inequality if they were able to independently rotate the measurement bases of each atom. The horizontal dashed
lines in FIg. 3B set the bound required to violate the CHSH inequality, and the result is transparent. 
In my opinion, the above evidence would already offer material of sufficient quality to deserve publication in Nature
Communications. 
But the authors are in fact stretching their conclusions, when, in the introduction, they write that their work
"presents the first experimental observation of Bell correlations sufficient to demonstrate nonlocality in momentum-entangled

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

pairs of atoms". What they really did in my opinion is to show a sufficient level of correlation in their system such that a Bell
test, if conducted, would lead to conclude on the nonlocal nature of the entangled state of their atoms. But, strictly speaking,
"Bell correlations" is what you exhibit once a Bell test is conducted, not before. Their experiment does not conduct such a
test, but it only exhibits what one could call a "nonlocality witness" -- i.e. unlike the direct violation of a Bell inequality, one
needs to work out the algebra assuming the laws of quantum mechanics to show that their experimental apparatus would
have the potential to violate a Bell inequality. This may sound like a pedantic distinction, but in the context of Bell nonlocality
it is really not, and in particular when it comes to the claim of a first observation. 
In fact the authors try to push the analysis of their experimental results further, and they cook up an inequality (Eq. 7) whose
violation, in their words, "rules out a wide range of LHV theories". I confess that I have a hard time following their logic here.
One has to go to the end of the Supplemental Material to understand the actual meaning of this sentence, and to discover
that the LHV theories excluded by the violation of this inequality are not particularly appropriate to describe the experiment in
question -- since the averaged results of the measurements on one of the atoms "are assumed to be components of a vector
of length 1". I do not really see an immediate connection between this assumption and the experiment conducted here, and
the authors do not offer any further justification. If no connection can be established between the experiment and the
excluded theories, this result is empty in my view: one has evidence for the violation of a theory which is not meant to
describe the experiment in the first place. The authors should really justify what the significance is of this result, or remove its
discussion entirely, which may greatly add to the clarity of the paper. In my opinion, the fact that one can compose the results
of an experiment so as to violate an inequality which would apply to the local-realism description of a different physical
system does not tell anything about the nonlocality of the experiment in question. I find this to be the weakest point of the
manuscript, and I sincerely invite the authors to reconsider its appropriateness. 
More generally, I dislike that the authors put the attribute "Bell" to anything which smells like a test of nonlocality. E.g., Eq. 5
which is qualified as a "Bell correlation function". I presume that the "Bell" attribute comes from the fact that Eq. 5 looks like
the kind of objects that would appear in a Bell inequality, except that it is not, because it involves measurements in a single
basis only. 
I find that, when speaking of Bell nonlocality, the language that one uses should be particularly sharp, and
regrettably the language used here is not. See for instance the sentence "To verify that the observed Bell correlation function
demonstrates non-classical non-local behaviour, we construct the following nonlocality criterion...". Why "non-classical non-
local"? Morever the following Eq. 7 is actually a locality criterion, not a non-locality one. Moreover the authors state that the
violation of the locality bound in Eq. 7 witnesses entanglement: can the authors show this statement rigorously? Once again,
demonstrating nonlocality implies entanglement, but they do not demonstrate nonlocality in the strict sense, so it is unclear
to me whether entanglement witnessing follows from their results. 
Finally, in the conclusions it would be interesting that the authors could speculate on the future possibility of conducting Bell
tests with large space-like separations between the atoms, i.e., with a similar level of scrutiny as that used for entangled
photons. I guess that this element (the space-like separation) would be particularly important to pursue the ambitious goals
that the authors put forward in their conclusions such as "examining decoherence theories in quantum systems influenced
by gravitational field interactions". 
In conclusion, I think that this manuscript potentially contains experimental results of sufficient quality to deserve publication
in Nature Communications. But the theoretical analysis lacks clarity and sharpness of language, and should be
reconsidered to a large extent.
