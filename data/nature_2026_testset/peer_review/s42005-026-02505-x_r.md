Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper describes the use of Deep Kernel Bayesian Optimization (DKBO) to optimize the parameters of plasma photonic
structures for reflecting and transmitting high-power laser pulses. It demonstrates that DKBO was effective in optimizing laser
/ plasma parameters to meet specific goals – namely complete reflection of incident pulse energy, increased peak intensity
of the reflected pulse via compression, and 50/50 pulse splitting -- even in a modestly-sized parameter space (7 parameters)
with few iterations. The work is presented clearly and is an appropriate subject matter for Communications Physics as it

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

demonstrates the flexibility of both TPPS devices and BO techniques for solving scientific optimization problems. 
However, given the manuscripts’ focus on Deep Kernel Bayesian Optimization, I would expect a greater comparison
between DKBO and conventional GP s used in Bayesian optimization. The authors mention that most optimization runs
converged after evaluating approximately 30 points (10 random and 20 BO iterations). I am skeptical that a deep kernel
function with the structure the authors described (d-300-150-q) would be properly trained with such a small amount of data.
As a result, it's unclear whether the NN kernel offers any advantages over conventional kernel functions. A comparison of
ing the objective function with and without a NN kernel (and its effect on optimization performance) is necessary to justify
their use, especially if the authors want to justify the wide applicability of this technique to other optimization problems in
physics. As such, I cannot support acceptance of this manuscript until a ing / optimization performance comparison of DKBO
versus conventional BO (Matern kernel with automatic relevance determination) is included. 
Additionally, I have some minor comments on the text: 
The authors claim that the typical number of optimization parameters is limited to 20 for most applications of BO, this is
misleading as recent analysis with minor modifications to BO kernels has shown effective scaling to hundreds of parameters
(https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02229). This is especially relevant to performing optimization on simulations, which can be
relatively expensive to evaluate. 
In Section 2, the authors discuss the possibility of using simulations in multiple dimensions which offer tradeoffs between
computational cost and accuracy. This is an ideal use case for multi-fidelity BO
(https://journals.aps.org/prresearch/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.5.013063 ) and should be discussed in the text
and/or conclusion. 
Staying on this point, the authors should explicitly state the computational cost of their simulations (time, number of CPUs,
etc) to justify the need for optimization efficiency 
Given the current set of citations, I would also suggest citing the review article of BO here
https://journals.aps.org/prab/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevAccelBeams.27.084801 
The table of optimization parameters (Table 1) should be introduced and referenced in the simulation setup section to
improve readability

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author) 
The authors report a numerical study on Bayesian optimization of design Plasma Photonic 
Structures. They developed a new type of way, Bayesian optimisation with neural networks, to do 
the job. Recently, machine learning techniques have been widely used in laser-plasma interactions 
to optimize the multi-parameter process. I did not find any noteworthy progress compared with 
previous works. I think Scientific Reports is a more suitable journal for this work. 
Response to reviewer 
We thank the reviewer for this comment. We would clarify that the Deep Kernel Bayesian 
Optimisation described in this work builds upon an established foundation of previous work 
on deep kernel Gaussian processes for regression tasks (Ref №27, Deep Kernel Learning), and 
for optimisation tasks (Ref №1, Deep Kernel Bayesian Optimisation & Ref №2 Few-Shot 
Bayesian Optimization with Deep Kernel Surrogates). Recent research has utilised standard 
Bayesian optimisation methods for laser-plasma and traditional accelerator applications (Ref 
№23, 32 and 43). Our manuscript describes an alternative and novel Bayesian optimisation 
model, which we now justify using an “ablation study” described in Section S4 of the 
Supplementary document. We show that it is a very effective tool for optimisation and design, 
which we believe will appeal to the wider science and physics communities, and we 
demonstrate that it is an excellent tool for discovering new physics. In particular, it discovers a 
new method of pulse compression, which we believe would be of interest to the laser-plasma 
community and developers of next generation high power lasers. It is an excellent design tool 
for plasma-based optical components for beam manipulation.

difference. Which step does the neural network work? Neural networks typically need huge sample 
data. Why not here? Could you explain this?

can be used as drop-in replacements for standard kernels, with benefits in expressive power

We have also provided a more comprehensive description of the training and algorithm in the 
Methods section, where the neural network architecture is described in more detail. We

application differs from a conventional regression setting (for which DKL was originally

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
The authors use machine learning techniques to optimise the design of transient plasma optics in 1D 
Particle-In-Cell simulations. These transient plasma optics are of interest because they are capable of 
manipulating high intensity laser pulses without being damaged; the authors describe plasma-based 
versions of a mirror, a 50/50 beamsplitter, and a pulse compressor. This is a novel and interesting 
use of machine learning, though the manuscript as written did not convince me that the complexity 
of the machine learning architecture was necessary for the problem. It feels like the physics itself has 
not been explored much and this has instead been treated as a way to demonstrate a new 
computing tool.  
I have lots of questions below which should be addressed before I would recommend publication. I 
appreciate this is overwhelming, I’m sorry! – I did enjoy reading the paper but I think that to publish 
it in a Physics journal it needs to demonstrate more physics.  
1. The authors don’t seem worried by how one would make these transient photonic structures in 
practice. Producing the necessary initial plasma densities (~1020 cm-3) is infamously difficult as they 
lie between those obtained from gas and solid targets. I did appreciate the authors simulating a 
slightly more realistic initial density profile (a Gaussian rather than a top-hat) and was reassured that 
this only slightly reduced the reflectance. I would suggest adding a line about this in Section 2.1, 
saying that these example top hat density profiles are idealised and more intended to demonstrate 
the algorithm, and that simulations were also run with a Gaussian density profile and gave similar 
results. Is it possible to run the 2D simulations in Fig. 5 with a more realistic profile, such as a radial 
Gaussian profile (like a circular-nozzle gas jet), or a Gaussian in x and uniform across the whole 
domain in y (like a slit-nozzle gas jet). 
Response to reviewer 
We have already demonstrated formation of TPPSs experimentally (Ref.3), which is why we 
appreciate the comment on realistic density profiles. To compare optimised designs of TPPS 
mirrors we have started with a simple density profile, and then introduced more realistic 
plasma profiles, and finally included ionisation, step by step, as shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 6, 
Suppl. Fig. S5 and Suppl. Fig. S13. The design process evolves from “ideal” models to more 
“realistic” models. As recommended, we have clarified the process in Section 2.1, where we 
start with an idealised top-hat plasma profile with smoothed edges, and go on to test a 2D 
radial Gaussian density profile and a 1D Gaussian Profile. All designs yield a reflectivity above 
98%.  
2. I think the authors are making a mistake by varying the probe pulse intensity and duration as 
input parameters. In practice, a plasma optic will be designed to give the best optical properties 
ideally for any given probe pulse, and at least for a particular given probe pulse. Either the probe 
pulse parameters should therefore be fixed during the optimization, or the same plasma optic 
should be tested with multiple probe pulses to give an average reflectance. I appreciate the authors 
probably aren’t going to re-run all their simulations, but could they run another few 1D simulations 
with their optimal parameters and lower/higher probe pulse intensities to demonstrate that the 
plasma optics work well (or not so well!) for a wide range of probe pulses? Similarly, the plasma 
optic will only have a certain spectral range for their reflectance (just like dielectric optics), so my

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

guess is that 10 fs-duration probe pulses with wider spectral bandwidth will have lower 
reflectances. Can the authors test if this is the case and discuss this?  
Response to reviewer 
The reason why we chose to optimise using a wide range of parameters is that we did not 
know a-priori the optimum values for the probe intensity/duration. Mirror bandwidth is 
determined by the spacing of regularly spaced and stationary layers, which constrict the 
minimum probe duration (and the maximum spectral width).  However, TPPSs have a variable 
nonstationary structure, with potentially a larger bandwidth, which require a general method 
of optimisation. A sensitivity analysis of the probe intensity and duration has been carried out, 
while keeping all other parameters fixed at their optimised values. These tests have been 
conducted for all three metrics described in the main text. In response to the reviewer 
comments, we have also analysed the sensitivity to a probe pulse duration reduced to 10 fs, 
and a probe intensity increased or decreased by one order of magnitude compared with that 
used in the main study. This is described in Supp. Sec. S3, beginning on page 6, with the results 
shown in Supp. Tab. S3 in the updated document. 
3. The intensity strongly impacts the compressor optic in particular. The compression is possible 
because of the spectral broadening of the probe pulse inside the plasma. This process looks very 
much like the red-shifting and self-steepening that happens in a laser wakefield accelerator. The 
authors should probably reference this effect and test how it changes for lower probe intensities, 
which wouldn’t drive such a strong plasma wave. Also, the incoming probe pulse is not chirped, but 
is the reflected probe pulse? – this could be an unwanted property for a real plasma compressor 
optic.  
Response to reviewer 
This is a very valid and perceptive comment, but the answer is surprising. The red shifting 
observed in a TPPS arises from a Doppler shift due to concertinaing of the layers (Fig. 5 and 
Suppl. Fig. S10), which shifts and, in the case of compression, broadens the spectrum (Suppl. 
Fig. S3). Each layer of the TPPS has a different velocity and the spatial period is not constant. 
Group velocity dispersion in the time varying medium results in compression (Fig. 4). This is 
distinct from compression observed in the density wake of a laser wakefield accelerator 
(LWFA), which gives rise to photon acceleration/deceleration and group velocity dispersion of 
co-propagating laser pulses.  
We have now included a sensitivity analysis, as described above. We did indeed observe a 
reduction in the peak intensity of the reflected pulse, from 173% for the optimised 83 fs 
duration probe, to 68% for a 10 fs probe pulse with the same intensity, because of the 
increased bandwidth, as shown in Suppl. Table S3. This implies that compression is not very 
effective for very short pulses. However, the pulse compressor has a very high reflectance.  
Small reductions in intensity are observed for probe intensities of 1017and 1015 Wcm-2.  
We have updated our spectral broadening plots in Supp. Fig. S3 to include the spectral phase 
difference between incident and reflected probe pulses. This shows that the phase difference 
across the spectrum varies by less than π/2.

Response to reviewer 
We have included further information on the NN feature extractor in the Methods subsection 
5.2 of the main text. There we explain why the original 7 parameters are represented as a 50-
dimensional latent space, and how these parameters feed into the covariance function, which 
uses automatic relevance determination (ARD). This 50-dimensional representation is learned 
during each iteration of Bayesian optimisation, and the specific values do not have any 
conventional meaning. Instead, they are guided by fitting the Gaussian process surrogate to 
maximise the log marginal likelihood (LML) for that iteration.  
We now also discuss the network architecture and how it differs from those used in regression 
settings, where the number of data points is significantly larger than those typically 
encountered in Bayesian optimisation. We have provided a new algorithm block on page 25 to 
more clearly show the full process, step by step. Section 5.2.2 discusses the joint fitting of the 
GP and NN. 
7. The neutral network architecture has four layers with size 7-300-150-50. This seems the wrong 
way around (maybe a hang-over from the original DKL paper using 1000s of input entries?), and will 
result in lots of the 300 neurons in the first latent layer being wasted or duplicated. Each layer 
should be a relatively small change from the previous for the highest efficiency, so it should be more 
like 7-13-25-50 maybe. Crucially, the original DKL is an encoder, which efficiently reduces lots of 
inputs to a smaller and more tractable latent space over which to perform the optimisation. The 
authors’ scheme, on the other hand, does the opposite – it converts a small parameter space to a 
much higher-dimensional latent space. I can see how this could be important if there are 
discontinuities, branch cuts / hysteresis, or other complex features in the original parameter space 
that can’t be resolved by a standard Matérn kernel, but I haven’t seen any evidence that this is the 
case here. Can the authors justify using this neural network architecture? 
Response to reviewer 
We now include a discussion on network architecture in section 5.2.1. Here we contrast our 
architecture with the one described in the original DKL paper. We highlight the differences in 
their application of DKL and ours, primarily due to the varying number of data points. Our 
architecture is similar to the originally described one in DKL ([d – 1000 – 500 – 50 – 2]), but 
with a smaller size. The first layer is larger in both cases: 300 in our approach and 1000 in 
theirs. 
The main differentiating factor is the size of the feature-space representation. Due to the 
smaller number of data points in BO, we choose to send a more expressive representation to 
the kernel. This larger representation (q>d) allows the kernel to better discern differences 
between data points in the reduced-data regime of BO. This is achieved through the use of 
ARD. 
We’ve included an ablation study in Supplementary Section S4, where we removed the 
feature extractor of the network and used only the base Matérn kernel. The simple regret 
values for this comparison are shown in Supplementary Figure S6.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This paper describes the use of Deep Kernel Bayesian Optimization (DKBO) to optimize the 
parameters of plasma photonic structures for reflecting and transmitting high-power laser pulses. It 
demonstrates that DKBO was effective in optimizing laser / plasma parameters to meet specific 
goals – namely complete reflection of incident pulse energy, increased peak intensity of the 
reflected pulse via compression, and 50/50 pulse splitting -- even in a modestly-sized parameter 
space (7 parameters) with few iterations. The work is presented clearly and is an appropriate subject 
matter for Communications Physics as it demonstrates the flexibility of both TPPS devices and BO 
techniques for solving scientific optimization problems.  
However, given the manuscripts’ focus on Deep Kernel Bayesian Optimization, I would expect a 
greater comparison between DKBO and conventional GP models used in Bayesian optimization. The 
authors mention that most optimization runs converged after evaluating approximately 30 points 
(10 random and 20 BO iterations). I am skeptical that a deep kernel function with the structure the 
authors described (d-300-150-q) would be properly trained with such a small amount of data. As a

The authors use machine learning techniques to optimise the design of transient 
plasma optics in 1D Particle-In-Cell simulations. These transient plasma optics are of 
interest because they are capable of manipulating high intensity laser pulses without 
being damaged; the authors describe plasma-based versions of a mirror, a 50/50

learning, though the manuscript as written did not convince me that the complexity of 
the machine learning architecture was necessary for the problem. It feels like the 
physics itself has not been explored much and this has instead been treated as a way to 
demonstrate a new computing tool.

but I think that to publish it in a Physics journal it needs to demonstrate more physics. 
1. The authors don’t seem worried by how one would make these transient 
photonic structures in practice. Producing the necessary initial plasma densities 
(~1020 cm-3) is infamously difficult as they lie between those obtained from gas 
and solid targets. I did appreciate the authors simulating a slightly more realistic 
initial density profile (a Gaussian rather than a top-hat) and was reassured that

duration as input parameters. In practice, a plasma optic will be designed to give 
the best optical properties ideally for any given probe pulse, and at least for a 
particular given probe pulse. Either the probe pulse parameters should therefore 
be fixed during the optimization, or the same plasma optic should be tested with 
multiple probe pulses to give an average reflectance. I appreciate the authors 
probably aren’t going to re-run all their simulations, but could they run another 
few 1D simulations with their optimal parameters and lower/higher probe pulse 
intensities to demonstrate that the plasma optics work well (or not so well!) for a 
wide range of probe pulses? Similarly, the plasma optic will only have a certain 
spectral range for their reflectance (just like dielectric optics), so my guess is 
that 10 fs-duration probe pulses with wider spectral bandwidth will have lower 
reflectances. Can the authors test if this is the case and discuss this?
