Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Ding et al. investigated the function of citrulline-degrading bacteria in the protection of plants from
Fusarium wilt. Under continuous cropping, the concentration of citrulline became high, and it enhances the fusaric acid
production in Fusarium oxysporum. The authors then analyzed the metagenomes of both healthy and diseased soil samples
and found that multiple pathways of citrulline anabolism were upregulated in healthy samples. By adding citrulline to the
soil, several bacterial genera became highly abundant, among which the authors focused on Pseudomonas for further
characterization. Pseudomonas sp. YDTA isolated from the soil had a capacity to degrade citrulline. Genome analysis
revealed a gene cluster for citrulline metabolism, among which arcB and argH were shown to be involved in citrulline
degradation by the analysis of gene disruption mutants. The authors then heterologously expressed arcB genes in
Escherichia strains isolated from soil. EO-arcB showed citrulline degradation capacity. Finally, the authors inoculated either
P. YDTA or EO-arcB to Fusarium-treated soil, and analyzed the growth of several plants, including watermelon, and found
that citrulline degradation capacity mitigates the disease caused by Fusarium. This study investigated a key metabolite in
the rhizosphere, and potentially offers a promising strategy for controlling disease, however, there are several issues to be
addressed before drawing a conclusion. 
Major concerns:

was rinsed with sterile water, and the collected soil (possibly by centrifugation?), was used for citrulline quantification.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors propose a link beween citrulline accumulation and Fusarium wilt, investigate taxa associated with citrulline
degradation, and propose a biocontrol strategy based on citrulline degradation. The topic and scientific approach are very
interesting. They confirm a previous paper (Ref 10) from the same authors, which has already revealed that metabolites like
citrulline affect the rhizosphere microbiome and lead to a high incidence of Fusarium wilt (L45), and here the first set of
results permits an extension of previous findings. 
The part starting L121 (‘Citrulline turnover function was relatively dormant in continuous cropping diseased soils’) is not very
clear and has shortcomings. First, it relies on metagenomics, which is not appropriate to assess whether or not a given
function is dormant, as the later relates to gene expression conditions and not the occurrence/amount of the corresponding
genes. Likewise, the authors conclude on reactions steps that are ‘upregulated’ (L135, L142) and reactions that ‘were in a
dormant state’ (L137), which is scientifically incorrect. Second, it is based on the comparison of only two treatments, with n =
3 samples per treatment. This level of n = 3 is really too low for robust comparisons. Third, stats were done at P < 0.1 level,
which is not stringent enough. 
The part starting L147 displays the same type of ambiguity, as it is concluded that taxa were ‘subject to substrate induction
by citrulline’, but again there was no data on gene expression. The data show ery difference than an increase. More
important, the data correspond to relative abundance, and qPCR data on true abundance are lacking. Gene expression was
indeed monitored, but this was done with individual bacteria in vitro, and the statistics displayed in Fig S10 need to be
checked, because they are visually counter-intuitive. Pseudomonas arcB and argH mutants were assessed, but without the
wild-type as control. 
In the last part, starting L191, the authors develop a biocontrol strategy by focusing on Escherichia, because (L208) ‘in the
rhizosphere soil of these crops was consistently greater than 2% (Data S3)’. This is surprising, as E. coli is considered
poorly adapted to soil systems, but when I checked for the data I only found data for families in Data S3, and the
corresponding family (Enterobacteriaceae) is given at 2.08, 1.97 and 1.75. The E. coli constructs needed ITPG for arcB
expression in vitro, but from the description it is not clear whether and how ITPG was added in the plant experiment. Overall,
it is not clear enough for a reader what was exactly done.

Citrulline metabolism in bacteria needs better clarification. T he authors quantified 
citrulline (Fig. 3F ,H, Fig. 4A). There is no data on the converted compounds, such as 
arginine and ornithine. Do these bacteria catabolize or anabolize citrulline? These

A: Thank you for raising this important point regarding the metabolic of citrulline in 
the bacterial system. We fully agree that clarification of the downstream metabolic 
products is essential to distinguish between catabolic and anabolic processes. 
In this study, we aim to clarify that citrulline metabolism in the studied Pseudomonas 
strain is primarily catabolic. This conclusion is supported by targeted measurements of 
downstream compounds —arginine and ornithine —in both the wild -type and gene 
knockout mutants (ΔarcB and ΔargH), as now presented in the updated Figure 3H. 
1. In the wild-type strain, citrulline levels decreased over time, while both arginine and 
ornithine accumulated, indicating active turnover of citrulline through catabolic 
pathways. 
2. In the ΔarcB mutant, citrulline -to-ornithine conversion was nearly blocked, and 
arginine accumulated, suggesting that arcB is essential for the catabolic conversion of 
citrulline into ornithine. 
3. In the ΔargH mutant, the conversion of citrulline to arginine was almost completely 
blocked, and ornithine accumulated significantly, indicating that argH is essential for
