Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript entitled "Mechanical Rejuvenation of Senescent Stem Cells and Aged Bone via Chromatin Remodeling",
the authors reported a marked reduction in intracellular traction force and cellular mechanics in senescent bone marrow
mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). They demonstrated that the moderate mechanical stimuli effectively restored cellular
force, enhanced chromatin accessibility at the FOXO1 locus, activated its expression, and reversed cellular senescence and
bone aging. This study unveils that optimizing mechanical stimulation provides a promising, straightforward approach to
counteract age-related bone degeneration and systemic inflammation, offering significant potential for clinical translation.
The presented findings are new and interesting, and reviewer has the following comments. 
1) In Fig.1E results, the authors performed Western blot (WB) analysis on several key proteins involved in
mechanotransduction. However, given the multitude of cellular mechanotransduction pathways, the authors should clarify
whether this investigation focuses on a predefined, specific pathway or represents a broader screening of multiple
associated pathways. This clarification would significantly enhance comprehension of the research scope and improve the
accuracy of the study. 
2) In Fig.2 staining results, the authors used different senescence markers (p16 in some panels, p21 in others) across
various experimental models. While both p16 and p21 are established markers of cellular senescence, they are implicated
in distinct mechanistic pathways. The selective use of these markers may lead to confusion. It is recommended that the
authors provide a justification for this experimental design choice to clarify whether it reflects a targeted investigation of
specific senescence pathways or a practical consideration based on assay optimization. 
3) In lines 242~246, there appears to be an error in the figure citations. The text references "Figure 1E~H" but based on the
context and the described content, these panels correspond more appropriately to “Figure 3E~H”. It is likely a typographical
error, and the authors are advised to correct the citation to ensure accuracy and proper alignment with the presented data. 
4) In Figure 4F, the Western Blot results for p53, the internal control GAPDH bands appear excessively faint, suggesting
potential issues such as insufficient protein loading or suboptimal detection conditions. It is strongly recommended that the
authors repeat it with standardized protein quantification and equal loading validation to ensure credible results. 
5) In Figure 7D staining results, the scale bars appear inconsistent. The nuclei in the p21 fluorescence channel are
conspicuously larger than those in other channels. please check it.

Reviewer comment: 
In the manuscript entitled "Mechanical Rejuvenation of Senescent Stem Cells and 
Aged Bone via Chromatin Remodeling", the authors reported a marked reduction 
in intracellular traction force and cellular mechanics in senescent bone marrow 
mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). They demonstrated that the moderate 
mechanical stimuli effectively restored cellular force, enhanced chromatin 
accessibility at the FOXO1 locus, activated its expression, and reversed cellular

Response: We thank the reviewer for the constructive comments and positive

(1) In Fig.1E results, the authors performed Western blot (WB) analysis on several 
key proteins involved in mechanotransduction. However, given the multitude of 
cellular mechanotransduction pathways, the authors should clarify whether this 
investigation focuses on a predefined, specific pathway or represents a broader 
screening of multiple associated pathways. This clarification would significantly 
enhance comprehension of the research scope and improve the accuracy of the

Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. Our study specifically 
focused on a well-established and canonical mechanotransduction axis linking 
cytoskeletal contractility to nuclear mechanosignaling, rather than performing a broad, 
unbiased screening of all mechanotransduction pathways. In particular, we examined 
representative components across the key structural and signaling nodes of this cascade: 
1. Nonmuscle Myosin IIa and its phosphorylated form (Ser1943) , which 
generate intracellular traction forces and serve as indicators of actomyosin 
contractility; 
2. Lamin A/C, the principal nucleoskeletal component that transmits cytoskeletal 
tension to the nuclear envelope and maintains nuclear mechanical integrity; and 
3. Yes-associated protein (YAP) , a force-sensitive transcriptional coactivator, 
whose nuclear localization provides a downstream readout of mechanical input. 
This axi, actomyosin cytoskeleton - nucleoskeleton (Lamin A/C) - nuclear 
mechanosignaling (YAP) , constitutes a central route by which physical forces are 
converted into biochemical responses in stromal cells. We have updated the main text 
to clarify that our Western blot analysis was designed to interrogate this classical 
mechanotransduction pathway, rather than serving as a comprehensive screen of all 
possible mechanotransduction networks.

We appreciate the reviewer’s important concern regarding the identification of BMSCs. 
Bone marrow aspirates were obtained from the posterior superior iliac spine, which is

confirmed age-related functional alterations by SA-β-gal staining, p21 immunostaining, 
and γ-H2AX detection (Supplementary Section 2, Fig. 2-5). Together, these phenotypic, 
functional, and senescence-related assays provide strong evidence that the cells used in 
this study are bona fide bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem/stromal cells 
(BMSCs).

Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. We have revised the 
Introduction to clarify how biochemical signaling pathways converge on BMSC 
senescence. Specifically, we now describe that senescent BMSCs undergo broad 
transcriptional reprogramming, particularly in genes governing proliferation, 
differentiation, and extracellular matrix organization. These changes are tightly 
regulated by multiple biochemical processes: metabolic dysregulation (e.g., impaired 
mitochondrial function, altered NAD⁺/SIRT axis) perturbs redox balance and energy 
homeostasis, activating stress-responsive pathways; transcriptional regulators such as 
p53, FOXO, and NF-κB orchestrate the expression of senescence-associated genes, 
linking cellular stress to growth arrest and the senescence-associated secretory 
phenotype (SASP); and epigenetic modifications, including histone acetylation, 
methylation, and DNA methylation, consolidate the senescent state by repressing 
proliferation-related genes and altering chromatin accessibility. Collectively, these
