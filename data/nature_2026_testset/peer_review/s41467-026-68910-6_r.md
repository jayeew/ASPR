Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Xiaoyu Tang and colleagues present a manuscript detailing the biochemical characterization of a tripartite enzymatic
complex catalyzing Friedel-Crafts-Acylation in the biosynthesis of the antibiotic tetramate reutericyclin A. This enzymatic
complex bears similarities to a previously characterized system able to catalyze acetylation of phloroglucinol. The protein
complex was structurally characterized via Cryo-EM, revealing a dodecameric structure, analogous to a previously
characterized pathway in archaeal mevalonate biosynthesis. Bioinformatic analyses showed that key catalytic residues of
the primary metabolism enzyme were lost, and new, potentially critical residues for a new catalytic functon(acetylation)
acquired. Based on these findings, the authors pose the hypothesis that the enzymatic complex was repurposed during
evolution to acquire a new catalytic function. Finally, the authors show that acetylation is an essential feature for antibiotic
activity in both reutericyclins and phloroglucinols, highlighting the biological importance of this modification. 
The paper is very well and clearly written and contains a lot of data. The experiments were carried out with a range of state-
of the-art methodologies, and the results are nicely presented in main and SI Figures. Overall, this study deserves
publication in Nature Communications. However, there are a couple of issues that should be improved or taken care of
before publication:

comprehensive enzymatic study is beyond the scope of this paper, but it would be nice to provide some initial

accepted?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled "Evolutionary repurposing of a metabolic thiolase complex enables antibiotic biosynthesis" by Liao
et al. presents an interesting and informative structural and functional investigation of SmaATase, a multimeric thiolase
enzyme complex from S. macaca involved in the biosynthesis of the antimicrobial compound reutericyclin A. This secondary
metabolic pathway is proposed to have evolved from the central metabolic pathway for mevalonate biosynthesis in archaea
by functionally repurposing the tripartite enzyme complex that couples 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase (HMGS)
and acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase (ACAT) reactivity. Biochemical characterization of SmaATase revealed the HMGS activity
of the proposed archaeal ancestor was lost, and instead of Claisen condensation activity the ACAT homolog catalyzes a
non-canonical Friedel-Crafts C-acetylation on a pyrrolidine-2,4-dione scaffold. This is similar to another bacterial tripartite
enzyme complex, PpATase from P. protogens, that performs a Friedel-Crafts C-acylation of monoacetylphloroglucinol
(MAPG) to diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG, an antimicrobial compound) and phloroglucinol (PG). Notably, although the
HMGS activity of the archaeal ancestor is not preserved in SmaATase or PpATase, the authors were only able to observe
the C-acetylation activity in the presence of the complete tripartite complex, suggesting the evolutionary trajectory may have
prioritized chemical specialization over quaternary structural modifications. This point is further illustrated by comparison of
experimental structures, including new cryo-EM results of SmaATase, that show strong structural similarities in the overall
subunit organization and Co-A binding site, and all complexes contain the canonical thiolase

manuscript.

Response to Reviewers' Comments to Author:  Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author) Comments: In their manuscript, Liao et al study bacterial tripartite complex systems analogous to the thiolase/HMG-CoA synthase/DUF-35 protein used by archaea to catalyse the first steps of isoprenoid synthesis. The end products of these reactions ultimately generate molecules with antibiotic activities. It is without any doubt a study of high quality, bringing to light an enzymatic complex involved in secondary metabolism that most probably originates from a central carbon metabolism involved in isoprenoid biosynthesis. In the forward direction, SmaATase accepts acetyl-CoA or phenyl acetate (PA) as acetyl donors and transfers the acetyl group to preRTC-A.
