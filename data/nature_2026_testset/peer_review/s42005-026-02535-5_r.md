Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper presents a novel strategy of investigating the role of self-seeding mechanism in N2+ air lasing. By adopting a
single 800-nm cylindrical vector beam, both radially and azimuthally polarized 391-nm N2+ lasing with the polarization
being closely same to those of the driving vector pulses are generated. By demonstrating distinct polarization and spatial
distributions between the N2+ lasing and the second harmonic generation (SHG) in argon using the same driving beam, the
authors argue against the contribution of the second harmonic as a self-seed for 391-nm N2+ lasing under low gas pressure
conditions. Numerical simulations suggest that the observed vector N2+ lasing originates from amplified spontaneous
emission (ASE). This is a well-written paper that offers valuable insights into the 391-nm emission mechanism of N
₂⁺
, and
thus my recommendation is to accept it for publication in Communications Physics, providing the following points are
addressed. 
1. To secure the proposed ASE mechanism, the contribution from the residue second harmonic of the fundamental 800-nm
pulses generated in the Q-plate should be excluded. 
2. I believe that the ASE mechanism should be condition-dependent, because the self-seeding mechanism might still play a
role under other experimental conditions, such as few-cycle pumping or high pulse energy at elevated gas pressures. This
point should be emphasized in the abstract and summary section. 
3. For comparison, the authors generate SHG in argon. Why do not generate SHG directly in air? Are there any difference for
the polarization and 
of SHG generated between air (molecular gas) and argon (atomic gas)? 
4. On Page 11, Line 1, “pulse durations of 6 mJ and 8 mJ” should be changed to “pulse energies of 6 mJ and 8 mJ”. 
5. The most recently published air lasing papers especially for the air lasing application are lacking. Please update the
reference list.

mechanism behind the 391 nm emission in the N₂⁺ ion. One of the central unresolved 
questions in this field has been whether the second harmonic (SHG), generated in the laser-
induced plasma , indeed serves as a seed for triggering lasing. For roughly a decade this 
hypothesis has been regarded as the most plausible one, yet no direct experimental

The authors propose an elegant method to test this assumption: they employ cylindrical

Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for this thorough, insightful, and highly 
encouraging evaluation of our work. We are grateful for the recognition of the novelty and 
significance of our approach and conclusions, and we have carefully addressed all the questions 
and suggestions raised, which we believe further improve the clarity, rigor, and scope of the

Comment 1: Even if SHG does not play a role as a seed, important questions remain: what 
fraction of the inversion is created instantaneously upon ionization (via shake -up or mixing

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

processes)? Does the initial molecular orientation and rotational coherence influence the 
B↔X population dynamics? 
Response 1: We thank the reviewer for this concern. In our current understanding of N2+ lasing, 
when the pump wavelength is centered at 800 nm, only a minor inversion is created due to the 
off-resonant one-photon absorption. In the 1-μm pumping cases, three-photon absorption plays 
a significant role , giving rise to  substantially enhanced amplification (Phys. Rev. Lett. 133, 
113201,2024). In our recent studies, we found that the B-X coherence induced by three-photon 
absorption during ionization is strong, which can interfere with the seed  (Nat. Commun. 16, 
9654,2025; Phys. Rev. Lett. 135, 223802,2025).  However, the relative contributions of 
population inversion and coherence are still unclear and will be explored in future studies. 
In our previous study, we have demonstrated that the initial molecular orientation determine s 
the polarization state of N 2+ lasing (J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 15, 3805 –3811,2024), which results 
in an anisotropic gain as we discussed in this work and leads to the amplification of spontaneous 
emission into a CVB . Given that linearly polarized light can generate significant air lasing 
signal whereas circularly polarized light produces almost none, we believe this strongly 
indicates that rotational coherence plays an important role in the B –X population dynamics , 
which could originate from partially rotational inversion (Phys. Rev. A 96, 051401, 2017) or 
transient population inversion (Optica 7, 586–592, 2020). However, in this work, employing 
CVBs as the pump does not allow us to directly address this question about the B–X population 
dynamics. 
 
 
Comment 2: How universal is the "SHG-free" conclusion? The study uses CVBs, but would 
lasing remain equally independent of SHG for linearly or circularly polarized pumps? 
Response 2: According to the experimental results presented by this paper, t he current SHG-
free conclusion applies to the most common condition in N2+ lasing: ~35-fs multi-cycle pulse 
pumping at low gas pressure. We believe SHG-free will apply to a general condition because 
the spatial mode of SHG is independent of both the pumping wavelength and gas pressure. As 
we discussed in the third paragraph of  introduction, a linearly polarized pump generates an 
HG01-mode SHG, whereas the air lasing is always observed with the fundamental HG00 mode. 
In our current study, N 2+ lasing pumped by single -cycle pulses in 1 -bar air also exhibit s 
gaussian mode (see SI in Nat. Commun. 16, 9654,2025). The SHG with HG01 mode induced 
by the linearly polarized pump strictly follows Eq. (1). 
This mismatch of spatial mode between the SHG and air lasing pumped by the linearly 
polarized beam motivates this study.  As the reviewer commented , employing CVBs are an 
“elegant method” because SHG signal can be “ turned off” when pumped by an azimuthally 
polarized beam , which makes  the "SHG-free" conclusion  solid. More interestingly, when 
pumped by a circularly polarized beam, air lasing can be “turned off” while SHG possessed a 
comparable intensity with linearly polarized case (Appl. Phys. Lett. 95, 121502, 2009) . Thus, 
SHG-free conclusion for N2+ lasing applied to linearly or circularly polarized pumps. 
In addition, while SHG-free is a general conclusion, SPM can be a significant self seed when 
the gas pressure is high and the spectrum is broad. 
 
 
Comment 3: What is the role of rotational coherence ? In particular, does rotational 
coherence enhance the 391- nm gain? Does the output intensity depend on the pump- pulse 
delay relative to a preceding alignment pulse?

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

Response 3: Previous studies have demonstrated that rotational coherence plays an important 
role in N2+ air lasing for polarization state and intensity . Most wide investigation s are about 
transient alignment. In both pump-seed or pre-pump experiments, revivals have been observed 
when scanning time delay between two pulses (Phys. Rev. X 3, 041009, 2013; Phys. Rev. A 96, 
041401, 2017), resulting in intensity modulation. Another contribution from rotational 
coherence is permanent alignment, which arises from initial molecular orientation  created by 
angularly preferential ionization (J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 15, 3805 –3811,2024). Compared with 
transient alignment, permanent alignment makes the time-averaged alignment degree greater 
than 1/3, controlling the polarization state of the air lasing via anisotropic gain.  
In CVB pumping case, if there is a preceding alignment pulse  with CVB mode , an intensity 
modulation will be definitely observed  due to the transient alignment. Furthermore, if the  
preceding alignment pulse is a linearly polarized beam  instead of a CVB, it  will lead to a  
nonuniform doughnut shape because different azimuthal angle has different revival time. This 
should be consistent with the Ref. Phys. Rev. A 96, 041401, 2017. 
Revision: We added a statement to talk about transient alignment. 
 
 
Comment 4: The authors report only the total pulse energy (3 –6 mJ range). It would be 
helpful to also provide the estimated peak intensity in W/cm^2 - for example, in Figs. 1 and 
2. 
Response 4: We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. When there is free space, the estimated 
peak intensity of 3-mJ gaussian beam is about 7.5x10^15 W/cm^2. However, due to defocusing 
effect, the plasma diameter is at the level of 100 μm. And the peak intensity of LG01 is 1/e time 
that of LG00. Thus, the peak intensity is estimated to be the order of 10^14 W/cm^2. 
 
Revision: We added the estimated peak intensity at the experimental method.
