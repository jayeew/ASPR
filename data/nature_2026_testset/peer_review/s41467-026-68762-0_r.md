Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper examines the reasons for vertical gradients in oxygen isotopes in the snow cover on sea ice on MOSAiC, and
attribute to mass flux from the underlying sea ice and replacement of up to 40% of the snow pack with sea ice sourced ice.
This is a potentially important process for the evolution of the snow cover that has previously been overlooked, and has
potentially important implications for the snow cover evolution, interpretation of oxygen isotope analysis of the snow cover,
and to some extent, impacts on other sea ice processes. This also has some implications for interpretation of similar data in
the Antarctic, where the reason for the gradient in snow there was (I believe) assumed to be due to a different process. If the
conclusion here is true, that has significant implications for interpreting various Antarctic sea ice processes. 
The paper demonstrates this to be plausible, and I also believe it is. I had not considered this before, and agree that it has
some important implications, so I believe merits publication. The question (raised in prior reviews) is the analysis robust
enough to make the claims it does, and has it adequately exhausted other possibilities. On this point, I am not quite sold (see
my reasoning below). But, this paper has been through the ringer, with several prior reviews and revisions, and addition of
one of the prior reviewers as a coauthor. While I will suggest some possible improvements (see below), I do not think it is
productive to propose extensive additional analysis to try to pin the hypothesis down better. I think that would require
significant subsequent modeling, experiments, and/or field studies. My assessment is the community may be better served
by publishing the paper now, even if the main conclusion remains uncertain, and let the debate play out in the literature.
However, the authors should consider after addressing my main points below, whether they believe this might change their
conclusion significantly, and how strong a claim they want to make. 
My main criticism is the analysis is (perhaps necessarily) quite simple and in some ways not well constrained, but the results
are presented with more certainty than is justified. While the authors explore some alternative explanations, they ignore
others, or calculate estimates based on a rather generous assumptions. For instance, the abstract gives a pretty narrow
estimate of the mass redistribution (I also note there is a greater range in the text from the two analysis methods used). 
So,
my main suggestion is that the authors at least discuss the potential impact of these processes and uncertainties (or counter
them, if they disagree)and then either provide broader bounds on their estimates (if possible) or temper the certainty
expressed in the paper. I’ll detail my main points below. 
1. The 53 mm number is arrived at based on equation 1 over 150 days. I believe this will severely overestimate the rate of
mass change. First, (1) is based on a temperature gradient of 100 K/m, while the mean observed gradient is about half that.
If, the rate of TGM was linear, it would be better to make (1) temperature gradient dependent, even in a semi-heuristic way.
Because the equilibrium water vapor gradient is not linear, I believe it will be worse than this (I thought about this a little, but I
think the authors should be able to make some good arguments of what might affect the rate in (1)). And because the
temperature gradient varied, the rate will vary by day, and perhaps also not in a linear way. To be fair, you do acknowledge
this briefly in the discussion, but you should make some attempt to quantify at least the error bounds. The simplest
hypothesis here is the rate should be at least halved, which changes the results substantially. 
2. Furthermore, is 150 days the right amount of time? There are several reasons to doubt this. First, there are many days with
low temperature gradients when TGM might not even work (this is part of the argument above). Second, when I look at

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

Figure 3, I do not see a linearly continuous process. Instead, it seems to reach a kind of steady state in a few months. If the
process was continuing as proposed, and at the same rate, from Jan-May, I would expect the lower pack to get close to 0
ppt, and I do not see that. So, it seems more plausible (based only on the isotope data) that this process happened primarily
in the first couple months. If so, equation(1) gives you much less change again. 
3. Is the vertical gradient in isotopes explainable only by this process? If so, then the end-point mixture analysis does indeed
support the hypothesis. However, the assumption that I had always thought explained this gradient (from Antarctic data) was
simply that the initial snowfall on sea ice happened in warmer conditions, where the precipitation is initially less depleted.
Then you get this vertical gradient occurring naturally. If that is true, then the mixture analysis has substantial uncertainty.
From Figure 3, it does look like this could be the case, and there is little gradient in Nov and Dec. I should say, this is one
thing I do like about the conclusion here, is that it made me rethink this. In the Antarctic, lots of young ice will form in open
water in the pack when it is already quite cold and far from the ice edge, so at least some of the initial snow on young ice
may actually be quite depleted – so you are suggesting a potentially alternative explanation and important process! 
That
said, I think at MOSAiC, the idea that the precipitation early in the year may be less depleted seems reasonable, so the
authors should address this point. 
4. Now, I agree there is going to mass loss from the ice that is transported up through the snow. But you claim that all of this
is then taken up by the snow cover. To be fair, that is the claim in Sturm and Benson, too. 
But I am not sure that will
necessarily be true. I think that whether that occurs depends on the relative magnitudes of the vertical vapor flux vs the rate
of solid-to-vapor flux during TGM at each level in the pack. If the former is large, then a lot of that ends up in the upper pack,
where it may just escape through the surface, possibly aided by wind pumping or wind mixing during blowing and drifting
snow events (another thing not mentioned here is that if that ends up in the upper pack which appears to be mobile and
redistributed by snow, then it then undergoes further processes and spatial homogenization, which would then lessen the
impact on those locations that were thin). Your assumption is essentially that TGM is fast relative to this flux. I would like to
see some discussion of this and if it may be significant, some estimate of how much it may impact your results. 
In short, my position is there are several processes that may significantly affect your estimates. Most of these are rather hard
to quantify (except perhaps the first), but they should at least be addressed in the text, and, if necessary, provide more
realistic bounds on your numbers. 
More minor details: 
1.Equation 1 does not give units. Indicate ITE is in mm. 
2.I found the explanation of why you excluded effects of salinity based on the deuterium excess quite confusing. 
I think you
are right – I just think the text could be clearer. 
3.The thermal resistance argument is odd to me. While it is potentially true that this mass adds to the thermal resistance, I do
not know how it gives a very useful result. Given everything else going on, I have a hard time believing this is a first order
problem. You do not change the thermal conductivity in (5), yet it will drop because of the reduced keff of depth hoar, and in
the upper pack in should increase as the snow becomes denser. I guess the claim is that one must account for this added
mass for modelling, as it might maintain the snow depth. I suppose, but given my points above, I think it’s hard to say how
much, and on a larger scale, is probably not significant compared to other poorly modeled processes. Given you claim the
upper part of the pack is modified by wind redistribution, it is difficult to claim this added mass is really important in
maintaining snow depth. I don’t think this adds to the paper and I suggest dropping it, and use the space to better address
my main points above. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
General comment: 
Based on valuable data collected during the MOSAiC expedition, this manuscript analyzes the processes driving snow
metamorphism on Arctic sea ice. Utilizing manual measurements from repeated snowpits, along with analytical results from
laboratory analyses (i.e., stable water isotopes, salinity, micro-CT), the authors document the evolution of the snowpack's
microstructural and physico-chemical properties from November 2019 to May 2020. The data presented are highly relevant,
and the work reasonably supports the conclusions and claims. 
While I found the topic highly interesting and particularly significant for improving the representation of snow cover in sea ice
models, the manuscript’s weakest point is that it sometimes lacks clarity and organizational coherence. In particular, I found
it challenging to distinguish between what is drawn from the literature, what was directly observed or measured in the
field/laboratory, and what is attributed to the authors’ own interpretation. Several statements, particularly in the discussion,
appear rather speculative, making it difficult for readers to clearly identify the key findings and their implications. 
Furthermore, I would appreciate more detailed information on the spatial and temporal variability of the sampling. Ideally,
this could be summarized in a table showing the total number of snowpits conducted during the campaign, the coordinates
of the sampling locations, the total snow depth for each snow sampling, the number of replicates per sample collected, the
sampling dates, and other key parameters necessary to contextualize the experimental setting. Even after multiple readings,

Lastly, I noticed several expressions in English that are excessively colloquial and should be revised to better align with the

current form. However, with major revisions—particularly in terms of organization and clarity—the work could become
suitable for consideration. I have provided constructive feedback, including specific comments, to assist the authors in

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript combines observational data of oxygen isotopes, snow temperature, and stratification to discuss the
contribution of sea-ice source sublimation to snow mass balance. It is important to study the mass balance of snow, and the
surface heat flux and optical properties determined by the properties of snow, which is a work worthy of publication. Based
on the previous round of review comments, the authors have made corresponding revisions. Here, regarding the rigor of the
work, I have the following comments: 
1)the temperature gradient and temperature at the interface between snow and sea ice are the major factors to drive water
vapor transport and mass transfer from sea ice into the snowpack. However, the temperature gradient of snow cover and the
temperature at the snow-sea ice interface depend not only on the near surface air temperature and snow depth, but also on
the thickness of sea ice. The latter regulates the temperature at the snow-sea ice interface by adjusting the temperature and
its gradient through the ice layer. The manuscript's discussion on the impacts of sea ice thickness and its seasonal changes
on the vapor transport from ice to snow seems to be insufficient. 
2)The result presents the cumulative contribution of sea-ice source vapor to the mass balance of snow cover. I wonder if this
cumulative amount can vary seasonally, and it is necessary analyze the differences in driving mechanisms between various
seasons, as well as clarify which season has the the relatively large contribution of sea-ice source vapor. 
3)Snow pit observation: Although the actual sampling sites are close in each repeated observation area, does there still
exist local heterogeneity in the snow cover and sea ice themselves, such as their respective thicknesses, and how does this
affect the research results? 
4)The authors mentioned that the temperature gradient at the bottom of the snow layer is greater than that at the top. So, if
the snow is further divided into two equal parts and the temperature gradient of lower layer is calculated to evaluate its
impact on the upward flux of sea-ice vapor, what difference will the results be? 
5)Implications: The impact of sea-ice-sources vapor recrystallization on the radiation characteristics of snow cover and
satellite remote sensing inversion may also be worth noting and discussing. 
6)Line 413 “Alternative processes also come into play in Antarctica, for example, in some areas, a persistent snowpack from
the previous years is present on the sea ice”: Actually, shouldn't there be more multi-year sea ice in Arctic ice and its
associated old surface snow cover? 
7)flooding processes: This process does not only occur in situations of snow overload, but local depressions formed by the
compression of sea ice surfaces can also lead to floods, which also occurred on the MOSAiC camp floe (e.g., Lei et al.,
2022, Elementa: Science of the Anthropocene, https://doi.org/10.1525/.elementa.2021.000089) 
8)Fig. 2: 1) What changes occur in vapor flux when the temperature gradient is negative. Furthermore, are these negative
values all transient behaviors, and do these transient behaviors have a fundamental impact on vapor flux? 2) Can the
relationship between temperature gradient and sea ice thickness be provided simultaneously? 
9)Fig.4: Here you attempt to provide information on seasonal changes. However, changes in the attributes of sampling sites
can also affect the analysis results. So can you focus only on level sea ice here, or even only on level second-year ice.
Because the measurement results of sampling sits over the leads and ice ridges can affect the identification of seasonal
changes. 
10)Fig.5: “. This indicates that d-excess could be used as a potential proxy for the amount of metamorphic recrystallization in
saline snow on sea ice”: Can it also be used as a classification of sea ice types, such as distinguishing between first-year
ice and multi-year ice, in the case without taking ice cores for analysis. 
11)Fig. 6: What does the Fs < 0 mean?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript has been revised with responses in accordance with my comments from the previous round. I have no
further critical comments here, only a few minor suggestions. 
The study was conducted based on samples collected up to May. After June, the snowpack entered the melting period.
Surface melt ponds began to form, ice crystals started to break down, and connected brine channels even developed. 
Given this, during the melting season, What is the destination of the components in the snowpack that originally from the
recrystallized vapor from the underlying sea ice? What impacts and implications might this have? Could appropriate
discussions be added using other MOSAiC indirect observation data during the melting season, to enhance the
comprehensiveness of the study and its disciplinary impact?

Dear Reviewer 3, We would like to express our gratitude for conﬁrming the manuscript has been amended according to your previous comments and for suggesting the above minor revisions.  We decided to conduct this snow study investigation up until May because after this period the snow begins to melt, after a short period of time in the Arctic (~1-2 weeks) all the snow has disappeared on level ice due to the small snow thicknesses found in this region. For this reason, there will be no inﬂuence on the sea ice in summer as the snow is replaced by the replenishing surface scattering layer. We believe this melt period beginning after June is out the scope of our study as we are dealing with cold winter snow metamorphism.  Nonetheless in response to your above comment, we have considered the snow components will melt and contribute to the summer freshwater budget (i.e. meltwater run-oƯ, meltponds etc.). Due to the process outlined in this paper the melt water will be slightly more enriched which needs considering in the isotope budget if trying to decipher processes contributing to the isotopic signal. We hypothesise that melt processes will inﬂuence isotopic signal more than the sea-ice-sourced snow. As a result, we believe this deserves a follow-up investigation but we do not wish to incorporate any melt or summer processes into this study to maintain clarity.   Kind regards Amy Macfarlane
