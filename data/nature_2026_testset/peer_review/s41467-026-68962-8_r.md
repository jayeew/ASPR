Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Zuodong Ye et al presents an iPSC derived cholangiocyte model system to study mechanisms involved
in Biliary Atresia and specifically the role of CMV infection in accentuating this process is modeled and investigated. Using
this modeling combined with bulk and single cell RNA sequencing approaches and other techniques the authors determine
a mechanistic link between CMV infection and Biliary Atresia via TGF-B EMT and disruption of barrier function. This is an
interesting paper using a relevant model to examine the underlying mechanisms for this disease. My main concerns listed
below have to do with the quality and lack of quantitation of some of the data presented (such as infection, IF and western
blots) and the consequent conclusions drawn from the data, which need to be strengthened and addressed. 
The manuscript does not adequately describe how cholangiocyte organoids were infected. For example, it is unclear if spin
infection was used or if the mock infection protocol was identical. How long was the infection? Was it in suspension, in a
pellet or in a plate? These details should be included for reproducibility. 
It is implied that the model supports HCMV replication and viral production - referring to the single cell RNA sequencing
data, the manuscript frequently mentions that the model demonstrates "cells with high levels of viral replication" (line 347) or
"producing new viruses for transmission." However, this claim overstates the evidence presented. GFP expression alone is
not sufficient to confirm the production of infectious virions necessary for transmission. To definitively demonstrate that
cholangiocytes support productive HCMV infection, the authors would need to use the supernatant from infected organoids,
which should contain infectious virions if replication is complete. This supernatant could then be used to infect other
organoid susceptible cell line models to confirm productive infection. 
What is the percentage of infected organoids or percentage of infected cholangiocytes per all cells that are obtained with this
model? From Fig 1F and 1G, the infection efficacy does not seem to be very high. The authors should dissociate the
organoids to single cell level and quantify infection rates together with other relevant markers by Flow cytometry. 
Line 124 – 125 Figure 1 A states that the morphologies were different and organoids had a thicker boundary. This is not very
clear in the representative image. Can the average length of the boundary be measured and quantified with numbers? 
Figure 1D: Why are the copies per cell decreasing over time? In addition, the control shows copies in fibroblast cell after 48
hours. This should also be quantified at a later timepoint to see if the viral counts go down because of lysis of infected cells.
This could also serve as a reason for decrease in copies in the organoid model. If not, then viability of organoid needs to be
measured with time. This graph also needs an appropriate negative control such as a heat-inactivated virus. 
Figure 1F and G is not very convincing by the image provided. It is not apparent that the signal for CK19 and SOX9 are
lower in infected organoids. This needs to be quantified by measuring the intensity of the IF signal. 
Figure 2 and 3 represent data from the bulk RNA sequencing analysis comparing infected and uninfected cholangiocyte
organoids and should be merged into one figure. Figure 2D and 3C should be presented in supplementary. Figure 2B and
3B can be merged. Figure 2E, 3D and 3E are validation of DEGs from the RNA-seq experiment. They can all be merged into
one supplementary figure. 
Figure 2D and 3C: Is this data from the RNA seq? Or validated in the same organoid models and donors? If so, the number
(N) needs to be indicated in figure legends and individual points need to be indicated on the graph. 
Figure 3F: This is the only functional readout of EMT transition presented, and similar to previous comment on Figure 1F
conclusions cannot only be made based on one representative microscopic image. The intensity of the signal needs to be
quantified and other functional readouts of EMT transition at protein level needs to be measured. Using flow cytometry

(Remarks to the Author) 
Review of Ye et al 
This manuscript by Ye et al entitled "HCMV infection disrupts cholangiocyte organoid barrier functions by promoting TGF-B-
mediated EMT" seeks to expand our knowledge base concerning cytomegalovirus infection of neonates, with an eye on
discerning whether HCMV could be an etiological agent involved in the development of biliary atresia (BA), a life-
threatening disease due to cholangiopathy of the bile ducts. The authors utilize an exciting new organoid model of
cholangiocytes to study the effects on growth and gene expression after HCMV infection. We know little about the ability of
HCMV to infect these cells, and the authors are the first to really show that cholangiocytes can be infected, albeit at a very
low level of permissiveness characterized by only a miniscule amount of viral shedding from what looks to be a very small
proportion of cells within the organoid themselves. The authors delve into gene expression changes both using bulk RNA
seq and single cell RNA seq on these infected organoids and (not surprisingly) find a dramatic number of changes after
infection. They also examine several actual BA patient tissue samples to look for evidence of viral DNA within these tissues
and whether or not some of the changes they see within the organoids with respect to gene expression hold true within
these tissue samples. They try to make the case that HCMV infection is potentially driving the development and progression

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

of BA via a TGFbeta driven transition to EMT. While some of the data is compelling and the utilization of a 3D platform for
infection is exciting, this reviewer has several issues with the interpretation and presentation of data that need to be
addressed. Major points where explanation/further analysis are warranted are outlined below. 
Major points: 
1. On pages 3 and 4 the authors try to establish the connection between HCMV and BA development. Clearly, there is very
little concrete evidence here and this is brought to bear by the literature cited saying there were HCMV positivity rates in BA
studies that ranged from ~8-~77%. This is a ridiculously broad spread. Unfortunately, their examination of patient tissues
was not tremendously more informative, landing at the higher end of this range (at 65.5%. However, the problem is that non-
BA patients showed 35% positivity, thus leaving this association somewhat fuzzy. 
2. Figure 1: 
Fig 1: This figure begins their analysis of infection in the cholangiocyte organoids. Fig 1A-C talk about the initial
interpretations of these infected organoids with respect to size and shape and number. There are many things that need to
be much more clearly defined in these experiments. First is the way in which the infections are performed. In the methods
section on p 22, the authors describe an infection protocol where organoids are disassociated, presumably counted
(although this is not explicitly stated), infected at an MOI=1, then asked to "reform" into organoids by resuspension in
Matrigel. Several questions arise: are the mock organoids treated in the same way? I am presuming yes, but again, this is
not explicitly mentioned. How long were the cells exposed to virus prior to rinsing and "reforming"? What percentage of cells
are actually infected in this manner? You are infecting at an MOI=1, but do all cells take up the virus? This could (and
should) very easily be discerned via fixing and staining the disassociated/infected cells at 1-2 hpi with an antibody to either
pp71 or pp65 (tegument proteins). All cells should possess punctate spots of either of these proteins either within the
cytoplasm or within the nucleus of all cells if the virus is capable of binding and entering these cells. This is incredibly
important information, as it is clear from all the organoid images that are showing GFP positivity that not all cells are
productively infected. Which leads to the question - what percentage of cells are GFP+ in these organoids? This must be
explicitly stated somewhere. 
This "reforming" of organoids and the subsequent lowering of numbers and size could all be due to cell death precipitated
by the virus. This is not discussed or taken into consideration anywhere. Are cell numbers declining due to virus induced
apoptosis or necrosis? This could easily be examined if instead of "reforming" organoids, the cells were allowed to stay in
suspension as single cells (or as a monolayer)? Additionally, have these experiments been carried out for a longer period of
time (the longest appears to be ~12 dpi). Is this time taken due to cell death/destruction of the organoids? 
The problem continues when data is presented in bulk RT-qPCR and enumeration of HCMV DNA copies within the
organoids. Again, how many of these cells actually harbor genomes, express transcripts and shed virions? Given the sparse
localization of GFP signals (and the small proportion of cells in "cluster 5"), the answer is probably not very many. 
In Fig 1E, the authors talk about taking the supernatant from the infected organoids and also seeding infected cells onto
fibroblast monolayers. Information needs to be given regarding whether this supernatant was diluted and how many cells
were seeded onto the wells of fibroblasts shown. As it is, this figure gives very little information except to see a minute
quantity of virus is shed, presumably by a very few cells within the organoids. 
Lastly, the immunofluorescence images in Fig 1F and G are not nearly high enough magnification to conclude that GFP+
cells are showing "reduced expression" of these markers. And are these whole organoid Z stack reconstructions? They do
not appear to be single sections, but this needs to be explicitly stated. The choice of marker proteins to examine should also
be explained. 
3. Fig 3: This figure focuses on the infected organoid's expression of EMT-associated genes (again, in bulk RNA format).
The authors are exploring whether there may be a role of EMT in BA pathogenesis and whether infection could be
promoting this phenotype. 
Importantly, the authors neglect the literature on HCMV and glioblastomas that has shown promotion of EMT in this
pathogenesis of glia. Certainly, the RNA seq and confirmatory RT-qPCR data show quite clear changes occurring in the
mRNA levels of these genes as called out (that are involved in EMT and in fibrosis). 
The authors point out two proteins that are a signature for EMT - upregulation of N-cadherin (CDH2) and downregulation of
E-cadherin (CDH1). The RNA data analysis certainly shows this trend. However, as presented in Fig 3F, the claims
regarding GFP+ cells showing the most changes in the levels of these proteins cannot be substantiated. These images,
once again, are much too low magnification to definitively say anything about co-localizing signals. 
4. Fig S3: This figure is troubling in several ways. First, why is EBV (which the authors claim is not infecting the cells)
changing their size and shape similarly to HCMV? Secondly, CDH1 is not downregulated by HCMV infection in these
experiments (as it was in Fig 3). Lastly, the IF images shown in S3H and I are just not convincing. 
5. Fig S4: This same phenomenon of mRNA increases for both CDH1 and 2 are seen with the BTS treatment, which the
authors say causes EMT. However, once again the protein imaging that is presented in S4G does not show the predicted
changes (neither E nor N-cadherin are increased in these images). Additionally, why is the Para treatment not behaving like
predicted? No explanation is offered.

6. Fig 4: Once again, the IF images showing ZO-1 staining are not at all convincing, much higher magnification is needed to
discern specific apical versus basolateral staining. In addition, several papers have shown that HCMV infection causes loss
of ZO1 integrity in different types of cells (these should be cited). Thus, this would not be a novel finding. The Rhodamine
exclusion experiment shown in Fig 4C, while clear in the mock-infected organoids is not at all convincing in the infected
organoids, once again due to the imaging conditions. Additionally, why is the HCMV-infected organoid pictured in the

Were these all done at the same time/conditions? Why do the CMV

7. Fig 5: The authors convincingly show that in their infected organoids, TGFb is increased at both the mRNA and protein

infection (these should be cited). Once again, however, these data are inconsistent with those presented in Fig 3 with
respect to CDH1/E-cadherin changes. Here, there are essentially no changes at either the mRNA or protein level after
infection. Additionally, it would be very informative to see what percentage of cells were GFP+ in the HCMV versus
HCMV+SB organoids - they appear to be dramatically different. 
8. Fig 6: These images once again are not high enough magnification to make definitive calls regarding colocalization of
signals. Frankly, the images shown in Fig S6 are dramatically more convincing and should be included in the main figures. 
9. Fig 7: 
This single cell RNA seq data clearly shows differences between the infected and uninfected organoids and there
are clear "shifts" between the percentages of cells within the 5 different clusters. It is also clear that all clusters have some

brings me to the crux of the problem: what percentage of the cells within each cluster are actually expressing virus and viral
proteins/mRNA!! You should be able to tell this from your single cell data. Yes, cells within all the clusters express viral
mRNA - but what actual percentage of those cells are expressing? And do all the other cells have latently infected
genomes?? Or are they real bystander cells?? 
Importantly, certainly there are shifts in the proportions of cells within the clusters, but this doesn't mean actual viral
protein/RNA expression is what's driving this shift. Additionally, and very importantly, what constitutes GFP+ vs GFP- cells?
How are these gated?? A recent paper studying RNA seq changes in cerebral organoids from the Terhune lab showed that
there was not really a "GFP-" population, but instead, a GFPlow population of cells. This must be clearly defined here.

Overall, the premise and major results of this resubmission are greatly improved and the authors have done a large amount
of additional experimentation to satisfy the reviewers' comments and should be commended for this effort. 
I think the major conclusion as stated at the end of the introduction, that CMV can directly infect cholangiocyte organoids and
induce TFGb/EMT pathways is justified by their data. Unfortunately, I think a lot of their extra efforts in trying to define the
infection parameters with respect to gfp+/gfp- cell populations is creating more confusion/problems than it may be worth…. 
The main issue is this: they show in figure 1g a FISH where they have used a viral-specific probe to assess their infected
organoids. They show ~30% of the cells are FISH/genome+ - but of this population, only ~10% are also gfp+ (and actively
shedding virus and making viral proteins). So that means another 20% of the cells have genomes (at low copy numbers) and
according to their RNA seq data are expressing a limited set of viral genes. 
However, the MAJOR problem is that within
these organoids, 70% of the cells have no sign of viral proteins/genomes! In and of itself, this reviewer doesn't have a real
problem with this…. some cell types are not hugely permissive for infection…. 
However, the real problem is that when they are grouping their infected cells into "gfp+" (which is a very small proportion of
the population) and gfp- (which is essentially 90% of the organoid cells), they are in essence "handicapping" the gfp-
population, because the LARGE majority of those cells are uninfected and presumably behaving a lot like their mock
infected cells. 
This certainly looks to be the case when you look at the single cell analysis – the distributions of cells and
expression of marker proteins examined looks much more like mock infected organoids because they are 70% mock-
infected cells! 
Despite this "disadvantage", there still are clear differences in some of the parameters that are examined, however, this
reviewer thinks it is very difficult to interpret data presented as "gfp-", when it is in essence a heavily weighted uninfected
population. 
Perhaps the more appropriate way to show the data is to use the gfp+ data that has been generated (which has clear
distinctions from uninfected organoids) and the "infected organoids" (which is clearly a mixed population of cells, but shows
