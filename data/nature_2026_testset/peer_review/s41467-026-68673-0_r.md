Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript introduces a novel post-treatment strategy using γ-ray irradiation to enhance the interface quality of carbon
nanotube field-effect transistors (CNTFETs). The authors convincingly demonstrate that high-energy, low-dose γ-rays
decompose organic residues at the CNT/dielectric interface without compromising the structural integrity of the CNTs,
resulting in lower off-state current, improved subthreshold swing (SS), and increased radiation tolerance. Additionally, quasi-
gate-all-around (quasi-GAA) architecture is proposed to mitigate irradiation-induced charge trapping. The study is well-
structured, experimentally rigorous, and shows potential for implementation in foundries. However, several key issues
require clarification or further investigation before the manuscript can be considered for publication. 
Major Comments: 
1. Raman Spectral Shift (Figure 1f): 
The G-band peak in the Raman spectra appears to shift slightly post γ-ray exposure. The authors should clarify whether this
shift is statistically significant and, if so, discuss its possible physical origins. 
2. CB-LBDE to CB-HBDE Evolution (Supplementary Fig. 6): 
Do aligned CNT films exhibit a similar increase in CB-LBDEs post-irradiation as seen in random networks? Furthermore, at
doses beyond 3 Mrad(Si), e.g., 10 Mrad(Si), is there continued transformation toward CB-HBDEs? Additional discussion or
supporting data would be helpful. 
3. Dielectric Robustness under Scaling: 
Given that the dielectric stack used is ~10 nm thick, it is unclear whether thinner dielectrics (e.g., sub-1 nm EOT), typical in
advanced nodes, would remain robust under γ-ray exposure. The authors should discuss potential leakage or degradation,
and whether interface state density (Dit) or CV measurements have been performed to assess this. 
4. CNT Diameter and Chirality Distribution: 
Key structural parameters such as diameter and chirality, which affect bandgap and device behavior, are not provided.
Please clarify the type and distribution of CNTs used in this study. 
5. Aligned CNTs Device-Level Results: 
The authors conduct material analysis on aligned CNTs, but no device-level results are presented. Could similar
improvements in Ion, Ioff, and SS be expected for aligned CNTFETs post-irradiation? Inclusion of such data would
strengthen the manuscript. 
6. Substrate-Dependent Vth Shift (Figure 4b, d): 
Threshold voltage shifts differently for devices on Si/SiO
₂
 and PSPI substrates. Please elaborate on the mechanisms behind
this discrepancy. 
7. Role of Contact Resistance: 
The manuscript attributes Ion/Ioff trends to channel property changes. However, it remains unclear how contact resistance
effects were excluded. Has output characteristic (Id–Vds) analysis been performed pre- and post-irradiation? 
8. CB-LBDE to CB-HBDE Conversion Mechanism (Figure 3c, d): 
Is there direct evidence—experimental or theoretical—for the proposed conversion of CB-LBDEs to CB-HBDEs under
irradiation? 
9. Hysteresis Consideration (Figure 3): 
Has device hysteresis been evaluated post-irradiation? Since SS and Ion/Ioff are affected, hysteresis data could clarify
underlying charge dynamics. 
10. Non-monotonic Ion Behavior (Figure 3c–e):

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

Ion increases with γ-ray dose up to 1 Mrad(Si), but decreases at 3 Mrad(Si). Could you please clarify the mechanism behind
this non-monotonic response? 
11. Elemental Composition at Au/Al Interface (Figure 2c): 
What do orange dots represent? Please identify the elemental species present at the Au/Al interface. 
12. Extrapolation from MWCNTs to SWCNTs: 
The paper references previous MWCNT studies at 100 Mrad (Si), but SWCNTs are more susceptible to damage due to their
lower atom counts and bond redundancy. Could the authors provide Raman D/G ratios for SWCNTs post-irradiation to
confirm their structural integrity? 
13. PCz Decomposition vs. Self-Annealing: 
Could the observed effects attributed to PCz decomposition instead arise from high-κ dielectric self-annealing? Please
include EOT or κ-value data pre- and post-irradiation to support the mechanism. 
14. Decomposition Residue Effects: 
If PCz decomposition occurs, what becomes of the byproducts? Could they affect device parameters such as SS or Ion? 
15. Contradictory Effects of Gap State Removal: 
The manuscript claims that the removal of gap states leads to increased Ion. However, reduced gap states should lower both
Ion and Ioff by suppressing trap-assisted tunneling. Please clarify the mechanism enabling the observed Ion enhancement. 
16. Dielectric Symmetry in Quasi-GAA: 
Why are different dielectrics used for top and back-gates? Would using the same dielectric improve gate control symmetry? 
17. Dual Role of HfAlOx: 
HfAlOx serves as both a dielectric and a dopant. Could back-gate bias modulate doping levels via dipole effects? How is the
doping level quantified, and what minimum film thickness ensures reliable n-type doping? 
18. γ-Ray Induced Degenerate Doping Mechanism: 
What is the underlying mechanism for degenerate doping under γ-ray exposure? Is there a key data point that
unambiguously demonstrates this phenomenon? 
19. Electrostatic Control in Top vs. Back Gate (Figure S10): 
Top-gate devices exhibit higher SS and Ioff. Why is electrostatic control poorer in this configuration? 
20. Scalability to Submicron Channel Lengths: 
Current quasi-GAA devices use ~4 µm channels. Have short-channel (Lch < 1 µm) devices been tested? How do parasitic
effects scale with irradiation? 
21. Transient Defect Dynamics (Figure 3e): 
The SS and Ioff recoveries at high dose suggest transient defect kinetics. Can the authors provide time-resolved
measurements to distinguish between dose-rate and time-dependent recovery? 
Minor Comments: 
S1. Normalize drain current to CNT width for comparability (Figures 2e, 3a–b, 4a,c). 
S2. Use consistent y-axis scaling in Figures 4a and 4c to aid comparison. 
S3. Clearly define the color legend in Figure S10. 
S4. Clarify whether on/off ratio is measured at Vds = 2 V and whether it is width normalized. 
S5. Consider violin or box-and-whisker plots in Figures 3 and 4 to better represent data distribution.

1. Redundant abbreviations (Line 193). 
2. Use of “Efficiency.” as a standalone term (Line 202) lacks context. 
3. Incomplete or undefined formula (Line 279).

Comment (1):  Raman Spectral Shift (Figure 1f): The G-band peak in the Raman spectra appears to shift slightly post γ-ray exposure. The authors should clarify whether this shift is statistically significant and, if so, discuss its possible physical origins. Our reply: Thank you for your insightful comments and careful evaluation of our work. We sincerely appreciate your observation regarding the Raman spectral shift in Fig. 1f and the opportunity to clarify this phenomenon.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

Upon re-examining the original experimental data for the CNT networks deposited on Si/SiO2 substrates, we confirm a consistent redshift of ~2 cm-1 in the G-band peak position after γ-ray exposure. This shift is statistically significant and is intrinsically linked to the irradiation process.  High-energy photons generate abundant electron-hole pairs in the insulating SiO2 layer. While electrons rapidly migrate out, holes exhibit exceptionally low mobility and become trapped at defect sites (Nature Electronics 4, 243-253 (2021)). This process leads to progressive positive charging of the substrate – a mechanism also responsible for threshold voltage shifts observed in γ-irradiated top-gated CNTFETs. The accumulated positive charge electrostatically dopes the CNT network, elevating the Fermi level analogous to n-type doping. This electronic modification directly induces the observed G-band redshift through electron-phonon coupling (Physics reports 409, 47-99 (2005), Advances in Physics 60, 413-550 (2011)). We are grateful for this thoughtful question, which prompted us to rigorously validate the shift and deepen our mechanistic understanding of charge trapping dynamics in irradiated CNT-substrate systems. Note of modification:  We have added “Supplementary Note. 9” in supplementary materials. Comment (2):  CB-LBDE to CB-HBDE Evolution (Supplementary Fig. 6): Do aligned CNT films exhibit a similar increase in CB-LBDEs post-irradiation as seen in random networks? Furthermore, at doses beyond 3 Mrad(Si), e.g., 10 Mrad(Si), is there continued transformation toward CB-HBDEs? Additional discussion or supporting data would be helpful. Our reply: We sincerely appreciate the reviewer’s insightful inquiry regarding the dose-dependent evolution of chemical bonds in irradiated CNT films.

Our experimental data comprehensively demonstrates that aligned CNT films exhibit identical transformation behavior to random networks under γ-ray irradiation. As shown in Fig. 1d-h, Fig. 3e, and Supplementary Fig. 6, both architectures display matching trends in Raman D/G ratio evolution – characterized by an initial increase at low doses (~ 3 Mrad(Si)) followed by a progressive decline at higher doses – along with parallel XPS spectral shifts indicating CB-LBDE to CB-HBDE conversion. This consistency originates from the shared irradiation mechanism: The structural reorganization occurs primarily in the organic sorting/alignment polymers (PCz and 2-butene-1,4-diol), while the CNT backbone remains intrinsically radiation-tolerant (Fig. 1d). Regarding the dose-response trajectory beyond 3 Mrad(Si), our multi-point experimental dataset (spanning 0-20/100 Mrad(Si) for aligned/networked film) provides robust evidence of continuous CB-HBDE formation. At intermediate doses including 10 Mrad(Si), we observe a monotonic decrease in D/G ratio (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 6b) accompanied by progressive XPS signatures of bond reorganization.  We have strengthened the discussion to explicitly articulate the consistency in aligned/random CNT films and the continuous evolution in chemical bonds.
