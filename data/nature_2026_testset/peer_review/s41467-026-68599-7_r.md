Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study introduces PRS-hub, a web-based platform for computing polygenic scores (PGS) using a range of methods. The
authors use the platform to derive PGS for various diseases and traits based on European and African GWAS. A set of
single-source PGS methods is applied, as well as three multi-source approaches. The PGS are evaluated and compared
using the UK Biobank (UKB), which is divided into training and testing subsets—with the training set used to tune the PGS
and the test set used to evaluate performance. The authors explore the impact of LD reference sample size and the number
of variants considered, and they report the computational efficiency (in terms of time and memory) for each method. 
Key findings include that LDpred2 performs best for most traits when using European GWAS, and that LDpred2-meta (a
combination of population-specific PGS) outperforms dedicated multi-source methods when using both European and
African GWAS. The computational efficiency of LDpred2 is also highlighted. The authors report that ensemble approaches
combining PGS across methods perform best. They suggest an optimal LD reference sample size and note that using
denser genomic coverage can negatively affect PGS performance. 
This study makes some novel contributions, but there are several major and minor issues that should be addressed. 
Major comments: 
- The authors overstate the novelty of the study in several places. For example, they claim “there are no widely comparative
studies that are sufficiently tailored to externally benchmark the efficacy of various methods, nor are there standardized
platforms for efficient, large-scale PRS computation.” This claim is overstated. Several independent comparisons of PGS
methods already exist (e.g., PMID 33945532, 38908374, and two studies already cited by the authors), in addition to
comparisons within individual PGS method papers. Furthermore, at least two studies (PMID 38908374, 39292536) present
software pipelines offering standardised implementations of multiple PGS methods. Comparisons of multi-source PGS
approaches are also available (PMID 39323095, and preprint https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.03.27.25324773). The authors
should contextualise their findings within the existing literature and clearly identify their novel contributions. One such
contribution might be the availability of a public, web-based platform where users can submit GWAS for PGS analysis—
although the authors should acknowledge the recent PGSfusion preprint (https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.08.05.606619), which
offers similar functionality. 
- Since PRS-hub is presented as a community platform with a web interface and computing back-end, the manuscript should
address data privacy and access. It is unclear how sensitive data are handled—for instance, if users upload GWAS
summary statistics or individual-level genotype data as LD reference panels, what measures are in place to ensure data
security and compliance with data transfer agreements (DTAs)? This is a critical practical issue for potential users. 
- The results section focuses on identifying the “best” method per trait, but this can be misleading without statistical context.
No statistical tests or confidence intervals are reported to assess whether differences in performance (e.g., AUC or R²) are
significant. Simply reporting which method performed best for each trait is observational. The analysis would be
strengthened by formal comparisons, such as pairwise tests or confidence intervals for top-performing methods. 
- Line 245-247: The authors say ‘weirdly’ SBayesR performance is unstable as LD reference sample size increases This

requires more explanation. Could there be an error in how LD reference panels were generated? The poor performance of
SBayesR is surprising given previous studies. A useful sanity check would be to run the method using the LD matrices
provided by the developers (https://cnsgenomics.com/software/gctb/#LDmatrices). 
- The authors compare PRS-CS performance using 1.1M versus 6.6M variants and find lower performance with the denser
variant set. This is counterintuitive and inconsistent with previous findings (e.g., PMID 38689000), which suggest denser
SNP sets can improve prediction. The authors should assess this across other methods that can handle 6.6M variants (e.g.,
MegaPRS). 
- An important limitation is the lack of evaluation of methods that do not require individual-level data for tuning. For example,
LDpred2-auto, PRS-CS-auto, and X-Wing’s LEOPARD algorithm are designed for such use cases. This reduces the
practical utility of the comparison. 
- The study does not characterise method performance across different GWAS sample sizes, trait architectures (e.g.,
heritability, polygenicity), or populations. For example, single-source method performance using only African-ancestry
GWAS is not explored. Including this would improve the generalisability of the findings. 
Minor comments: 
- Line 124: The authors mention two ensemble methods, but it seems they used the same ensemble strategy separately for
single- and multi-source PGS. This distinction should be clarified. 
- The explanation of LDpred2-meta is ambiguous. Please clarify whether the European and African PGS were combined via
meta-analysis of effect sizes or inclusion in a joint regression model. If it is the latter, the name “-meta” is misleading; in other
approaches this is referred to as “-multi”. 
- Terminology (PRS vs PGS): The manuscript uses “polygenic risk score (PRS)” even for non-disease traits. It would be
more accurate to use the more general term polygenic score (PGS), as adopted by the Polygenic Score Catalog and recent
literature. 
- Clarify how training and testing subsets in UKB were used. For example, line 182 introduces the ensemble method, but it is
not clearly stated that both the selection of the best PGS and the training of the ensemble model were performed in training
data, with evaluation in test data. 
- 
- Utility of odds ratio analysis: The manuscript reports OR/SD for disease traits (e.g., ~2.3 for schizophrenia), but it’s unclear
what additional information this provides over AUC or R². Clarify the unique value of OR/SD comparisons. 
- LD reference sample size analysis is interesting but appears to be limited to European GWAS. It would be valuable to
repeat this using African GWAS. Also, potential ancestry mismatch between LD reference and GWAS populations should be
discussed. 
- Lines 264–266: The authors report SBayesR as the slowest method, which contradicts previous benchmarks (e.g., PMID
36585786). They also mention tuning iteration numbers (line 552), but the SBayesR developers do not recommend this.
This may unfairly bias the reported performance. By default, GCTB uses 10,000 iterations. 
- Line 275: The phrase “acceptable range” should be clarified. What constitutes an acceptable range? 
- It seems unusual that memory usage was highest when using LD reference panels derived from fewer individuals. Since
several methods use precomputed LD matrices, sample size should not affect downstream memory use. This result needs
further explanation. 
- Line 319: From prior experience with X-Wing, I’m surprised by the reported run time. Please clarify whether the LEOPARD
step was included, as this significantly increases runtime. 
- The authors should consider including SBayesRC, which has been shown to outperform many other methods (e.g., PMID
38689000). 
- Line 345–346: The Ni et al. paper cited actually concludes that SBayesR and MegaPRS perform best. Therefore, this
citation contradicts the authors’ claim that their results are consistent with prior studies. 
- Line 469: “dockized” appears to be a typo. Please correct. 
- Lines 541–543: The text suggests LDAK uses the GCTA heritability model, but it specifically does not. See:
https://dougspeed.com/heritability-model/ 
- Please clarify how each method handles the MHC/HLA region. This can substantially influence PGS performance,
particularly for traits like autoimmune diseases.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a benchmarking study of polygenic risk score (PRS) methods based on UK Biobank data. The
authors introduce PRS
‐
hub—a web
‐
based platform that standardizes the computation and comparison of multiple PRS
methods. They evaluate 13 methods (including eight single-ancestry and two multi-ancestry approaches, as well as an
ensemble strategy) across 36 complex traits using metrics such as AUC, odds ratios, and Pearson R², computational cost,
and the effect of linkage disequilibrium (LD) panel sample size. The authors claim that there are no frameworks for
systematic evaluation of PRS methods. This claim misses the method comparisons and frameworks presented in Pain et al.,
2021 and, more recently, 
Monti et al., 2024. 
To quote: Monti et al. “evaluate polygenic scores derived via seven methods in five biobank studies (totaling about 1.2
million participants) across 16 diseases and quantitative traits, building on a reference-stan-dardized framework.” 
Pain, Oliver, et al. “Evaluation of polygenic prediction methodology within a reference-standardized framework.” PLoS
genetics 17.5 (2021): e1009021. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1009021 
Monti, Remo, et al. "Evaluation of polygenic scoring methods in five biobanks shows larger variation between biobanks than
methods and finds benefits of ensemble learning." The American Journal of Human Genetics 111.7 (2024): 1431-1447. 
Neither paper is being referenced in the manuscript, marking an oversight that significantly limits the novel contributions of
the proposed paper. Pain et al has been published in 2021 and Monti et al has been published in July 2024, 8 months
before submission of the manuscript at hand. 
Even the secondary claim that ensembling of PRS methods improves results has been reported by Monti et al., 2024. 
Strengths 
1. Comprehensive and Standardized Framework: 
The PRS
‐
hub platform provides an automated pipeline that supports a wide variety of PRS methods under standardized
data processing protocols. This uniformity helps minimize methodological variability and facilitates direct method
comparisons. 
2. Detailed Performance and Computational Analysis: 
Beyond prediction accuracy, the manuscript thoroughly investigates computational efficiency—including CPU hours,
memory usage, and the influence of LD reference panel size. This practical perspective is valuable, particularly for
researchers facing computational constraints. 
3. Inclusion of Multi-Ancestry and Ensemble Strategies: 
The paper evaluates not only single-ancestry methods but also applies and compares multi-ancestry approaches. 
Comparison to Monti et al. 
Scope and Data: 
• Monti et al. evaluated seven polygenic scoring methods across five biobanks (totaling about 1.2 million participants) and
across 16 diseases/quantitative traits. Their work is notable for its cross-biobank analysis and for highlighting that variation
in PGS effect sizes between biobanks exceeds the variation observed between methods. 
• PRS
‐
hub (current manuscript) is based primarily on UK Biobank data (with an analysis also involving an African ancestry
subset), evaluating 13 methods over 36 traits. The paper focuses on systematic benchmarking within a single resource. 
Methodological Focus: 
• Monti et al. emphasize the influence of hyperparameter tuning and the enhanced performance of ensemble PGS
approaches. They demonstrate that cross-validation tuning yields reliable improvements and note that no single method
consistently dominates across traits and biobanks. 
• PRS
‐
hub similarly finds that ensemble methods (and specifically the integration of the best
‐
performing scores from diverse
methods) can lead to incremental improvements. PRS-hub extends the comparison by including a broader set of methods
and the impact of varying LD panel sizes. 
Software and Practical Utility: 
• Monti et al. have released an open-source workflow software (prspipe) along with an online interactive resource. 
• PRS
‐
hub provides a web-based platform and a workflow software pipeline. 
Generally, there is significant overlap in findings—particularly regarding the benefits of ensemble approaches and the
challenges of variable performance—that suggest the incremental novelty of the present manuscript is somewhat limited
relative to Monti et al. 
Assessment of the Citation Omission 
Given that Monti et al. was published in July 2024 and the current manuscript was sent out for review in March 2025, the
authors had ample time to become aware of and incorporate that study into their discussion. Considering the relevance and
prominence of Monti et al. in the field, the authors should address this gap in a revision by: 
• Citing both Pain et al. as well as Monti et al. appropriately. 
• Discussing how the findings from Monti et al. compare to their own. 
• Clarifying the unique novel contributions of PRS-hub. 
The manuscript presents a valuable resource in the form of PRS-hub and offers a benchmarking study that will be of interest
to the community. However, in its current form, the absence of a discussion of Pain et al. and in particular Monti et al., a study
and similar resource that substantially overlaps in scope and findings, weakens its positioning and novelty.

(Remarks to the Author) 
I would like to thank the authors for their thorough and thoughtful response to my previous comments. The manuscript is
much improved, particularly in terms of situating the study within the context of existing research, which clarifies its impact. 
Major comments 
1. “Inclusion of a larger number of SNPs does not improve PGS performance” – This is an important finding. While relatively
little work has been done on this question, the SBayesRC paper reports that increased SNP coverage does improve
prediction when functional annotations are incorporated. Did the authors use functional annotations when running
MegaPRS, using the BLD-LDAK annotations as recommended in the MegaPRS paper? I recommend the authors
acknowledge this result and discuss why their findings may differ from the SBayesRC paper. 
2. SBayesRC exclusion – The manuscript notes a preliminary comparison with SBayesRC, but argues that the method
could not be fully integrated due to additional input requirements (functional annotations) and use of LD matrices from a
different reference. Linked to my previous comment, MegaPRS also allows inclusion of functional annotation - Could the
authors clarify whether MegaPRS was run with annotations in this study? Furthermore, the claim that SBayesRC could not
be included because it uses different LD reference data is not convincing, as developers include code for calculating LD
matrices from any sample, so LD matrices could have been generated using the 1KG sample, as they were for other
methods, such as SBayesR and LDpred2. Please can the authors clarify their reason for excluding SBayesRC. Saying it
goes beyond the remit of the paper is fine, but should be flagged as a limitation. 
3. The reported result that MegaPRS underperforms ptclump is surprising and inconsistent with findings from several other
studies. Even without considering functional annotations, I would not have expected this method to perform so badly. This
discrepancy warrants further discussion/explanation. 
4. Their comparison of methods using summary statistics alone to tune PGS is very limited. Several of the methods they
include are do not require tuning, but the authors do not highlight this (e.g. sbayer, sblup, sdpr), and several other methods
include an option for sumstat tuning, but they are not evaluated (e.g. PRS-CSx, MegaPRS, XWING). The authors say they
do not include XWING sumstat tuning approach (LEOPARD) because it subsamples the GWAS and would therefor be
incomparable to the other methods. However, this argument is flawed, as the GWAS subsampling is only used to tune the
PGS, the final SNP-weights are based on the full GWAS sumstats. The are practical advantages to sumstat-tuning that the
authors are not recognising (discussed here: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39379999/), and it will limit the utility of the
PGS produced by PGS-hub. It is fine to exclude, but it should be noted as a limitation, and their rationale for not including
XWING’s LEOPARD step should be clarified. 
Minor comments 
1. I appreciate the inclusion of statistical tests comparing methods. Could the authors clarify whether the pairwise t-tests
across traits account for correlation between traits? It isn’t a major point, but if not, this should be highlighted as a limitation,
or a test that accounts for the non-independence across phenotypes should be used. 
2. “Existing frameworks for PGS computation are constrained by standardized automatic pipelines and cross-ancestry
coverage” – This phrasing is unclear. It appears to frame standardisation and cross-ancestry coverage as drawbacks,
though these might be considered advantages. Could the authors clarify? 
3. In the new flowchart, the training and testing datasets are shown with the same sample size. Is this an error, or does it
reflect use of cross-validation? 
4. SBayesR runtime improved as expected since the revision which omits the grid search. However, it remains surprisingly
slow compared to reports in previous studies. Can the authors explain why? 
5. The claim that PRS-CS runtime increases monotonically is not consistent with the results. The pattern appears in
LDpred2 and lassosum2, but not PRS-CS. Could this reflect specific features of the bigsnpr implementation (e.g. more
iterations to converge)? For methods that do not use pre-computed LD matrices, runtime differences may instead reflect on-
the-fly LD estimation.

Addressing Prior Concerns: 
The major points from my initial review—lack of context to prior work, overstated novelty, and absence of statistical
validation—have been addressed comprehensively. The rebuttal letter provides detailed evidence of these revisions and
their locations in the manuscript. 
Novelty and Positioning: 
The revised text now correctly acknowledges earlier benchmarking frameworks such as Pain et al. (2021) and Monti et al.
(2024). The authors position PGS-hub mainly as a web-based automation and comparison platform integrating a broad set
of methods, rather than as a conceptually new benchmarking paradigm. This is an appropriate and realistic framing. 
However, while the implementation and scale are commendable, the conceptual novelty remains limited—particularly since
Monti et al. (2024) already reported ensemble improvements and multi-biobank benchmarking. The present manuscript
extends this with additional methods and computational metrics, but the scientific conclusions are largely confirmatory. 
Technical Rigor: 
The newly added statistical validation (bootstrapping and pairwise tests) strengthens the robustness of the comparisons.
The expanded section on data privacy and compliance is well written and appropriate for a public web resource. 
Clarity and Presentation: 
The manuscript is clearer and better structured than before. The figures and tables (including Supplementary Tables S12–
S13 and Extended Data Fig. 6) now provide meaningful statistical context. Some language inconsistencies remain, but
these are minor and editorial. 
Remaining Concern: 
The main findings reinforce rather than advance the state of knowledge established by prior benchmarking studies. The
contribution lies primarily in the integration and accessibility of the PGS-hub platform, rather than in methodological or
conceptual innovation.

12. Utility of odds ratio analysis: The manuscript reports OR/SD for disease traits (e.g.,

Authors’ Reply: Thank you for your comment regarding the utility of OR/SD. We agree 
that AUC and R² are standard performance metrics in PGS evaluation. However, OR 
per SD offers complementary and important value by quantify ing the increase in 
disease odds per unit increase in standardized PGS,  as highlighted in the paper by 
Wand et al. ( Nature. 2021 Mar;591(7849):211 -219), which emphasizes improving 
reporting standards for polygenic scores in risk prediction studies, OR per SD holds 
significant complementary importance. This metric quantifies the increase in disease 
odds per unit increase in the standardized PGS, offering a clear and interpretable 
measure of the association strength between the PGS and the disease trait, positively 
corrected with AUC or R². But, this measure is particularly interpretable in a clinical or 
epidemiological context to convey the risk of a particular individual and aligns with 
common reporting standards in large-scale studies (e.g., PGS Catalog).

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 42 -->

One key advantage of OR per SD, as compared to AUC or R², lies in its 
individual-level interpretability. While AUC and R² provide valuable summaries of 
overall performance across the entire study population (e.g., discriminative ability for 
AUC and proport ion of variance explained for R²), OR per SD directly quantifies the 
incremental risk for an individual with a higher PGS. This is particularly relevant for 
translating PGS research into clinical or public health contexts, where understanding 
personalized risk is of paramount importance. 
Additionally, it is important to note that the OR per SD is conceptually and 
statistically related to both AUC and R². In regression frameworks, these metrics are 
generally positively corrected with AUC or R² , reflecting th eir shared foundation in 
quantifying the strength of association between PGS and disease status. However, 
each metric serves a distinct purpose: while AUC and R² excel in characterizing 
population-level performance, OR per SD offers unique insights into in dividual-level 
risk stratification, making it a valuable addition to the standard metrics.
