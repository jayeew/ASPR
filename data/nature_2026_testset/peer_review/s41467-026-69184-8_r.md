Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Zhao et al examines the molecular and functional effects of natural product derivative Uvarigranol D
(UGD) on Nav1.7 voltage gated sodium channels. Since these channels are primarily expressed in sensory neurons and
human gain of function variants are associated with genetic pain disorders, the authors well-justify their inquiry in terms of
relevance to clinical 
pathology, and potential for drug development pain management. Using voltage clamp recordings from
DRG neurons (TTx- sensitive vs insensitive) and using overexpression systems (Nav transgene expression in HEK cells),
the authors demonstrate that UGD has modest selectivity among six Nav subtypes, which is a key feature to avoid systemic
toxicity. Results from current clamp recordings indicate UAG is a modestly effective inhibitor of DRG action potentials. In vivo
results from a rat model of neuropathic pain (sciatic nerve constriction injury) demonstrate UGDs efficacy after IP injection.
Evidence in the form of electrophysiology and modeling simulation results support a mechanistic hypothesis, where UGD
binds to Nav1.7 in the slow inactivated state through association with residues found in the DIV pore helix and
transmembrane pore helix (T1389, 
Gln360, Ile394, Lys1395, Phe1737 and Tyr1744). Beyond drug-channel interactions,
these findings shed light on the biophysical and structural determinates of slow inactivation of Nav1.7 channels. However, it
should be noted that previous work has established presentence for pore-mediated effects on slow inactivation of sodium
and other related channels. The discussion section should provide greater context to the proposed UGD-Nav1.7 binding site
compared to those which have been previously structurally determined 
(Reviewed, PMID: 37270609). In our appraisal, the
authors carry out important experiments and present interesting findings, but the results fall short of being persuasive of the
stated key findings, which limits the impact of this study. 
Major concerns: 
1) The structural evidence of UGD-Nav1.7 engagement is tenuous. 
We understand that the results from 
molecular modeling
and mutagenesis studies support the domain IV pore binding site. However, the functional results can result from allostery or
membrane effects. Evidence in the form of direct binding or structural determination would mitigate these concerns. 
2) The evidence that IP injected UGD reaches and is acting on peripheral nerves expressing Nav1.7 channels is weak. We
understand that the in vivo efficacy is supportive, but UGA could be active centrally or through non-neuronal and off-target
effects. Establishing drug distribution after IP injection could mitigate these concerns. 
3) The evidence that IP injected UGD produces an analgesic effect rather than an anesthetic effect is not demonstrated. 
4) The X-axis of ALL drug concentration-effect plots (Figures 1E, 2H, 3B, 3D, 5D, 6C) should be plotted using log 10
micromolar scale, which is the standard in pharmacology and easiest for the reader to evaluate drug potency. As shown, the
scales exaggerate the differences among the data sets. 
5) The example current traces in Figure 4C do not match the quantified comparative activation and inactivation kinetics
shown in Figure 4D. 
6) The in vivo effects of UGD 
lasts hours. This suggests that the drug has a slow off rate in its association with the channel
target. This effect should be tested in vitro. 
7) Action potential recordings are notorious for historious effects, where APs fail to fire over time. Do the Nav currents and
DRG action potentials recover when UGD washed off? 
8) The current magnitudes shown in Figure 5A are not interpretable on this scale and needs revision.

9) The ECG traces at the top of Figure 7E are low resolution and should be revised. 
10) The manuscript has many grammatical errors and should be carefully revised. These are just a few. From Summary,
Lines 72 “bound binds”? 
Lines 543 “If UGD selectively binds to the inactivated states of VGSCs, then it would be more
efficacies at modulating Nav1.7 than Nav1.2..” 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Zhao et al describes the activity of Uvarigranol D (UVD), a small molecule discovered from a high
throughput screen against the analgesic target Nav1.7. UVD has similar potency at human Nav1.1-1.8 (2-7 uM), but
interestingly, shows greatest efficacy for blocking human Nav1.7. The authors show that Thr1398, unique to human Nav1.7,
is essential for the activity of UVD. The authors perform further mutations to identify the binding site of UVD and demonstrate
that UVD is analgesic in a rodent model of neuropathic pain, without causing motor or cardiovascular adverse effects.
However, the manuscript has several major issues that require addressing: 
1) In Figure 3C, the authors show a sequence alignment of the DIII pore loop, demonstrating that human Nav1.7 has a
unique T1398/I1399 sequence motif that is not present in the other human Nav subtypes, which have an ‘MD’ motif instead.
It is this motif that the authors hypothesise is critical for the activity of UVD at human Nav1.7 (specifically the T1398). This
“TI” motif has already been shown to critical for the potency of the Nav1.7 selective inhibitors ST-2262 and ST-2560. In the
publication on ST-2560 (PMID: 38715413), the authors show a sequence alignment of Nav1.7 across different species
(Figure 1B). The TI motif is only present on human and primate Nav1.7, while the MD motif is present on dog, mouse, and rat
Nav1.7. This therefore raises the question as to whether UVD has the same efficacy at blocking rat Nav1.7 as human
Nav1.7. This is relevant, given analgesic activity was assessed in a rat model of pain. Therefore, the authors need to test the
potency and efficacy of UVD on rat Nav1.7. 
2) The authors consistently state that “Nav1.7 displays left-shifted voltage-dependent activation and inactivation curves
compared to other VGSC subtypes” – see for example Lines 127-130 and Lines 648-652. This infers that Nav1.7 has a more
hyperpolarised voltage-dependence of activation and inactivation compared to all the other Nav subtypes – which is not the
case. While reported values for V1/2 of activation and inactivation for NaV channels vary between studies, likely to due to
differences in cell background and presence/absence of auxiliary subunits, the V1/2 of activation and inactivation for
Nav1.1-1.7 are generally reported to be similar. Indeed, the references cited throughout the manuscript do not support the
statement that Nav1.7 shows “the left-shifted voltage-dependent activation and inactivation”. 
3) The authors conclude that “Thr1398 determines the more hyperpolarized voltage-dependent activation / inactivation of
Nav1.7 than VGSCs that contain Met at the equivalent position”. As stated above, many other Nav subtypes containing the
MD motif do have a similar voltage dependence of activation and inactivation as Nav1.7 in other studies (V1/2 of
activation/inactivation for the other Nav subtypes were not reported in this study). In this study, mutating Thr1398 to a Met in
Nav1.7 only led to a 9.4-mV shift in the V1/2 of activation and an 8.6-mV shift in the V1/2. This is not enough to support the
conclusion “By reducing the energy barrier for activation, Thr1398 serves as the main determinant for the low-voltage
dependence of Nav1.7, underscoring the molecular mechanism of Nav1.7 contribution to the “threshold currents”. As stated
above, not all species even have the “TI” motif in Nav1.7, which includes rodents. Inclusion of the V1/2 of
activation/inactivation for the other Nav subtypes (including rodent Nav1.7) and mutation of the ‘MD’ motif in other Nav
subtypes (in addition to Nav1.2) would be required to explore the contribution of the amino acids in the DIII pore loop to the
V1/2 of activation/inactivation. 
4) There is no mention in the methods of blinding for the behavioural tests. Assessment of mechanical thresholds in rodents
is highly subjective, and to avoid investigator bias, the experimenter must be blinded to the treatments each individual rodent
received. Individual values should also be plotted on 6E. 
5) The authors conclude that UVD is state dependent, with preferential binding for the ‘slow inactivated state’. However, the
potency of UVD does not differ greatly between protocols in the resting state (IC50 2 uM) and the slow inactivated state
(IC50 0.29 uM), showing only a 7-fold shift. Whereas other compounds that show state dependence, such as local
anaesthetics, are often reported to show a 50-fold shift. If it is hypothesised that UVD preferentially binds to the slow
inactivated state, then the potency and efficacy of UVD should be assessed at the other Nav subtypes using the slow
inactivated state protocol. If the conclusion is “In addition, the left-shifted voltage-dependent inactivation due to Thr1398
renders a more proportion of the Nav1.7 channel to be in the inactivated state under resting membrane potentials. UGD
binds to and stabilizes the slow inactivated state, causing a much greater maximal inhibition of Nav1.7 than other VGSC
subtypes” then why aren’t other state dependent inhibitors such as local anaesthetics, which also prefer the slow-inactivated
state, selective for Nav1.7? 
Minor: 
Lines 122-124 
“ST2262 exhibited over 10-fold selectivity at inhibiting Nav1.7 than Nav1.1-1.5 and Nav1.8 19, and it inhibited thermal pain
in non-human primates 19.” 
It is reported ST-2262 exhibited 200-fold selectivity over Nav1.1-1.6 and Nav1.8.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Nav1.7 is a voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC) subtype that plays a critical role in the physiology of sensory neurons. It
is predominantly expressed in these neurons, including dorsal root ganglion (DRG) and trigeminal ganglion neurons, which
are involved in transmitting sensory signals such as pain, temperature, and touch. Nav1.7 is essential for amplifying
threshold currents, meaning it facilitates the generation of action potentials (AP) in response to minimal depolarizing stimuli.
One of the distinguishing biophysical properties of Nav1.7 is its ability to activate and inactivate at more hyperpolarized
membrane potentials compared to other sodium channel subtypes. This characteristic makes it particularly well-suited for
fine-tuning neuronal excitability and enhancing the responsiveness of sensory neurons to weak inputs. Nav1.7's role in
amplifying subthreshold inputs is crucial for encoding pain signals and other sensory modalities, making it a key player in
nociception. It is now well established that dysfunction due to variants in the SCN9A gene, which encodes Nav1.7, has been
implicated in various pain syndromes and channelopathies. For example, gain-of-function mutations can lead to conditions
such as inherited erythromelalgia or paroxysmal extreme pain disorder, characterized by severe pain, while loss-of-function
variants result in congenital insensitivity to pain. This makes Nav1.7 a highly attractive target for the development of novel
analgesics designed to selectively modulate its activity, but so far with limited success! 
In this paper the authors clamed that Uvarigranol D (UGD) selectively inhibits human Nav1.7 (~100% inhibition) more
effectively than other VGSC subtypes (<50%), despite similar apparent affinities. The residue Thr1398 in hNav1.7 was found
to be critical for UGD's higher inhibitory efficacy. 
A 50% reduction in other VGSC subtypes, in my opinion, is quite significant and warrants careful consideration. For
example, a 50% reduction in Nav1.5 function is known to be associated with Brugada syndrome, or conduction disorders a
potentially life-threatening cardiac arrhythmia. This highlights the critical role of Nav1.5 in maintaining normal cardiac
electrophysiology, and any drug that inadvertently inhibits Nav1.5 to this extent could pose a serious arrhythmogenic risk in
humans. For this reason, numerous investigations in the field have deliberately avoided targeting the pore of Nav1.7
channels due to the sequence similarities shared with other VGSC, including Nav1.5. This cautious approach helps to
prevent off-target effects and ensures the specificity of potential therapeutic interventions. Therefore, while Uvarigranol D

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

(UGD) demonstrates promising selectivity for Nav1.7, its partial inhibition of other VGSC subtypes, particularly Nav1.5, could
raise safety concerns in humans that need to be thoroughly evaluated in the context of therapeutic development. The
authors could have employed electrical stimulations to investigate whether cardiac arrhythmias could be triggered both in
the presence and absence of the drug. This approach would provide valuable insights into the drug's potential effects on
cardiac function and its safety profile. 
The substitution of Thr1398 with methionine in hNav1.7 led to slower inactivation kinetics and depolarizing shifts in voltage-
dependent activation and inactivation. This finding suggests that Thr1398 likely exerts an allosteric influence on key
structural components involved in the gating mechanisms of Nav1.7. Specifically, Thr1398 may play a role in stabilizing the
conformations necessary for efficient channel opening and closing, thereby fine-tuning the channel's biophysical properties.
Therefore, it is crucial for the authors to provide direct evidence that UGD binds to and interacts specifically with Thr1398 in
hNav1.7. This can be achieved through structural studies, such as high-resolution cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) or X-
ray crystallography, to visualize the precise binding interactions between UGD and the channel. These techniques, which
have become more accessible and widely used in recent years, enable the detailed identification of specific contacts
between UGD and critical residues like Thr1398. Such studies would provide invaluable insights into the molecular basis of
UGD's selectivity and inhibitory mechanism, significantly advancing our understanding of its interaction with Nav1.7.
Thr1398, offering insights into how this residue contributes to the selectivity and inhibitory efficacy of UGD. This approach
could reveal more other sites that were not reported in the paper. Additionally, molecular docking simulations and site-
directed mutagenesis studies could complement the structural data by elucidating the binding dynamics and confirming the
functional importance of Thr1398 in mediating UGD's effects. For instance, introducing alternative mutations at Thr1398 and
assessing their impact on UGD's inhibitory potency would further validate the role of this residue. Such studies are essential
to substantiate the proposed mechanism of action and to strengthen the therapeutic potential of UGD as a selective Nav1.7
inhibitor. Without this evidence, the specificity and safety profile of UGD remain speculative, especially given its partial
effects on other VGSC subtypes. 
It is unclear why the authors did not investigate or report the effects of the drug on touch and thermal sensations, as these
are critical components of sensory processing mediated by Nav1.7. Given that Nav1.7 is predominantly expressed in
sensory neurons and plays a key role in amplifying subthreshold stimuli, it is reasonable to expect that any pharmacological
modulation of this channel could influence tactile and thermal perception. By omitting these assessments, the authors miss
an opportunity to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the drug's sensory impact, particularly its potential therapeutic or
adverse effects on mechanosensation and thermosensation. This gap is especially significant considering that altered touch
and thermal sensations are hallmark features of various neuropathic and pain syndromes targeted by Nav1.7 inhibitors.
Including these data would strengthen the study by demonstrating whether UGD selectively modulates pain-related
pathways or affects broader sensory modalities, which is essential for understanding its therapeutic profile and safety in
clinical applications. Future studies should address this limitation by systematically evaluating the drug's effects on a wider
range of sensory functions. 
Given these significant limitations, my overall enthusiasm for the paper is notably diminished. The lack of crucial data, such
as the structural confirmation of UGD's interaction with Thr1398 and its effects on touch and thermal sensations, raises
concerns about the study's comprehensiveness and rigor. These omissions leave critical questions unanswered about the
drug's specificity, mechanism of action, and broader physiological impact. 
Furthermore, the potential safety concerns related to UGD's partial inhibition of other VGSC subtypes, particularly Nav1.5,
which is vital for cardiac function, were not adequately addressed. This oversight further weakens the study's translational
relevance and raises doubts about the drug's viability as a selective Nav1.7 inhibitor. 
While the findings have some merit, particularly in identifying Thr1398 as a key residue, the lack of supporting data and the
limited scope of the study reduce its overall impact and potential contribution to the field. Addressing these issues in future
work would be essential to enhance the credibility and significance of the findings.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The concept that human Nav1.7 plays a crucial role in pain signaling and serves as a valuable target for the development of
selective inhibitors aimed at pain management is well established, therefore the study lacks novelty. Numerous studies have
demonstrated that mutations in SCN9A, the gene encoding Nav1.7, are linked to inherited pain disorders, including
congenital insensitivity to pain and inherited erythromelalgia. Consequently, the pursuit of Nav1.7-selective inhibitors with
strong efficacy has been an ongoing objective in the field of pain therapeutics. While significant progress has been made in
identifying and characterizing such inhibitors, challenges remain in achieving the desired selectivity and potency, properties
necessary for clinical success. 
My concern regarding the potential cardiac side effects of UGD was not adequately addressed. The preliminary data
provided by the authors, which involved measuring action potentials in mice, is not convincing, as mice are inherently
resistant to cardiac arrhythmias. Unlike in humans, where spontaneous arrhythmias can be indicative of underlying
electrophysiological disturbances, arrhythmias in mice typically need to be provoked under specific conditions. For instance,
while a heterozygous knockout of Nav1.5 (SCN5A) does not produce an overt phenotype, these mice exhibit an increased

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

susceptibility to arrhythmias when subjected to appropriate stressors compared to their wild-type littermates. This raises
concerns that the current experimental approach may not be sensitive enough to detect potential proarrhythmic effects of
UGD, necessitating more rigorous testing in models that better reflect human cardiac electrophysiology. Figure R1 is
problematic, as no assessment of the potential effect on the overshoot and Vmax were not quantified at concentrations lower
than 10microM. 
The author argues that since UGD is derived from the medicinal plant Uvaria grandiflora, which has been used for centuries
in traditional Chinese medicine, the likelihood of this compound causing cardiac arrhythmias is minimal. However, this
assumption lacks scientific validation, as no studies have specifically investigated the proarrhythmic potential of UGD. The
long history of traditional use does not inherently guarantee cardiac safety, particularly when considering that many natural
compounds can have significant electrophysiological effects, some of which may only become apparent under specific
conditions or at pharmacologically relevant doses. Without targeted investigations, including electrophysiological studies in
relevant models and thorough assessment of its effects on ion channels such as Nav1.5 and Kv channels, it is premature to
dismiss the possibility of cardiac side effects.
