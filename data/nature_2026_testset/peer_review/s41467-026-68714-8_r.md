Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Overall, this is a thoughtful, well presented, and comprehensive (very detailed!) in vivo investigation of the nature timing
white development in humans. 
This is a highly impressive manuscript, both in terms of scope and ambition. 
The work
contains a number of important methodological innovations which have produced novel and noteworthy findings in the field
of developmental neuroscience. 
The inclusion of multiple independent developmental datasets to demonstrate replicability
is now mandatory for a landmark developmental imaging study. 
In order to systematically examine the spatial maturation of
white matter tracts the authors developed and validated a technique for sampling microstructural measures ~100 nodes
along major tracts. 
Consequently they were able to capture regional variation at a “resolution” not previously reported. 
The successful completion of this study entailed the screening/QC, harmonisation, processing, and analysis of three very
large and complex data sets. 
The authors are to be commended for providing a detailed and informative Reproducibility
Guide with relevant code. 
The Discussion contains a sophisticated and comprehensive exploration of the findings. 
I note the findings relating to the
existence of a S-A axis are consistent with this group’s previous work. 
Minor Comments 
1. While the authors have credibly demonstrated the existence of two axes of white matter development in their cohorts it is
not clear if, and to what extent, these two developmental processes interact? 
Within the limitations of cross-sectional data is
it possible to provide a multidimensional estimate of microstructural maturation for the major association tracts of interest? 
2. There are a range of factors that contribute to neurodevelopment and it would be informative to know what role relative
brain age (the size of the cohorts would allow for normative modelling), pubertal stage and sex had on the relative timing of
regional changes in white matter microstructure. 
What drives variability in the timing of the white matter maturation? 
3. 
Potentially beyond the scope if this work but have the authors examined if the timing of the white matter developmental
changes have any functional significance? 
That is the “so what factor” 
- are they associated with cognitive performance or
behaviour? 
In summary, this manuscript represents a significant achievement. 
Congratulations.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a comprehensive analysis of white matter maturation across a wide developmental age range (5–
23 years), using data from three large-scale cross-sectional neuroimaging cohorts. The authors leverage diffusion-weighted
MRI (dMRI), focusing on mean diffusivity (MD) sampled along white matter tracts. The authors report that age-related effects
on MD are pronounced in the superficial white matter relative to deep white matter. In the superficial white matter, the age

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

effects correlate with the maturation hierarchy position of the cortical areas connected by these tracts. This is a novel and
well-conducted study that advances our understanding of white matter development. However, there are major limitations
that either need to be discussed or addressed: 
Major Comments: 
Comment 1: The authors infer that changes in MD and other diffusion metrics reflect underlying myelination. While this
interpretation is commonly made, it is important to acknowledge that diffusion MRI (particularly metrics like MD) is a non-
specific marker that reflects a broad range of microstructural properties beyond myelin (Piredda et al. 2021; van der Weijden
et al. 2023). Although prior studies have reported correlations between diffusion metrics and myelin content, such
associations are limited in scope, typically within pathological contexts (e.g., demyelination/dysmyelination) or specific
cortical maturation patterns. These findings cannot be extrapolated to normative development of white matter. This limitation
is particularly relevant given recent findings by Lynch et al. (2025), which demonstrate that developmental changes in
perivascular space volume can significantly alter diffusion characteristics. Of note this study was carried out in the HCP-D
dataset. These results underscore the need for caution in attributing changes in dMRI solely or primarily to myelination
processes. Overall, no single imaging modality provides a definitive measure of myelin. Therefore, adopting multimodal
approaches with orthogonal sensitivity is essential to mitigate modality-specific biases and enhance interpretability. 
Comment 2: Superficial white matter has a complex histology and neurodevelopment. In the late fetal and early postnatal
period, remnants of the subplate contribute to the microstructural makeup of the superficial white matter (Van Dyken et al.
2024; Kostović et al. 2019, 2020). Notably, the subplate and its remnants are characterized by a hydrophilic extracellular
matrix, which undergoes heterogeneous dissolution across the brain (Kostović et al 2014). This tissue composition is
associated with increased diffusivity on diffusion-weighted imaging (Nazeri et al 2022). The authors should discuss this
biological complexity and that the observed differences in diffusion metrics between deep and superficial white matter may
not necessarily reflect differences in the tracts themselves but could instead be driven by residual developmental features
such as subplate-derived extracellular matrix composition or perivascular space (discussed under comment #1). 
Comment 3: The authors report small age-related effects on MD within the midportion of the corpus callosum, with slightly
larger effects observed in the HCP-D cohort and when using the NODDI-derived FICVF metric. One possible explanation is
that this portion of the corpus callosum lies adjacent to CSF, making it susceptible to partial volume effects. The use of
higher-resolution imaging in the HCP-D cohort and the free water correction applied in NODDI analysis may have mitigated
this confounding factor, thereby enhancing the sensitivity to true microstructural changes. 
Comment 4: The authors demonstrate a correspondence between white matter maturation and cortical hierarchy as defined
by the sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis, using a data-driven atlas-based approach to examine group-level effects. While
these findings are highly suggestive, longitudinal analyses at the individual level are needed to confirm them. Given that
such data are now available in large cohorts such as ABCD, the authors should either acknowledge this as a limitation or
incorporate longitudinal analyses to support their findings.
