Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a modeling framework for analyzing luminescence decay dynamics in Eu²
⁺
-activated multi-site
phosphors. The authors argue that traditional multi-exponential fitting lacks physical interpretability and propose a more
rigorous approach based on nonlinear rate equations incorporating donor–acceptor energy transfer, defect-mediated
quenching, and intra-species migration. They solve these equations using two complementary strategies: (1) metaheuristic
optimization (GA and PSO) coupled with Runge-Kutta numerical integration, and (2) physics-informed neural networks
(PINNs). Both methods converge to consistent rate constants, and the authors claim this validates their model and offers a
generalizable alternative to empirical fitting. 
While the work is well conceived and there may be merits in using emerging methods for decay analysis, the current
manuscript does not provide convincing arguments for the superior performance of these methods in the context of the
presented experimental data. The modeling is sophisticated, but the lack of comparative validation, experimental breadth,
and clarity in the physical interpretation of extracted parameters limits the impact. This makes me question whether the
manuscript is best suited for a broad-scope journal like Nature Communications, or whether it would be more appropriate for
a specialized venue focused on luminescence modeling or computational photophysics. 
My Specific Concerns are as follow:

1. The manuscript introduces PINNs as a complementary solver to GA/PSO but does not demonstrate their added value.
There are no quantitative comparisons of accuracy, robustness, or computational efficiency. Without residual plots, overlays,
or generalization tests, the claim of complementarity remains unsubstantiated. 
2. The rate equations do not include spatial diffusion terms. While nonlinear population coupling mimics energy migration,
the absence of gradient-driven diffusion modeling limits the physical fidelity. This raises concerns about whether the
extracted parameters are truly more meaningful than phenomenological exponential fits. 
3. In nonlinear systems, excitation fluence should modulate decay dynamics. The manuscript does not explore fluence-
dependent measurements, which could provide a critical experimental handle to validate the model’s assumptions and
distinguish it from linear alternatives. 
4. Terms like “killer site” and “energy diffusion” are used without rigorous definition. A clearer connection to established
nomenclature (e.g., non-radiative traps, migration pathways) would improve accessibility and scientific precision. 
5. Figures showing decay curve fits and parameter trends (e.g., Fig. 5) are informative but do not include error metrics,
sensitivity analyses, or overlays that would allow the reader to assess model fidelity across methods. 
6. The authors suggest that their framework applies to other systems (e.g., perovskites, semiconductors), but no examples or
simulations are provided to support this claim.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors propose an approach to fit the experimental spectral features with a set of nonlinear rate
equations with several unknown parameters. To find the appropriate values of the parameters, they compared the
performances of two different classes of metaheuristic approaches, that is, GA/PSO and PINN. The proposed approach(es)
can properly incorporate the effect of two-body interactions between color centers, which is not the case in the conventional
multi-exponential fitting because it implicitly assumes that the kinetics is described by a set of separate first-order linear rate
equations. In addition, the authors claim that the PINN-based approach is in principle advantageous in scalability and
accuracy in higher-order derivatives, though no numerical evidence is presented on these points. 
This is an interesting and practically important work, which clearly highlights how this kind of highly complicated kinetic
model can be fitted to real experimental data and can provide fruitful physical insights. However, to correctly evaluate the
novelty and impact of the work, I would like to raise some questions. 
* Rate-equation-based fitting itself is not new; it has been an ordinally approach to find rate constants from experimental data
and done for decades. For example, in Nat. Commun. 16, 1367 (2025), the exciton dynamics was modelled by a set of rate
equations including several unknown rate constants, and the rate constants were determined in such a way that the
numerical solution of the rate equations reproduced the experimental TR-PL spectra. While that work is based on linear rate
equations, it looks obvious that essentially the same approach can be applied to nonlinear rate equations as long as they
are numerically solvable. What is essentially different from these previous works, and what is the unique difficulty in the
present case? 
* In the abstract and introduction, the authors claim that computational cost of solving ODEs has been the major obstacle.
However, no quantitative discussion is given on this point. How long does it take to solve the (nonlinear) ODEs (eq. 1)
numerically? Is it computationally infeasible to optimize the parameters (rate constants) in traditional strategies such as trust
region methods? 
* The use of PINNs for the estimation of rate constants is also not new. For example, in J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 16, 3562 (2025),
a PINN (called CRNN) is successfully applied to estimate rate constants from transient spectroscopy data. What is a key
difference that makes the present work particularly important? 
In my opinion, the manuscript is suitable for publication if the above questions are successfully addressed. Otherwise,
Nature Communications may not be an appropriate platform to publish this paper. In that case, journals more specialized in
physical chemistry or photochemistry, for instance, JPCL/JPCC, may be the best choice.

should justify why this approximation is valid for the La-Ca-Si-O-N host. 
 
Given that the 5d → 4f transition energy of Eu 2+ is approximately 18,800 cm ⁻¹, and the phonon 
energies in typical nitrides and oxynitrides (e.g., Si ₃N₄: 300–1200 cm⁻¹) are at least one order of 
magnitude smaller, more than 15–20 phonons would be required to bridge this energy gap. Because 
the multiphonon relaxation rate decreases exponentially with the number of phonons involved, such 
nonradiative processes are practically negligible. 
Therefore, it is not necessary to determine the exact phonon energies for La2.544Ca1.456Si12O4.456N16.544, 
as the multiphonon relaxation channel is inherently suppressed by the large energy gap. Unlike the 
organic molecular luminescence or intra 4f transitions for rare ear th ions, the 4f-5d transition for 
Eu2+ and Ce3+ is conventionally treated with no consideration of multiphonon relaxation, which is 
domain routine. We appreciate the reviewer ’s insightful comment that allowed us to clarify this 
point more explicitly.

authoritative literature in phosphor optical physics, we found that the intrinsic Eu2+ radiative decay

Poort, S. H. M., Meijerink, A. & Blasse, G. Lifetime measurements in Eu2+-doped host lattices. J. Phys. 
Chem. Solids 58, 1451–1456 (1997). 
 
We acknowledge that our original wording (“slightly higher than normal”) was based on less rigorous 
recent sources and therefore inaccurate. The intrinsic Eu² ⁺ radiative lifetime strongly depends on 
the host crystal -field strength and covalency. Consequently, our fitted value of k r ≈ 106 s⁻¹ lies 
well within the established intrinsic range for Eu2+ in inorganic hosts. We have revised the manuscript 
accordingly, replacing the phrase “slightly higher than normal” with “within the typical range 
reported for intrinsic Eu2+ radiative rates (0.56 –2.5 × 106 s⁻¹)” and have added the above 
authoritative references.

As you would also appreciate as an expert in this field, the radiative rate (k r) discussed here refers

4) The claim that PINNs do not require "large pre-labeled datasets" (at p.18 line 440) is misleading. 
Indeed, they still require experimental decay curves. 
 
We thank the reviewer for this valuable clarification. We fully agree that a certain amount of 
experimental data—specifically, the measured decay curves —is inevitably required for meaningful 
PINN training. 
 
Our intention was not to claim that PINNs can operate without any data, but rather to emphasize 
that their dependency on large, pre-labeled datasets is substantially reduced compared with purely 
data-driven methods such as GA/PSO–Runge–Kutta optimization. In PINNs, the total loss function 
comprises three components: physics loss, boundary/initial -condition loss, and data loss. Among 
these, the experimental data loss typically accounts for only about one-third of the total, meaning 
that the data primarily guide the overall shape of the decay curve rather than dominate the 
optimization process. 
 
To demonstrate this reduced data dependency, we performed an additional test in which the number 
of experimental data points was reduced to 25% of the original dataset (i.e., 75% of the data were 
removed). The PINN model still reproduced the decay curves almost identically, whereas the 
GA/PSO–Runge–Kutta process failed to converge under the same condition. The correspon ding 
results are now presented in the Supplementary Information.

5) The auxiliary loss maximizing initial -terminal population differences seems ad-hoc and requires 
better justification. 
 
We thank the reviewer for this insightful and technically precise comment. You are correct that the 
auxiliary loss term maximizing the difference between initial and terminal populations is not a strict 
physical requirement of the rate equations. It was introduced purely as a computational safeguard 
to prevent non-physical convergence during early training iterations. 
In some cases, when the initial weights of the neural network were poorly initialized, the early-stage 
decay curves generated by the PINN exhibited reversed or oscillatory shapes—for example, starting 
at a lower population and increasing over time, which i s physically impossible for luminescence 
decay. Such instability also led to unrealistic parameter values. Restarting the training or reinitializing 
the PINN process resolved the issue but at significant computational cost. 
To mitigate this problem efficiently, we added an auxiliary loss term that maximizes the difference 
between the initial and terminal population values, effectively guiding the training toward physically 
monotonic decay behavior  from the very beginning. Once the training stabilized, this term had 
negligible influence on the final results and did not affect the fitted rate constants. 
From an optimization standpoint, this auxiliary term acts as a heuristic constraint  to prevent 
convergence to non-physical local minima. As is generally accepted in heuristic and hybrid learning 
algorithms, introducing such weak priors or regularization terms to enforce physically consistent 
behavior is a common ad -hoc modification. In our case, it simply encodes the well -established 
physical expectation that all population levels should decrease monotonically over time during the 
decay process. 
We have revised the corresponding explanation in the manuscript to clarify this motivation and to 
explicitly state that this auxiliary loss does not alter the underlying physics of the rate equations but 
serves only to stabilize the optimization process.

We sincerely appreciate the reviewer’s sharp and technically insightful comment. It is uncommon 
nowadays to receive such an expert-level question, and we truly value the opportunity to elaborate 
on the theoretical distinction between metaheuristic algorithms and traditional trust-region methods 
such as the Levenberg–Marquardt approach. 
 
Trust-region and Levenberg –Marquardt methods are fundamentally gradient - or Hessian -based 
optimizers relying on first- and second-order derivatives. While they are efficient for smooth, convex

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 24 -->

21 / 23 
 
problems, they provide no guarantee of global convergence. In nonlinear optimization problems 
with multiple coupled rate constants and quadratic intera ction terms —as in our system —the 
objective function becomes highly multimodal. Under such conditions, local optimization methods 
easily get trapped in local minima and rarely escape, even when the trust-region radius is carefully 
tuned. Metaheuristic algorithms such as GA and PSO were originally developed to overcome this 
limitation by performing global stochastic searches across the parameter space, enabling robust 
convergence to near-global optima. 
 
A natural question then arises: why do we again employ stochastic gradient-based methods in the 
case of the PINN? 
The answer lies in the dimensionality of the optimization space. The optimization problem in our 
Runge–Kutta–based numerical analysis involves eight decision variables. In such a low-dimensional 
space, the objective landscape is highly multimodal with a dense distribution of local minima, 
making global optimization methods such as GA and PSO more suitable. 
 
In contrast, the PINN optimization problem contains roughly 9,000 parameters (8,968 network 
weights and biases plus 8 physical parameters). In such high-dimensional nonconvex landscapes, it 
is well established both theoretically and empirically that saddle points are far more prevalent 
than true local minima. Owing to this structural property, stochastic gradient–based optimization 
methods can effectively escape saddle regions via inherent gradient noise, leading to more stable 
convergence behavior. Stochastic gradient-based methods such as Adam are naturally effective in 
this regime because the ir inherent gradient noise facilitates the escape from saddle regions with 
negative curvature. Therefore, while GA/PSO is advantageous for low-dimensional global exploration, 
stochastic gradient methods become more efficient and stable in high-dimensional neural network 
training. 
 
An additional practical question concerns the crossover between the effectiveness of global search 
(metaheuristics) and local gradient –based methods. Although this boundary cannot be precisely 
defined, it is generally accepted within the optimization community that the transition occurs around 
a dimensionality of roughly 100 . That is, for problems with fewer than ~100 decision variables , 
metaheuristic algorithms such as GA or PSO often outperform gradient-based methods in achieving 
global convergence, whereas in much  higher dimensions, gradient -based optimization becomes 
more advantageous due to its scalability and efficiency.
