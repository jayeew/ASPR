Second, if 136Ce can be measured, researchers are already doing so. Isotope analysts understand that using 136Ce offers

for publication in this journal since no actual measurements were performed. 
Furthermore, although the relative mass difference in 136Ce/140Ce is twice that of 142Ce/140Ce, the fractionation predicted
by the authors is even greater than that. A more detailed explanation of this discrepancy is needed.

REVIEWERS' COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
In the example presented by the authors, Schnabel et al. measured 138Ce/136Ce using an amplifier with the same
resistance, whereas 140Ce was measured with a lower-resistance amplifier. When amplifier resistances differ, tau
correction becomes difficult and the measurement precision deteriorates. Indeed, 140Ce/136Ce is not reported in Schnabel
et al. 
Regarding the Xe correction, it does not concern Xe originally contained in the sample. As long as Ar plasma is used, trace
contamination by Xe is unavoidable. In particular, when the remaining amount of gas or liquid Ar becomes low, the Xe
content increases, and the Xe signal intensity can vary over the course of a measurement. For reference, Schnabel et al.
corrected 136Xe based on the signal intensity of 134Xe and the natural 136Xe/134Xe abundance ratio, but this requires that
the isotopic ratios of Xe (and Ba) do not vary during the measurement. As shown in their Fig. 5, Ba can remain in very small
amounts after column separation and may exhibit significant isotopic fractionation depending on its quantity.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

Therefore, multiple Xe isotopes need to be monitored in order to perform accurate corrections, but the number of collectors is
insufficient. The fact that something is “technically” measurable is not the same as being able to obtain precision adequate
for scientific interpretation.

I accept authors rebuttal to my comments. The revised ms. can now be published as is.

Reviewer 1  
This manuscript combines HERFD-XANES/EXAFS with DFT calculations to elucidate the adsorption 
structure of cerium in Fe-Mn oxides and proposes the use of +Ce/140Ce as a new paleoredox proxy. The 
measurement data are clear, and the manuscript is very well written. However, there are two major issues. 
First, although the adsorption structure of cerium is of strong interest to certain researchers, the subject 
matter is too specialized to appeal to the broad readership of this journal. I feel that it would be mo re 
suitable for a specialized journal such as GCA. 
Second, if 136Ce can be measured, researchers are already doing so. Isotope analysts understand that 
using 136Ce offers greater advantages because the relative mass difference is larger. However, in practice, 
+Ce has an abundance that is two orders of magnitude lower than that of 140Ce or 142Ce, and when 
measured by MC-ICP-MS, correction for 136Xe is required. As a result, measurement precision sufficient 
for use as a paleoredox proxy has not yet been achie ved. While I acknowledge the value of presenting 
theoretical fractionation factors for 136Ce/140Ce, I do not consider it impactful enough for publication in 
this journal since no actual measurements were performed. 
We agree that the low abundance of 136Ce (140Ce/136Ce ~475) makes it difficult to obtain stable 140Ce and 
136Ce signals simultaneously. However, 136Ce measurement is technically feasible using 1010-13Ω 
resistors in multicollector ICP-MS, as demonstrated by Schnabel et al. (J. Anal. At. Spectrom., 2017, 32, 
2360–2370). The authors also showed how to monitor the 136Xe, and other isobaric,  interferences. The 
Xe concentration in marine sediments is low (e.g. Table 1 in  doi: 10.1016/j.margeo.2013.07.010; Table 
5.1 in doi: 10.1017/CBO9780511545986), and most likely much lower than that of 136Ce in FeMn crusts. 
There is an opportunity today to advance understanding of Ce cycling in natural systems by combining 
HERFD-XANES/EXAFS, 136Ce/140Ce measurements, and ab initio calculations on spectroscopy-derived 
atomic structures. We think our approach provides a coherent, mechanistically based foundation for the 
study of Ce across a wide range of terrestrial and marine systems.  
The conclusion has been edited to comply with the helpful reviewer’s comments. 
 
Furthermore, although the relative mass difference in 136Ce/140Ce is twice that of 142Ce/140Ce, the 
fractionation predicted by the authors is even greater than that. A more det ailed explanation of this 
discrepancy is needed. 
Calculations had been performed in the  gas phase. Accurate vibrational frequencies are crucial for 
estimating fractionation factors with precision . Nakada et al. (2017) showed that better agreement 
between calculated and experimental vibrational frequencies is achieved when using an implicit solvation 
model (Table EA1 in Nakada’s article). All calculations have been redone using the continuum solvent 
model (SMD) to simulate the water solvent. The ratios of the 136Ce/140Ce and 142Ce/140Ce fractionations 
are now close to 2. The DES and INC models have +300 vibrational frequencies, and Ce is bonded to 
different ligands (O, OH, H 2O), which stretches the error bars in the calculations. The ratios vary, to 
some extent, with the parametrization of the calculations. In the revision, the calculations have been

Reviewer 2 
This study provides a detailed analysis of the adsorption and oxidation mechanisms of Ce on Mn oxide 
surfaces, which are crucial for understanding the stable isotope fractionation of Ce —a to pic whose 
importance has been increasingly recognized in recent years. Using HERFD -XAFS and quantum 
chemical calculations, it clarifies the adsorbed species on Mn oxide surfaces, which had been problematic 
in previous studies. Furthermore, based on this, t heoretical calculations were performed to attempt a 
quantitative estimation of the isotope fractionation. This study adequately addressed the problems in 
previous studies concerning Ce adsorption and provides a very robust physicochemical investigation. It  
represents highly significant work in physicochemical standpoint on surface reactions of minerals. 
However, at the same time, considering the potential significance of Ce stable isotopes and the paper's 
purpose based on the introduction, the description and discussions are clearly insufficient in the context 
of Earth Science. In this regard, the manuscript ultimately fails to clarify what it aims to conduct and 
which points are crucial. Whether this paper should be published in this journal or other geosci ence 
journals is ultimately the Editor's decision, but in its current version, I think it is inadequate. Several 
issues are listed below, and substantial revisions will likely be necessary. 
(i) The title states “Global Ocean,” but this is clearly an exaggerati on. Furthermore, there is no 
consideration or description of global behavior or Ce in this regard. There is no evidence for claiming 
that the samples used in this study represent the global ocean or individual oceans. Additionally, in L.123, 
it is stated t hat the EXAFS spectrum for D20 -1B differs from the others —what does this imply when 
considering the global context? 
The expression “global ocean” in titles is customarily used when data from around the world’s oceans 
were collected. A search in the Web-of-Science of the expression “global ocean” in the titles of articles 
reported +1,600 occurrences. Our three samples come from the three largest oceans: the Pacific, Indian, 
and Atlantic Oceans. There is a vast literature on the geochemistry of Ce in marine f erromanganese 
(FeMn) crusts, and all studies show  that Ce is uniformly 4+ and associated with vernadite ( δ-MnO2). 
Furthermore, its isotope fractionation is remarkably constant (e.g., Takahashi et al., 2007), implying that 
its chemical and structural forms are uniform throughout the oceans, as our study confirms. A map with 
the location of the FeMn crusts has been added to the revision (Figure 1), to show the wide distribution 
of the sampling. 
Here is what we wrote: “ The three spectra are similar, except at the second oscillation at k = 4.7-6.0 
Å-1, where D20-1B shows a more symmetrical pattern.”. The difference is clearly minor and can lead to 
misleading conclusion. Data analysis (Table S2) shows that Ce has the same bonding environment in the 
three samples. What differs slightly is the proportion of Ce on the edge and vacancy sites. This variation 
among natural samples is expected from one sampling site to another, and even along the same FeMn 
crust. The text has been edited in the revision to avoid confusing the reader.

adsorbed species in previous studies, adequately pointed out in the introduction, is important when 
considering the potential use of Ce stable isotopes. However, there is no description of how the

understanding of the adsorbed species clarified in this study ultimately contributes to existing knowledge

In a masterful series of papers published in 2013 -2017, Nakada, Takahashi, and Tanimizu showed 
experimentally that FeOOH, Ce(OH)4, and δ-MnO2, the three main solid phases assumed to control the 
geochemistry of Ce in terrestrial and marine environments , had distinct δ 142Ce isotopic signature s at

the environment becomes more oxic, it was inferred that current and past redox conditions of natural 
systems could be inferred from Ce isotopic data.  
As pioneering as these studies were at the time, measurements were performed on model solid phases, 
which raised questions on their represen tativity to real-world systems. Below are two quotes from 
Nakada et al. (2013), which illustrate the limitation of the previous studies. 
“The notable result obtained from the EXAFS spectra signatures was that the spectrum of the Ce adsorbed 
on δ-MnO2 was similar to that of spontaneous precipitation of Ce (Fig. 6A) [i.e., Ce(OH) 4]. A study of

(Takahashi et al., 2007), which was also observed in our XANES spectra. However, the EXAFS spectrum

concentration of Ce in our experiment compared with that of the real deep ocean, where the Ce 
concentration is several pmol/kg”.  
Indeed, the EXAFS data from Nakada et al. (2013) were obtained on Ce-sorbed δ-MnO2 at a molar 
ratio of 0.0199, corresponding to a Ce concentration of 3.2 wt% , which is about 20 times the typical 
concentration of Ce in hydrogenetic FeMn deposits a nd about 50 -100 times that in diagenetic FeMn 
deposits. We showed that Ce is not precipitated in hydrogenetic FeMn crusts, as previously considered, 
but complexed to two surface sites on δ-MnO2, and we calculated the isotope fractionation factor of the 
two surface complexes.  
The Nakada et al.’s (20 13) study was  also inconclusive on the oxidation mechanism of Ce(III) to 
Ce(IV): 
“Two formation processes for the oxidation of Ce(IV) are possible. The first is oxidation after adsorption

McLennan, 1988; Takahashi et al., 2000, 2007). The other process is oxidation in the liquid phase followed 
by precipitation as Ce(OH) 4 or CeO2 (De Baar et al., 1988; German and Elderfield, 1989; Braun et al., 
1990).” 
This question is answered in our study for the DES complex. 
The reviewer asks that we “clarify which points are correct and which points should be modified [in

Nakada is cited 8 times in the manuscript . However, we cannot be more precise and “clarify which

In the example presented by the authors, Schnabel et al. measured 138Ce/136Ce using an amplifier with 
the same resistance, whereas 140Ce was measured with a lower-resistance amplifier. When amplifier 
resistances differ, tau correction becomes difficult and the measurement precision deteriorates. Indeed, 
140Ce/136Ce is not reported in Schnabel et al. 
 
Tau correction is applied when using 1013 Ω resistance amplifiers: 
https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/ja/d4ja00066h 
https://wims.igc.usp.br/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Ant%C3%B4nio-C.-Jardim.pdf 
Schnabel et al. (2017) used 1011 and 1010 Ω resistors, and therefore, no tau correction was applied. 
 
We now cite in the revision Szakas et al. (2025), which measured the 136Ce/140Ce ratio with 1011 Ω 
resistors. 
 
Schnabel et al. (2017) do not deal with stable isotope fractionation. The authors used the 136Ce/140Ce 
ratio for mass bias correction, which is why it is not reported. 
 
Regarding the Xe correction, it does not concern Xe originally contained in the sample. As long as Ar 
plasma is used, trace contamination by Xe is unavoidable. In particular, when the remaining amount of 
gas or liquid Ar becomes low, the Xe content increases, and the Xe signal intensity can vary over the 
course of a measurement. For reference, Schnabel et al. corrected 136Xe based on the signal intensity 
of 134Xe and the natural 136Xe/134Xe abundance ratio, but this requires that the isotopic ratios of Xe 
(and Ba) do not vary during the measurement. As shown in their Fig. 5, Ba can remain in very small 
amounts after column separation and may exhibit significant isotopic fractionation depending on its 
quantity. 
 
Therefore, multiple Xe isotopes need to be monitored in order to perform accurate corrections, but the 
number of collectors is insufficient. The fact that something is “technically” measurable is not the same 
as being able to obtain precision adequate for scientific interpretation. 
 
The statement about multiple Xe isotopes is incorrect, as the monitored Xe isotope is inteference-free 
and the known Xe ratio was used for interference correction in Schnabel et al. (2017).

performed to attempt a quantitative estimation of the isotope f ractionation. This study adequately 
addressed the problems in previous studies concerning Ce adsorp tion and provides a very robust 
physicochemical investigation. It represents highly significant  work in physicochemical standpoint
