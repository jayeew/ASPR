Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this preprint the authors introduce a numerical model for adsorption in soft crystals. The model incorporates lattice
deformation at adsorbed sites and hardening. 
In discrete, particle deposition, models of surface growth (like this one) where the bulk is not compact and voids appear it is
always difficult to define the "interface". As one can see in the pictures, there is no such an object as an interface, it is more
like a diffuse front above some denser bulk of adsorbed sites. The authors have opted for defining the "interface" n(x,t) as the
fraction of y sites adsorbed at column x at time t. So one is not describing the "interface" between adsorbed and empty
phases, but the adsorption fraction at in column x. 
Numerical simulations show that the model exhibits several dynamical regimes and a phase diagram is presented (Fig. 1).
For large enough chemical potential and low temperatures there is a spinodal regime where the adsorbed and empty
phases are separated by a diffuse phase boundary. One of the main results of the paper is the finding that the adsorbed
fraction field seems to be rough and obeys dynamic scaling properties. Local height-height correlations as well as power
spectral density seem to follow scaling laws, akin to standard kinetic surface roughening. The scaling properties are,
however, unusual and the authors claim that the scaling functions differ from those found in the standard theory of kinetic
roughening. 
The paper deals with an interesting problem with implications in chemical physics, statistical mechanics, materials science,
and more. The paper is well written and results are interesting so the draft is potentially publishable. I have, however, some
questions that need to be clarified before making a decision. 
My main concerns are related with the interface scaling and anomalous exponents. 
1) The main problem I see is the substrate length l_surf(t) that plays the role of the fixed system size Lx in standard kinetic
roughening. This region, where the correlation functions and the interface can be defined (far from the corners) is shrinking
with time. I suspect this is the reason why usual generic dynamic scaling (Ramasco et. al. in Ref[48]) does not apply fully in
this case. This must be the origin of the troubles to comply with standard theory, which was conceived for fixed system size
Lx. Note that in kinetic roughening system size Lx determines when correlations saturate (become time independent) as
fluctuations growth stops when the correlation length covers the total length of the system \xi(t) \sim Lx. Here Lx is replaced
by l_surf(t) as the relevant scale, with the particularity that l_surf is decreasing with time. Therefore, I suspect, unusual things
will happen when \xi(t) \sim l_surf(t), and being all time dependent this will probably change the scaling functions of the
standard theory. I did not worked out the details but I am moderately sure that this is the case. If the authors agree with this
interpretation this should be discussed in the paper and some parts of the paper should be adapted accordingly. It is not that
the generic kinetic roughening theory fails but rather this problem with varying system size is out of the limits of the theory. 
2) In the same line of thought I would like to see in the review response a plot of n(x,t) profiles, like those in SM Fig. 4, but
restricted to the region that it is used to obtain the interface scaling, without the corners. I just want to understand what the
interface looks like. 
3) I would also like to see S(k,t) at different times in a log-log plot, also restricted to the "interface" region in the same

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript, "Elastic heterogeneity governs anomalous dynamic scaling in a soft porous crystal", K. Mitsumoto and K.
Takae present a study of the effects of heterogeneous elastic deformation on the adsorption kinetics by means of Monte
Carlo simulations and theoretical analysis. The authors demonstrated different adsorption kinetic mechanisms depending on
the position on the phase diagram. They highlighted the importance of the corners in the sorption process into the soft host
matrix. The distribution of adsorbate molecules during adsorption and desorption steps was investigated, as well as the
behavior of different scaling coefficients. The authors argue that they observed anomalous scaling governed by adsorption-
induced elasticity. The paper is well-written; however, in my opinion, some issues should be addressed before the
manuscript can be recommended for publication. 
1. I am confused about the role of host-adsorbate interactions in the process. As far as I understand, in the absence of strong
deformation, the adsorbate molecules would not feel each other or the host nodes. However, the external potential should
exist to shift the chemical potential and create a driving force for adsorption. Also, if it is strong enough, it may govern the
kinetic process, and its effect can be stronger than the elastic effect. Please, add the explicit explanation of how your
Hamiltonian accounts for such effects or discuss the assumptions/limitations of your model. 
2. I think the author might find these publications interesting (Guyer R. A. and Kim H. A., 2015, Phys. Rev. E 91 042406;
Bakhshian S. et al, 2018, Sci. Rep. 8 8249; Kolesnikov A.L. et al, 2021, J. Phys.: Condens. Matter 34; Kolesnikov A.L. et al,
2024, Langmuir, 40, 23806). 
3. Please add the time steps in Fig. 2 for a better comparison between different cases. 
4. Some notations are reused in the text, which complicates the reading. For example, \alpha is used as a roughness
exponent and as a coupling constant in the deformation. Also, \mu_{ads} is introduced instead of \mu, …

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is my second report on this manuscript. I find the responses convincing and the new figures included in the
supplementary material adequate and informative. Even I do not fully understand why the generic scaling theory of Ramasco
et al fails to describe this system my guess at present is that some of the requirements (implicit or explicit) for that theory do
not apply here. The new fig 7 (SM) clearly shows that the surface develops a spatial mode, which gives the peak in the
S(k,t), and the surface below that characteristic wavelength becomes rough. However, the surface is NOT rough above the
oscillation pattern wavelength. Ramasco theory, in principle, applies to scale invariant surfaces, and this system is NOT
scale invariant in strict sens because scaling stops above the wavelength. This means the correlation length grows up to the
wavelength scale and seems to saturate just there, not at the system size. 
Simulations are convincing and clean so this work seems to imply that Ramasco et al theory should be modified in case one
wants to include the possibility of a large scale pattern. 
In any case, the scaling functions and exponents seem to describe the surface statistics and the numerical simulations fit the
picture, even if some of the implications of Ramasco et al. (like super-roughening implies alpha_loc = 1) need to be relaxed
in this case. 
In my opinion the paper can be published in its present form. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I have no further comments and recommend the manuscript for publication.

We appreciate the second reviewer’s constructive comment. In our model, the host-adsorbate interaction is in-
corporated via both the deformation of the host framework and the chemical potential µ. A high affinity between
the host and the adsorbate leads to a larger chemical potential. As noted in the reviewer’s comment, the chemical
potential also depends on the external gas pressure of the adsorbate. Thus, in our model, both the host-adsorbate
affinity and the gas pressure act as driving forces for adsorption. In the revised manuscript, we have added an explicit
explanation of the chemical potential just below Eq. (6), “ µ depends on the guest-host interaction energy and the
gas pressure of guest particles. In addition, we assume that the chemical potential is uniform inside the host. This
means that the migration of adsorbate particles inside the host is governed by elastic interactions arising from the
host deformation and exclusion between guest particles, while the effect of the chemical potential acts only at the
entrance/exit boundary of the host. We have added a paragraph in the right column of p.8, “In our model, the
chemical potential ...”. Furthermore, the statement “the chemical potential depends on the gas pressure” may lead
to a misunderstanding about the independence of chemical potential µ and pressure P in the osmotic ensemble. To
avoid the confusion, we have added the term mechanical before P , and a sentence in the right column of p.8, “Gas
pressure of guest particles is related ...”.
