Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors explore an important area in protein translation and folding: how do protein complexes
assemble? 
There is a lot of conjecture within the field regarding whether such assembly can occur during translation, and if
so, by what mechanism. In this work, the authors have studied R2TP, a HSP90 co-chaperone using a range of cell-based
assays (including proteomics, smFISH, and single-molecule imaging). 
Through these carefully designed and executed experiments, the authors demonstrate a number of key findings. These
include a rather complex mechanism for co-translational assembly formation for this ATP-dependent chaperone system,
including identifying mRNA clients involved in co- and post-translational interactions, and the formation of “translation
factories”. 
It is on this latter point which is what is remarkable about this study: the authors present 
a rather unexpected
mechanism of “co-chaperone” channelling, which brings translating mRNA into close proximity into biomolecular
condensates, and thus offers a very plausible alternative mechanistic understanding for how protein complexes can be
formed. 
This is significant because the current hypothesis is that complexes form because translating polypeptides are
brought into close proximity via polysomes. 
The specifics of this mechanism are (in my opinion) a little more tenuous and
could benefit from more clarification (see below), however overall, there is a very plausible (and exciting) hypothesis that is
being put forward. 
In summary, this manuscript offers a set of important findings that will attract broad interest across several fields (including
protein translation, chaperone interactions, protein folding, and biomolecular condensate formation), and will no doubt
inspire related works. 
A few considerations that may help to improve the clarity of the manuscript for a broader audience: 
1. Puromycin treatment offers a reliable means to detect active translation, however, do the authors detect and ribosomal
proteins or rRNA within their proteomic analysis? 
2. On a related point, do the authors have evidence for polysome formation or of ribosomal complexes? Polysome formation
is of course the default mode by which translation would occur within cells, however some biochemical evidence may
provide some unequivocal proof. 
3. Within the “translational factories” (cha-cha) mechanism it was not clear to me whether the biomolecular condensates (P-
bodies) contained actively translating ribosomes or whether they were simply sequestering mRNA? Often P-bodies are
associated with compromises within proteostasis (e.g. regulation), however here the authors suggest a more functional role
for localised translation. The authors show that condensates can form from mRNA and local translation is likely to occur.
What is missing (or at least not clear to me) is whether there is protein-based evidence that the condensate formation results
in assembly formation. 
This may need to clarify the biomolecular condensate description further to give unequivocal
credence to the cha-cha and “translational factories” concepts. I anticipate that the authors are correct, but viewing the
mechanism from the lens of mRNA localisation alone might considered limiting. 
4. For the non-specialist reader, a description of the significance of the recruitment of POLR2A and PRPF8 mRNAs would
be helpful.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript “Co-translational determination of quaternary structures in chaperone factories” by Manon Philippe#, Soha
Salloum# et al., investigates the function of HSP90/R2TP chaperones in co-translational protein complex assembly. Manon
Philippe#, Soha Salloum# et al. find evidence for HSP90/R2TP co-translational interactions with many canonical as well as
new partners. Furthermore, they find that the chaperone induces the formation of dedicated translation factories,
accumulating mRNAs encoding for complex partner subunits and thus favoring chaperone-client co-translational
interactions. 
This manuscript offers a significant contribution to the field of protein synthesis and maturation pathways by proposing a
potential new mechanism, termed cha-cha (co-translational chaperone channeling), for macromolecular complex assembly.
However, additional validation is necessary to fully support its central claims. 
First, the RIP-seq experiments were not performed in the presence of Cycloheximide treatment to prevent ribosomal runoff.
Cycloheximide, a small molecule, inhibiting translation elongation by ribosomal E-site binding ("Structural basis for the
inhibition of the eukaryotic ribosome" de Loubresse,et al., Nature, 2014) is commonly used during selective ribosome
profiling experiments capturing co-translational interactions, in vivo (For example: Ingolia N.T.et al.; Genome-wide analysis
in vivo of translation with nucleotide resolution using ribosome profiling. Science. 2009; Selective ribosome profiling to study
interactions of translating ribosomes in yeast, Galmozzi C.G at al., 2019; A rapid protocol for ribosome profiling of low input
samples, 2023, Meindl Aet al., NAR). As ribosomes dissociates highly rapidly in response to various stresses and nutrients`

stress response which may indirectly result in decreased chaperone binding. For example, stress granule formation, limiting
mRNA accessibility. These results can suggest, that the RIP-Seq results capture an ensemble of chaperone-mRNA
interactions, both translation-dependent and independent ones, under conditions where there is no translation stress, and
mRNAs are not sequestered. 
To further support this possibility, Puromycin treatments seem to have a low impact also in the PARP-3-SUN-TAG
experiments (Figure 5), again suggesting translation independent functions. This point needs to be discussed and
addressed. 
*In the following figures, an enlargement of the foci images is required (as presented in figure 4A for example): 
Figure 5A, 6B, all of figure 7, figure S4F, figure S5A. These enlargements are important to evaluate co-localization analysis. 
*It is not clear from the text why RPAP3 needs to be in its nascent form to promote condensates of the U5 snRNP component
PRPF8 (figure 5). Is RPAP3 co-translationally folding, assembling and functional? or does an exposure of RNA binding
domain sufficient? 
*There is a mistake in references numbering, from reference 45 on (Pal, M. et al. Structural Basis for Phosphorylation-

46 should be 47 and so on.

This study provides evidence for the co-translational role of the HSP90/R2TP chaperone complex in assembling multi-
subunit macromolecular complexes in eukaryotic cells. It proposes an interesting mechanism termed “cha-cha” (co-
translational chaperone channeling), whereby R2TP facilitates protein complex assembly not via mRNA co-localization, but
through localized condensates — termed R2TP factories — that act as hubs for translation and assembly. These factories
accumulate client mRNAs and facilitate efficient subunit interaction via chaperone mediation.

are not due to general ribosome collapse.

Puromycin treatment offers a reliable means to detect active translation, however, do the authors detect any ribosomal proteins or rRNA within their proteomic analysis?  The Reviewer raises an interesting point.  We always detect ribosomal proteins in our R2TP AP-MS experiments. However, they are never above the background noise and therefore can not be considered as specific interactants. Our hypothesis is that there are too few ribosomes bound to R2TP in an ocean of cellular ribosomes, and therefore the R2TP-bound ribosomes do not emerge from the background. We tried to estimate this effect more quantitatively. The estimated number of ribosomes is around 3 000 000 per cell (https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?s=n&v=1&id=107552), while R2TP is moderately expressed. Assuming that the abundance of R2TP is reflected by its mRNA levels, this yields 30 000 copies per cell as the mRNA ratio of RPAP3 vs ribosomal proteins is 0.01. Assuming that 1% of R2TP is bound co-translationally to polysomes, themselves having on average ~10 ribosomes, this implies that only 0.1% of ribosomes would interact with R2TP. Even if 10% of R2TP is bound co-translationally to polysomes, it still means that only 1% of ribosomes would interact with R2TP. These numbers are likely beyond the detection threshold. Of note, the search for rRNA in our RUVBL1-GFP and RPAP3-GFP RIP-seq experiments was not possible as rRNAs are systematically depleted during library preparation.   We therefore did two new experiments to detect the interaction of R2TP with ribosomes. First, we generated a RPS6-GFP knock-in cell line and performed smFISH to test whether ribosomes are present in the POLR2A mRNA foci where R2TP also accumulates (Fig. R1 below). 4. For the non -specialist reader, a description of the significance of the recruitment of POL R2A and

We thank the Reviewer for this suggestion. We have added a small paragraph in the discussion of the

‘POLR2A and PRPF8 are both large proteins (>210 kDa) that must be assembled into large 
macromolecular complexes, RNA polymerase II and U5 snRNP, respectively. Even though these two 
proteins are not part of the same complex, they need to be taken in charge and stabilized by the same 
chaperone, HSP90/R2TP, as soon as they come out of the ribosome. Indeed, we have previously shown 
that the cytoplasmic unassembled POLR2A becomes unstable upon HSP90 or R2TP inhibition (Boulon 
et al, 2010). Likewise, PRPF8 is rapidly degraded in absence of its co-chaperones (Erkelenz et al, 2021). 
The risk for these large proteins, if they are not properly taken in charge  by their chaperones, is that 
they get misfolded, interact with the wrong partners, aggregate or become unstable. Chaperone 
factories have a high local concentration of R2TP and may thus favor their co-translational interaction 
with the chaperone, likely p reventing them from these unwanted events. It is unclear why the other 
mRNAs that also associate with R2TP are absent from the R2TP factories. It may be that POLR2A and 
PRPF8 have particularities favoring polysome condensation, such as a stronger or higher  valency of 
binding to HSP90 and R2TP, or a controlled ribosomal pausing providing more time for binding the 
chaperones and condensing the ribosomes.’
