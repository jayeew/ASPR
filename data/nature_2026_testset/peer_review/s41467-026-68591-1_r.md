Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, Liu et al. present an approach to the design of network-forming glasses by introducing a versatile node-linker-
modulator network, showcasing examples involving titanium, zirconium, and boron-based systems. While the authors
present a diverse array of node-linker-modulator combinations, the structural frameworks proposed are largely conceptual.
The paper is quite superficial and does not provide an in-depth scientific discussion of the experimental observations.
Without the support of NMR and elemental analysis, the structural claims remain highly speculative, raising concerns
regarding the actual compositions and identities of these materials. Furthermore, the thermal analysis, primarily conducted
via DSC, requires a more thorough and nuanced interpretation to provide clarity on the role of the modulators in influencing
glass transition behaviours. Similarly, the X-ray total scattering data analysis does not offer sufficient insight into the network
connectivity or long-range structural order, which strongly limits the reliability of the presented conclusions. In this regard, we
do not recommend this manuscript for publication. Below are the specific comments to address the primary issues: 
1. Scheme 1 is overstating the science reporting in the paper. The matrix view suggests that most of the possible
combinations of nodes, linkers and modulators have been made experimentally. However, only some of those are included
in the paper. The authors should clearly visualize which combinations have been done. 
2. We note that approximately 3 mL of ethanol was used in each synthesis, and although the samples were placed in a petri
dish at 80 °C for an hour, it remains uncertain whether the ethanol, if acting as a modulator, was fully removed. Without NMR
analysis, this is difficult to confirm. Even though TGA for the three networks without additional modulators shows stability up
to 150 °C, this does not conclusively verify ethanol removal. It is further requested that TGA is conducted on all samples to
provide comprehensive and sufficient evidence supporting sample stability. 1H NMR spectroscopy and elemental analysis
of digested samples should also be performed in order to provide important insights into the chemical identity and
composition of the materials. TGA under an oxygen atmosphere (burning the materials to TiO2, ZrO2 or B2O3 to get
information on the metal/boron content) is also important to get the full composition of the materials. The trends discussed in
the manuscript can only be understood and rationalised if the exact chemical compositions of the materials are known. 
3. The definition of the molar ratio used in the manuscript is very confusing. It took us a long time to figure out that a low
molar ratio correlates with a high modifier content. Since all the data in the text are discussed in terms of modulator
concentration, it would be more convenient if the data were plotted as a function of modulator content so that the degree of
network connectivity decreases from left to right. 
4. All DSC traces lack clear indicators of endothermic or exothermic signal direction, which must be added for clarity. The
origin of the signals assigned to a low-temperature glass transition is unclear. Given the low temperature of the glass
transition, it is unreasonable to assume that the Tg is associated with coordination dynamics involving modulator-linker
exchange at the metal nodes. The metal-oxygen and boron-oxygen bonds are much too strong to be broken at temperatures
below or around room temperature. Thus, the Tg signal must be assigned to the configuration dynamics of the linker and
modulator molecules. The current experimental data does not prove whether these molecules are part of a coordination
network. Further, it is important to compare the DSC data with reference measurements on the pure modulators and the
amorphous materials without any modulator. If the modulator-free materials do not exhibit a glass transition, the chemical
origin of the glass transition in these materials is very different to that of typical melt-quenched glasses, such as ZIF glasses.
These differences should be clarified and discussed in detail. In addition, the authors claim that more than 50% of the
samples have only a weak Tg, so it is mandatory to report DSC data of the modulator-free networks as well. Also, the
authors, citing the elimination of thermal history, only show DSC data from the second heating, and what happened in the
first heating is also a matter of considerable interest. There are also tests where the DSCs were all performed below 0 °C,
and we think it is necessary to provide DSC data up to pre-decomposition temperatures.

5. Panel a and b in Fig. 3 appear to be labelled the other way around in the figure caption. Moreover, the DSC data shown in
the supplementary information is inconsistent with the data shown in Figure 3b. This points towards some problems with
data evaluation and representation in the manuscript. The opposite trend in the change in Tg of the Ti-PEG5-TMBA-x%
versus the rest of the samples is of interest, but only the use of PEG5-TMBA alone to illustrate this does not seem to be
sufficient, and the fact that more modifiers have higher Tg values requires explanation. This hints towards a wrong
interpretation of the glass network structure and the dynamics of the framework at the glass transition. Furthermore, the Ti-
PEG5-EtOH sample cannot be found in Fig. 3c as described. The readers must have access to these details, such as the
thermal analysis data of this mixed-metal sample is missing. 
6. Page 4: “The trend in heat capacity jump is an indication that the configurational degree-of-freedom of the corresponding
super-cooled liquid becomes larger when the amount of TMBA is increased“. It is highly likely that the thermal events
observed in the DSC are not properties of a hybrid network but properties of individual molecules present in the amorphous
materials. 
7. One of the most substantial issues with this paper is the lack of structural models of the inorganic building units. The
authors show structural models of the Ti- and Zr-oxo clusters in Scheme 1. However, these models are not used to assign
peaks in the PDF analysis. Are the chemical structures shown in Scheme 1 and Fig. 1a just cartoons? The lack of structural
models for the inorganic units means that the results of this work are difficult to transfer to other systems. PDFs are
inadequately assigned and analysed. Supplementary Fig. 3 only assigns peaks below 3.3 Å. We can clearly find a Ti-O-Ti
distance of about 3.8 Å in the provided reference cluster structure. A systematic simulation of the PDF for each atom-atom
distance and overall PDFs is necessary. Long-range PDF analysis (beyond 30 Å) and differential PDF with various
modulators are also recommended to provide additional structural information. It must be mentioned that both XRD and X-
ray total scattering seem to be tested at room temperature, which characterises what should be a supercooled liquid or liquid
state, NOT a characterisation of the vitrified glass. If the glass is to be elaborated on, scattering data should be recorded
below Tg. Finally, the corresponding scattering factor S(Q) data must be provided, as they contain valuable reciprocal space
information of the materials. 
8. In addition, attempts were made to find some structural correlation evidence in the FTIR, but the FTIR spectra also only
investigated the Ti-TB network. Individual monomers, as well as the rest of the network, were not investigated, and no
inferences could be made. A detailed FTIR analysis, including spectra of the pure modifier, linker and modifier-free
amorphous network, would shine light on the chemical structures of these amorphous materials. 
9. PXRD data must be reported for all compounds under study to clearly demonstrate their amorphous nature. 
10. For the XRD data of B-GDPA-TMBA-x% in Supplementary Fig.15. There seems to be a certain amount of crystallinity. If
these tests were all at room temperature (i.e. (supercooled) liquid phase), it is reasonable to suspect that the so-called
“glassy” phase at lower temperatures may be even more crystalline. 
11. Given their alkoxide-based chemistry, it is to be expected that all of these amorphous materials are sensitive to water
and hydration. It is known that titanium and zirconium alkoxides hydrolyse under a humid atmosphere and eventually form
crystalline or amorphous zirconium and titanium oxides. Similarly, boron alkoxides hydrolyse to borates. The authors should
provide data (DSC, TGA, XRD, FTIR) on the water stability of these amorphous materials. 
12. The porosity of coordination polymer glass or MOF glasses has long been an important property, as in the authors'
previous work Nat Commun 15, 7339 (2024). This raised the natural question of whether the different modulators can be
removed and the effect on porosity after their removal. 
13. There are several typographic errors in the Supplementary Information PDF file, which make it difficult to evaluate the
Supplementary Information fully. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an impressive and groundbreaking study on molecular network-forming glasses, addressing a
highly relevant challenge in the field of reticular and glassy materials. The innovative approach of introducing network
modulators to enhance configurational motion and enable vitrification in strong-bonded network systems is both novel and
impactful. The proposed node-linker-modulator design offers a flexible, universal framework that opens the door for
designing diverse network-forming glasses with tunable properties and significant potential applications. The systematic
demonstration of this approach with different node types, linkers, and modulators further enhances the value of the study,
highlighting its robustness and versatility. 
The authors effectively show the scalability and practical benefits of this modular approach, especially in terms of controlling
viscosity and glass transition properties. This work not only advances our fundamental understanding of glass formation but
also brings an exciting expansion of reticular chemistry into the realm of functional glass materials. The successful synthesis
of fluorescent molecular network-forming glasses and their integration into electroluminescent devices is particularly
noteworthy, showcasing the translational potential of the research. This study will likely inspire new directions in the design
of molecular network materials.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a high-quality manuscript reporting on a novel node-linker-modulator strategy for constructing molecular network-
forming glasses. By controlling the introduction of network modulators, which can induce dynamic exchange and reduction
in network connectivity during the formation of networks, the authors prepared a series of new metal-organic networks and
covalent organic network glasses with various nodes and linkers. The effect of the addition of modulators on the formation of
glasses was also investigated. This manuscript is of novelty and is well written. I suggest that authors should address the
following issues: 
1. The heat-capacity jump is an essential indicator for the glass transition in the studied glasses. As shown in the method
section, the DSC heat flow was measured. But how was the heat-capacity values obtained? Please describe this in the
manuscript. 
2. In fact, their MOF glasses were obtained by a melt-quenching route despite the temperature was relatively low during
synthesize. This is why the Tg can be observed. 
3. The mass change curves should be given, which helps understand the DSC response of the synthesized materials during

heating. 
4. In the Figs 1i and 2f, the Angell plots are used to show the change in fragility with increased network ratio, but the result of
decrease in fragility derived from visual observation is not convincing. Since both Tg and viscosity data have been obtained,
the fragility index should be calculated and given. 
5. The method and data for confirming the chemical structure of the synthesized network glasses should be added. 
6. The endothermic or exothermic direction should be marked in the DSC figures.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have improved their manuscript substantially compared to the previous version. In particular, they have focused
their study on fewer compounds and now provide more in-depth structural and compositional characterisation for at least
some of the materials. This is an improvement, as understanding the composition is essential to rationalise the observed
physicochemical properties. 
Nevertheless, several substantial issues remain that, in my view, significantly compromise some of the hypotheses put
forward in the manuscript. 
The following points must be fully addressed before I am able to recommend publication of this work: 
1. The authors now report 1H NMR data to quantify the ratios of the different organic building blocks. However, ICP and EA
results are only provided for eight samples. Given the importance of the inorganic nodes (Ti, Zr, B) and oxygen content in
determining the average connectivity of the proposed clusters, this dataset is too limited. Without a robust assessment of
metal, oxygen and carbon stoichiometry across the series, including the variations with increasing modulator content, the
claim of systematic tuning of connectivity remains insufficiently substantiated. 
2. The authors interpret increasing viscosity and reduced heat capacity jump at the glass transition as evidence for higher
connectivity. However, they do not discuss the possibility that the “bridging” organic units (e.g., TB, GDPA, PEG-5) might
simply coordinate both ends to the same inorganic cluster, yielding intramolecular chelation rather than extended network
formation. This might be relevant for all these materials, as it would also contribute to relatively low Tg values. The
manuscript would benefit from an explicit discussion of this possibility and, ideally, experimental approaches to distinguish
between true network connectivity and localised chelation. However, I understand that the latter is very difficult to achieve. 
3. The authors use an experiment mixing TMBA and TTIP, observing isopropanol release, to argue for dynamic exchange of
modulator and linker. However, this experiment does not convincingly demonstrate exchange in the final glass materials. In
the TMBA-TTIP experiment, the system begins out of equilibrium. Isopropanol evolution results from condensation and
evaporative shifting of the equilibrium. There is also water in the system, which shifts the equilibrium. In contrast, the glassy
materials are prepared such that isopropoxide and water (likely the source of the oxide anions proposed to be present in the
Ti16 and Zr3 clusters) have already completely reacted and isopropanol release is complete. At room temperature or below,
alkoxide exchange at Ti, Zr, or B centres is expected to be extremely slow, given the high bond strengths and activation
energies. The authors have not provided experimental evidence for such exchange in the glasses, particularly above Tg,
where some mobility might occur. This remains an important open question. 
4. The manuscript proposes that a Ti16 cluster is the main inorganic building unit in the Ti-based glasses. However, the
reported PDFs do not convincingly support this hypothesis. The experimental PDFs lack clear features beyond ~7 Å. Given
the expected dimension of a Ti16O16 cluster of about 1 nm, distinct interatomic correlations should be visible to at least 8-

glasses.

First, I would like to apologise for the delay in providing my comments on this second revision. I have been away on summer
vacation and was unfortunately unable to address the manuscript earlier. 
The authors have made additional efforts to strengthen their work and have included new compositional analyses. While
some of these partially support the hypotheses, the fundamental structural assumptions remain insufficiently substantiated,
and several major issues persist. 
The central concern continues to be the reliance on Ti16O16 clusters as the representative inorganic building unit of the
reported coordination polymers. This assumption is not convincingly supported by the data. The Ti contents measured by
ICP deviate substantially for some materials, suggesting variations in oxygen content and implying the presence of either a
distribution of Ti-oxo clusters with different compositions and (very importantly) connectivities or the presence of a very
different kind of cluster type. Literature evidence highlights that Ti-oxo clusters can vary widely in alkoxide/Ti ratios (e.g. only
about ~1.2 for Ti17O24 clusters versus 2 for the envisaged Ti16O32). These differences have major implications for network
structure and network connectivity and, therefore, for any composition-structure-property relationships. Mass spectrometry

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

indeed suggests the presence of an ensemble of different clusters, some of them very likely lighter ones, such as Ti17O24.
By contrast, the PDFs cannot resolve the nature of the inorganic clusters, as the low Ti concentration in the samples means
that the signal is dominated by the organic units. For example, the PDF shown in Figure 1c is from Ti-TB0.67TMBA1.33.
The Ti content, according to ICP, is only 4%! Thus, the correlations in the PDF are strongly dominated by the organic
building blocks of the material and identification of the Ti16O32 cluster is not possible. I suspect that a PDF of the organic
building units TB and TMBA, without the addition of Ti(OiPr)4, will have similar features to the ones shown for the Ti-oxo-
based materials. Consequently, the PDFs (similar to the MS and DLS data) do not provide sufficient evidence for the
predominant existence of Ti16O16 clusters (or Zr3O clusters for the Zr-based materials). The correlations at 2.1 Å and 3.5 Å
(page 9) may also be assigned to C···C distances in the organic units. 
The DLS data are unable to substantiate that the Ti-oxo clusters present in the materials are of the type Ti16O16, since
clusters of different composition and connectivity (alkoxide groups) but similar size are well documented. Hence, the
discussion of the network connectivity based on the sole presence of Ti16O16 clusters is not supported by the experimental
data. The corresponding text in the manuscript, including the discussion of the computational model presented in Figure 1b,
requires thorough revision. The authors should explicitly acknowledge and discuss the limitations of the currently available
experimental evidence regarding the structural building units of the materials. 
The newly introduced control experiment involving ligand exchange on a pre-synthesised Ti16O16 cluster is also not
informative. Similar to the control experiment in the previous revision, the new experiment also starts out of equilibrium. The
significantly higher acidity of benzyl alcohol compared to ethanol is the thermodynamic origin of the ligand exchange. The
experiment does not provide evidence for kinetic ligand exchange at the very low Tg values reported in the manuscript, and
as such, is not considered evidence for the proposed dissociative processes in the glasses. 
Overall, while I acknowledge the authors’ efforts and agree that the reported systems are intrinsically complex, the evidence
for the proposed Ti16O16 cluster model remains weak (the same is true for the Zr3O cluster model of the Zr-based
materials). As such, the structure-property relationships discussed in the manuscript are mainly composition-property
relationships; the structural and connectivity parts are not sufficiently reliable, and the conclusions risk overstating the level
of structural understanding. Nevertheless, I believe the work could still be suitable for Nature Communications if the authors
explicitly and openly acknowledge the limitations of their structural model, reframe their conclusions accordingly, and
present their findings primarily in a phenomenological manner. Substantial revisions would therefore still be required before
the manuscript could be considered for publication. 
There are also some other issues remaining: 
- The new PXRD measurements (Bruker instrument) show essentially no scattering signal from the sample. Even for
materials composed of light elements, one would expect diffuse scattering representative of the amorphous structure, its
absence is concerning and suggests that the data quality is insufficient. The blue PXRD pattern shown in Figure R11a
features a very strange line shape in the region from 20° to 35° 2theta, suggesting substantial instrumental problems. 
- The vertical axis in Figure 1i is incorrectly labelled.

a versatile node-linker-modulator network, showcasing examples involving titanium, zirconium,

Experimentally, a Ti-TB coordination polymer could be dissolved in TMBA with heating due to the 
exchange of hydroxyl group of TB and TMBA. Comparably the Ti-TB coordination polymer cannot

between linker and modulator is key to the evaporation-based glass synthesis, where a volatile 
modulator (i.e. ethanol) is used as solvent to dissolve the linker and node followed by evaporation.

This is an interesting point. In supramolecular and polymer chemistry, formation of loops (one 
linking unit with both ends bonding to the same node) often requires elaborate synthetic controls. 
The general difficulty for loop formation can be understood from a thermodynamic perspective: (a) 
considering entropy, formation of loops poses additional constraints on how the node and linker 
connect and limits the number of configurations available for the system; considering enthalpy,

would not contribute significantly to the physical properties of the alkoxide glasses reported in this 
paper.

Response: We thank the reviewers for their time and input, especially the perspective from (likely) 
a more physical community. From a material chemistry perspective, the main message of this paper 
is the modular synthesis of a series of glassy coordinative and covalent networks. Thus the structural 
and connectivity analysis is mostly meant to support: (1) the presence of coordinative network at 
low modulator content; (2) the gradual reduction of modulator content give increasing network 
connectivity and viscosity, enabling the evaporative synthetic method. Thus, the fundamental 
structural assumption is not the predominant presence of the Ti 16 cluster. Instead, the key 
assumption that the structural analysis is trying to support is that the present titanium node is similar
to the Ti16 cluster in terms of size and stoichiometric concerns. And this “similarity” is defined with 
respect to the tunability range of the modulator content. For instance, even in extreme cases where 
the Ti17O24(OEt)20 is a dominant species, the currently founded modulator content, in the lower limit, 
still support the presence of a coordinative network. Thus, we are not trying to propose a molecularly 
precise metal node or quantitatively precise connectivity, although in the quantitative analysis, it is 
found that the Ti16 based analysis give a relatively good match with physical properties. However, 
we also agree that the current narration may mislead certain authors from different background, and 
we revise the paper to clearly state the limitations.  
The central concern continues to be the reliance on Ti16O16 clusters as the representative inorganic 
building unit of the reported coordination polymers. This assumption is not convincingly supported
