(Remarks to the Author) 
Crinkler (CRN) effector proteins represent a family of pathogen-secreted molecules that manipulate host cell immune
defenses during infection. This manuscript demonstrates that ADP-ribosyltransferase-associated CRN (AACRN), a CRN
homolog, has evolved beyond its pathogenic origins to function as an epigenetic parasite in free-living eukaryotes. The
authors present molecular evidence showing that AACRN mono-ADP-ribosylates both EZH2 and TRAF6 via its mART
domain, thereby disrupting H3K27me3-mediated chromatin silencing and suppressing NF-κB/IRF3 signaling to establish an
immunologically permissive environment for expansion. The data presented supports the authors' conclusions; however,
several critical issues require attention.

mRNA transcription or enhanced protein degradation, similar to the TRAF6 degradation demonstrated in this study?

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript describes the discovery of proteins which have similarity to functional domains found in some CRN effector
proteins from pathogenic eukaryotes. The authors focus most of their efforts on characterising one of these, AACRN, and
show that it has ADP-ribosylation activity. Intriguingly, it appears to use this toxin-like activity to promote its own expression
and replication, by suppressing the epigenetic mechanisms that cells would normally use to control mobile elements. The
host genome has evolved an antidote to AACRN to keep its activity in check. This was a very interesting manuscript to read. 
CRN effectors were first described from oomycete plant pathogens. The unifying feature of these candidate effectors is a
highly conserved N terminal peptide sequence, while the C terminal section of these proteins can be highly diverse. Since
the proteins studied in this manuscript lack the classical N terminal leader (header) characteristic of CRN proteins, it seems
misleading to call them as such. I would strongly suggest that the AACRN proteins be renamed throughout the manuscript.
This will also impact on the title for the manuscript, which will need to be changed accordingly.

western blot figures show uneven band intensity, suggesting technical issues with protein transfer to membranes, and
making it difficult to interpret the results. Some of these figures would also benefit from signal quantification to more

Q1: The fundamental question remains: why do these degenerated effector proteins maintain intact 
mART activity and continue expanding in free-living eukaryotes? This evolutionary paradox

Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful question regarding why these degenerated 
effector proteins retain intact mART activity and continue to expand in free-living eukaryotes. To 
address this evolutionary paradox, we incorporated new analyses and expanded our discussion.
First, by examining publicly available transcriptomic datasets, we found that all five zebrafish 
AACRNLγ members remain largely silent during embryogenesis, whereas three members 
(AACRNLγ2, 3, and 5) are strongly induced following SVCV infection (Figs. 5a and 5b). Similarly, 
in M. miiuy, AACRNLβ expression is markedly upregulated upon SCRV infection and coincides 
with increased DNA damage and heightened transposon activity (Figs. 5c-5j). These results indicate

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

8
that pathogen infection is a key trigger for AACRNL activation, while under basal conditions 
AACRNL remains repressed to minimize host fitness costs.
Second, our findings support a model in which AACRNL family expansion reflects a balance 
between host-driven degeneration and the element’s intrinsic capacity for self-propagation. 
Consistent with host-imposed silencing, several AACRNL homologs show evidence of functional 
decay—for example, zebrafish AACRNLγ1 and AACRNLγ4 retain intact coding sequences but 
appear transcriptionally inactive (Figs. 5a and 5b), and multiple fish species (including M. miiuy, A. 
alosa, and P . platessa) harbor clearly degenerated AACRNL copies (Fig. 3c). Nevertheless, viral 
infection provides episodic opportunities for reactivation. Once induced, AACRNL not only 
promotes genomic instability but also suppresses immune signaling, generating a transient 
“replication window” that facilitates transposon mobilization and ensures propagation of active 
AACRNL elements.
Thus, we propose that AACRNL preserves mART catalytic activity because this activity 
remains essential for its occasional but crucial bursts of replication under infection-induced 
permissive conditions. We have now incorporated these data and mechanistic considerations into 
the revised Discussion (Lines 317-334).
Q2: Mechanism of EZH2 Protein Reduction- The observed decrease in EZH2 protein expression 
following AACRN β-Myc expression (Figure 4H) requires mechanistic clarification. Specifically, 
does this reduction result from decreased EZH2 mRNA transcription or enhanced protein 
degradation, similar to the TRAF6 degradation demonstrated in this study?
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comment regarding the mechanism underlying 
the reduction of EZH2 protein levels upon AACRNβ-Myc expression. To clarify this point, we 
performed additional experiments. Quantitative RT–PCR analysis showed that overexpression of 
AACRNβ-Myc does not alter EZH2 mRNA levels, indicating that transcriptional regulation is not 
involved (Fig. 4i). Furthermore, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays demonstrated that AACRNβ-
Myc accelerates the degradation of EZH2 protein (Fig. 4j). These results support that AACRNβ 
primarily reduces EZH2 abundance by promoting its protein degradation rather than affecting its 
transcription, consistent with our observations on AACRNβ-mediated regulation of TRAF6 stability.

Q3: What is the impact of AACRNβ mutation in host chromatin regulation and gene expression? 
Understanding these broader genomic impacts would strengthen the conclusions regarding 
AACRN's role as an epigenetic modifier. This comment is mostly related to my point no. 1.
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. To address the broader chromatin 
and transcriptional impacts of AACRNLβ, we performed additional analyses focusing on key 
repressive epigenetic marks and transposon activity.
First, we examined whether AACRNLβ affects other major heterochromatic modifications 
beyond H3K27me3. Our results show that AACRNLβ does not alter the levels of H3K9me3, 
indicating that its impact on host chromatin is selective rather than globally disruptive (Fig. 4b). In 
addition, as noted in our response to Comment 1, AACRNL is transcriptionally activated during 
viral infection. We further demonstrate that AACRNL activation during virus infection correlates 
with increased DNA damage and reactivation of multiple transposable elements, supporting its role 
as a stress-responsive epigenetic disruptor (Fig. 5). Importantly, knockdown of AACRNLβ rescues 
the SCRV-induced loss of H3K27me3, confirming that AACRNLβ is important for virus-mediated 
release of Polycomb repression. Mechanistically, we show that AACRNLβ enhances the expression 
of canonical DNA-damage–responsive genes (tp53, p21, gadd45a) in a mono-ADP-
ribosyltransferase–dependent manner, and similarly promotes the transcription of LINE and ERV 
retroelements through its enzymatic activity. These results indicate that AACRNLβ exerts broad 
effects on chromatin stability and genome regulation, primarily through its catalytic function and its 
ability to antagonize H3K27me3-mediated silencing during viral infection.
Together, these findings demonstrate that AACRNLβ functions as a potent epigenetic disruptor 
that promotes DNA damage, removes H3K27me3-dependent repression, and activates transposon 
expression in an mART-dependent manner, particularly under viral infection.

This manuscript describes the discovery of proteins which have similarity to functional 
domains found in some CRN effector proteins from pathogenic eukaryotes. The authors focus most 
of their efforts on characterising one of these, AACRN, and show that it has ADP-ribosylation

information would also help the reader.
