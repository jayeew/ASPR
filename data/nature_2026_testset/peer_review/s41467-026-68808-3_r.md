Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is, potentially, a very compelling manuscript describing a 19bp deletion that is common in African ancestry, is about 1kb
away from APOE, and greatly reduces the APOE4 effect on Alzheimer’s disease risk. Some solid cell-based assays suggest
that the effect on reducing risk is related not to APOE directly but to APOC1. The 19bp deletion is near an SPI1 binding site
located between APOE and APOC1. Binding of SPI1 here in microglial cells (but not neuronal cells) has a repressive effect
in a luciferase readout. Surprisingly, adding SPI1 to microglial cells had no effect on APOE expression but reduced APOC1
expression. That said, some important issues need addressing (particularly concern about sequencing center/platform
confounds) before the results can be taken at face value. 
The flow of the statistical analyses is muddled and hard to follow. It makes sense to search, initially, for structural variants on
an APOE4 haplotype whose allele frequency is high in AFR and lower in other ancestries. That is defensible. Having
landed on the 19bp deletion the next step should be a logistic regression in (genome-wide) AFR ancestry subjects that
covaries for age, sex, cohort, the APOEε2 and APOE-ε4 as they have done, but with the important addition of sequencing
center and platform as a covariate. This is an important potential confound that can drive spurious associations. And some
have argued that additional variant-level filters are required: 
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33861770/ 
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9372872/ 
Subsequent analyses should include 
a) APOE4-stratified analyses 
b) restriction to local AFR ancestry at the APOE locus 
c) examining the effect of the 19bp deletion in EUR ancestry (small number of carriers and so may not be significant but will
still be informative and potentially help with fine-mapping/ruling out other nearby variants). 
I would suggest a flow diagram as Figure 1 outlining this approach. 
Why does the results section start with a Fisher’s exact test rather than the logistic regression approaches described shortly
afterwards in the section on local ancestry. Unless there’s an important reason to keep the Fisher’s exact, the story would be
more straight forward removing all mention of Fisher’s and just sticking with the logistic regression throughout as suggested
above. At some point a chi-square test is used as well. I would suggest sticking to logistic regression throughout and
removing Fisher’s and chi-square tests unless there is a good reason to use them. This seems the most defensible as the
logistic regression allows for adding important covariates like age and sex and sample sizes (1,522 AD cases and 3,929
controls) with a relatively common indel like this (12% MAF) should allow for logistic regression. 
I did not see any mention of how relatedness between samples was handled. This can have strong effects in ADSP and
should be addressed. 
“Our search prioritized larger structural variants as opposed to common SNPs, which we reasoned would have been picked
up from association studies.” The 19bp deletion has an MAF of 0.12. Is it not well-tagged by a nearby SNP and, if so, has
that SNP not been identified in association studies? If the deletion is not tagged by a common SNP can it be readily imputed

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Brutman et al. identify a 19bp deletion (rs148353395) located ~1.1 kb downstream of the APOE gene within the predicted
SPI1 (PU.1), microglial transcription factor binding site. This deletion is significantly enriched in individuals of African
ancestry—particularly among those with the APOE-ε4/ε4 genotype—appearing in approximately 60% of African American
ε4 homozygotes. The variant is associated with an approximately 8-fold reduction in Alzheimer's disease (AD) risk and
delayed disease onset. The protective effect of the deletion was validated in multiple large-scale cohorts, including ADSP
and the NIH All of Us dataset, which also helped disentangle the neurological and lipid-related functions of APOE-ε4. By
integrating long-read sequencing, population-scale association analyses, and functional assays, the study provides strong
evidence that this structural variant modulates APOE-ε4-associated AD risk and contributes to ancestry-specific differences
in disease susceptibility. However, several analytical and mechanistic gaps remain: 
Major:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

1. Identification of the 19bp deletion (Lines 95–127). 
• The study used 11 individuals to identify the 19bp deletion – how did they select the non-African individuals? And what
was their race/ethnicity? Also, Fig 1a sample numbers don’t match this selection. 
• Can the authors clarify the rationale for selecting a ~75kb window surrounding APOE for variant discovery (Line 106)? This
parameter seems arbitrary. 
• It would be helpful to explicitly discuss linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the 19bp deletion and APOE-ε4 (rs429358) in
African ancestry—Figure 5a, rs148353395 shows moderate LD with rs429358 (r² = 0.48 in African ancestry), indicating
partial linkage. 
2. Confirmation of the protective effect of the 19bp deletion (rs148353395) using a PheWAS analysis in the NIH All of Us
dataset (Lines 163–207). 
• The authors stated that when limiting the analysis to individuals of African ancestry, they observed a reduced risk for AD
and related neurological traits. This ancestry-restricted analysis is based solely on global ancestry inferred from PCA,
without incorporating local ancestry at the APOE locus, which the authors themselves emphasized earlier (line 142–143) as
critical for accurate interpretation. 
• While the authors suggest a reduced AD risk in this African ancestry subgroup, they do not provide the odds ratio or p-
value in the main text. Supplementary Table 6, which includes the PheWAS results for rs148353395 in African ancestry
samples, states that the odds ratio is 1.29 and the p-value is 0.287, indicating no significant association, and even
suggesting an opposite direction to what’s claimed. 
• The authors then showed protective effect of 19bp deletion (OR = 0.56, p = 0.0058) when adjusting for APOE-ε4 and ε2
(Line 199-207), which is consistent with findings from the ADSP. As the 19bp deletion is in cis with APOE-ε4 (Line 199), and
the authors propose it modifies ε4-associated risk, it would be helpful to show a PheWAS restricted to APOE-ε4 carriers. 
3. In the functional assays (lines 209–237), the authors show that the 19bp deletion abolishes SPI1-mediated repression in
the microglia HMC3 cell line, but not in the neuronal SH-SY5Y line. 
• Can the authors clarify why the 19bp deletion appears to functionally affect SPI1-mediated regulation in microglia but not in
neurons (for example, is there cell-type specific SPI1 expression, or chromatin context driving this difference)? 
• In the luciferase assay using the APOE 3′UTR, the 19bp deletion restores reporter expression, suggesting that SPI1
represses transcription. However, SPI1 overexpression in HMC3 cells did not reduce APOE expression. Can the authors
explain this discrepancy? 
• While the authors assessed APOC1 and lncRNA ENSG00000280087 following SPI1 overexpression, the regulatory
effects of SPI1 may be broader. A more comprehensive transcriptome analysis (like RNA-seq) would help define more
comprehensive affected genes. 
• It’s unclear whether the transcriptional changes observed upon SPI1 overexpression truly mimic the biological effect of the
19bp deletion. Could the authors directly test the effect of the 19bp deletion itself (eg., CRISPR ko) on gene expression? 
Minor: 
• The authors provide LD structure plots in Figure 5a–b, but they are not discussed in the main text. 
• Line 194, (Fig. 3e) should be (Fig. 3d).

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
A common 19bp APOE enhancer deletion is protective against Alzheimer’s disease in African Americans 
The study aimed to investigate the potential mechanisms underlying the reduced risk of APOE in individuals of African
ancestry. Using the phased long-read sequencing from 1KG, the study prioritized larger structural variants that haven’t been
previously identified by GWAS and found 19bp deletion (rs148353395) ~1.1kb past the 3’UTR of APOE that was in cis with
APOE e4 allele. Through validation in the ADSP, it was demonstrated that the deletion frequency was significantly higher in
individuals of African ancestry than in individuals of European ancestry (0.066-0.22 vs 0.0005, respectively) and that this
deletion was protective against AD in individuals with local African ancestry. A heterozygous or homozygous 19bp deletion
in individuals with APOEe4/e4 genotypes also showed a 3-year median delay in onset of AD compared to those without the
deletion. PheWAS with All of Us datasets also showed that the deletion was associated with elevated hyperlipidemia and

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

hypercholesterolemia but was not associated with AD risk in individuals of African ancestry. To understand the functional
consequences of the deletion, the study utilized a luciferase assay, which showed that the deletion was associated with
reduced APOE expression in a human-derived neuronal cell line but had no impact on a human-derived microglial cell.
Finally, the study also identified a few SNPs between the coding regions of APOE and APOC1 that disproportionately
contribute to either neurological or lipid-related traits. Overall, this study provides compelling evidence into the complex and
understudied mechanisms by which the APOE e4-associated risk for AD is attenuated in individuals of African ancestry, and
the work highlights the importance of considering genetic and local ancestry structure in understanding disease risk and
prediction. 
Major concerns 
1. The function of astrocytes is not discussed or investigated in the paper. In addition to microglia and neurons, it would be
beneficial for the paper to examine--or at least discuss the possible implications of--the functional consequences of the
deletion SNP in astrocytes, given their role in ApoE secretion. 
2. One of the main arguments in this article is that the 19 bp deletion variant is potentially protective against AD in
individuals of African ancestry. Since the APOE e2 allele is widely known to have similar protective effects, it is unclear why
the e2 haplotypes were excluded from the survival analysis that tested whether rs148353395 affected AD age of onset. 
3. While the authors give some description about their random forest machine learning approach (also included in their
code), it is unclear how features were selected and whether they used a validation dataset. 
Minor concerns 
1. The authors evaluated long-read sequencing Human Genomes Databases to find variants in cis with the APOE-e4 risk
allele. While some explanation was provided regarding the specific allele-ancestry comparisons they performed to identify a
19-bp deletion variant, rs148353395, it is unclear whether the individuals in each comparison group had one or two copies
of the APOE alleles (e3 and e4). 
2. One consideration to note is the relatively small sample size for the long-read sequencing data. It may strengthen the
discussion to briefly acknowledge this limitation and note that future replication in larger, independent cohorts will be
important to support these findings further. 
3. It may be helpful to estimate and account for genetic ancestry in the downstream ADSP analysis to align more closely with
the methodological approach used in the All of Us analysis. 
4. Underneath the genotype distribution bar graph of the 19 bp deletion-variant in figure 2a, the authors included an “odds
ratios” table with what seems to be raw counts of genotype dosages across APOE haplotypes in cases and controls. This
table is missing values for e2/e4 groups (depicted in the graph), which are important to the article’s central argument that the
rs148353395 deletion offers similar protective effects against AD as the APOE-e2 allele. Additionally, the AD e4/e4 group is
repeated twice with different count values. 
5. Based on the text in the results section, the second label appears to be a possible typing error where the repeated E4
homozygote counts are actually those from the AD e3/e4 groups. Aside from this apparent typo, adding labels (e.g., “n=”)
directly to the figure and providing a more detailed legend would enhance clarity and improve interpretability. 
6. In figure 3e, data points for APOE-e4 and APOE-e2 are plotted in the same color, which are also hard to distinguish from
the 19-bp deletion data points depicted in an overlapping color scheme. The notation for the grouping labels in this figure
are also difficult to interpret. Having different color gradients representing corrected and uncorrected groupings across
haplotypes, and keeping consistent label structure, would improve the cohesion and interpretability of this figure. 
Other notes: 
-The manuscript could be further strengthened by including a brief, clear explanation of the rationale for selecting each
dataset for the corresponding analysis. 
-The third paragraph of the discussion could be further enriched by expanding on its relevance in the context of African
ancestry to reiterate the significance of the study. 
-Larger font for all figures (hard to read) 
-Results section/ The authors describe the identification of the 19-bp deletion variant “present in three alleles”. This wording
is confusing since the identifications provided (HG03486mat, HG02486mat, and HG02011-h2) are database-specific
sequence identifiers, not alleles. (“We identified a 19bp deletion (rs148353395) ~1.1kb past the 3′UTR of APOE that fit our
criteria (Fig. 1a), present in three alleles.”)
