Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors develop an interesting method involving intricate Lewis acid-base interactions between a
hole-selective self-assembled monolayer (SAM) and ammonium ligands to construct a thin, localized 2D/3D perovskite
heterojunction at the buried interface. This structure not only reduces defect density but also facilitates charge extraction and
templates perovskite crystallization, without compromising the bulk optoelectrical properties of the 3D perovskite.
Encouragingly, this method enables high performance in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with different bandgaps, and the
resulting perovskite/organic tandem solar cells achieve a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 27.11%. 
Overall, this is an excellent study that would make a timely contribution to the field. Reviewers appreciate the efforts
presented here, which differ distinctly from conventional studies on buried interface treatment in perovskites. The work
establishes in-depth correlations between SAMs and ligands, offering new insights for fabricating high-quality perovskite
devices. However, several issues need to be addressed before consideration for publication in Nature Communications. My
concerns are listed as follows: 
Please analyze why the CbzBT molecule is effective. What considerations guided the molecular structure design to form a
thin, localized 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction at the buried interface? Such structure-property relationships would provide
valuable new knowledge to the perovskite community. 
As noted in the text, this method facilitates the formation of a thin layer at the buried interface. Please clarify the thickness
and compactness of this layer. It is well known that a layer that is too thin may result in poor, uneven coverage, while one
that is too thick could hinder charge transport at the buried interface. 
To better demonstrate the versatility of this approach, the authors should showcase perovskite device performance using
distinct ligands (e.g., PEAI and BAI). 
As shown in Fig. 4C, the target cell retains ~95% of its initial PCE after 700 hours of operation. For comprehensive
experimental reproducibility, detailed technical information on device encapsulation—including but not limited to
encapsulation materials, layer thicknesses, and curing conditions—should be fully documented in the Supporting
Information (SI). Notably, many previous reports on perovskite cells use different device structures for stability tests
compared to those used for the best-efficiency devices. 
In the stability tests, please report actual device efficiencies rather than relative values. Additionally, providing stability data
for single-junction devices would help identify bottlenecks in each subcell of the tandem configuration. 
The authors describe an effective method for constructing the thin layer at the buried interface and report outstanding
performance in small-area tandem devices. Is this approach scalable for large-scale fabrication? 
In Figure S22, the authors compare the electroluminescence (EL) of control and target devices at the same voltage. A more
appropriate comparison would use the same current density: given the differing open-circuit voltages (Voc) of the two
devices, injecting current (as in standard LED measurements) and maintaining constant current density would yield more
meaningful results.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors introduced CbzBT-based self-assembled monolayers containing Lewis basic S heteroatoms
to anchoring Lewis acidic ammonium ligands. The retained ammonium ligands form a low-dimensional perovskite layer at
buried interface, which reduced defect density and facilitated charge extraction. Eventually, they achieved a certified
efficiency of 26.3% in a monolithic perovskite-organic tandem solar cells. This manuscript provides a new aspect for buried
interface modification in wide-bandgap perovskite. I recommend publication in Nature Communication after addressing the
following issues. 
1. The authors should determine the n-value of the 2D perovskite layers. 
2. The authors suggested that the ammonium in Lewis acid is the key for interacting with the CbzBT-based self-assembled
monolayers to construct the 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction. What are the specific requirements for the choice of
ammonium ligand? Have the authors considered or tested any other conventional ammonium ligands such as PEAI or BAI?
In this regard, what are the considerations or advantages that led to the selection of OAmI as the ligand for constructing the
heterojunction in this work? 
3. Considering the relatively strong solubility of DMF, please confirm that the SAM layer at the bottom interface cannot be
removed during the washing process. The authors should clarify why DMF was chosen over other milder solvents such as
isopropanol or ethanol as they also giving enough solubility to remove excess ligands. 
4. The GIWAXS measurement might not be sufficiently sensitive to probe the buried interface of the perovskite film. I suggest
the authors to exfoliate the perovskite film to expose the bottom interface for the GIWAXS characterization. 
5. More characterizations should be included to demonstrate the presence of the heterojunction. 
6. In Supplementary, GBAC and PI were abbreviated and should be given full name for clarification. 
7. In Fig. 1c, “Binding” was misspelling. In Fig. 4i, the right part of the EQE should be “Rear cell”. The author should review
the manuscript more carefully. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Chen and coworkers report wide gap perovskite solar cells with improved Voc. They study the buried hole selective
interface of perovskite and a SAM. Specifically, the modify the buried interface by forming a thin localized 2D/3D perovskite
heterojunction. The authors claim that the resulting buried 2D/3D perovskite structure would reduce defect density and
improve hole extraction, while at the same time the crystallization of the perovskite on top is improved. The resulting PSCs
reach a Voc exceeding 90% of their respective thermodynamic limits. Ultimately, these wide gap cells are combined with a
narrow gap organic cell in tandems, that achieve > 27% efficiency (certified 26.3%). While I commend the authors for the
impressive device results, I felt that the paper lacks scientific rigor. While I do not doubt the validity of the achieved
improvements, I am not convinced by the story the authors tell to explain the origin of these improvements. I hope the authors
are able to revise their manuscript to significantly improve the scientific rigor and assure that all claims are supported by
scientific evidence. Some comments: 
1) Please explain the claim: “Given that most LDPs exhibit more p-type characteristics than their three-dimensional (3D)
counterparts …”. What is meant by p-type in this case. Do you mean p-type doping? Where does the doping come from,
then? 
2) Same with “p-type self-assembled monolayers (SAMs)”. Again, do you mean p-type doping? If so, where does it come
from? 
3) Scheme 1 is very illustrative, but I am missing experimental evidence that would support the validity of this scheme. 
4) Where is the experimental evidence for “applying solvent (dimethylformamide, DMF) washing (w/ SW) on the as-formed
ligand layer results in poor coverage and non-uniform distribution of ligands on the substrate”? 
5) Correct: “Solent washing” in Figure 1d 
6) I believe each SAM molecule can localize one bulky cation. The authors themselves say: “We anticipate that a
“monolayer-like” ligand will remain…” So, I do not understand how this could lead to the formation of a 2D perovskite there? I
assume, the formation of a 2D perovskite would need more space molecules. And it would indeed need some space
molecules that are mobile so that a layered 2D perovskite can form in the first place. 
It is in this context very surprising that

the 2D layer is claimed to be found in XRD, as in XRD in order to lead to some notable diffraction signal several planes
would have to form, possibly requiring a substantial amount of spacer molecules. 
7) I am not convinced by the slight PL blue shift being due to quantum confinement in the 2D perovskite. With the ample
presence of 3D material with a narrower gap, all excitons created in the possible 2D material should be transferred to the 3D
material and recombine there. Only in the case where the exciton diffusion length in the 2D material would be too small, one
would anticipate some PL form the 2D. 
8) The discussion: “As depicted in Fig. 2G, the crystal growth presented a temporarily increased PL intensity, followed by a
notable decrease due to the formation of grain boundaries and thermal effects.” Is scientifically very weak. What is meant by

makes sense if a proper error estimate is conducted and cherry picking is strictly avoided. 
11) To understand if the surface of the perovskite is favorable for electron extraction, one needs to deposit the corresponding
electron extraction layer and measure the electronic alignment in contact. Otherwise, any reasoning about better/worse
electron extraction is very vague and not rigorous.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
In this manuscript, the authors develop an interesting method involving intricate Lewis 
acid-base interactions between a hole-selective self-assembled monolayer (SAM) and 
ammonium ligands to construct a thin, localized 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction at the 
buried interface. This structure not only reduces defect density but also facilitates 
charge extraction and templates perovskite crystallization, without compromising the 
bulk optoelectrical properties of the 3D perovskite. Encouragingly, this method enables 
high performance in perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with different bandgaps, and the 
resulting perovskite/organic tandem solar cells achieve a power conversion efficiency 
(PCE) of 27.11%. 
Overall, this is an excellent study that would make a timely contribution to the field. 
Reviewers appreciate the efforts presented here, which differ distinctly from 
conventional studies on buried interface treatment in perovskites. The work establishes 
in-depth correlations between SAMs and ligands, offering new insights for fabricating 
high-quality perovskite devices. However, several issues need to be addressed before 
consideration for publication in Nature Communications. My concerns are listed as 
follows: 
Reply: 
We appreciate very much the recognition from reviewer 1 for our work's novelty and 
impact. We have carefully considered the suggested improvements and conducted the 
necessary experiments and analyses to address these comments, and updated the 
manuscript accordingly.  
1. Please analyze why the CbzBT molecule is effective. What considerations guided the 
molecular structure design to form a thin, localized 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction at 
the buried interface? Such structure-property relationships would provide valuable new 
knowledge to the perovskite community. 
Reply: 
The effectiveness of CbzBT-B stems from its deliberately engineered structure. The 
commonly used SAM with extended conjugation (e.g., CbzNaph) exhibits a higher 
HOMO energy level, which enlarges the energy offset with the VBM of WBG 
perovskites. Meanwhile, the all-carbon-hydrogen structure at the apex of the molecular 
skeleton lacks the functional groups necessary for inducing molecular interactions with 
the upper layer.  
Therefore, our molecular design was guided by two key considerations: (i) introducing

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

effective interactions with the ammonium ligands, and (ii) tuning the energy level of 
the SAM to better match with the WBG perovskites. Given the structural versatility of 
SAM molecules, incorporating heteroatoms provides an ideal route to functionalization. 
Building on previous studies, sulfur’s electron-rich outer shell enables electrostatic 
attraction with the ligands (cations). At the same time, introducing an asymmetric 
molecular framework and optimizing the linker allows precise control over energy 
levels and enhances overall stability. Our experimental results confirm that CbzBT-B 
effectively localizes the ligands and facilitates the formation of a 2D/3D heterojunction 
during perovskite crystallization. Additionally, the well-aligned energy level between 
SAM and perovskite further improves the efficiency and stability of the devices. 
2. As noted in the text, this method facilitates the formation of a thin layer at the buried 
interface. Please clarify the thickness and compactness of this layer. It is well known 
that a layer that is too thin may result in poor, uneven coverage, while one that is too 
thick could hinder charge transport at the buried interface. 
Reply: 
We fully agree that an excessively thick interfacial layer could impede charge transport, 
whereas an ultrathin yet discontinuous layer may lead to uneven coverage. To explicitly 
determine the spatial distribution and morphology of LDPs, we supplemented our study 
with targeted SEM with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analyses using 
FPEAI ligands, which contain characteristic F atoms that are distinct from other 
elements in functional layers. Cross-sectional SEM and EDX results show that the 
heterojunction is predominantly localized at the buried interface, with an estimated 
thickness of ~10 nm (Fig. R1A). Furthermore, top-viewing SEM and EDX of the buried 
interface reveal that LDPs are mainly distributed along grain boundaries rather than 
uniformly across the surface.  
Based on these observations, we propose the following formation mechanism during 
the annealing process: as perovskite grains grow and coalesce, most ligands are 
expelled to evolve grain boundaries to form confined LDPs. A smaller fraction of 
ligands remains within the grain, passivating intrinsic defects. This mechanism 
accounts for the formation of a localized heterojunction with discontinuous and island-
like morphology, which is capable of effectively passivating interfacial defects without 
compromising charge extraction. We would like to clarify that our approach does not 
produce a continuous and compact film at the buried interface.  
We have accordingly revised the discussion and Scheme 1 in the main text to better 
elucidate the nature of the localized 2D/3D perovskite heterojunction.

To explicitly identify the spatial distribution and morphology of LDPs, we have 
conducted SEM combined with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) for the

in Fig. S13. The buried interface SEM and EDX show that LDPs are mainly distributed 
along grain boundaries. Based on the EDX results and the presence of the LDPs
