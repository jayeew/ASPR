Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Mace et al. investigates the genetic and molecular origins of idiopathic hypersomnia (IH) through a cross-
species approach using Drosophila and zebrafish models, informed by human genomics data. The study identifies the
synaptic adhesion molecules beat-Ia in Drosophila and its vertebrate ortholog CADM2 as key regulators of sleep, with
knockdown experiments revealing increased sleep duration and other hypersomnia-like phenotypes. The research
emphasizes the developmental role of beat-Ia in organizing a wake-promoting NPF circuit (NPF SEZ neurons) that
stabilizes arousal in adulthood, with disrupted function leading to excessive sleepiness. These findings are corroborated in
zebrafish, where CADM2 disruption similarly increases sleep, and treatment with an NPY receptor agonist rescues this
phenotype, suggesting a potential therapeutic target for IH. This is an important paper for identifying an evolutionarily
conserved signaling pathway regulating sleep. However, I have several comments that could help improve the manuscript: 
1. The evidence that beat-Ia acts in NPF SEZ neurons to regulate sleep/wakefulness is not strong. In Figure 7B, it appears
that the knockdown of beat-Ia reduces the Syt-GFP signal across the brain, not just in NPF SEZ neurons, suggesting that
beat-Ia knockdown may generally reduce presynaptic sites in many, if not all, neurons. Additionally, NPF wakefulness-
promoting neurons are typically clock neurons, not SEZ neurons. To directly prove that beat-Ia affects the development of
NPF SEZ neurons, the authors could identify a specific driver for these NPF SEZ neurons and manipulate it directly to
demonstrate that it is indeed an arousal-promoting neuron. They should also knockdown beat-Ia only in these NPF SEZ
neurons and examine the sleep effects. 
2. The side antibody immunostaining shown in Figure 7D indicates expression in only some SEZ neurons during
development, with no expression in adults, which is surprising. According to the Fly Cell Atlas, side appears to be broadly
expressed in most neurons during the adult stage in the brain. The authors could use a CRIMIC-T2A-Gal4 line of side
(Bloomington 81175) to confirm this expression pattern. Additionally, they should examine whether the knockdown of side
leads to a loss of presynaptic sites in NPF SEZ neurons. 
3. Drosophila NPF neurons are sexually dimorphic. It is important to determine whether these sleep/wakefulness
phenotypes also exist in both male and female flies. While the authors do not need to repeat all behavior experiments using
male flies, it would be beneficial to perform the beat-Ia knockdown experiments using the elav-GAL4 and NPF-GAL4 drivers
in males to assess whether the phenotypes are consistent across the sexes. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This report is a functional evaluation of a gene associated with idiopathic hypersomnia in humans, using the Drosophila and
the zebrafish models. The authors initially performed a large neuronal knockdown screen in Drosophila for over 200
candidate genes previously identified through GWAS as linked to hypersomnia. They then focus on one of the hits, beat-Ia,
and dissect its role in Drosophila, showing that its downregulation during development in a subset of peptidergic neurons,
NPF expressing neurons, results in hypersomnia. Using synaptic reporters and genetic manipulation of neuronal activity,
they go on to provide evidence that beat-Ia downregulation affect the connection between those peptidergic neurons and a
subset of ventrally located neurons that modulate sleep-wake. Finally they generate a mutant zebrafish for the vertebrate
homolog of Beat-Ia and show that this mutant displays moderately higher sleep amounts than controls. Interestingly, they

find that this latter phenotype can be normalized with an agonist for the vertebrate homolog of NPF. All of this represents a
large amount of work. So far, compared to insomnia, fewer studies have investigated genes associated with idiopathic
hypersomnia. This work suggests that developmental synaptic defects could be responsible for hypersomnia in humans,
although obviously more work would be required to confirm that hypothesis. I would had nevertheless the following
concerns, that would need to be addressed to validate the conclusions of this study: 
-Controlling for the genetic background is essential for this type of study. In most of the figures, the authors show a single
genetic control: the Gal4 driver combined to a UAS thought as non-interfering (UAS-luciferase or UAS-mCherry). First, the
genotype of this control (i.e. either UAS-luciferase or UAS-mCherry) should be mentioned for all the data. Second, the
authors should also show UAS/+ controls data in all the experiments beyond the initial screen shown in figure 1. These
controls are currently only shown in Figure 8, 9, and in supplemental Figure 1. It is understandable to limit the number of
controls when performing a large screen. However it becomes important to include those UAS/+ controls when investigating
gene function in depth. All researchers working on Drosophila sleep know that the genetic background can modify sleep to a
large extend, comparable to the phenotypes of the mutant flies shown in this study, so these controls should be included to
definitely confirm the conclusions. 
-The temporal mapping experiments shown in figure 5 involve raising the temperature to 29°C. The authors should
emphasize the fact that flies switch from diurnal to nocturnal sleep/wake patterns at 29°C, as this influences the
interpretation of the data. This well-known effect of high temperature on sleep makes the day sleep phenotype harder to
detect since there is a ceiling effect during the light phase. Thus as it stands it is not clear that Beat-1a is not required at the
adult stage to regulate sleep. The authors should try alternative strategies to temporally control UAS expression, such as the
GeneSwitch system. 
-In the image shown in Figure 7 concerning the developmental defects associated with Beat-Ia knockdown, the synaptic
input to the dorsal fan-shaped body (dFB) appears to be significantly reduced. Given the prominent role of this structure in
sleep-wake regulation it is surprising that the authors do not show a quantification of the signal for this structure. This should
definitely be included in the study for further consideration about the function of this gene, as it may explain some aspects of
the knockdown phenotype during sleep deprivation. 
Furthermore, a Gal4 expressed in those dFB neurons should have been
included in the targeted spatial knockdown screen shown in figure 6. 
-The circadian clock and sleep homeostasis are the main driving forces in sleep/wake regulation. The authors assess the
circadian parameters of the beat-Ia knockdown flies, but do not show whether those flies have a normal sleep homeostatic
response to sleep deprivation. The authors have performed sleep deprivation experiments and should provide this data to
complete the assessment. 
-To assess sleep quality in the knockdown flies, arousal threshold tests were performed at ZT 13-23 (figure 4B) while in
several of the experiments, the effect of the knockdown is seem during the light phase, so arousal threshold should be
investigated at ZT 1-12 as well. 
-The authors use a UAS-TrpA1 transgene to activate the neurons postsynaptic to NPF positive neurons. The quantification
they use is a bit unusual in that they calculate a 22 to 29°C sleep difference within genotypes instead of doing a straight
comparison between genotype at 22°C and then at 29°C. As we mentioned earlier, sleep is very different at 22°C compared
to 29°C. It looks as if the authors struggled to find an effect. To strengthen the conclusion, the authors could try another of the
several genetic tools used to increase neuronal activity. 
Minor comment: 
In the bar graphs, the data points are frequently almost in the same color as their corresponding bar, which in many cases
prevents the reader to appreciate the spreading of the data inside the bar. I suggest the authors to use more contrasting
colors.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Major Concerns 
1. The idea that synaptic adhesion factors are involved in sleep disorders is not new (Hildebrandt et al., 2009; Park et al.,
2020). The main novelty of this study is its focus on hypersomnia and the suggestion that developmental factors control
neuronal networks involved in wakefulness maintenance. Given the conservation of molecular regulatory mechanisms in
flies, fish and humans, it is not immediately clear how this paper advances our understanding. However, brain structures are
not highly conserved across species and even with the exquisite anatomical detection of fly brains, it is unclear how these
findings can be translated to humans for clinical use. In this sense, the impact of this study may be limited. While Drosophila
and zebrafish models are powerful genetic tools, validation in other mammalian models would increase confidence that the
results have relevance to human sleep disorders. For example, mouse models could be used to validate CADM2 as a
regulator of sleep and to test potential therapeutic interventions. The paper briefly describes the RNAi and CRISPR/Cas9
knockdown but does not elaborate on how off-target effects were controlled. A more thorough discussion of how off-target
effects were controlled would increase confidence in the specificity of the results. For instance, since only the RNAi strategy
was used to reduce beat-Ia levels, a rescue experiment is required. This could be done by generating a UAS line using the
degeneracy of the genetic code, knocking down endogenous beat-Ia while expressing an exogenous beat-Ia for the rescue
experiment.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

2.The organization of data in a scientific manner is not entirely satisfactory. Some behavioral results are also not sufficiently
supported. There is a large amount of variation between control strains in several datasets, which could be due to
background differences. Indeed, it should also be mentioned that temperature changes (such as using tubGAL80ts) can
affect sleep duration in control flies (Lamaze et al., (2017); Jin et al., (2021)). However, this is especially relevant for the
analysis of mutant strains, since sleep in Drosophila has been shown to be highly dependent on genetic background (Cirelli
et al., (2005)). In this regard, it would be informative to know whether any of the authors outcrossed their strains in this study
(this is not mentioned in Methods). In Fig. 6A, the fact that only knockdown of the purple group of beat-Ia RNAi show a
reduction in sleep duration raises questions about whether the current results are affected by genetic background
differences in flies.In the behavioral experiments with zebrafish, a similar problem exists wherein even in the control group,
the differences in sleep duration between Fig. 4 and Fig. 9 are significant. Such findings are challenging to accept as
convincing. 
3. The results indicating statistical insignificance are often inadequately labeled and explained within the figure legends. It is
imperative to explicitly state instances where statistical differences are absent to maintain transparency and ensure accurate
data interpretation. It would be beneficial to include the appropriate statistical analyses to substantiate the conclusion of "no
change" in Fig. 4D-E, thereby enhancing the robustness of your findings. Some of the statements in the manuscript are not
supported by statistical evidence. For example, lines 139-140, “Excess sleep in beat-Ia knockdown flies was driven by an
increase in sleep bout length, suggestive of more consolidated sleep” could be supported with statistical evidence. 
3. The temporal mapping experiments, restricted to pre-eclosion and post-eclosion stages, are too broad. Given the pre-adult
lethality of beat-Ia null mutants, the authors' conclusions can be largely predicted within the current framework. Fruit flies
have essential embryonic, larval, and pupal stages before eclosion, but the authors' results do not provide sufficient
resolution to fully understand the developmental process affected by beat-Ia knockdown. Since this is a developmental
defect, it is important to know when this defect occurs. The authors propose that the functions of beat-Ia and its receptor side
are transiently active during the mid-pupal stage. Consequently, it raises a question: would inactivating GAL80 solely during
the pupal stage be sufficient to induce defects? If not, could there be a possibility for beat-Ia and side to be expressed and
active at earlier stages of development? 
4. I am not convinced that only the suboesophageal zone (SEZ) is specifically responsible for hypersomnia since the
authors do not seem to fully understand the expression pattern of beat-Ia. They cannot exclude the possibility that beat-Ia
may function in other areas labeled by the NPF-GAL4 line. I noticed that the fan-shaped body and other superdorsal areas
also showed a loss of synaptic density following knockdown using NPF-GAL4. Furthermore, the phenotype in downstream
neurons labeled by R10G06-GAL4 is not as strong as in NPF-GAL4, which indicates that the SEZ may not be the only
region regulating this phenotype—other brain areas might also be involved. Therefore, the authors should remain open to
other possibilities and reword their conclusions accordingly or provide more solid evidence to support their claims. 
5. In Fig. 7B, the data show a marked reduction in synaptic density not only in the SEZ but also in the fan-shaped body. The
fan-shaped body seems to be more related to sleep than the SEZ. Further explanation and additional experiments are
required to understand the function of the fan-shaped body.

Reviewer #1

In this resubmission, Mace et al. have added valuable control experiments (e.g., parental controls, controls for temperature
effects on sleep) to improve data quality. They clearly demonstrate that loss of beat-Ia or its vertebrate homologue cadm2b
induces a sleep propensity phenotype. However, the manuscript still fails to address the core concern previously raised by
me and other reviewers: whether beat-Ia specifically regulates sleep through its function in NPF SEZ neurons. 
Several observations suggest that beat-Ia knockdown has presynaptic defects broadly in the brain, not NPF SEZ neuron-
specific, to affect adult sleep:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

Representative images (Fig 7B) indicate a brain-wide reduction in syt-GFP signals upon beat-Ia knockdown, implying
widespread synaptic developmental defects. 
The authors removed data on side expression in the SEZ and acknowledged its broad expression pattern. Given that side
encodes a receptor for beat-Ia, this strongly suggests that beat-Ia also acts broadly to regulate presynaptic bouton formation. 
Knocking down beat-Ia in cholinergic neurons (the primary excitatory synapses in the insect brain) produced the most
significant sleep effect, further supporting a general role. 
To rigorously test whether beat-Ia functions specifically in NPF neurons to regulate adult sleep, a rescue experiment is
essential. The authors should check the sleep in 
NPF-Gal4 > UAS-beat-Ia cDNA; elav-Gal4 > UAS-beat-Ia RNAi flies 
This would determine if restoring beat-Ia expression only in NPF neurons rescues the sleep phenotype caused by pan-
neuronal knockdown. The authors already have all the transgenic lines for this experiment (similar to Fig S3). 
Having said that, even if beat-Ia acts broadly during development to influence adult sleep, its role in modulating "sleep need"
at the cellular level remains significant, and I think it is more interesting to study. While identifying another "sleep/wake
circuit" is useful for translational research, elucidating how local synaptic changes translate to sleep need would offer
deeper mechanistic insight into sleep itself.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have adequately addressed several concerns raised by the other reviewers and myself. However, I remain
deeply concerned by the absence of appropriate genetic controls, particularly the UAS/+ conditions, in the results presented

mCherry is also adding a change in the genetic background not present in the experimental Gal4 > UAS condition. As I
emphasized in my initial review, it is well recognized within the Drosophila sleep research community that genetic
background can significantly influence behavioral phenotypes and must be rigorously controlled. 
Other point: in the newly added Supplemental Figure 9, why do the authors use nsyb-GeneSwitch and not elav-GeneSwitch,
which would appear to more logical to match the experiments with elav-Gal4?

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
The manuscript by Mace et al. investigates the genetic and molecular origins of 
idiopathic hypersomnia (IH) through a cross-species approach using Drosophila and 
zebrafish models, informed by human genomics data. The study identifies the synaptic 
adhesion molecules beat-Ia in Drosophila and its vertebrate ortholog CADM2 as key

These findings are corroborated in zebrafish, where CADM2 disruption similarly 
increases sleep, and treatment with an NPY receptor agonist rescues this phenotype, 
suggesting a potential therapeutic target for IH. This is an important paper for identifying 
an evolutionarily conserved signaling pathway regulating sleep. 
 
We thank the reviewer for the supportive comments

1. The evidence that beat-Ia acts in NPF SEZ neurons to regulate sleep/wakefulness is

We agree with these important points and have addressed each of them. 
a. We re-analyzed all Syt-GFP data, now rigorously quantifying number and size of 
synaptic puncta. We do not detect an overall difference throughout the whole 
brain, while we do find a reduction in puncta size in the SEZ region, consistent 
with our initial observations. We also describe brain to brain variability in puncta 
of the dorsal fan shaped body (dFB). As noted by all Reviewers and described in 
more detail below, we extensively explored a role for the dFB in our sleep 
phenotypes and found no evidence for a role of this region. We now include this 
negative data in the revised submission. 
b. Using the FlyWire connectome and BrainCircuits.io we identified a split-GAL4 
driver specific for the NPF SEZ neurons, as suggested. Thermogenetic activation 
of these neurons indeed promotes arousal during the day (see new

Removing the side expression data does not alter the conclusions of the manuscript.
