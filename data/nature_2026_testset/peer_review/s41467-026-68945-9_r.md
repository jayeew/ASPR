Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Kan et al. conduct a fairly comprehensive Earth system modelling (ESM)-based experiment to assess the
impact of global forestation on runoff. For this, in addition to a control simulation, they run the ESMs with the tree restoration
potential map from Bastin et al. 2019. They combine the bottoms-up modelling with a simple top-down Budyko framework to
partition the drivers of runoff changes among atmospheric and land processes. While the paper is well-written and the
experiment is logically designed, I have some reservations about the use of the Bastin et al. dataset and the over-
interpretation of the Budyko framework. As a result, I suggest major revisions before the manuscript can be considered for
publication in Nature Communication. I detail my concerns below. 
Major Comments
 
1. Although I understand to some extent the logic behind using the Bastin et al. 2019 data, I am not sure if it represents the
“maximum potential” as the authors describe. Most criticisms of the dataset make it clear that the tree restoration potential
has been vastly overestimated. Hence, this cannot be considered as the potential. I request the authors to motivate the use
of this dataset better. One possible option is to do the analysis for various degrees of restoration potentials but I am not sure
how computationally feasible this option is. If not, this represents a weakness in the experiment design and the results are
not exactly a realistic scenario, which the authors are aiming for. 
2. Another source of uncertainty and one that is fully acknowledged in the study is the issue of fixed rooting depths
prescribed in the ESMs. Even if the rooting depths show inter-pft variability, there is large variance intra-pft and this would
cause a lot of spatial variability in soil water uptake. I acknowledge that this is not something that can be easily addressed
but should, ideally be discussed. For example, In line 337, the authors say that ET is enhanced but with shallower root
depths, the vegetation might not have the capacity to access potentially depleted soil moisture. 
3. This is not exactly a suggestion but more of an observation: it is interesting that the runoff increases in the tropics and
decreases in the boreal regions. As the boreal regions are fairly humid, it would be interesting to see if they go past a key
aridity threshold (humid to dry-subhumid for example). 
4. Much of the results and the consequent conclusions depend on how good the hydrological model within ORCHIDEE is?
Considering that LSMs are not intended for hydrological modelling, the authors should provide more details on how the
runoff 
from the model is validated, and to what degree can they be trusted? In this regard the manuscript is very light on the
details. 
5. One of the biggest advantages of the study is the fully coupled model which simulates the remote effects of local
forestation, and which is very nicely shown in the study. However, the authors predominantly focus on moisture recycling.
How about the impact of forestation on albedo changes and potential subsequent changes in the atmospheric circulation?
Does the increased atmospheric moisture affect the stability of the atmosphere, thus affecting precipitation? In this regard, I
find the discussion of the results very sparse and superficial. 
6.Calling the Budyko parameter as “water retention capacity” is not exactly correct. The partitioning of precipitation into
evaporation is not only because more water is stored in the watershed but also due to lateral flow out of the watershed. The
parameter is more an integral of all the factors that could not be explained by the original non-parametric Budyko hypothesis.

was not originally intended to be used at grid-cell level but more at large catchment-scale, and at very long-term timescales
(greater than 20–30 years). While a lot of studies have over-used the framework, I am not exactly sure how to suggest
improvements here but at the very least, these limitations have to be acknowledged. For example, line 215: The applicability
of the hypothesis in cold regions is questionable. 
8. Line 163: 80% seems a bit misleading in arid or semi-arid ecosystems considering the . Maybe also mentioning the
absolute values is a bit more transparent. 
9. Line 287: The average numbers are quite small (near-zero). Please show the standard deviation (or the spread). 
10. Line 348 – 357: Seems like this is a summary of the results but not discussion.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Fei Kan et al. addresses an important and timely topic: how global forestation influences runoff, with a
particular focus on latitudinal divergence driven by forest-climate feedbacks. Using coupled land-atmosphere models and
the Budyko framework, the authors find that forestation leads to increased runoff in tropical regions but reduced runoff in
boreal regions. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating hydrological considerations into afforestation
strategies aimed at climate mitigation. The manuscript is well-written and the technical details are carefully presented.
However, I have several concerns regarding model uncertainty and methodological robustness that should be addressed
before the manuscript can be considered for publication in Nature Communications. 
This study bears strong resemblance to previous work on deforestation, particularly in terms of methodology and key
findings. While the authors conduct additional analyses, the central conclusion—emphasizing the dominant role of forest-
climate feedbacks and regional divergence—is not entirely novel. Beyond these points, are there any additional innovative
insights that distinguish this study from prior literature? 
Ma, S., Zhou, S., Yu, B. & Song, J. Deforestation-induced runoff changes dominated by forest-climate feedbacks. Science
Advances 10, eadp3964 (2024) 
The authors adopt a “full-potential forestation” scenario. Is this scenario similarly idealized as those used in earlier studies?
How realistic is it under current and future climate and land-use constraints? Could comparable forestation pathways
realistically occur in future scenarios? A critical issue is the reliability and accuracy of the simulated runoff responses. To
what extent have these runoff outputs been validated against observations? If observational constraints are limited, how
confident can we be in the attribution results derived from these model simulations? 
The study primarily relies on IPSL-CM, with some additional analyses using CESM, to simulate forestation effects.
Dependence on one or two models raises concerns about the robustness of the findings, especially given the relatively
coarse spatial resolution of IPSL-CM. Prior studies have demonstrated substantial inter-model variability in forestation-
induced changes in the water cycle: some models emphasize moisture recycling, while others highlight atmospheric
circulation adjustments. Even within CMIP6 LUMIP experiments, large discrepancies exist among models under
comparable scenarios. I recommend integrating CESM results more prominently into the main text and discussing model
uncertainty in greater detail. It is difficult to place confidence in results derived primarily from a single model. 
De Hertog SJ, et al. Effects of idealized land cover and land management changes on the atmospheric water cycle. Earth
Syst Dynam 15, 265-291 (2024) 
Portmann, R. et al. Global forestation and deforestation affect remote climate via adjusted atmosphere and ocean circulation.
Nat Commun 13, 5569 (2022). 
Lawrence DM, et al. The Land Use Model Intercomparison Project (LUMIP) contribution to CMIP6: rationale and
experimental design. Geosci Model Dev 9, 2973-2998 (2016). 
Lines 37–47: This section emphasizes carbon benefits, whereas the focus of this study is on the hydrological cycle. Please
consider rebalancing the discussion to align with the core research objective.

The Budyko attribution approach is applied, yet the introduction lacks any description or justification of this method. 
Several statements in the introduction are not original contributions and should be supported with appropriate citations. 
The introduction discusses forestation-runoff relationships extensively but provides limited context on latitudinal divergence,
which is central to this study. 
The results section describes percentage changes, but some figures appear to display absolute numerical changes. Please
clarify this inconsistency. 
Figure 1 reveals several unexpected findings that warrant more careful discussion. For example, precipitation changes
exceed evapotranspiration changes in magnitude. This suggests that large-scale atmospheric circulation shifts and
cascading moisture recycling effects may play a substantial role. In Southeast Asia, minor changes in evapotranspiration
appear to trigger pronounced precipitation increases. However, these phenomena are not reproduced by CESM,
highlighting the model dependence of the results. Supplementary Figure 4 statistics should be incorporated into the main
text to strengthen the argument. Does CESM support the key findings of IPSL-CM simulations? The results for warm regions
appear to differ considerably. The results for China in Figure 1d and Supplementary Figure 4d seem inconsistent with prior
studies. Have the authors compared their findings with recent global forestation-water cycle simulations? There appear to be
notable discrepancies. 
Li, Y. et al. Divergent hydrological response to large-scale afforestation and vegetation greening in China. Sci. Adv. 4,
eaar4182 (2018) 
King JA, Weber J, Lawrence P, Roe S, Swann ALS, Val Martin M. Global and regional hydrological impacts of global forest
expansion. Biogeosciences 21, 3883-3902 (2024). 
The use of the Penman-Monteith model for PET calculation could introduce potential biases. Recent studies suggest that
the Penman equation overestimates PET because the assumption of a saturated surface is not satisfied over most land
surface. I recommend considering the energy balance approach (PETe) instead. 
Zhou, S. & Yu, B. Reconciling the Discrepancy in Projected Global Dryland Expansion in a Warming World. Glob Change
Biol, 31: e70102 (2025). 
Zhou, S., & Yu, B. Physical basis of the potential evapotranspiration and its estimation over land. Journal of Hydrology, 641,
131825 (2024). 
Additionally, how have the authors accounted for internal climate variability in the Budyko attribution? 
The Budyko attribution approach primarily considers first-order controls (mean climate and land surface characteristics) on
runoff. Yet, forestation-induced changes in precipitation characteristics (e.g., rainfall intensity, fraction of precipitation as
snow) may substantially alter runoff generation processes and soil moisture availability for evapotranspiration. 
Finally, can the authors provide observational evidence to support their model-based findings? Robustness checks relying
solely on model-derived data are less persuasive; validation against independent observations is critical to establish
confidence.

[Response] We appreciate your constructive comment regarding the potential 
overestimation of actual afforestation potential by using the Bastin’s dataset1. To 
address this important concern and better motivate the use of the dataset, we have set 
up three potential forestation scenarios based on the Bastin’s dataset: 
 
The first scenario is the full-potential forestation scenario (Bastin_Full), which 
assumes planting trees to reach the land’s theoretical maximum carrying capacity

under current climate conditions, representing the idealized maximum afforestation 
potential.  
 
The second scenario, Bastin_Refined_Climate, is based upon Bastin_Full but 
excludes areas with frequent fire and regions with additional climate constraints due 
to forest-induced albedo changes. This scenario addresses two major critiques of the 
Bastin’s data: the overestimation of tree cover potential in grassland and savanna 
ecosystems with frequent fire, and the modeling of new tree cover in water-limited 
regions where trees reduce albedo and induce net climate warming2–4. Applying these 
constraints lead to a lower estimated afforestation potential, particularly in tropical 
grassland and savanna ecosystems across Africa (Fig. R1b).  
 
Furthermore, the third scenario, Bastin_Refined_Climate&Land, is an extended 
version of Bastin_RefinedClimate that additionally excludes croplands, built-up lands 
and wetlands. This represents the most feasible forestation scenario. We excluded 
urban and agricultural areas due to their critical importance for human sustenance and 
socio-economic activities. We applied the wetland precautionary exclusions to protect 
large soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks that may be vulnerable to losses following the 
establishment of trees5,6. Compared with the Bastin_Refined_Climate scenario, the 
further reduction in afforestation area in Bastin_Refined_Climate&Land 
predominantly occurs in cropland-dominated regions like Europe, African croplands, 
Southeast Asia, and eastern South America (Fig. R1c).

“Two sensitivity experiments with constrained forestation

thus, our full-potential forestation scenario (Bastin_Full) represents the upper bound 
of global forestation potential. In this regard, we designed two additional scenarios 
(Bastin_Refined_Climate and Bastin_Refined_Climate&Land) by progressively

Bastin_Refined_Climate builds upon Bastin_Full by excluding regions with frequent 
fires and where forestation would produce net warming due to reduced surface 
albedo. This refinement addresses two major critiques of the Bastin et al.’ s dataset: 
overestimated potential in fire-prone grassland and savanna ecosystems, and the 
neglect of forestation-caused regional warming due to reduced albedo65,66. Following 
Fesenmyer et al.64, we used the 500 m MODIS Burned Area dataset to remove all non-
cropland areas experiencing two or more fires during 2002–2022, assuming that such 
frequencies substantially limit tree density114. We further excluded regions where 
albedo-induced radiative warming exceeds the cooling from carbon sequestration of 
potential forestation, based on the dataset of Halser et al.115. These constraints 
markedly reduce the estimated afforestation potential in tropical grassland and 
savanna ecosystems, particularly across Africa (Supplementary Fig. 1b). 
 
Bastin_Refined_Climate&Land incorporated additional constraints on 
Bastin_Refined_Climate by excluding croplands, built-up areas, and wetlands to 
ensure land-use sustainability and feasibility. Urban and agricultural areas, given 
their essential roles in food production and human livelihoods, were masked out using 
the ESA CCI Global Land Cover dataset for 2000. Wetlands were excluded using the 
Global Lakes and Wetlands Database (GLWD, version 2) to prevent potential losses 
of large soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks following afforestation. Compared with the 
Bastin_Refined_Climate scenario, the further reduction in forestation potential in 
Bastin_Refined_Climate&Land predominantly occurs in cropland-dominated regions 
like Europe, African croplands, Southeast Asia, and eastern South America 
(Supplementary Fig. 1c).” (Lines 627-657).

Bastin_Refined_Climate, reduced forestation area over tropical grasslands and 
savannas leads to less strong tropical runoff increases. In 
Bastin_Refined_Climate&Land, the further exclusion of cropland and urban areas 
weakens, but not reverses, the decrease runoff in some high-latitude regions. We have

We conducted a validation of the simulated runoff against observed streamflow data

Supplementary Texts, we provide details on this streamflow database and describe the

direct human interventions (e.g., dam construction, irrigation), we screened the

streamflow; (5) Only catchments for which four homogeneity tests (i.e., the standard

no break-point in the time series at the 1% significance level were selected. The 
catchmental irrigation area and reservoir capacity, and quality control for catchment 
boundaries, are all derived from the GSIM metadata.

with vegetation responses to climate. Many observation-based studies instead used the 
space-for-time substitution to quantify potential climate feedbacks of land cover

atmospheric backgrounds. This assumption neglects the influence of forest change on 
atmospheric circulation, causing remote hydrological responses by redistributing

moisture and changing precipitation patterns. Given such limitations of conventional 
observation-based methods, there is no ground truth for validating our modelled 
hydrological feedbacks. 
 
Nonetheless, for regions where precipitation sources largely from local ET (i.e., with 
strong local moisture recycling), the signature of forest-atmosphere feedbacks might 
be detectable in observational data. To explore this possibility, we analyzed the spatial 
sensitivity of surface moisture balance (P minus ET) to tree cover. In this analysis, we 
employed precipitation from the Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation

forest cover fraction. The difference between precipitation and ET sensitivities
