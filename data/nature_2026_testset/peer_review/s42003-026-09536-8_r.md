Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
SUMMARY: 
The article provides a detailed description of sulci development from 0 to 1 year after birth, across 4 timepoints. The study is
well justified by the lack of any previous analysis that takes into account the sulci variability and by the relevance of defining
normative models of their development to better understand any possible deviation from it. The analysis of 12 primary sulci
and their characterization through 5 metrics (both macrostructural and microstructural) provide a detailed account of sulcal
emergence, considering their spatial distribution and heterogeneity. Furthermore, it introduces a model linking sulcal depth
with other sulcal features, offering new insights into the complex dynamics of early cortical folding. 
This study is per se interesting and offers a rigorous analysis of sulci development. Nevertheless, some relevant and
preliminary key points need to be further clarified in both the Methods and Discussion sections to strengthen the scientific
impact of the work. Particularly, one notable limitation is the reliance on an adult brain atlas, which raises concerns given the
known anatomical differences between adult and infant brains. Although some justification is provided, it would be important
to consider whether alternative approaches using infant-specific atlases could have yielded more accurate or relevant
insights. Also the rationale behind the choice of the sulci analysed in this work should be more clearly justified: some sulci
are not uniquely defined in neuroanatomy, while others could have been considered (e.g., cingulate sulcus, precentral
sulcus, postcentral sulcus). Additionally, the inclusion of the role of white matter tracts in the cortical folding process could
further enhance the work, especially given the growing body of supporting literature. 
ABSTRACT: 
1. The abstract relies primarily on qualitative descriptions and would benefit from the inclusion of key quantitative results
(e.g., sample size, depth measurements, or growth rates over time for relevant sulci) along with a few core statistics. This
would help enhance its scientific rigor and clarity. 
2. The abstract mentions the ‘microstructural density’ without describing it. The reader could benefit from a simplified
explanation of its meaning in the context of the study. 
3. How do these findings inform our understanding of neurodevelopmental disorders and delays? This point should be
clarified, especially considering that no pathological population was analyzed in the present study.

This work investigates the postnatal development of cortical sulci in infants from birth to one year of age using a variety of

analytical techniques. The study, conducted with longitudinal infant MRI data, examines major sulci that emerge in utero.
Findings show sulci emerging earlier in utero are deeper at birth but deepen at a slower rate postnatally, while later-
emerging sulci are shallower at birth but deepen more rapidly. Sulcal depth, accompanied by sulcal span, cortical thickness,
and tissue density increase over the first year, while curvature decreases. They use linear mixed models to demonstrate how
sulcal depth can be predicted by a weighted combination of the aforementioned metrics, with varying contributions across
sulci. The paper makes important observations about changes in cortical structure, but several issues with the methods and
some of the conclusions will require revisions before publication. 
Major concerns: 
1. Issues with methods - missing or incorrect info

1b. Cortical thickness is said to be calculated by FreeSurfer, but the description in the methods does not match the method

1e. How were the sulci manually labeled?

2b. Was the averaging for sulcal profiles done along global XYZ coordinates or within a sulcus-aligned local coordinate

2c. Was the profile analysis only conducted on two sulci?

5. Multiple different LMMs are introduced but not well justified - can the authors explain why each model was used? 
6. The authors suggest that a decrease in curvature could result from either sulcal widening or wall stretching.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study aimed to investigate several parameters of developing sulci in infant brains using MRI, which have not been
investigated in depth before, and also not been investigated using a longitudinal sample. Sulcal depth, span, curvature,
cortical thickness, and tissue density were measured for 12 sulci across the cerebrum. Sulcal span, curvature, cortical
thickness and tissue density were assessed for their potential relationship with sulcal depth. The study used a combination
of longitudinal and cross-sectional data, and used a combination of automatic and manual segmentation techniques to
produce maps of the infant brains from which the measurements were obtained per sulcus, per hemisphere and per infant
across several timepoints within the first postnatal year. They concluded that sulcal depth at birth and speed of postnatal
development were associated with the timing of the emergence of the sulcus in utero. Other measures were not correlated
with timing of sulcal emergence, but together were capable of predicting sulcal depth. They also investigated smaller-scale
changes in sulcal depth and determined that other variables such as tissue density and curvature may be linked to more
local sulcal depth, possibly with functional consequences for the development of cortical folding, and implications for future
studies in this field. 
I found this paper to be very clearly explained and well laid out. The abstract was very readable and summarised the paper
well. The subheadings in the results and discussion section in particular made these sections very clear and it was easy to
follow each step of the research process. All of the figures and tables were very helpful – I particularly appreciated the
schematic-type illustrations (Figure 1 and Figure 4a) in visualising the various sulcal changes. I particularly appreciated that
the accuracy of the use of an adult freesurfer brain file for infant brains was investigated and its suitability was confirmed, as
if this had not been checked it could have had an influence on the reliability of the results. 
Minor comments:

2. Line 146 – may be missing the word “infants” here. 
3. Line 677 – Fig 2b is referenced. This should be Fig 2c.

…” 
5. Supplementary Figure 1 – in the legend you refer to “baby”. For consistency across the manuscript, this could be changed
to “infant”.
