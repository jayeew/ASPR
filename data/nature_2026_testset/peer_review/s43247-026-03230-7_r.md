Response letter The following is the point-to-point response to all the comments. The page and line numbers in the following response refer to the revised manuscript with track changes.  Response to Referee #1: The authors seek to provide insight into the causes of an hydrogen isotope offset between measured xylem water and potential water sources for the plant. The authors make the assertion that if the correct water pools are measured with methods that don’t induce fractionation, then no offset will occur. They propose the correct pools to measure are mobile xylem water contained in the xylem conduits only and soil water sources held between field capacity and the wilting point. To facilitate making this point they develop a framework with 5 pools of water that are often combined when making soil water and xylem water isotopic measurements, and implicate this combining of pools as one reason for the offset. They bring in a meta-analysis of as many ecohydrologic studies as they can find that measure xylem water and sufficient potential plant water sources, and try to group them based on the pools (and combinations of pools) that were measured. They also retroactively try to calculate the potential Plant-source water d2H offset for plants within each study using a linear regression approach, calculating a dual isotope line for potential water sources, then calculating the d2H offset between the line and the plant. In the end, they make some recommendations on extraction methods that could get at the particular pools they recommend be measured.  While I applauded the authors for pulling together this extensive dataset, and their conceptualization, I find many problems they need to consider. In the end for a variety of reasons I hope I explain, I’m not sure that this framework and analysis advances the science behind using stable isotopes to understand water sources used by plants.  First, they say we urgently need to standardize methods for water extraction, and recommend a centrifuge technique for extracting soil water. However, they do not identify any study in their meta-analysis that uses this technique (Table 1). How can they recommend standardizing a technique that has not been used in their analysis? We agree with the reviewer that water uptake by plants follows a continuum along a soil water potential gradient and that plants tend to access water with higher potentials first, progressively drawing on more tightly held water as the soil dries. We also agree that this process is dynamic and cannot be rigidly reduced to fixed potential thresholds without consideration of physiological context (e.g., midday plant water potential). Rather, our aim was to provide a more nuanced and operationally useful conceptual model to highlight how mixing isotopically distinct pools—especially mobile (field-drainage) and hygroscopic water—can confound plant–source water comparisons. We did not intend to devalue earlier conceptual models but to offer an alternative lens that better aligns with isotopic dynamics during soil drying and methodological constraints of current extraction techniques.

In response to the reviewer’ s important point about including water above field capacity as part of the plant-accessible pool, we respectfully disagree. While we agree that plant water uptake operates along a continuum of soil water potential, numerous field and laboratory studies have shown that gravitational water (typically held at soil water potentials > −10 kPa) is highly transient and generally drains quickly following precipitation events, especially in well-structured or coarser-textured soils (e.g., Brooks et al., 2010; Sprenger et al., 2018a). This rapid drainage limits the temporal window during which plants can access this water pool. Moreover, the physical location of gravitational water, often in larger pores not in direct contact with root surfaces, further reduces its accessibility (Cowan, 1965; Jarvis, 2007). Landwehr and Coplen (2006)  proposed a concept of line -condition excess (lc -excess) to

lc-excess is defined as follows:  
lc-excess = δ2H – aδ18O − b ,                                    (1)  
where a and b represented the slope and intercept of LMWL. δ2H and δ18O in Eq. (1) were isotopic 
compositions of river water samples.

excess concept, Barbeta et al. (2019)  proposed using soil water line (SWL) and SW -excess to 
quantify the δ2H offset between plants and their source waters. SW-excess is defined as, 
 
SW-excess = δ2H – asδ18O − bs ,                                ( 2) 
where as and bs represented the slope and intercept of SWL. 
SW-excess provides a consistent metric to identify the δ2H offset between plants and their

of these critical sources. To compensate for the shortcoming, a newer metric, potential water excess 
(PW-excess), is proposed to quantify the δ2H offsets (Li et al., 2021) . This metric builds on the 
concept of SW -excess but also accounts for the variety of  other sources plants use. This metric 
considers all potential water sources accessible to plants in an ecosystem, such as soil water at 
different depths, groundwater, and other known water sources (e.g., rock moisture, fog). It is defined 
as, 
PW-excess = δ2H – apδ18O − bp ,                                ( 3) 
where ap and bp are the slope and intercept of the potential water source line (PWL).

isotope offsets between plant tissue waters and source waters because PWL considers more possible 
water sources that plants use than SWL. Despite the obvious advantages of PWL over SWL, a global 
comprehensive synthesis of the PWL-based isotope offset estimation is still lacking.
