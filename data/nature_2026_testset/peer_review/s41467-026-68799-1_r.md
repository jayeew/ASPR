Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This review report regards the manuscript “Ultrabright Multicolor X-ray Persistent Luminescence Enabled by Gd-mediated
Trap Clusters” submitted to Nature Communications. The manuscript presents an investigation into the use of Gd as a
bridging element to connect charge-trapping centers in BaFCl to luminescent centers. The central idea - that introducing Gd
creates clusters of defects and luminescent centers which can universally enhance persistent luminescence - is interesting
and of potential importance to the field. Finally BaFCl:Eu/Gd is combined with perovskite quantum dots to obtain persistent
luminescence over a wide range of colors and the materials is also tested for X-ray imaging applications. 
However, despite the promise of the concept, the manuscript suffers from several critical shortcomings in execution and
presentation. The work lacks focus, the conclusions are not consistently supported by experimental data, and there is a lack
of clarity in the methodology used to evaluate luminescence intensities which raises doubts about the reported intensities
(one of the more interesting results of the manuscript). These concerns raise doubts about the reliability of the results and
prevent me from recommending publication in its current form. Below I grouped the most important concerns per theme. 
Concerns regarding the proposed mechanism: 
1. Throughout the manuscript there is an ambiguity regarding the processes of charge transfer and energy transfer. There is
no clear distinction between the part of the process driven by charge transfer (trapping and detrapping) and the part related
to energy transfer (between Gd3+ ions and from Gd3+ to the activator). It is unclear what the recombination center in this
material is. 
2. It is proposed that Frenkel defects play an important role in the persistent luminescence mechanism. For this the authors
refer to references 7 and 33. In reference 7 the involvement of Frenkel defects is implied based on DFT calculations but
notably no Frenkel defects are observed in EPR. Similar conclusions can be drawn from reference 33, so the statement is
not supported by any experimental evidence. 
3. The mechanism proposed in Figure S22 implies the simultaneous detrapping of both electron and holes and subsequent
recombination at Gd3+. Based on results from numerous other persistent phosphor, it is highly improbable that this takes
place at the same temperature. 
4. Monte Carlo simulations are included in the manuscript but they are not coupled to any of the experimental results. The
only conclusion that follows from the ‘simulation’ is that the energy transfer is more probable is the donor and acceptor are
closer together which is a well known experimental observation. 
Experimental details: 
There is a lot of information in the supplementary but key information is missing. 
1. How were persistent luminescence intensities in cd/m² obtained? Given that the intensity of persistent luminescence is
one of the ‘selling points’ of the manuscript this information is important. 
2. What was the excitation intensity or wavelength during quantum yield measurements? The quantum yield of persistent
phosphors is known to be intensity dependent so this is important information. 
3. How were DFT calculations performed? DFT is used a lot throughout the manuscript but very little information about the
calculations or the results is available. Instead only the interpretation of the results is included in the manuscript. 
4. The stability of several materials in ‘air’ is recorded over 60 days ‘under repeated irradiation at one-day intervals’.
SrAl2O4 is stable in air so presumably the decrease in intensity is due to radiation damage but there is no way to tell due to
the lack of details in the manuscript. 
Quantification of persistent luminescence intensities and comparison to benchmarks: 
1. The results of persistent luminescence intensities are scattered over several figures but none of the figures have tics or
numbers on the y-axis so it is impossible to compare intensities. I would have liked to see the afterglow and TL of the Eu,
Gd, Eu/Gd and Eu/Lu sample in one figure with comparable intensities. 
2. To quantify the performance of the BaFCl:Eu/Gd the material’s persistent luminescence is compared to other persistent
luminescent materials such as SrAl2O4:Eu,Dy, CaAl2O4:Eu,Nd, … However, the comparison is flawed because these
materials, benchmarks for UV/VIS excited persistent luminescence, contain much lighter elements so the X-ray attenuation
will be lower. The initial intensity for commercial SrAl2O4:Eu,Dy powder after excitation with UV light is in the order of 10
cd/m² which is higher than the intensity reported here for BaFCl:Eu/Gd. 
3. The quantum yield is reported under UV excitation and the values obtained for SrAl2O4:Eu,Dy and BaFCl:Eu/Gd are
compared to each other. This is not a fair comparison as SrAl2O4:Eu,Dy exhibits trapping and detrapping under UV
excitation while BaFCl:Eu/Gd does not. 
4. For some of the BaFCl compositions the initial intensities (but not the decay curves) are reported in cd/m², a photometric
unit taking into account the eye sensitivity. However, the emission of BaFCl:Eu,Gd is almost entirely (>80%) located under
400 nm where the eye sensitivity is zero (< 0.01). This raises a question as to how this can give rise to such a high intensity
of 4.53 cd/m². Especially considering that SrAl2O4:Eu,Dy which has an emission that almost perfectly coincides with
maximum eye sensitivity (~1) has an emission intensity of at most 10 cd/m² and is only 5 times weaker than the
BaFCl:Eu/Gd phosphor (according to 2f). 
5. Afterglow times up to 168 hours are reported but this is highly dependent on the sensitivity of the equipment used to
record the persistent luminescence. It is standard practice to report the time needed to reach a certain threshold intensity or
at least report the intensity of the light after the stated persistent luminescence time (and the integration time used after
168h). 
6. In the conclusions it is stressed that the Sm2+ intensity is increased by a factor 150.3. However, in Figure 5 the red
luminescence isn’t visible anymore after 120 seconds. This raises more questions about how the results are presented in
the manuscript.

are normalized so there is no information about how intense the luminescence of the quantum dots is in the end. Also, how
efficient is the light from the persistent luminescence of BaFCl:Eu/Gd converted to visible quantum dot emission?

Previous studies have shown that shallow traps with depths around 0.69 eV are 
optimal for room-temperature PersL emission, whereas deeper traps (>0.9 eV) mainly 
contribute to photostimulated luminescence under optical excitation [Adv. Mater. 
2020, 32, 2003881; Chem. Sci. 2019, 10, 5452 –5460; Matter 2023, 6, 4261 –4273]. 
Liu et al. further demonstrated that short -range displ acements of fluoride ions 
generate transient shallow traps, while long-range displacements form long-lived deep 
traps. Electrons in shallow traps can spontaneously escape to the conduction band 
over time, accompanied by defect self-healing, whereas electrons in deep traps require 
external optical or thermal stimulation to migrate to luminescent centers (Nature 2021, 
590, 410–415). 
Therefore, we selectively analyzed the TL spectra within the 300–400 K range to 
highlight the contribution of shallow traps that  dominate PersL behavior at room 
temperature. According to the reviewer’s helpful com ment, Fig. S20 has been revised 
accordingly, and the following clarification has been added to the revised manuscript:
“The deep-trap population is markedly reduced in Gd/E u co-doped BaFCl compared 
with Eu-doped BaFCl, and the corresponding trap depth increases from 0.9 eV to 0.95 
eV , indicating that the bright PersL observed at room temperature is predominantly 
governed by shallow traps rather than deep ones.”

Electrons sto red in shallow traps may be thermally released to the conduction 
band and subsequently captured by luminescent centers, or directly transferred to 
luminescent centers through quantum tunneling. Dellbecq et al. reported that, in 
KCl:AgCl and KCl:TlCl system s, the PersL decay resulting from tunneling 
recombination between trapped holes and electrons follows a linear dependence of 
luminescence intensity (I) on the inverse of time ( t-1) [J. Phys. Chem. C 2009, 113, 
20493]. This t-1 decay behavior has since been  widely recognized as a characteristic 
signature of tunneling -assisted recombination in persistent phosphors [Phys. Rev. B 
1974, 9, 4497; Nat. Mater. 2012, 11, 58; J. Alloys Compd. 2010, 504, 177]. Following 
these precedents, we analyzed the PersL decay pr ofiles of BaFCl:Eu, BaFCl:Eu/Gd, 
and BaFCl:Gd at room temperature, as well as the PersL spectra of BaFCl:Gd at 
different temperatures (89, 129, 169, 209, 249K), to evaluate the possible contribution 
of tunn eling. As shown in Fig. R 2a–c, the PersL intensity  of all examined samples 
does not exhibit a linear correlation with t-1, indicating that quantum tunneling is not 
the dominant recombination mechanism in our system. Moreover, as the temperature 
increases from 89 K to 249 K, the PersL intensity rises signi ficantly, suggesting that 
the release of trapped electrons is predominantly thermally activated rather than 
tunneling-assisted.

Response 4: Thanks for the reviewer’s kind comment. Liu et al. (Nature 2021, 590, 
410–415) reported that “through elastic collisions between high -momentum X -ray 
photons and light fluoride ions, anion Frenkel defects  are generated within the crystal 
lattice, which can trap thermalized low-energy electrons and enable long-lived photon 
storage. Specifically, fluoride vacancies and interstitials act as electron and hole traps, 
respectively. Displacements of fluoride ions  over short and long distances lead to the 
formation of transient shallow and long -lived deep trap states, respectively.”  In this 
context, the introduction of different activators does not alter the intrinsic nature of 
these traps but instead modulates their depths and distributions. As shown in Fig. S18, 
the deep -trap population is markedly reduced in Gd/Eu co -doped BaFCl compared 
with Eu -doped BaFCl, while the corresponding trap depth increases from 0.90 eV 
(450/500) to 0.952 eV (476/500) . In contrast, th e shallow trap depth remains nearly 
unchanged, but its thermoluminescence (TL) intensity is significantly enhanced. The 
reduction in deep -trap population upon Gd incorporation indicates a decreased 
electron-trapping efficiency, suggesting that charge migration from deep to shallow 
traps is not promoted in Gd/Eu co-doped BaFCl.  
Electron migration among different trap states is highly temperature -dependent 
and is strongly suppressed at low temperatures. To examine the possible presence of 
such trap -to-trap m igration, w e measured the PersL spectra of BaFCl:Gd and 
BaFCl:Eu/Gd at 89 K. As shown in Fig. R 3, the PersL intensity of Gd 3+ is 
substantially reduced, consistent with the behavior observed at room temperature. 
These results suggest that carrier migration among trap states plays a limited role in 
the observed enhanced afterglow. Furthermore, the energy transfer processes from 
Gd3+ to Eu2+, Sm2+, Tb3+, and Mn2+ have been extensively reported in previous studies

Comment 6: The spatial resolution is approximately 25.7 lp/mm, whereas the 
corresponding dose rate on the same  material is only 0.32 μGyair/s. Why does one

Response 6: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. In X-ray imaging, 
image quality is primarily governed by the scintillation brightness and the 
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the detected photons. Under higher radiation doses, a 
larger number of X-ray photons interact with the scintillator, generating strong er light 
output and consequently improving image contrast and spatial resolution. Therefore, 
conventional X-ray imaging typically requires relatively high radiation doses to obtain 
clear and detailed images, which inevitably raises concerns regarding radia tion safety. 
Enhancing the performance of X -ray detection materials is thus crucial for achieving

This work reports a new strategy for developing multicolor X -ray–excited 
persistent luminescence (PersL) materials, which can be further extended to design 
highly efficient PersL systems and enable multifunctional optical applications. By 
constructing Gd3+-mediated cluster traps within alkaline-earth fluorochloride hosts to 
minimize energy loss during electron migration, the authors achieve efficient emission 
across the violet, yel low, and red spectral regions. Notably, bright PersL is realized 
within a single, robust, and scalable host, thereby minimizing compatibility issues and 
simplifying integration into multifunctional optical devices.  In particular, the X -ray–
excited PersL ac hieved through cluster construction is remarkably efficient. 
BaFCl:Gd/Eu exhibits an integrated PersL intensity 3.5 times higher than that of the 
benchmark green -emitting SrAl ₂O₄:Eu/Dy. The initial luminance of ≈4.53 cd m ⁻² is 
exceptionally high for a viol et PersL material. Furthermore, the authors demonstrate 
the use of these PersL materials as excitation sources for perovskite quantum dots to 
achieve full -color time -domain dynamic displays, as well as for low -dose, high -
resolution delayed X-ray imaging. 
Owing to the scalable preparation method, high performance, and excellent air 
stability, these materials show clear potential for commercialization. Overall, this is a 
highly valuable contribution and suitable for publication. I recommend acceptance after 
the authors address the following points to strengthen the manuscript.

BaFCl:Eu, whereas only PersL from Eu3+ was observed. Why was no PersL from Eu3+

no Eu3+ PersL is observed in the BaFCl:Gd/Eu system. Could the authors clarify this 
phenomenon? 
2. Both the X -ray tube voltage and current influence the PersL intensity. The 
authors reported that “Elevating the X-ray tube voltage from 10 to 50 KV resulted in 
an additional 65.8-fold enhancement in PersL intensity...”. Was the X-ray tube current 
kept fixed? If so, please provide the detailed value. 
3. The photograph in Figure 2i lacks a scale bar, which should be included for clarity.
