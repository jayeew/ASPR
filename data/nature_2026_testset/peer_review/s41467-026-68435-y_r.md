Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors reported that they prepared and applied triangle Au nanostructures capped with a CTAC layer
for CO2 reduction in claimed “metal-cation-free” H2SO4 electrolytes. They concluded that high FE for CO in acidic
conditions was due to the so-called “electric-field-driven” effect and “bioinspired proton blocking” effects. 
1. “electric-field-driven” effect 
(a) 
No experimental evidence to support the “electric-field-driven” effect claimed by the authors 
Tip-enhanced electric field intensification is not a new concept. It was studied and reported for electrocatalysis as early as
2016 (Nature 537, 382–386 (2016)). Since 2016, several experimental methods have been developed to characterize and
prove this “tip-enhanced electric field intensification”, for example, conductive atomic force microscopy; Kelvin probe atomic
force microscopy; stark tunning shift of adsorbed species (such as CO in this study) by in-situ ATR-SEIRAS spectra and so
on. However, there is no experimental data or evidence in this manuscript to support the so-called “Tip-enhanced electric
field intensification” claimed by the authors. 
Theoretical calculation simulation is a useful tool to help explain some experimental phenomena and results. However,
merely relying on computational simulation but not any experimental evidence or data is unacceptable. The conclusion is
not convincing to this reviewer. In addition, most information/statements provided by the simulation results in this study have
already been reported and discussed in the literature of the last few years (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jallcom.2024.175549). 
(b) There is no clear correlation between the good catalytic performance of triangle Au and the “electric-field-driven” effect 
The authors claimed that the higher intrinsic catalytic activity of AuNTs compared to those of AuNPs and AuCPs is due to
the “tip-enhanced electric field”, which is not convincing based on the results in this work. As AuNTs, AuNPs and AnCPs
have different sizes and shapes/morphology and each of them exposes different facets and structural edges, all these factors
can lead to different activity (normalized by ECSA) and Tafel slope. The authors didn’t discuss these factors, but instead
assigned the improved activity to the “electric-field-driven” effect, which didn’t make any sense. 
The result of ATR-SEIRAS used to show that “the stronger potentials enhance the adsorption of these intermediates” doesn’t
make sense! As mentioned in point 1, the local electric field should be characterized by a Stark tunning shift of adsorbed
species (such as CO in this study) using in-situ ATR-SEIRAS spectra. 
Except for the calculation and the authors’ own explanation, no clear or supporting experimental results suggest the
correlation between improved activity and the “electric-field-driven” effect. 
2. “bioinspired proton blocking” effects 
(a) What’s the indeed similarity between Aquaporin and the CTAC layer?

(b) Aquaporin has a water transport channel. What’s the channel structure of the CTAC layer? 
(c) How can the author demonstrate that CTAC forms a double layer shown in Figure 1e? Is there any experimental
evidence or data to support this structure? Otherwise, the figure is very misleading. 
(d) Based on the authors’ explanation, the selectivity of CO2R in an acidic solution is due to the presence of the cationic
CTAC layer. However, CTAC-capped, PVP-capped, and SDS-capped AuNTs all show >50% FE for CO, which indicates
that the cationic layer is unlikely the main reason for the good selectivity of CO2R in acidic conditions. 
3. “metal-cation-free” H2SO4 electrolytes 
The authors claimed that they conducted CO2R in a “metal-cation-free” solution. However, they didn’t describe how they
pretreated the electrolyte to obtain such a “metal-cation-free” solution. 
Is it a real “metal-cation-free” solution? 
Did the authors use ICP-MS to measure the M+ concentrations before and after the experiments to confirm it is a “metal-
cation-free” solution? 
Is there any M+ impurity introduced into the solution by the catalysts or materials used in the experiments? 
Please read the reference Nat. Catal. 2021, 4, 654−662 and study the pretreatment methods in this reference where the
“metal-cation-free” condition is strictly created, discussed, and studied for electrocatalysis.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents exciting results in the field of CO2 reduction in acidic media, showing excellent CO current
densities and suppression of HER. The authors use two approaches to accomplish this: electric-field enhancement via
tuning the catalyst shape, and charge-selective layers to limit local proton concentration. While neither approach is
particularly novel on its own, their combination with application to CO2RR in acidic media without alkali metal cations is.
These strategies may also be broadly applicable to other areas of electrocatalysis and engineered microenvironments. 
The authors present compelling, technically sound data. However, I have a number of suggestions and clarification points
that should be addressed before it is suitable for publication. 
My first few comments center around the fact that the authors motivate their paper with the need to suppress HER,
accomplished via the cationic surfactant bilayers. However, most of the paper focuses on the field-enhancement effects of
the shape-controlled nanoparticles. I am a little confused about the interplay between these two effects, and have the
following comments: 
1.The electric field enhancement should be better motivated in the introduction 
2.The paper would be more impactful if it was divided into three sections in which the shape and the electric field were both
investigated independently, and then a final section with both strategies combined at once. 
3.In the “electrochemical study of tip effect” and “CO2 electroreduction in metal-cation-free acidic media” sections, the goal is
“To validate the predicted enhancement of CO2R by [enhanced electric field]”. However, both the field and the cation effects
are convoluted here because two of the shapes have cationic surfactants, while the commercial catalyst particles do not.
Therefore, while the shape is likely enhancing the electric field, it makes it difficult to ascribe performance benefits solely to
the shape when the surfactant is also likely playing a role. This also speaks to my above comment 2 that distinct sections
that clearly decouple these effects would be beneficial to the story’s overall impact. 
4.The electric field measured at the catalyst surface will be dependent on electrolyte conditions. What were the simulation
conditions used in Figure 2? Additionally, the simulations were conducted for the case (I believe) with no surfactants. 
The
extended and distributed charges present in the surfactant bilayer will undoubtedly influence the electric field, and possibly
even screen it. The authors even note this on page 12, line 341. This critically would mean that the advantages of both
individual approaches may not sum together, again motivating comment 2. Electric field simulations with the bilayers should
be conducted. 
Additional comments: 
5.What evidence is there that the bilayers form on the NTs? And on the NPs? Could they be monolayers? I’d imagine the
shape of the particles will also impact the conformity of the surfactant layer. 
6.For the PNP simulations, the surfactant layer was set at 22 um. That seems very large – how was this number chosen?
Especially compared with the thickness of the EDL. 
7.The authors present in situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements to demonstrate that the surfactant remains stably adsorbed to the
gold surface. 
What potential were those measurements conducted at? Is the adsorption stable at all potentials? The authors
note that there is potential-dependent adsorption, so I would assume not. Also, the system was monitored for only 45

8.The Tafel slopes were fit using only 3 data points. Tafel slope fitting is prone to extreme error/subjectivity (see for example

and perhaps add additional points to the fit? 
9.What carbon black was used in the catalyst inks? Was it also a Vulcan XC-72 as used in the commercial catalysts?

1. Novelty 
We fully acknowledge the wealth of prior li terature on tip -enhanced electric field effects,  
principally leveraging such fields to attract alkali metal cations and enhance their local concentration 
on catalyst surfaces (e.g., Nature 2016, 537, 382; Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2020, 132, 8784; Nano Lett. 
2022, 22, 1963; Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2023, 62, e202309351; Adv. Mater. 2023, 35, 2301127; J. Am. 
Chem. Soc. 2023, 145, 11323; Energy Environ. Sci. 2024, 17, 2897). However, we emphasize that the 
novelty of our work lies in its focus on a metal -cation-free strongly acidic system, where the tip -
enhanced electric field serves a fundamentally different purpose: polarizing  and activating nonpolar 
CO2 molecules. 
In our study, the locally intensified electric field at the sharp tips of the Au nanotriangles (AuNTs) 
catalysts polarizes CO2 molecules, leading to a shift and deformation of their electron cloud, an increase 
in their dipole moment, bond length elongation, and bond energy weakening. This mechanism directly 
enhances CO2 activation in the absence of alkali metal cations, which distinguishes our approach from 
the previously reported strategies. By integrating this field effect with a bioinspired proton -blocking 
mechanism, we achieve efficient CO2 electroreduction in a strongly acidic medium—a significant step 
forward for the field. Additionally, we have expanded the discussion (Lines 48–49, 54–56, 62–64, Page 
2) to highlight the distinctiveness of our work compared to prior studies. 
2. In-situ ATR-SEIRAS characterization of tip-enhanced electric field intensification 
To address the reviewer's concerns regarding the experimental evidence for tip -enhanced electric 
field intensification, we have performed in-situ ATR-SEIRAS spectroscopy to monitor the Stark tuning 
shifts of adsorbed CO on Au nanostructures  under varying applied potentials. To enhance the 
comparative analysis and ensure a more rigorous evaluation of the electric field effect, we have included 
Au nanodisks (AuNDs) as a new control sample.  The synthesis and characterization of AuNDs have 
been detailed in our response to R1Q1(b). 
The newly included data (Fig. R1) provides direct experimental evidence supporting the existence 
and influence of the locally intensified electric field at the AuNT s tips. Fig. R1a and b present the in-
situ ATR-SEIRAS spectra of adsorbed CO on Au nanotriangles (AuNTs) and Au nanodisks (AuNDs), 
respectively, across a range of applied potentials. A systematic blueshift in the CO stretching frequency 
is observed with increasing negative potential. The tuning rate of the Stark shift, quantified in Fig. R1c, 
is significantly larger for AuNTs compared to AuNDs, suggesting a stronger localized electric field 
effect at the tips of AuNTs. This is attributed to the geometric field enhancement at the nanostructure 
tips, which concentrates the applied field more effectively compared to less sharp nanostructures. 
Stark tuning shifts arise from the interaction between the applied electric field and the dipole 
moment of adsorbed CO. The significantly larger shifts observed for AuNTs prov ide compelling 
evidence of a stronger localized electric field. This field enhancement would facilitate the polarization 
of CO2 molecules, thereby lowering the activation energy barrier for their reduction. The comparison 
between AuNTs and AuNDs underscores the critical role of nanostructure morphology—sharp corners

3. Kelvin probe force microscopy evidence and challenges in conductive AFM 
To further substantiate the presence of tip -enhanced electric field intensification, we have 
conducted kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements on AuNTs  and AuNDs. The results 
reveal a pronounced enhancement in surface potential at the apex of the  triangular tips (Fig. R2a–c),

the local electric field intensity, these findings provide strong experimental evidence that sharp tip 
geometries indeed e nhance the localized electric field, thereby modulating charge distribution at the 
nanoscale. These results, in combination with our in -situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements, reinforce our 
hypothesis that the tip-enhanced field facilitates CO2 polarization and activation.

By itself, this shift may not fully account for the substantial changes in reaction energies observed for 
*CO2 and *COOH, as the reviewer correctly pointed out. However, we find that the d-band center shift 
should not be viewed as an isolated factor but rather as part of a broader set of electronic effects that 
collectively influence CO2 adsorption and activation. 
In particular, the d-band center shift modifies the electronic structure of the catalyst, thereby 
influencing the degree of charge redistribution upon CO 2 adsorption. This, in turn, affects the 
polarization of *CO2, which plays a critical role in its activation. While the direct effect of the d-band 
shift is modest, its indirect role in modulating dipole -field interactions and local charge distribution 
remains significant. 
2. Discussion on the dipole-field model and its relevance to our work 
The dipole-field model, as highlighted in Nat. Catal. 2021, 4, 1024, suggests that the interaction 
between the *CO 2 dipole moment and the interfacial electric field is a domina nt factor in adsorption 
stability. Our findings align with this perspective, as we also observe that the electric field significantly 
enhances CO2 adsorption due to polarization effects. 
To provide a more comprehensive perspective, we compared the d-band c enter shift with the 
dipole-field interaction and found that these two factors work in a complementary manner: the d-band 
center shift modifies the electronic structure, which indirectly influences how the dipole -field 
interaction manifests at the interface. 
3. Theoretical limitations of the dipole-field model 
A key limitation of the dipole -field model, as discussed in J. Chem. Phys. 2023, 158, 144701, is 
that it does not explicitly differentiate between electrostatic interactions and charge transfer effects. 
This distinction is crucial in electrochemical environments, where both factors can play a role in 
determining adsorption and reaction energetics. 
Additionally, the applicability of the dipole -field model varies across different catalyst systems. 
For example, on Au surfaces, CO 2 adsorption is known to be strongly influenced by local electronic 
states rather than purely by dipole i nteractions. Recognizing these limitations, we have clarified under
