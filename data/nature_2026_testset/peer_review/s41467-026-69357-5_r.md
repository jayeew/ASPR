Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper discussed the improvement in electrical conductivity of copper through the addition of graphene and deformation
of composite followed by annealing. It discusses the microstructure of the composite and hypothesizes the mechanisms for
enhanced conductivity. While the approach for enhancing conductivity is interesting, I would recommend publication of this
paper only after significant information is added on the material and synthesis approach, the measurement approach,
material evolution, first principles calculations and clarifying some language concerns. 
1. Pg 2, Ln 33 – 34: Please clarify why an increase of 10% conductivity is equivalent to effect of an external high pressure of
about 10 gigapascals – this is not clear anywhere in the paper. 
2. Pg 3, Ln 58 – 61: The authors discuss recent papers where Gr/Cu composites were manufactured showing enhanced
electrical conductivity under different conditions, and state that these strategies do not alter intrinsic electrical conductivity of
the metal matrix, 
and still rely on their structural integrity, namely single-crystallization and high purity – which is inherently not true. All the
papers reviewed here have added Gr to Cu, meaning they are not relying on single crystallization and high purity. This
discussion has to be changed for accuracy. 
3. However, what is more surprising is the authors are doing exactly the same as the papers they cite as ref 11-14 - which is
adding Gr to Cu in their study. This leaves the reviewer rather confused because the authors’ premise is to change the
intrinsic electrical properties of Cu, but they are doing so by adding Gr(?). The premise (Pg 3, Lines 73 – 76) then seems
contradictory and therefore unclear – is the hypothesis to add Gr to Cu so that Gr acts as highly conductive channel? Or is it
increasing the defects in Cu alone? And if the measurements are made in the annealed condition, how does that justify the
existence of “local giant stresses”? The paper seems to be written for increasing defects in Cu, especially the DFT model,
but the results are shown for Gr. Please clarify the hypothesis further. 
4. Pg 3, Line 85: What was the purity of copper and/or alloy composition? What trace elements were present? Since all
these components affect conductivity prior to and after straining the composite, it is crucial to present this data. 
5. Pg 3, Line 84 – please provide the graphene content incorporated. This is important information know as the electrical
conductivity of a copper/graphene composite is assessed. Studies have shown that conductivity of such composites is a
strong function of graphene content. 
6. Pg 5, Line 123: For such a measurement to hold, it is crucial for the authors to discuss the uncertainty in measurement
afforded by the system, as well as its calibration of a known-conductivity sample by the system. Given that the authors
present conductivity at various sample diameters, uncertainty in measurement for each of these conditions must be
presented, along with the temperature of measurement. There are error bars for the conductivity in Fig 1g – are these
referring to standard deviation from repeated measurements? What measurement standards were used? What was the gage
length used? All these contribute to uncertainty in measurement and need to be discussed. Since the Methods section states
that the conductivity tests were performed by the authors on the system they developed (Pg 20, Ln 541), it is not clear what
purpose the certification documents in SI are serving – please clarify. 
7. Pg 4, Figure 1g: How was the Cu wire manufactured? Was it made using the same Cu that was used in the synthesis of
the Gr-Cu wire? Several details are missing to assess the viability of these results. 
8. Pg 5, Line 124: Pure copper has a range of electrical conductivities depending on its purity – for example, 6N copper is
~103% IACS in electrical conductivity. Thus, it becomes important to discuss the electrical properties of the copper that was
used in the study for its relevance to the mechanism.

9. In all microstructure images, while the scale bars are evident, it is not clear what these bars represent. No text is evident
around them to understand what they are – please correct this. 
10. The authors discuss lattice distortion at multiple locations in the paper. Since the material is a composite (Copper and
graphene are immiscible as they note in Line 208, Pg 9), then there are two lattices to consider. However, the language in
the paper doesn’t specify which lattice is being considered at specific locations. Please clarify this information for further
clarity. 
11. There are no images of the graphene species in the extruded or drawn/annealed samples. There is also no discussion of
the fate of evolution of the graphene films to whatever becomes of them in the extruded condition. There is an assumption
that they become flakes? How and why is not discussed at all. Please provide this information. There is a discussion of
‘nanolayers’ but it is not clear if this refers to graphene or copper. For example, Fig 2f refers to a nanolayer along grain
boundary – is this graphene? Is the chemical species carbon? The diffusion region seems like it might be oxide at the
interface. Did the authors perform EDS? Significant clarifications are required to reduce confusion in these areas. Similarly,
in Fig 3 A-d, it seems like the authors are showing two nanolayers formed along the grain boundaries – are these graphene?
The image 3f seems to suggest so, but it is not at all clear either in the caption or in the paper text. Same issue with Fig. 3g
and 3h – what layers are being discussed here – what chemical species? All of these chemical species, and material
structure needs to be clarified. 
12. Pg 9, Ln 206 – 209: how did the authors exclude any possible contribution from impurities? This is challenging to assess
with the information presented here, since no information on the feedstock purity has been provided. Additionally, if the
copper and copper/graphene wires were made from foils that were extruded, there should be oxides present significantly, as
the lower size of the copper foils without graphene will have a native passivation layer. Was this removed prior to
manufacturing the wires? How? Please clarify. Also, unless the extrusion and drawing were performed in a closed
environment, the wire will have oxides – please clarify how this is not evident in the results. 
13. If the first principles calculations are comparing an FCC copper cell and another cell, also copper, but with changed
crystal parameters and fully relaxed atomic positions (Pg 21, Ln 577 – 583), then yes, the electron-phonon coupling will be
lower in cell with relaxed atomic position. But that is not a true representation of the Gr/Cu system whose properties the
authors are trying to explain – rather it is only a part of the composite system, the copper part. This therefore cannot be used
to explain the conductivity improvements in Gr/Cu composite, as the authors are attempting to do in Pg 13 Ln 325, and in
comparison, with Figure 4b with experimental measurements from cold-drawn and annealed Gr-Cu composite system. The
DFT model will need to be evolved to accommodate Gr species as well. 
14. The paper discusses only the role of deformation or strain in copper leading to conductivity increase while largely
ignoring the role of graphene. There is a growing body of literature that shows that in composites such as those
manufactured in this work, the graphene plays an important role in providing high velocity pathways for phonons and
electrons. Please interpret your results in that context, since the paper is not discussing the role of graphene beyond pinning
grains, which may not sufficiently explain conductivity improvement. 
15. The language in the paper will benefit from grammar and spell check. The issues are too numerous to point out in this
review unfortunately but are significant to make it challenging to understand the authors’ arguments at places. Kindly
consider editing closely for clarity.

y = −0.01x +1 (1)

R4: Thank you for your question. Our work indicates that abundant GBs with distorted

strength simultaneously. It is possible to achieve high electrical conductivity by 
applying to many other two-dimensional (2D) materials /copper systems on the premise

Q5: The author achieved extremely excellent electrical conductivity through structural

R5: Thank you for your question. Traditionally, eliminating stress can reduce electrons 
scattering resulting in improving the electrical conductivity. This stress usually 
originates from the cold deformation process. Hence, annealing is usually employed to 
eliminating stress to restore the electrical conductivity of conducting metals. However, 
the effect based on the annealing is very limited. In this work, giant interfacial 
compressive stress was induced due to the different TEC during annealing, thus

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 59 -->

significantly suppressing electron-phonon coupling and reducing phonon-caused 
electron scattering. This giant interfacial compressive stress has been demonstrated 
based on the TEM characterization and synchrotron X-ray micro-diffraction (μ-XRD) 
as well as the measurements of Raman spectra. And this compressive stress is 
completely different from the stress caused by the cold deformation. Moreover, with 
introduction of the interfacial compressive stress, defects such as dislocations and 
vacancy have also been eliminated through annealing.
