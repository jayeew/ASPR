Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper by Cunha Neto et al describes the identification of transcripts that are differentially expressed in ectopic cambia,
with a view to understanding how this enigmatic property of woody vines is regulated. The authors identify members of the
KNOX family as being preferentially expressed in these structures and show that they are under positive selection. The
manuscript is engaging and exceptionally well-written, describing the background and experimental programme in a series
of clear logic steps, leading to a balanced discussion. However, there are two pieces of data that I believe would further
strengthen the authors arguments: 
The first is spatial gene expression data. This could come in the form of RNA-in situ hybridization (challenging in woody
species), but probably a graphical representation of the authors existing RNA-seq data, showing section number on the x-
axis (including a representation of position along the radial axis) and relative expression on the Y is enough to assist the
reader in visualising expression along the radial axis of the plant. 
The second is in planta evidence of W. floribunda genes of interest regulating cambium, e.g. over-expression or cross-
complementation in e.g. Arabidopsis. 
(Remarks on code availability) 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors try to characterize the regulatory network underlying ectopic cambia forms in Japanese
wisteria. The results are interesting and may be interested by the related readers. However, the current version is
descriptative and lack of functional validation. Several suggestions could be useful for improving your manuscript. 
1.What factors are essential for determining EC formaton in plants? What are the differences between EC and vascular
camibum? Comparation of common genes derived from the transcriptomic data in two species may provide some clues. 
2.Funcitional analysis of key KNOX genes associated with EC formation in planta is necessary. 
3.It is unclear how to sample EC for transcriptome analysis. 
4.What KNOXs are specific for the regulation of vascular cambium while what KNOXs are for EC? Along this logic, the
authors should extract the useful information from the phylogenetic tree of KNOX genes.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
In this manuscript, the authors try to characterize the regulatory network underlying ectopic cambia forms 
in Japanese wisteria. The results are interesting and may be interested by the related readers. However, 
the current version is descriptative and lack of functional validation. Several suggestions could be useful 
for improving your manuscript. 
 
We appreciate the feedback. Below, we address each of the suggestions.  
 
1.What factors are essential for determining EC formaton in plants? What are the differences between EC 
and vascular camibum? Comparation of common genes derived from the transcriptomic data in two 
species may provide some clues. 
 
The manuscript (and its references) provides a substantial amount of information on how 
EC forms in terms of developmental anatomy. This topic has been investigated in several papers, 
showing that there is a common developmental series leading to the formation of EC; hence, we 
turned to the question: What is the molecular regulation of this EC formation? Our paper presents

the first exploration of candidate genes involved in EC formation. From our transcriptomes, we 
were able to compare differentially expressed genes across species (Japanese wisteria v. 
Common bean) and cambium types (both across species and within Japanese wisteria). These 
comparisons provide a solid basis for identifying genes associated with cambium formation in 
Japanese wisteria, with a focus on one key regulator: the KNOX genes. We welcome the reviewer's 
suggestions on alternatives for comparisons among the candidate genes. Additionally, in this 
revised version, we have included functional analyses to validate the role of KNOX genes (see 
below), which represents a major step towards elucidating the genetic basis of these vascular 
variants.  
 
2.Funcitional analysis of key KNOX genes associated with EC formation in planta is necessary. 
 
That was a very important suggestion, also recommended by Reviewer 1. Thank you! As 
mentioned earlier, we successfully included the first experimental essay to explore the function of 
Japanese wisteria KNOX genes through heterologous expression in Arabidopsis. These results 
are presented in the new section “KNOX genes from Japanese Wisteria show canonical KNOX-like 
activity in heterologous functional assays”.  
 
3.It is unclear how to sample EC for transcriptome analysis. 
 
Thanks for your comment. Indeed, we missed the chance to clearly explain how the samples were 
collected. To fix this, we added a new figure (Fig. 2) showing a 3D view of the tangential 
cryosectioning approach and a high-magnification cross-section of the EC cambium of Japanese 
wisteria, illustrating the tissue amount collected for each sample. These updates greatly improve 
the description of our methodology and how we obtained these important results. We truly 
appreciate the suggestion! 
 
4.What KNOXs are specific for the regulation of vascular cambium while what KNOXs are for EC? Along 
this logic, the authors should extract the useful information from the phylogenetic tree of KNOX genes. 
 
We appreciate this comment. We amended the results to provide a more direct description of the 
KNOX proteins that are potentially more closely associated with the development of each cambium 
type in our species. To that end, we examined the results already presented in Table 5, which 
describes the transcript clusters that were differentially expressed in each contrast, thus providing 
a clue about which proteins are associated with typical v. EC. These changes are noted in lines 177-
180.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
Currently, the genetic mechanism underlying ecological atypical vascular architectures in plants remains 
unclear. This manuscript illustrated an association of ectopic cambia in the woody vine Japanese wisteria 
with KNOX gene expression. Undoubtly, their finding is meaningful. In the resubmited manuscript,the 
authors adderssed most of my concerns except for functional validation. Although they ectopically 
expressed wisteria vine Cluster_1332, the ortholog of KNAT2/6, in Arabidopsis, and found that the

We thank the reviewer for their 
thoughtful and positive feedback on our 
findings. Both Arabidopsis and poplar do not 
naturally exhibit ectopic cambia; both 
present secondary vascular growth; 
therefore, they are equally suitable for the 
heterologous overexpression of KNAT2/6 for 
functional validation. We opted to use 
Arabidopsis given the efficient 
transformation time and in-house expertise. 
In our first revisions, we demonstrated that 
the transgenic plants displayed typical KNOX 
phenotypes, indicating homology between 
the Japanese wisteria and Arabidopsis 
copies. In the current revision, as suggested by the reviewer, we investigated whether the 
Arabidopsis transgenics in fact displayed vascular defects in the inflorescence stem, leaves, and 
roots in both long- (~30 days) and short-day plants (~90 days). We did not observe significant 
changes to the vascular anatomy; notably, ectopic cambia were absent. A new figure is included 
in the supplemental materials (Supplementary Fig. S5) (see image on the side). The absence of 
ectopic cambia in Arabidopsis transgenics indicates that misexpression of KNAT2/6 alone is 
insufficient to induce this phenotype. In this work, we were careful not to overstate that KNAT2/6 
alone is the sole contributor; rather, it is clearly part of the regulatory network that produces 
ectopic cambia in Wisteria.
