Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript proposes an algorithmic strategy for generating and analyzing unfolding configuration maps using a graph-
based representation. While the dedication to developing this novel approach is evident, this review identifies several areas
requiring clarification and substantiation. Our critical assessment focuses on clarifying the core motivations for specific
structural choices, quantifying the claimed time efficiency, and formally demonstrating algorithmic completeness. The
detailed comments below are intended to guide the authors in strengthening the theoretical and practical foundations of their
work. 
1. We request a clearer demarcation of the functional distinction between the "shell graph" and the face graph within the
algorithmic description. The necessity of the shell graph is not immediately evident, particularly if the face graph were
augmented with edge attributes specifically representing the physical connections between faces. A detailed explanation of
why the face graph is insufficient for the algorithmic core would significantly strengthen this section. 
2. The decision to exhaustively search all possible configurations raises significant concerns, as the time complexity
appears to grow geometrically or exponentially. We request a robust justification for prioritizing completeness over
computational tractability. For real-world engineering applications, a more tractable approach, such as a focused search for
an optimal subset of unfolding paths, might be preferable. 
3. The paper mentions that current methods for probing the configurational space rely on searching for vertex connections on
templates and sequentially connecting them. To justify the motivations, we would appreciate a more explicit and detailed
discussion on the primary computational and/or structural limitations (e.g., non-completeness, redundancy, scaling issues)
inherent in these existing, sequential connection-based methods. 
4. Regarding the recursive procedure for generating non-adjacent regions, please clarify the operational scope: Is this
recursive operation applied to every configuration discovered by the algorithm? We require an analysis of the resulting
impact on the algorithm's time. Additionally, the paper must explicitly state the formal termination criterion or the mechanism
employed to guarantee that the algorithm has identified all possible folding states (i.e., its completeness). 
5. The claim that the algorithm can efficiently incorporate new templates (e.g., Fig. 4) is a powerful one. However, it seems
intuitive that incorporating a new template would necessitate either a complete re-execution of the algorithm or an
exhaustive re-checking of all previously saved configurations for unfolding possibilities. Could you provide a detailed
technical explanation of the underlying mechanism that avoids this apparent computational redundancy and specifically
clarifies how the time complexity is reduced in practice for this multi-template incorporation? 
6. The paragraph introducing microscale (thermal motion) and macroscale (actuation/stress relaxation) folding in Section 3
appears as an abrupt thematic shift. We seek confirmation on the intended implication: Does the current work provide a
method for computationally identifying the optimal actuation pathway (e.g., minimizing folds or actuator usage) required to
transform a given template into the final target shape? 
7. We seek clarification on the rationale for incorporating misfolded structures into the algorithm's framework, particularly as
this process typically involves computationally intensive methods like "tedious combinational analysis" or "direct simulations
or experiments." Furthermore, we observe an apparent redefinition or abandonment of the early concept of "distinct
intermediate folding states" in the later stages of the paper. This conceptual shift requires a clear explanation and
justification. 
8. Minor Editorial Corrections 
• Please ensure consistency in terminology, using either "Figure" or "Fig." throughout the text, but not both interchangeably. 
• In Fig. 2.6, the arrow direction labeled "no" appears to be pointing in the inverse direction of its logical flow. Please verify
and correct. 
• There is a typographical error in Section 1; the word "maintatining" should be corrected to "maintaining." 
• In Fig. D2, the x-scale looks strange. It doesn't seem to be the original or log scale.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, the authors presented a new graph-based algorithm for identifying fold sequences. Specifically, by considering
the face graph of the given structure, the authors analyzed the possible sequences of link removals. Isomorphic
configurations are also identified and grouped into one. Finally, the algorithm gives the full discrete folding landscape. The
authors further considered the case with misfolds and showed that their method can be extended to multifarious templates.
Overall, the work is interesting and well-written. It provides valuable insights into the study and design of folding pathways. 
I have a few minor comments: 
1. The main text focuses on the cube, and the cases of dodecahedron and icosahedron are discussed in the SI. The two
other platonic solids (octahedron and tetrahedron) are briefly mentioned in the main text in Fig. 5. It may be better to include
a more unified discussion to highlight the applicability and results of the algorithm on all these platonic solids. 
2. For completeness, it will be great to add an illustration of the face graph of dodecahedron and/or icosahedron in the
supplementary information. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary: 
This manuscript introduces a graph-based algorithm to systematically enumerate all discrete folding pathways of polyhedral
shells. By tracking edge cuts and the evolution of “folding states” (identified by changes in graph connectivity), the method
builds a configuration space linking a shell’s 2D templates to its closed 3D form. The authors demonstrate their approach on
Platonic solids, including examples with multiple targets and misfolds. 
Comments: 
-> Is misfold detection truly autonomous? (p.6) 
The algorithm includes misfolded states only if provided in advance. Given that the paper notes these must be “identified
through tedious combinatorial analysis or detected dynamically…” and then manually added, can the authors clarify whether
the method can discover misfolds on its own or depends entirely on prior input? 
-> Can the authors provide a formal complexity analysis? (Appendix D, p.11) 
The manuscript claims sub-factorial scaling and presents supporting plots, but offers no theoretical analysis. Could the
authors clarify whether any bounds or guarantees on runtime or completeness can be established? 
-> Are all enumerated folding pathways physically realizable? 
The method assumes topologically valid sequences correspond to feasible folding motions, yet excludes geometric
constraints such as collisions. How might this affect the relevance of the landscape for real-world folding? 
-> Could the algorithm description be clarified? (p.4): 
The phrase “random link” implies a non-deterministic method, although the search is exhaustive. Would rephrasing reduce
ambiguity? Also, could the authors clarify how the method handles disconnected cuts and distinguish clearly between a
'configuration' and a 'folding state'? 
Overall, this is a thoughtful contribution with solid algorithmic, but it overstates automation and physical applicability.
Clarifying these points and improving exposition would significantly strengthen the manuscript.

Reply to: Reviewer #1  Reviewer 1.1: “The manuscript proposes an algorithmic strategy for generating and analyzing unfolding configuration maps using a graph-based representation. While the dedication to developing this novel approach is evident, this review identifies several areas requiring clarification and substantiation. Our critical assessment focuses on clarifying the core motivations for specific structural choices, quantifying the claimed time efficiency, and formally demonstrating algorithmic completeness. The detailed comments below are intended to guide the authors in strengthening the theoretical and practical foundations of their work.”  Authors: We thank the reviewer for the constructive comments and suggestions, which helped us improving our work.  Reviewer 1.2: “1. We request a clearer demarcation of the functional distinction between the "shell graph" and the face graph within the algorithmic description. The necessity of the shell graph is not immediately evident, particularly if the face graph were augmented with edge attributes specifically representing the physical connections between faces. A detailed explanation of why the face graph is insufficient for the algorithmic core would significantly strengthen this section.”  Authors: The shell graph is employed to associate the face graph with the corresponding edges, mapping the physical connections among faces. The reviewer is right that the same can be done with edge attributes, but with the shell graph we can efficiently identify the adjacency of physical edges and thus construct contiguous physical cuts in a straightforward manner. To clarify this point, we added to the end of the first paragraph on section 2:    “These two graphs have complementary information: The face graph is used to uniquely identify configurations and validate them as folding states and the shell graph to obtain adjacency information among the face graph edges.”  Reviewer 1.3: “2. The decision to exhaustively search all possible configurations raises significant concerns, as the time complexity appears to grow geometrically or exponentially. We request a robust justification for prioritizing completeness over computational tractability. For real-world engineering applications, a more tractable approach, such as a focused search for an optimal subset of unfolding paths, might be preferable.”  Authors: It is true that, when it is clear what is the optimal subset, more tailored algorithms are efficient. However, these optimization strategies are usually sensitive to the folding mechanism, energy, length and time scales, and therefore system specific. A catalog of all possible configurations and folding paths allows a statistically relevant and unbiased exploration of the folding process. This is particularly relevant in stochastic folding, as folding is affected by kinetic traps defined by the nature and structure of the configuration landscape, rather than properties of the individual folding state, as the free energy in the case of systems in thermodynamic equilibrium. Nevertheless, the insights obtained with the proposed algorithm might inform faster, tailored strategies able to consider more complex structures in reasonable time. In the new version, we clarify this point in the fourth paragraph of section 1, as:   “Methods which favor optimal subsets instead of completeness may prove to be more efficient, but this is only possible when the optimization strategy is already known. A complete knowledge of the folding landscape allows the possibility of optimizing folding paths for not only time or yield, but also for robustness, avoiding common bottlenecks by following alternative less probable kinetic paths.”  Reviewer 1.4: “3. The paper mentions that current methods for probing the configurational space rely on searching for vertex connections on templates and sequentially connecting them. To justify the motivations, we would appreciate a more explicit and detailed discussion on the primary computational and/or structural limitations (e.g., non-completeness, redundancy, scaling issues) inherent in these existing, sequential connection-based methods.”  Authors: For methods in which all sets of combinatorial possibilities are probed and tested, the pool of candidates for folding states scales factorially with the number of necessary cuts needed to unfold the structure which, in turn, scales with the number of edges in a polyhedral structure. Strategies that concern the exploitation of vertex connections will only obtain folding states computed by adjoining vertex connections, these do not represent the complete set of possible folding states. Our method does not scale factorially and can achieve completeness. We added this information to the fourth paragraph of section 1:   “These methods fail in providing a scalable and complete procedure to probe the folding process, being not only computationally expensive but also structurally incomplete.“

Reviewer 1.5: "4. Regarding the recursive procedure for generating non-adjacent regions, please clarify the operational scope: Is this recursive operation applied to every configuration discovered by the algorithm? We require an analysis of the resulting impact on the algorithm's time. Additionally, the paper must explicitly state the formal termination criterion or the mechanism employed to guarantee that the algorithm has identified all possible folding states (i.e., its completeness).”  Authors: The recursive operation is not applied on every configuration of cuts but rather only on the folding states. We are interested in non-adjacent cuts, that are sets of free folding faces which do not share an edge. These are linear combinations of previously found folding states. Applying the algorithm to only folding states guarantees that the search is leading towards a linear combination of folding states with at least one free moving edge. Nevertheless, if the search was instead done with every configuration as an input, it would lead to many irrelevant configurations, which have already been found or do not have free moving edges. With the proposed method, we ensure an efficient and complete search.   To evaluate the efficiency of the algorithm, we decided to compare it with an alternative method to find non-adjacent region folding states. In the latter, we construct linear combinations of folding states that share no edges, following a series of rules which ensure that the number of cut edges in the folding state is enough to form a free folding state (i.e. a red link in the face graph). On Fig. 1 (below), we show a comparison of running times for each method and how it scales with the number of edges in the structure. The alternative (red) takes much longer times than the recursive method described in the paper and has strong memory limitations. The rationale for the explanation of its efficiency is the fact that the search for non-adjacent region cuts is performed in a configurational space which is already fairly known, thus the algorithm converges quickly to adjacent structures which have already been found.   
  Fig. 1 – Comparison of the running times between the method used by the algorithm to search for non-adjacent region folding states (blue) and an alternative method (red) which constructs linear combinations of previously found folding states.    The proposed algorithm stops when, after choosing every edge in the cut graph as a starting edge and, after selecting a specific starting edge, it does not have any adjacent edges as follow-ups in the follow-up queue. This process is then repeated with every folding state found on the first run as the initial configuration. When all folding states have been used as an input to the algorithm, the mapping is complete and the algorithm stops. To clarify the termination criterion, we have reformulated the end of the fifth paragraph of section 2:  “When all folding states have been used as inputs, the algorithm stops.”  Reviewer 1.6: “5. The claim that the algorithm can efficiently incorporate new templates (e.g., Fig. 4) is a powerful one. However, it seems intuitive that incorporating a new template would necessitate either a complete re-execution of the algorithm or an exhaustive re-checking of all previously saved configurations for unfolding possibilities. Could you provide a detailed technical explanation of the underlying mechanism that avoids this apparent computational redundancy and specifically clarifies how the time complexity is reduced in practice for this multi-template incorporation?”

Authors: Indeed, one of the advantages of our method is that, when a new template is included, we can take advantage of the folding landscape of other templates studied previously. As exemplarily shown in Fig. 4 for the cube, different templates lead to the same non-isomorphic folding states. When a new template is considered, for every new folding state we identify, we compare it with the full catalog of previous folding states. If it corresponds to one found previously (either with this template or another one), we do not need to continue exploring the other configurations generated from this folding state. For the sake of comparison, we ran our algorithm for the eleven templates of the cube with and without information of the results for the other templates. On Fig. 2 (below) we show that, even for the cube with only eleven templates, if all templates are considered separately, the algorithm needs to evaluate one order of magnitude more structures to map the complete folding landscape. We added this information to the main paper, as:   
  Fig. 2 – The cumulative number of configurations needed to be analyzed if templates are considered together (red) and separately (blue).    “We apply the algorithm with each one of these templates as an initial configuration and then group all the isomorphic configurations into one. Many of these templates lead to the same non-isomorphic structures, therefore we can exploit the catalog of folding states already found and reduce significantly the searching process by comparing new structures to structures previously found when dealing with other templates.”   Reviewer 1.7: “6. The efficiency of folding pathways and actuation depends on the topology of the landscape and the weight of the connection between states (e.g. time, energy, …). If the weight is also known, from the obtained landscape, it is possible to evaluate the efficiency of actuation pathways and search for the optimal ones under certain constraints. We introduced a sentence in the fourth paragraph of section 4:   “In fact, the resulting map can be used for optimizing the folding process in several systems by determining the transition rates between intermediate configurations, which can be thought of as edge weights in a network.”   Reviewer 1.8: “7. ------------------------------------------------  Reply to: Reviewer #3  Reviewer 3.1: “This manuscript introduces a graph-based algorithm to systematically enumerate all discrete folding pathways of polyhedral shells. By tracking edge cuts and the evolution of “folding states” (identified by changes in graph connectivity), the method builds a configuration space linking a shell’s 2D templates to its closed 3D form. The authors demonstrate their approach on Platonic solids, including examples with multiple targets and misfolds.”  Comments:  -> Is misfold detection truly autonomous? (p.6)  The algorithm includes misfolded states only if provided in advance. Given that the paper notes these must be “identified through tedious combinatorial analysis or detected dynamically…” and then manually added, can the authors clarify whether the method can discover misfolds on its own or depends entirely on prior input?”  Authors: The presented method focuses only on discovering the intermediate folding states between an initial and final folding state. The subset of misfolds that the algorithm can identify are the ones that correspond to intermediate folding states. To clarify, we added to the last paragraph of section 3:   “The folding pathway of these structures gives us not only information on new intermediate misfolded configurations found by the algorithm but also their pathways and how they can be avoided or enhanced.  Reviewer 3.2: “-> Can the authors provide a formal complexity analysis? (Appendix D, p.11) The manuscript claims sub-factorial scaling and presents supporting plots, but offers no theoretical analysis. Could the authors clarify whether any bounds or guarantees on runtime or completeness can be established?”  Authors: As suggested, we performed an empirical complexity analysis to better understand the scaling behavior of our algorithm. We measured the average number of iterations required to explore the full folding landscape for a range of polyhedral shapes, characterized by their number of edges. The results are shown in the figure below, where we compare the empirical data with four different functional models: factorial, polynomial, exponential, and power-law scaling. As initially claimed in the manuscript, the observed scaling is clearly sub-factorial. This is immediately evident when comparing the data to the factorial curve (approximated using Stirling’s formula), which grows much faster than our empirical measurements.  Among the candidate models, the polynomial fit yielded the best statistical metrics: R², Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). However, this fit required a high polynomial degree (n = 5), suggesting possible overfitting and limiting its usefulness for extrapolation. We also tested exponential and power-law fits, which provided comparable or slightly better scores (particularly the power-law fit), with the added benefit of interpretability and better generalization potential.  Although the current dataset does not allow us to unambiguously distinguish between exponential and power-law scaling, both clearly demonstrate sub-factorial growth. Thus, while we cannot yet provide a formal upper bound for runtime complexity, our results support the claim that the algorithm scales significantly better than combinatorial enumeration. This behavior is reminiscent of complexity profiles observed in other hard computational problems, such as constraint satisfaction problems (CSP), where worst-case exponential scaling contrasts with more favorable average-case performance, often exhibiting superlinear or power-law behavior. Finally, regarding completeness, the algorithm is guaranteed to be exhaustive in its exploration of the folding landscape for a given template. This is ensured by the systematic nature of the graph traversal and configuration labeling strategy, which prevents repeated exploration of identical configurations.  To clarify this point, we added the following sentence to the manuscript:  “Empirical complexity analysis also confirms that the algorithm scales sub-factorially with system size, supporting its applicability to large and complex structures.”

Reviewer 3.3: “-> Are all enumerated folding pathways physically realizable? The method assumes topologically valid sequences correspond to feasible folding motions, yet excludes geometric constraints such as collisions. How might this affect the relevance of the landscape for real-world folding?”  Authors: For the cases studied here, we performed molecular dynamics simulations that suggest that all folding configurations are realizable. Nevertheless, for more complex structures, it might be the case that the folding states, mainly obtained through topological considerations, might either be geometrically impossible (due to, for example, excluded volume constraints between faces) or unreachable through folding. While the former is simpler to verify, the latter will imply validation through direct simulations. We added this discussion to the conclusions, as:   “For all the polyhedra studied here, molecular dynamics simulations were performed, thus all the intermediate configurations and misfolds were validated, some even with resource to experimental toy models. However, for more complex structures this might not be the case. Structures discovered by the algorithm through a set of topological rules may be geometrically impossible or not reachable through folding alone. In principle, a method for geometric validation could be imposed to validate all complex intermediate folding structures, although this is outside the scope of this work. The challenge of capturing structures unreachable through folding alone does not have a straightforward resolution besides thoroughly inspecting molecular dynamics simulations or toy model experiments, which for complex structures may be impractical.”  Reviewer 3.4: “-> Could the algorithm description be clarified? (p.4): The phrase “random link” implies a non-deterministic method, although the search is exhaustive. Would rephrasing reduce ambiguity? Also, could the authors clarify how the method handles disconnected cuts and distinguish clearly between a 'configuration' and a 'folding state’?”  Authors: It is true that the search is exhaustive as noted by the reviewer. The random choice of the links is just to determine the order in which edges are sampled, but any other method is equally possible. To avoid this misunderstanding, we removed the word “random” and added a sentence to the second paragraph of section 2, as:  “Configurations are subsets of the cut graph, and folding states are discrete intermediate structures in the folding process that are defined by which of their edges are foldable.”  And to the fifth paragraph as:  “The algorithm will search for combinations of edge cuts in the cut graph of that specific structure, and the only valid configurations will be non-adjacent as all adjacent configurations have already been found.”
