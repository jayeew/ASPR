Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Lin, Meng et al. proposes a pathway whereby intermittent fasting inhibits TP53-driven but not Cdkn2a-
driven gliomas through gut microbiome mediated Methionine-m6A regulation. If proven, this pathway would enhance the
understanding how the gastrointestinal tract contributes to antitumor effects of intermittent fasting. The manuscript contains
many phenotypic and correlation analyses, however, the mechanisms of under the phenotypes remain largely unconfirmed. 
Major comments: 
1.In Figure 1g, the authors compared the AL and IF groups, but the response of IF was different in the TP53 and Cdkn2a
groups, so the authors should compare the differential genes of IF and AL in the Tp53 mutant group. And in the whole
manuscript is a comparison of AL and IF groups, it is unknown whether the author made the comparison in the IF response
group (Tp53) and IF-non-response (Cdkn2a). 
2.In Figure 1k, the authors observed down-regulation of TGF-β2 by using the inhibitor STM2457, but it was not known
whether STM2457 would mimic the antitumor effects of IF in TP53 group, and Cdkn2a should also be added as the control
group. 
3.In Figure 4, the authors should compare the differences in gut microbiota between IF-treated Cdkn2a and Tp53 GBM mice,
and whether the gut microbiota of mice in CDKN2a group was unchanged. In the fecal microbiota transplantation experiment
from IF mice, Cdkn2a mice treated with IF should also be added as the control group. 
4.To confirm that intermittent fasting is microbiome-dependent experiments in ABX-treated and germ-free setting should be
perform. 
5.Additionally, please provide data on whether methionine is decreased in the tumor and fecal after IF-treated in Cdkn2a
and Tp53 groups. 
6.16S rRNA seq has been performed in a cross-sectional study (same time, at the end of the experiments) with several
biases (cag effect and tumor sizes). It is important to perform a longitudinal study of the taxonomic microbial shifts in a paired
manner, and compare the effects of various diets at each time points and overtime. 
7.The reference “Methionine deficiency facilitates antitumour immunity by altering m6A methylation of immune checkpoint
transcripts” (The authors did not cited this reference) had demonstrated Methionine deficiency facilitates antitumour immunity
by altering m6A methylation, and the mechanism is explained in more detail than this manuscript. So in this manuscript, the
authors should address the mechanism in how IF-specific inhibition of TP53-mutated gliomas and the gut microbiota alter
methionine methylation in brain. 
Minor comments: 
1.On Line 186, “AL and IF tumor cells…..” should be “AL- and IF-treated tumor cells…..”. 
2.In Figure 2k, the authors should use scatter plots and label n values. 
3.On Line 221, “Figure 3e showed……” should be revised as “ In Figure 3e, the results showed that ……:. 
4.On Line 273, the sentence “including methionine (Figure 5g, 5h)…..” should be verified. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
1. Limited novelty: The connection between METTL3 and Glioblastoma (GBM) has been extensively reported in multiple

2. The selection of METTL3 and TGFB signaling as the key downstream targets appears to be cherry-picked, as the authors
did not provide a strong rationale for their choice. 
3. This study does not explore the contribution of METTL3 to TGFB2, and in Figure 1k, the METTL3 inhibitor STM2475 fails
to repress TGFb2. 
4. The authors should further explore whether the METTL3 inhibitor STM2475 or TGF-beta inhibitor would achieve a similar
protective effect against GBM as intermittent fasting (IF) treatment. 
5. Several key pieces of information are missing. For example, the sample size is missing in several figures. Regarding the
FMT model, it is unclear whether antibiotics were administered to the mice before the FMT. 
6. Figure 1e and 1f do not provide quantitative results of tumor size. 
7. For Figure 5, please also conduct hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to show the changes in tumor size among
different groups. 
8. It is not explained whether and how the findings of the METTL3-m6A-TGF-β signaling axis could explain the differential
response of Cdkn2a and Tp53 GBM mouse models to IF treatment. 
9. More than 40 methyl transfers from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) are known to occur to various substrates such as nucleic
acids, proteins, lipids, and secondary metabolites. It is unclear why the authors focused specifically on m6A. 
10. In Figure 5h, the sample size for the western blot (n=1 per group) is insufficient to draw any meaningful results. 
11. In Figure 5i, it is unclear how the authors performed the Pearson correlations, given the very limited sample size (n=2 per
group). Also, the authors should explain what kind of experiment was conducted. 
12. It is not unclear how IF treatment leads to a decrease in methionine-related gut microbial species. 
13. In Figure 5f, please explore the stool level and serum level of methionine and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) by targeted
metabolomics. 
14. The authors should incorporate germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice models to provide additional evidence supporting the
protective effect of IF treatment and its reliance on alterations in the gut microbiome. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Reviewer Summary: In this manuscript, the authors examine the effect of intermittent fasting on the pathogenesis of GBM,
stratified into groups driven by loss of TP53 and those with loss of CDKN2A. 
The authors found that IF only affected mice
with tumors bearing TP53 deletion, which they found correlated with loss of methionine levels in tumor tissue. 
They found
that this loss of methionine correlated to a loss of m6A, which they hypothesize leads to a reduction in TGFB signaling
through METTL3. 
The experiments performed in the study generally support the notion that intermittent fasting may regulate the gut microbiota
and influence metabolism in orthotopic GBM in vivo. 
The methodology is sound and well described. 
The strengths of the
manuscript are the mouse models used, the use of multiple omics platforms to measure gene- and metabolite-level data in
tumor tissue, and rescue experiments to show the effects of methionine supplementation and entacapone in reversing the
benefit of IF. 
There are a few weaknesses in the manuscript. 
First, the mechanistic link between IF and METTL3’s effect on TGFB is
unclear from these studies. 
The authors do not provide evidence for how IF specifically influences METTL3, other than
stating that TGFB likely has a reduction in m6A in IF mice. 
Further experimentation would help strengthen the link between
IF and TGFB’s effects on tumor growth in their models. 
Specifically, it would be useful to show the effects of METTL3
knockout in AL mice and METTL3 overexpression in IF mice. 
Second, while the authors show that FMT provides the same
benefit as IF, they do not demonstrate that the microbiota (or lack of certain species) is necessary for the benefit of IF. 
This
should be accomplished by treating AL mice with antibiotics to see if these mice have prolonged survival by limiting
methionine-producing gut microbiota. 
Third, the authors do not measure the metabolic effects of IF in their mice – what
happens to serum glucose, insulin levels, C-peptide, ketones – in serum of mice? 
Are mice adequately fasted? 
Is 24 hours
fast required, or is less time of fasting (e.g. 18 hours) beneficial? 
This would be relevant to patients, where a 24-hour fast
would be difficult to achieve. 
More typical fasting is overnight fast with first meal of the day being lunch. 
In addition, the
methodology regarding sample harvest from mice is not described – when are samples harvested, at the end of IF before
refeeding or somewhere in-between? 
These parameters should be specified in the methods for all samples taken from mice.
Unique aspects: The use of intermittent fasting in genotype-stratified mouse models would be helpful in establishing
targeted nutritional therapy in GBM 
Study design: Basic translational investigation to identify the effects of IF in mouse models of GBM 
Writing style: The manuscript style is succinct and straight-forward, though there are a few typos that need correction.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Lin et al. tried to link the genotype-specific tumor-inibiting effects of intermittent fasting (IF) on glioma development. However,
a lack of experimental rigor, statistic testing, and key comparision across different genotypes makes their argument hardly
convincing. 
The experiments throughout lack a critical control group, Cdkn2a-mutant gliomas. IF should have similar effects on these
mice, yet it is hard to explain why they are not nearly as responsive as Trp53-mutant mice. 
Throughout the manuscript, many statements are not backed up by quantification or do not provide the n value or the
timepoints of sample collection/imaging for each experiment.The Methods/Figure legends lack critical experimental details. 
Fig. 2k, the differences are very minor between the two groups. 
Fig. 3d,e,f, the authors should show H&E images to clearly demarcate the tumor areas, and perform individual statistic
testing to confirm differential expression of METTL3 and TGFB2 in tumor areas. 
Fig. 4b,c, it is hard to appreciate the difference in bacteriodota given the signifcant cross-sample variations. 
Kras overexpressing/mutation is uncommon in human GBMs, making these GBM models less relevant to human disease,
which should be acknowledged. 
Some overstatements need to be tuned down. For example, contrary to the authors' claim, GBM stratification by TP53 and
CDKN2a alone is hardly a well-recognized method; the decrease of excitotary neurons in scRNA-seq may not mean
anything since it has many explanations.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript by Lin, Meng et al. proposes a pathway whereby intermittent fasting 
inhibits TP53-driven but not Cdkn2a-driven gliomas through gut microbiome mediated 
Methionine-m6A regulation. If proven, this pathway would enhance the understanding 
how the gastrointestinal tract contributes to antitumor effects of intermittent fasting. 
The manuscript contains many phenotypic and correlation analyses, however, the

Major comments: 
1. In Figure 1g, the authors compared the AL and IF groups, but the response of IF was 
different in the TP53 and Cdkn2a groups, so the authors should compare the differential

Response: Thanks for the insightful suggestion. We apologize for any lack of clarity in 
our original manuscript. Initially, we compared differences between intermittent fasting 
(IF) and ad libitum (AL) conditions within the TP53-mutant genotype and observed 
phenotypic improvement in TP53-mutant mice upon IF. To further investigate why IF 
is effective specifically in the TP53-mutant subtype of GBM, all experiments from 
Figure 1 onward focused exclusively in the Tp53-subtype genetic background. Since 
CDKN2A-deleted mice did not exhibit improvement after IF, they were not initially 
included as controls. 
As recommended, we have now included a comparative analysis between CDKN2A-
AL and CDKN2A-IF groups as a control to better delineate the IF response specific to 
TP53-mutant GBM. Differential gene expression analysis between CDKN2A- and 
TP53-IF groups identified 2,741 significantly dysregulated genes, including 
METTL3—a key regulator of m ⁶A methylation—providing further support that 
METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification may underlie the efficacy of IF in GBM  (SF3b, 
3c). Additionally, enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of the TGF -β 
signaling pathway (SF3d, 3e). 
These new results reinforce that the transcriptomic changes induced by IF are specific 
to TP53-mutant tumors and are not universally present across genetic subtypes.
