Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the current manuscript, the authors use a previously recorded dataset to examine the influence of forward and backward
travelling alpha oscillations on human perception. Overall, the research question is interesting and follows recent trends in
the discussion about the functional role of alpha-band oscillations. However, the concept of travelling waves must be
explained better. The introduction covers the current state of research well, the methods and data analysis appear sound,
but better care should be taken for the correct description of independent and dependent variables. Moreover, the
interpretation and the discussion of the results need to be revised for internal consistency and line of argumentation. In
general, we suggest more clarity and precision in the description of the research. Please find a more detailed discussion of
major and minor issues below. Page and line numbers refer to the PDF provided for review. 
Major: 
Methods, line 501ff: Please describe the concept of travelling waves in more detail. For example, it would be helpful to show
a figure of the theoretical phase precession along the different electrodes, what role the factor time plays in this concept, and
what is meant by “the lower and upper quadrants” (line 509). In addition, we suggest a clearer demonstration of the traveling
waves you report in your data, how you calculated them, a clearer description and plot. Please also describe what you mean
by the “amount of waves”: Is this the number of waves, their amplitude, or something else? Later (e.g., figure 3), you use
“magnitude of waves”, what is meant here? Finally, and related to figure 2, please explain how the factor “time” is relevant
here: In the legend of figure 1, you mention that the travelling wave analysis focused on the -600 – 0 ms pre-stimulus
interval. This information is missing here. Does the same interval apply to all analyses? You mention that you picked the
maximum values of the 2D-FFT for further analyses, but then the time axis in Figure 2 is misleading. Showing intermediate
steps of the data analysis will greatly facilitate understanding your data processing pipeline. 
Methods, line 538ff: What is the dependent variable here? How did you define the group assignment? You mention that your
earlier studies used the parieto-occipital alpha power difference between high- and low-probability trials as a selection
criterion. Did you use the same approach here? If so, how was the selection performed (e.g., with a median split, or with a
fixed threshold)? You mention parts of this in line 204, but this information is missing from the methods section. Depending
on the parameter used for the group assignment, this could lead to a circular argument, where the groups are defined based

the bias and in which direction?).

(Remarks to the Author) 
This study investigated how travelling waves are influenced by expectation-based triggers in perceptual decision-making.
Specifically, the authors examined how the directionality of alpha-wave propagation is modulated by cue presentation. They
reported enhanced top-down alpha travelling waves associated with higher-probability cues. Furthermore, they claimed
individual differences in travelling-wave propagation: participants who relied more on prior expectations exhibited stronger
travelling-wave patterns compared with those who focused more on sensory inputs. 
Although the paper is interesting, I have a concern regarding the method used to extract travelling waves. While the authors
cited previous studies, I remain unconvinced that scalp EEG is sufficient to demonstrate genuine propagation of travelling
waves. How can one be certain that the observed patterns truly reflect travelling waves, given the crude spatial resolution of
scalp EEG? This seems to me a fundamental issue. Spatial resolution is limited, and interpretational ambiguity persists due
to volume conduction, reference choice, and source mixing. Combining 64-channel EEG with current source density (CSD)
or source reconstruction, together with surrogate testing, would greatly improve the reliability of travelling-wave analyses
derived from scalp EEG. Without applying such methods, it is difficult for me to be fully convinced by the details of the
reported results. 
Line 360- 
"Previous studies have shown that a single or pair of dipolar sources can >reproduce scalp voltage patterns 360 resembling
traveling waves propagating in a forward (i.e., occipital to frontal) direction. Although such a configuration of dipoles could in
theory generate a wave
‐
like pattern, it would be unlikely to show a functionally relevant phase gradient in both directions
modulated by cue predictability." 
In addition to the methodological issues regarding spatial resolution, the distinction between the travelling-wave view and
the oscillatory synchronization of dipoles view is important. Considering large-scale networks, one may ask whether the
observed patterns truly reflect sequential propagation of travelling waves, or rather synchrony and functional connectivity
between distant networks. The authors might consider discussing this alternative interpretation in more depth. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript examines how alpha-band traveling waves in the human brain convey predictive information during
perception. The authors used a visual detection task with probabilistic cues (low, neutral, or high likelihood of target) and a
quite large sample of 80 (!) participants. With EEG they focus on alpha oscillations (8–14 Hz), analyzing their propagation
direction across scalp electrode arrays from frontal to occipital regions. The key finding is an increase in “backward”
traveling alpha waves after predictive cues, especially in the hemisphere contralateral to the expected stimulus. These
backward (posterior-to-anterior) waves were linked with a preparatory top-down signal, and their strength correlated with the
degree of criterion shift induced by the cues. In contrast, participants less influenced by the cues (the “sensory-driven” group)
showed more forward-traveling waves and weaker criterion shifts. The authors also include a mediation analysis suggesting
that backward-wave activity influences decision bias via modulation of parieto-occipital alpha amplitude. In general, I find
that the study results support a predictive coding framework in which alpha-band dynamics implement top-down
expectations to bias perceptual decisions. The manuscript is clearly written and tries to answer to 
a timely question in
cognitive neuroscience, i.e. how predictive coding signals are implemented in neuronal oscillations. 
The study makes a new contribution by showing that prestimulus alpha traveling waves are modulated by explicit
expectations on a trial-by-trial basis, extending prior work on alpha power. The EEG analysis is interesting, as forward and
backward traveling waves are quantified across electrode lines and the authors show a lateralized increase in contralateral
backward waves, well aligned with the task design. Importantly, the strength of these backward waves correlates with
individual differences in cue-induced bias, which demonstrates their functional importance. 
The manuscript appears quite mature and polished, with structure and argumentation clear and well-balanced. It gives the
impression that it already went through few rounds elsewhere, maybe in other journals, and that process has likely helped to
sharpen the clarity ... 
Minor comments: 
I have only minor issues and clarifications. Addressing these would help improve clarity, but they do not require major new
analyses. 
* On lines 105–106 it is noted that all targets were presented in the lower left visual field. This means the contralateral
hemisphere is always the right one in this study. The authors acknowledge this later, but it would be useful to underline this
as a limitation for generalization, since only one hemifield was tested. 
* The terminology of “forward” and “backward” traveling waves could be explained more clearly at the start. Forward is
described as anterior-to-posterior and backward as posterior-to-anterior, but in predictive coding this mapping might confuse
some readers. A short clarification that “forward” here refers to top-down and “backward” to bottom-up (or vice versa,
depending on definition) would avoid misunderstandings. 
* In the Results Bayes factors are reported alongside p-values, which is very good. To help readers, maybe at least once
remind what counts as substantial evidence in BF terms (e.g. BF > 3 = "moderate evidence"). Adding a small parenthetical

remark when reporting a BF would help those less familiar with Bayesian stats. 
In sum, the strengths are much stronger than these few small points. The manuscript is quite solid and already well ironed
out. I recommend acceptance after minor revision, mainly to polish clarity for readers.

We apologize to the reviewers for the confusion about the concept of traveling waves. We have 
now included new figure (Figure 5) to describe better 1) how we define travelling waves along the 
line of electrodes and 2) how the 2D-FFT methods quantify them over time. The new plots clarify 
that we quantify them over a time window, in which we create images with electrodes as the x-axis 
and time as the y-axis. The 2D-FFT provides a 2D spectrum, in which the quadrants represent the 
spectral power for backward and forward waves as a function of temporal frequency.   
We report here the new included figure along with the caption: 
 
Figure 5. Waves analysis.  
To quantify the direction of travelling waves, prestimulus (-600 – 0ms) EEG signals were arranged 
as space x time maps by stacking seven electrodes aligned along the occipital–frontal axis, and a 
2D Fast Fourier Transform (2D-FFT) was computed on sliding 500-ms windows (step: 125 ms). In 
the resulting 2D spectrum, temporal frequency is represented on the horizontal axis, whereas

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

3 
 
spatial frequency reflects the phase gradient across electrodes. Because waves travelling from 
occipital to frontal sites (forward, FW) and from frontal to occipital sites (backward, BW) project 
onto opposite quadrants of the 2D-FFT, the maximum power in the lower and upper quadrants 
provides a directional estimate of propagation. To dissociate true spatial propagation from non-
spatial spectral structure (e.g., 1/f and local oscillatory pattern), the same analysis was repeated 
after randomly shuffling electrode order within each line, which preserves temporal dynamics but 
removes spatial phase structure. Directional wave strength was then computed as a log-ratio 
between the ordered (FW, BW) and shuffled (FWss, BWss) spectra for each frequency between 2 
and 45 Hz. This procedure was applied independently to each electrode. Finally, FW and BW wave 
power was averaged within the theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–14 Hz), and beta (15–30 Hz) bands to 
obtain frequency-specific travelling-wave indices for statistical comparison across conditions. 
We also thank the reviewers for pointing out the inconsistency in terminology. We agree that using 
multiple terms ("amount of waves," "magnitude of waves," "wave strength") interchangeably 
throughout the manuscript could create confusion in the reader. To clarify: all these terms refer to 
the same measure: wave power, quantified in decibels (dB) through the log-ratio of the 2D-FFT 
power spectrum between the actual electrode configuration and the shuffled baseline (see 
Methods, Traveling wave analysis). This metric reflects the spatial propagation pattern at a given 
frequency, with higher values indicating stronger directional propagation of oscillatory activity. We 
have now standardized the terminology throughout the manuscript to consistently use "wave 
power" (or "traveling wave power" where appropriate).  
We have also systematically added the qualifier "prestimulus" when referring to alpha power and 
traveling wave measures throughout the manuscript. We agree that this clarification is critical for 
two reasons: (a) it specifies the temporal window of analysis (-600 to 0 ms relative to stimulus 
onset), and (b) it emphasizes that these neural dynamics precede the presentation of decision-
relevant information, which is fundamental to our interpretation of traveling waves as preparatory 
mechanisms rather than stimulus-driven responses.

Regarding the group assignment, as in our previous work (Tarasi et al., 2022), we employed a 
median-split procedure based on the difference in parieto-occipital alpha power between high- and 
low-probability cues to define two subgroups of participants ("prior-driven " and "sensory-driven "). 
Specifically, we computed individual difference scores (high-probability minus low-probability alpha 
power) across parieto-occipital electrodes during the prestimulus interval (-600 to 0 ms relative to 
stimulus onset). Participants above the median were classified as prior-driven profile, while those 
below were classified as sensory-driven profile. Critically, group assignment was determined by the 
relative modulation of alpha activity as a function of cue probability, not by absolute alpha power 
levels or overall alpha strength. This approach isolates individual differences in the ability to flexibly 
tune posterior alpha oscillations to probabilistic context. 
Regarding the dependent variable in the traveling wave analysis, wave power was analyzed 
separately for forward (FW) and backward (BW) directions across hemispheres and cue 
conditions. We have now added complete methodological details to the Methods section: 
This measure captures the relative modulation of alpha power as a function of cue probability, 
rather than absolute amplitude differences.  To examine whether these interindividual differences in 
prior-driven processing were reflected in traveling wave dynamics, we divided participants into two 
groups using a median split based on their alpha power modulation index (following the same 
grouping procedure as in(Tarasi et al. 2023; Tarasi, di Pellegrino, et Romei 2022) ). Participants 
with alpha modulation values above the median were classified as the "prior-driven" group, while 
those below the median were classified as the "stimulus-driven" group. The dependent variable in 
the subsequent analyses was traveling wave power, which quantifies a fundamentally different 
property of neural oscillations than the spectral power modulation used for group assignment.  
We also appreciate the reviewers’ attention to potential circularity. However, the grouping variable 
(alpha power modulation) and the dependent measure (traveling wave strength) are conceptually 
and empirically independent, for the following reasons: the two measures capture fundamentally 
different properties of neural oscillations:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

6 
 
 Alpha power modulation (grouping variable): Reflects local spectral changes in oscillatory 
amplitude; an index of regional cortical excitability that is tuned by probabilistic context. 
 Traveling wave strength (dependent variable): Quantifies the spatiotemporal propagation of 
oscillatory activity across the scalp; an index of large-scale directional coordination and 
information flow across cortical regions. 
In other words, alpha power modulation reflects the extent to which local circuits respond to 
contextual priors, whereas traveling wave strength indicates how predictive signals propagate 
through the cortical hierarchy. Critically, at the empirical level, the two measures dissociated in their 
sensitivity to experimental manipulations, ruling out any trivial relationship: 
1. Prestimulus alpha power showed robust cue-related modulation: prior-driven exhibited 
strong desynchronization between low- and high-probability trials, consistent with their 
classification criterion. 
2. Prestimulus traveling wave strength, however, showed no cue-related modulation in either 
group. Instead, waves were lateralized, with stronger activity over the hemisphere 
contralateral to the cued location; however, this lateralization pattern was independent of 
cue probability. 
This dissociation is particularly evident in the prior-driven group: despite showing clear cue-related 
alpha desynchronization (the basis of their classification), they exhibited no corresponding 
modulation of traveling wave strength across these same conditions. If wave detection was merely 
a consequence of alpha power changes, we would expect parallel modulations. Instead, the two 
measures varied independently. 
This is also important in relation to the reviewers’ important technical point: could stronger waves in 
one group simply reflect better SNR due to higher alpha amplitude? Our data exclude this 
possibility: if wave detection were merely an artifact of alpha amplitude, we would expect prior-
driven to show weaker waves in the high-probability condition (where alpha desynchronization is 
exhibited and thus lower amplitude) compared to the low-probability condition (where alpha power

is higher). However, wave strength showed no cue-related modulation in either group, despite the 
clear power differences between conditions in the prior-driven task. This dissociation demonstrates 
that wave detection is robust across amplitude variations and not confounded by SNR. 
Finally, the observed pattern, lateralization without cue modulation, aligns precisely with our 
theoretical framework: 
 Alpha traveling waves serve as a conduit for transmitting top-down predictive signals 
toward the relevant sensory areas, regardless of the specific content of those priors. 
Hence, they show spatial selectivity (lateralization to the attended hemifield) but not 
probability-dependent modulation. 
 Posterior alpha power, in contrast, is shaped by prior content and serves as the marker of 
prior incorporation into the perceptual decision. Hence, it shows cue-related modulation in 
individuals who adopt a prior-driven strategy. 
This interpretation is further supported by: 
 The positive correlation between prestimulus wave strength and behavioral integration of 
priors (criterion shift) 
 The mediation analysis showing that prestimulus alpha power mediates the relationship 
between backward waves power and behavioral prior use. 
In summary, the grouping variable and the dependent measure are both conceptually and 
empirically independent. Their dissociation, particularly the presence of strong cue-driven alpha 
power modulation without corresponding cue-driven wave modulation in prior-driven individuals, 
rules out circularity and SNR confounds. Instead, our findings support the conclusion that individual 
differences in alpha power modulation reflect distinct computational strategies for incorporating 
probabilistic context into perceptual decisions, while alpha traveling waves index the large-scale 
propagation of predictive signals across the cortical hierarchy, serving as a mechanism for spatially 
routing top-down information independent of its specific probabilistic content. Together, alpha 
power modulation and traveling wave propagation capture complementary aspects of predictive

We thank the reviewers for this insightful comment. We fully agree that, taken in isolation, a 
criterion value of 0 indicates a neutral decision bias. However, in our paradigm, the neutral cue 
condition serves as a more appropriate reference point for interpreting shifts in response criterion 
across probability contexts. As shown in Figure 1D, the criterion in the neutral condition lies 
approximately midway between the values observed in the low- and high-probability conditions, 
and overall reflects a slightly conservative baseline; a common pattern in perceptual detection 
tasks where sensory evidence is highly uncertain and participants tend to adopt a cautious 
response strategy (Rahnev and Denison, Behavioral and brain sciences, 2018). Within this 
framework, the high-probability cue induces a significant liberal shift relative to the neutral baseline, 
while the low-probability cue elicits a more conservative shift, indicating that participants adaptively 
modulated their decision bias in accordance with the probabilistic information provided by the cue. 
Thus, even though a criterion of 0 is formally unbiased, it represents the relative liberal extreme 
within the context of our task, where the neutral baseline itself is conservative. In this sense, we 
would like to clarify that we have already employed comparative phrasing throughout the 
manuscript to indicate that bias direction is assessed relative to the other conditions rather than to 
an absolute zero reference. Specifically, in the behavioral results section (and in the caption of the 
figure 1), we systematically describe criterion shifts as "more liberal" or "more conservative", 
precisely to emphasize the relative nature of these modulations. This comparative terminology was 
deliberately chosen from the outset to avoid any implication of absolute bias categories and to 
accurately reflect that our interpretation is grounded in within-subject shifts across probabilistic
