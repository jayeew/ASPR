Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Chey et al is an original, elegant and complete study reporting a role for ASC in promoting PDAC
initiation and progression. The findings are shown to be relevant in human cancer and have therapeutic implications since
blockade of extracelllar ASC partially recapitulates the phenotypes observed in total and conditional KO animals. The study
also brings mechanistic data, showing that ASC impacts on metabolic programming of pancreatic malignant cells. This said,
the study would benefit from establishing causal links between and parameters such as proliferation and/or control of
apoptosis within malignant cells. 
Major comments. 
1) Is metabolic reprogramming needed to support ASC-dependent malignant cell proliferation? 
2) Are oxygen consumption and extracellular acidification rates modulated by ASC in malignant cells? 
3) Does extracellular ASC control OXPHOS and glycolysis? 
Minor issues. 
1) Assessing mitochoindrial superoxide anion production is needed to link mitochondrial dysfunction to ROS production. 
2) The qulality of Figure 1E-F should be improved. The fluorescence is saturated and it is difficult to assess colocalization in
those conditions. 
3) The discussion is quite long. It should be shortened. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study by Chey et al., entitled “Cancer cell-intrinsic ASC links innate immunity with mitochondrial metabolism in driving
inflammasome-associated pancreatic tumorigenesis”, the authors convincingly demonstrate that ASC (apoptosis-associated
speck-like protein containing a CARD) plays a critical role in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) by modulating the
tumorigenic process through three independent approaches: global genetic ablation, epithelial-specific conditional
knockout, and nanobody-mediated targeting of extracellular ASC in vivo. This multifaceted strategy unambiguously
establishes the relevance of ASC in PDAC development. However, despite this strength, several important aspects of the
analyses and their interpretations are either insufficiently developed, potentially misleading, or require further clarification or
experimental validation to reinforce the conclusions drawn. 
Major comments. 
1. The authors analyze PYCARD/ACS expression in human datasets. Is ACS expression associated more to a
classical/ductal tumor subtype or also to a basal subtype? The authors should probe other datasets (i.e., Bailey et al.),
classifying patients into subtypes to determine if ACS expression is more enriched in the classical versus the basal subtype,
for example. 
2. Fig 2. The authors state that “that elevated activation of ASC-containing inflammasomes is also a feature of the KPC

PDAC model.” However, ASC staining in KPC mouse at early, intermediate and late stages is needed to really understand
the dynamics of ACS expression and its kinetics across KPC tumor evolution. 
3. Do the KPC ACKO and ACSfl/fl mouse models present any adverse effects? Modulation of the inflammasome pathway
would likely make these mice more susceptible to bacterial infections. Information alone these lines should be mentioned in
the M&Ms or cited if this information exists. 
4. Figs. 3D & 5H: In both panels, a similar analysis to compare both models should be performed. Based on the data
presented, it appears as though the KPC and KPC APCKO models used in Fig 3D are less aggressive than those shown in
Fig 5H, yet PanINs were not assessed in Fig. 3D. 
5. Why is the tumor evolution very different between the ACS total ablation (KO) KPC model and the ACS conditional (fl/fl)
KPC model. Particularly for the wt KPC mice, where in the ACS total ablation KPC model shown in Fig 2-3, the mice were
sacrificed at 20 weeks, but in the ACS conditional KPC model, the mice were sacrificed at 12 weeks. In Figure 2-3, the KPC
wt mice used and scarified at 20weeks have a mean tumor weight of 0.45g and 60% area of lesions, but the KPC wt mice
used in the comparison with the KPC APC fl/fl mice in Figure 5 have a mean tumor weight of 1g and 70% area of lesions at
12 weeks. That is a big discrepancy between the control KPC wt mice for both comparisons. All models (KPC wt, KPC
APCKO KPC APCfl/fl) need to be analyzed at the same time point- either 12 or 20 weeks. Are the difference due to mixed
129Sv × C57BL/6 background? 
6. Page 13: The statement “These data therefore implicate the pancreatic ductal epithelium as the predominant cellular
source of tumor-promoting ASC inflammasome activation in PDAC.” Is overstated. It appears as through the cellular source
of tumor-promoting ASC inflammasome activation is also TME-based. 
7. The image shown in Fig 6C is misleading and not representative of the tumor burden calculated in Fig 6D, where in the
pancreata analyzed more than 50% was moderate PDAC. 
8. The authors performed RNA-seq comparing KPC and KPC:ASCKO mice, but the complete ablation of APC in the
KPC:ASCKO mice can confound the analysis. Specifically, in KPC:ASCKO mice, as shown in Figure 3, the pancreas is
practically normal, with very little tumor burden. Thus, it is almost like comparing KPC to a normal pancreas. Likewise, the
total ablation of APC in the KPC:ASCKO mice could affect the results. The authors should confirm that the effects observed
on mt- and nuclear-encoded genes are also observed in the APCfl/fl mice at 20 weeks (not 12 weeks), where one would
assume more tumor burden in the KPC APCfl/fl mice. RTqPCR for the target genes can be performed in lieu of another
RNAseq. Likewise, the authors need to sort tumor cells from both KPC and KPC:ASCfl/fl mice to even more specifically
show that the effects are tumor cell specific and not influenced by the TME. 
9. Fig 8. In line with the above comments, the data shown in Fig 8 should also be performed with 20 week old KPC wt and
KPC fl/fl mice or KPC tumor cells isolated from the pancreas of these two models. 
10. For many of the statistical comparisons, the KPC APCKO (and future KPC APCfl/fl) groups should be compared to the wt
group. The fact that for many panels, there is likely no significant difference between KPC APCKO and wt suggests even
more that the lack of tumor development in the KPC APCKO mice results in a pancreas that is almost normal, which
undermines the analyses to determine how ASC promotes PDAC via molecular pathways. 
11. Fig 8: The authors performed multiplexed immunofluorescence on KPC versus WT pancreas sections to determine the
spatial cellular context of the changes in mitochondrial biogenesis (and protein expression of UQCRC2, NDUFB8, etc)
within the pancreatic TME and their localization with activated ASC-containing inflammasomes. It is necessary to perform
these analyses as well in the areas of poor, moderate and/or well PDAC in the KPC APC fl/fl pancreas and in the pancreas
from the VHHACS-treated mice to show that the proteins and parameters are not observed in tumor cells in the absence of
APV. The analyses in the pancreas of VHHACS-treated mice are particularly relevant to show that the MOA of the treatment
is similar. The authors do not take full advantage of the pancreas samples from the VHHACS-treated mice to support their
claims and mechanistic analyses. 
12. Discussion: The authors state in the Discussion “novel cancer (epithelial) cell-intrinsic role for ASC-containing
inflammasomes in promoting the initiation and progression of PDAC”; however, the data, as is, does not really support this
claim. The completion of the above experiments in the KPC APC fl/fl mice and in the pancreas from the VHHACS-treated
mice will better support this claim. 
13. Discussion: Limitations of the study need to be discussed. 
Minor points 
1. The images in Fig 1 E-F and accompanying supplementary images are over exposed. Lower exposure images should be
shown. 
2. Panel 5H and 6D: “PanIN” instead of “PanIn” in the legend. 
3. Mouse Background Clarification: The use of a mixed 129Sv × C57BL/6 background is briefly mentioned. Given that this
can influence tumor progression and immune responses, the authors should clarify whether littermate controls were used
throughout and whether background effects may explain phenotype differences. 
4. Nanobody Specificity: More data or citation supporting the specificity of VHHASC for extracellular ASC specks in vivo
would strengthen the therapeutic relevance and rule out off-target effects. 
5. PRR Partner for ASC: Although the Discussion mentions the uncertainty regarding the PRR responsible for ASC
inflammasome activation in PDAC, no experiments or strong hypotheses are proposed. Even correlative analysis of PRR
expression in epithelial vs. immune compartments would be informative.

Major comments 
1) Is metabolic reprogramming needed to support ASC -dependent malignant cell 
proliferation? 
Author response: 
We appreciate the Reviewer ’s insightful comment which is based on our in vivo data (in the 
KPC PDAC mouse model) demonstrating that ASC -mediated pancreatic tumourigenesis 
(incorporating augmented cellular proliferation in the pancreatic epithelium) is associated with 
metabolic reprogramming. Given the brevity of this comment, we have assumed that the 
Reviewer is  now requesting in vitro  data (consistent with their other comments below). 
Accordingly, we have performed experimentation with two widely-used transformed human 
PDAC cell lines, PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2, whereby we have knocked -down ASC (encoded 
by PYCARD) with siRNA  (ASCKD). As shown in the new Supplementary Figure s S14a-h, 
successful siRNA-mediated knockdown of ASC expression (along with the expected reduced 
cleaved Caspase -1 levels) significantly reduced cell proliferation , colony formation  and 
viability coincident with reduced lactate levels and increased mitochondrial DNA copy number 
and gene expression in human PDAC  cells. This is consistent with our in vivo  data (e.g. 
Figures 4a, 5i, 7, 8c-g) that genetic ablation of ASC in KPC mice reduced cellular proliferation

within the pancreatic epithelium which associated with elevated mitochondrial gene networks 
and DNA copy number, and a reversion of energy metabolism from aerobic glycolysis (as seen 
in cancer cells; Warburg effect) towards mitochondrial respiration (as seen in non-cancerous 
(wild-type) mice with normal, lowly-proliferating cells). 
To further assess whether  metabolic reprogramming in PDAC cells aligns with  ASC-
mediated cell proliferation  and/or viability , we next treated ASCKD and control (scramble 
siRNA) transfected PANC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells with two glycolysis inhibitors, STF-31 and 
2-deoxyglucose (2 -DG), noting that cancer cells heavily rely on aerobic glycolysis for 
proliferation and viability (survival). In  contrast to  negative control (scramble siRNA) 
transfected PDAC cells, ASCKD PDAC cells were markedly less sensitive to impaired viability 
(proliferation) induced by glycolysis inhibitors  (Supplementary Figure S14 j), which supports 
the notion that ASC deficiency switches the cellular energy needs for viability and proliferation 
towards OXPHOS (i.e. mitochondrial respiration).  
While being mindful of the Reviewer ’s comment below to shorten the Discussion, we 
have briefly referred to these new human cell line data  in the Discussion on pages 19 (lines

419-422) and Methods (pages 26-27, lines 602-631, 788, 794-796, 802-803) sections. 
 
2) Are oxygen consumption and extracellular acidification rates modulated by ASC in 
malignant cells? 
Author response: 
A key finding of our study was that ASC -containing inflammasomes promoted mitochondrial 
dysfunction resulting in a bias towards aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) in PDAC, a s 
evidenced by  the blockade of ASC (in the KPC PDAC model)  which switched energy 
metabolism back towards mitochondrial-driven OXPHOS (i.e. cellular respiration). To address 
this comment by the Reviewer, we have therefore performed the Seahorse XF Mito Stress 
Test to measure key parameters of mitochondrial respiration (oxygen consumption rate; OCR) 
in the above human PDAC cells subjected to siRNA -mediated knockdown of ASC (ASCKD).

As shown in the new Supplementary Figures 14k and l, both basal OCR and maximal OCR 
capacity (induced by the proton ionophore FCCP , a potent uncoupler of mitochondrial 
OXPHOS) were increased in ASCKD versus control (scramble siRNA) transfected human 
PDAC cells, which further supports our notion that ASC deficiency during PDAC (e.g. in 
KPC:ASCKO mice) rewires energy metabolism back towards mitochondrial respiration. 
Furthermore, the spare respiratory capacity (SRC) of ASCKD PDAC cells was also elevated 
versus control PDAC transfected cells (Supplementary Figures 14k and l), indicating that ASC 
deficiency enhanced mitochondrial fitness in response to energy demands. We also refer to 
our new above-mentioned data (in response to comment #1) showing that extracellular lactate 
production – which is a primary source of extracellular acidification during glycolysis – was 
significantly reduced in PDAC cells upon siRNA-mediated knockdown of ASC (Supplementary 
Figure 14 h). Collectively, these data indicate that oxygen consumption and extracell ular 
acidification rates are indeed modulated by ASC in transformed (pancreatic) cancer cells.  
These new data are now briefly referred to in the Discussion (lines 483-485) on page 21, as 
well as in the Results (page 18, lines 414-422) and Methods (page 35, lines 821-829) sections.
