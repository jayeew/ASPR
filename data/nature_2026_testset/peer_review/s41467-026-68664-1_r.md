Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a study on the circular hydrogen production process using liquid Ga, integrating photothermal corrosion
and electrochemical reduction. The concept of regenerating Ga from GaOOH via an energy-efficient pathway is interesting
and addresses challenges in sustainable hydrogen generation. However, several mechanistic ambiguities, insufficient
experimental evidence, and incomplete data interpretation weaken the current manuscript’s impact. I recommend major
revision to address the following concerns, which are essential for validating the proposed mechanisms and strengthening
the practical implications of this work. 
1. The authors utilize sonication and photothermal effects to activate Ga surfaces for corrosion, achieving rapid H
₂
production (96 mL/90 min). However, the manuscript lacks a critical comparison with the "molecular scissors" mechanism
reported by Duan’s team (ACS Appl. Mater. Interfaces 2023, DOI: 10.1021/acsami.3c15841). In their work, liquid Ga-In
eutectic (eGaIn) dynamically captures oxygen atoms from molecules (e.g., H
₂
O, CH
₃
OH) via spontaneous surface oxidation,
enabling molecular clipping and H
₂
 production without external energy input (e.g., light or heat). Key distinctions to address:
Add a comparative table/schematic highlighting these differences. 
2. Quantify H
₂
 production rates (mL/g·h) under comparable conditions (e.g., Ga mass, reaction time) to demonstrate the
trade-offs between energy input (light vs. none) and efficiency. 
3. Discuss whether combining photothermal activation with Duan’s spontaneous exfoliation (e.g., using eGaIn instead of
pure Ga) could enhance durability. 
4. The single-cycle reduction analysis (EDS/morphology) does not demonstrate long-term recyclability. Multi-cycle
experiments (≥10 oxidation-reduction loops) with XPS/EIS are required to assess Ga’s structural stability, surface
passivation, and activity decay. Without cyclic data, claims of sustainability may lack credibility. 
5. The alternative alkaline dissolution-electrodeposition route is mentioned while not quantitatively compared to the acidic
electrochemical reduction method. A life cycle assessment (LCA) or energy density analysis (kWh/kg Ga) is needed to justify
the superiority of the acidic pathway in terms of energy efficiency and cost. 
6. The cost-saving argument relies on vague assertions rather than quantifiable metrics. A levelized cost of hydrogen
(LCOH) model incorporating Ga recycling efficiency, energy inputs, and material loss rates must be included to substantiate
economic feasibility. 
Other small suggestions
：
 
In Situ Characterization: Use in situ synchrotron XRD or AFM to track GaOOH→Ga phase transformation and interfacial
dynamics during reduction. 
Byproduct Identification: Analyze electrolytes via HPLC/ion chromatography to evaluate reaction selectivity and side
products. 
Cyclic Performance Testing: Quantify Ga mass loss and H
₂
 yield decay over ≥5 cycles to assess practical scalability.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents a novel circular H
₂
 production process based on the photothermal oxidation of liquid Ga and its
electrochemical regeneration, contributing to the advancement of sustainable hydrogen energy technologies. However,
further validation of the regenerated Ga’s H
₂

experiment with halogen lamp illumination without Ga particles (equivalent to Fig. S2) is missing. To quantitatively assess

photocatalytic research. The inclusion of xenon lamp-based data is essential for validating the claims made in the
manuscript.

where H

strengthen the argument for GaOOH’s role in the reaction mechanism.

 production capability
of the regenerated Ga has not been evaluated. To confirm the feasibility of the proposed circular process, the following

In addition, we would like to clarify that our process exclusively generates hydrogen at ambient pressure

We also demonstrated that the generated GaOOH can be reduced back into metallic Ga at above 95% 
(please see R1-5), enabling a circular process with a round-trip efficiency of 57.6%. Therefore, GaOOH 
should not be regarded as a commodity product, but rather as an intermediate state of Ga within the 
process. 
Regarding the scalability and economic of the process please see the techno economic analysis Table

R1-2 Line 64: According to the cited reference 14, sonication produces nanorods of gallium that are 
more efficient in producing GaO(OH) than are photothermal generated Ga nanospheres. For their 
current work, the authors produce polydisperse Ga nanospheres by sonication and the nanospheres are

The authors report a 12.9% maximum efficiency for their hydrogen production but don’t show how this

Ans: Thank you for your comment. 
Our process involves the synthesis of gallium nanospheres (Ga⁰) in ethanol (see Methods), followed by 
the photothermal oxidation  reaction in water.  We therefore cannot consider Ga nanorods, as they 
already represent an oxidized form of Ga. 
The electrochemical reduction efficiency is now detailed lines 104 to 108 (page 3): “Considering the 
molar masses of Ga as 69.723 g/mol and H₂ as 2.016 g/mol, for 1 kg of Ga an amount of H₂ eq ual to 
43.36 g can be produced with the potential energy of 1.44 kWh 28. The recovery energy for the 
electrowinning of Ga is approximately 2.50 kW/kg 29. Thus, the round -trip efficiency is 1.44 kWh/2.50 
kWh × 100 = 57.6% for our circular process, considering natural solar irradiation as a free source of 
energy.” 
The 12.9% efficiency value refers to the overall circular efficiency for H₂ production, including the 
electrochemical reduction process. The photothermal conversion efficiency of Ga nanospheres was 
reported as 25.33 ± 7.42% in reference 14 (DOI: 10.1039/c8nr08296k). This was presented in lines 147-
148 (page 4) as follows: “Considering the requirement of 57.6% electrochemical reduction efficiency, 
this amounts to the overall circular efficiency of 12.9% for H2 production.” 
We considered a light conversion to heat efficiency of 22.4% for the Ga particles following reference 
18, 26 and 30, and added the following for clarity (lines 144-147, page 4): “This experiment highlights 
the efficient light absorptio n of Ga particles, consistent with previous studies showing a comparable 
photothermal conversion efficiency comparable to gold nanoparticles of 20% to 30% 18,26,30. We use a 
rather conservative value of 22.5% in our calculations.” 
Thus, the overall efficiency is 22.4% x 57.6% ≈ 12.9% 
The overall circular efficiency of 12.9% for our system compares well with other established renewable 
hydrogen technologies: 
 Photovoltaic + Electrolysis:  Single-junction solar modules (~20%) coupled with commercial 
electrolysers (60–70% including overpotentials) result in an overall efficiency of 12-15%. 
 Direct Photochemical Water Splitting: Well-studied systems such as TiO₂-based photocatalysts 
typically operate at <5% efficiency. 
 Multi-junction “Artificial Leaf ” Systems: Advanced laboratory designs, such as Nocera’s 
artificial leaf, report efficiencies of approximately 10%. 
Therefore, despite being at a proof -of-concept stage, our technology is already comparable to the best 
commercially relevant approach (PV + electrolysis) and surpasses direct photocatalytic water-splitting 
methods, while uniquely offering seawater compatibility and circular gallium regeneration. 
References for review only: 
H. Song, S. Luo, H. Huang, et al., ACS Energy Letters 2022, 7, 1043. 
D. Hansora, R. Mehrotra, E. Noh, et al., Nat. Commun. 2025, 16, 4186.

To answer your question “How much gallium would be needed for continuous supply to a 100W fuel 
cell, e.g. for an hour”, we conducted the following calculations: 
 A 100 W fuel cell  (low temperature fuel cell)  running for one hour requires 100 watt -hours 
(Wh) of energy. 
 Using the lower heating value (HHV) of hydrogen (39.4 kWh per kilogram), this corresponds 
to approximately 6.0 grams of hydrogen if we consider an efficiency of the fuel cell of 50-55%. 
 Our reaction is as follows: 2 Ga + 4 H₂O → 2 GaOOH + 3 H₂ 
 We would need 2 moles of Ga for 3 moles of H2 (corresponding to approximately 6 g of H2)

Levelized Cost of Hydrogen (LCOH) 
The calculation of the Levelized Cost of Hydrogen (LCOH) is equivalent to spreading every discounted 
cost the plant incurs over its lifetime across the total discounted value of the mass  of H₂ it produces. 
The LCOH expresses the minimum H2 sale price for which the present value of revenues exactly offsets 
the present value of all costs over the plant’s economic life , commonly referred to as the break-even 
price of H2.

mass of H2 each year. Every cost stream (CAPEX, OPEX, end -of-life) is converted to the same

Australian dollars based on a generic location in Australia , converted to U.S. dollars ($) as of the rate 
of July 2025 (1 AUD = 0.66  USD) and discounted at a project -specific real rate ( r). Solar electricity 
production is assumed during the day.
