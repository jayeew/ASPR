Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Reviewer Comments (Design and Statistical Considerations) 
Overall, this manuscript is well-written, clearly structured, and easy to follow. The long-term follow-up and randomized, multi-
center design are major strengths, and the trial addresses an important clinical question in the management of
nasopharyngeal carcinoma. I have several comments for the authors to consider: 
Clarification of Randomization Method 
The manuscript states that “Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to one of four blocks…,” but it is unclear what “four
blocks” means in this context. I assume this refers to permuted block randomization with a block size of four, but this should
be stated explicitly. If this is the case, please clarify the block size and the rationale for this choice, along with how
stratification by stage and hospital was incorporated into the randomization scheme. 
Sample Size and Power Justification 
The current description (“The trial required at least a sample size of 492 patients…”) is not standard for time-to-event non-
inferiority trials. A complete justification should include: the non-inferiority margin, type I error level, target power, the
difference in PFS at which power was calculated, the required number of events, and assumptions about accrual and follow-
up periods. This information is essential for assessing whether the study was adequately powered to meet its primary
objective. 
Non-Inferiority Margin 
The choice of a 10% absolute margin for 10-year PFS appears relatively wide, particularly given the high baseline survival
rate (~71%). While I defer to clinical experts for the ultimate acceptability of this margin, a brief rationale based on prior
literature, expected effect sizes, and clinical relevance would help readers interpret this choice. 
Multiplicity in Toxicity Comparisons 
Multiple toxicity endpoints were tested, with statistically significant results reported for grade 1–2 deafness/otitis and
peripheral neuropathy. No adjustment for multiple comparisons is mentioned. Given the number of toxicity outcomes
evaluated, these findings may not remain significant after correction for multiplicity. I recommend clarifying whether
multiplicity adjustments were considered and, if not, noting this as a limitation. 
Toxicity as a Motivation for Non-Inferiority Design 
The original rationale for pursuing a non-inferiority design was the expectation that lobaplatin-based therapy would offer a
better toxicity profile. However, the long-term toxicity data presented here do not clearly support this premise. A brief
discussion of potential reasons (e.g., limitations in long-term toxicity data collection, competing late effects) would help

contextualize the findings. 
Handling of Missing Data in Long-Term Follow-Up 
In a 10-year follow-up study, losses to follow-up and missing data are inevitable. The manuscript should report the number
(and percentage) of patients lost to follow-up, the reasons where available, and whether any sensitivity analyses (e.g., worst-
case scenario) were performed to assess the potential impact on the results.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents the final 10-year follow-up results of a randomized phase III non-inferiority trial comparing
lobaplatin versus cisplatin as induction and concurrent chemotherapy in locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (LA-
NPC). The updated analysis confirms the non-inferiority of lobaplatin with respect to progression-free survival, consistent
with their prior report, and demonstrates no significant differences in locoregional recurrence-free survival, distant
metastasis-free survival, or overall survival. Importantly, in addition to previously reported reductions in acute toxicities
(hematologic, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, nephrotoxicity), this long-term analysis highlights lower rates of late grade 1–2
ototoxicity and peripheral neuropathy with lobaplatin. This is clinically meaningful given the survivorship implications in LA-
NPC. This publication also adds invaluable insight into the long-term outcome of LA-NPC in the era of induction
chemotherapy. 
I have several points and suggestions for improvement: 
1. Statistical Analysis: The study design and the non-inferiority margin should be explicitly stated in the Methods section. 
2. Results: 
- Outcomes: Always good to indicate the additional number of events (both local and distant) were observed with the longer
follow-up since the last report? 
- This reviewer will also request that the investigators indicate the cumulative number of locoregional and distant relapse
events in both group. 
- Line 194: The statement that “in the per-protocol analysis, the PFS difference was 2.4% (95% CI -5.6% to 10.5%), which
was lower than the non-inferiority margin of 10%” is potentially misleading. The 95% CI should be higher than the pre-
specified margin. 
- Line 200: The analysis should be described as univariable and multivariable, instead of univariate and multivariate. 
- Line 214: The value of “115%” is likely a typo 
3. Discussion 
- Line 229: The authors should consider specifying progression-free survival (primary endpoint of this study) rather than
“survival” 
- This study used a “de-escalated” chemotherapy schedule with 2 cycles of IC and 2 cycles of concurrent chemotherapy, in
place of the commonly used regimen of 3 cycles IC+ 3 cycles concurrent. Furthermore, the control arm was PF, rather than
the current standard GP or taxane-based triplet chemotherapy (per NCCN, CSCO guidelines). The implications should be
addressed explicitly in the discussion section. 
4. 
Follow-up and Missing Data 
- Attrition is a potential issue for long term follow-up. The authors should provide information on loss to follow-up and the
extent of missing toxicity data. 
- It would be more informative if the authors can provide a breakdown of progression and/or death events. 
5. 
Toxicity Reporting 
- For such a study, the key would be long-term toxicities. In particular, this reviewer would be interested to assess the long-
term effects on nephrotoxicity between lobaplatin vs cisplatin. The reporting of late adverse events deserves further
elaboration. Specifically, can the authors specify the assessment scale used for late toxicities? The RTOG/ EORTC late
toxicity grading 1 did not capture peripheral neuropathy and ototoxicity (which are the two main findings here) (appendix P32
in original publication). This information is critical for readers to interpret the clinical benefit of lobaplatin. 
- Please indicate the number of patients assessed for toxicity at each follow-up timepoint. 
- How was follow-up ensured for monitoring of late effects? 
- As this reviewer alluded to, was nephrotoxicity assessed since this is one of the main debilitating long-term toxicity of
cisplatin (Grant, et al. JAMA Oncology, 2025). 
- Reporting of the “worst grade toxicity” may not fully capture the clinical relevance of long-term adverse effects in NPC
survivors. For example, ototoxicity can fluctuate and may partially recover 2. Also, peripheral neuropathy may also improve
over time, although there may not be complete recovery 3. To better depict these dynamics, the authors may consider using
time stream plots or longitudinal toxicity curves to show how these late toxicities evolve. 
Without those, it is a missed opportunity for the readers to appraise the long-term “benefits”, if any, of lobaplatin and its
advantage over cisplatin. In fact, based on the key results highlighted in Table 2, this reviewer may contest that there are
little to no added benefits of administering lobaplatin vs cisplatin. Compliance may be improved with lobaplatin, but yet with
the reduced compliance, the outcomes and late toxicities appear comparable, and not necessarily fewer than cisplatin.

Which brings me to my key point – Cisplatin remains the dominant platinum agent in head and neck cancer. Thus far,
substitutes like nedaplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin have been shown to be “comparable” in clinical trials, but most have not
penetrated the clinic. In fact, for cisplatin eligible patients, cisplatin remains the preferred choice. The investigators ought to
discuss why this may be the case. Perhaps, the long term data of this trial will be helpful to inform the community about the
cross-utility of the platinum class of drugs.

This study employed a computer-generated randomization sequence based on a block 13 
design with a block size of four. For both clinical implementation and statistical 14 
considerations, we believe that a block size of four represents an optimal compromise for 15 
the following reasons 1-3: (1) ensures group balance—block randomization with a block 16 
size of four effectively prevents imbalances in the number of participants between 17 
treatment groups; (2) controls for known and unknown confounders—keeping treatment 18 
groups balanced with respect to predefined variables (e.g., sex, age, and disease stage) 19 
reduces the risk of confounding bias; (3) improves statistical power—balanced allocation 20 
across treatment groups enhances the statistical power of the trial, making the results more 21 
reliable; and (4) allows prespecification of the randomization scheme—unlike some 22 
adaptive randomization methods, permuted block randomization schemes can be fully 23 
prespecified before the trial begins, which facilitates implementation, arrangement, and 24 
management.25 
In our trial, the disease stage and hospital status were heterogeneous variables that could 26 
significantly influence the treatment allocation. An unbalanced distribution of these 27 
variables across treatment groups would compromise the validity of the comparison of the 28 
outcomes between the groups. Therefore, we adopted stratified randomization, a widely 29
