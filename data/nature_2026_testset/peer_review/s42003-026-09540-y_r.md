Reviewers' comments: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Aljedani et al presents a structural and biophysical investigation of how SH3 domains allosterically activate PI3K. The paper
includes a large amount of experimental work and the conclusion is potentially interesting in terms of understanding the
proteins investigated - but it probably do not rise to a new type of mechanism – which seems to be what the authors reach
for. However, the data is rather poorly presented which makes it difficult to evaluate and follow. As an example, I cannot
really evaluate if e.g. the small changes in the NMR spectra support the conclusions with the current figures. Therefore, I
cannot recommend anything else than a rejection with an invitation to resubmit. 
I would recommend rewriting the paper to focus more on the system under investigation - and less on protein interactions in
general – as it is not novel at a general level that protein interactions rely on an assortment of weak interactions. I especially
recommend a total remake of all the figures. The current set of figures hides how much work has actually gone into the
manuscript, which works against the paper reaching its full impact. The current figures e.g. do not show ITC; NMR, cross-
linking MS, SAXS data. Also, the authors might want to consider that the best way to present the data to the reader is
probably neither a huge pile of raw ITC traces (or NMR spectra) nor a huge data table. Please make figures that highlight the
important data and help the reader follow the story by e.g. presenting selected data that is compared side-by-side. 
Finally, I think the title of the paper is opaque due to the ambiguous term “functional selection”, which is likely to be
understood in an evolutionary sense. Perhaps you could make the title more descriptive instead by highlighting “non-
consensus tertiary interactions” or similar. The same applies to the use of “functional selection” through-out the manuscript. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This works investigates PI3K and its activation mechanism. The available cryo-EM models show weak density near the N-
terminus including the SH3, PR1, and BH domains of p85. The researchers utilize ITC binding assays, NMR spectroscopy,
cross-linking mass spectrometry, and functional assays in cells to derive the conclusion that the SH3 domains binding to the
N-terminal portion also make contacts with the C-terminal region of p85 and lead to relief of inhibition of the catalytic domain
p110. While transient interactions among domains would not be totally unexpected and are not novel in the field, such
interactions if biologically significant would introduce new insights about this protein-protein interaction and the activation
mechanism. However, while the data appear to be collected carefully, the conclusion from the available data are overstated.
While they do not refute the conclusion, they also do not unambiguously support it either. The primary pieces of biophysical

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

evidence the authors use to make this conclusion, described below, each have some issues. 
1. ITC binding assays. The largest effect in support of the effects are rather modest (anywhere from 1 to 3-fold). For example,
the authors made a point of emphasizing the affinities of Fyn p85deltaSH3 of 10 uM compared to the Fyn p85 PR1-BH-PR2
of 32 uM. This amounts to about 0.7 kcal/mol. If this difference was due solely from a C-terminal interaction and significant, it
would be about the value of kT (thermal fluctuation). Such an interaction would be very weak from an energetic perspective
and it does not clearly support the type of interaction that might be expected in an activation mechanism. 
2. NMR experiments. Supplementary Figure 4 and the information derived from it in Figure 3B show that p85 constructs
(large molecules) added to 15N Fyn SH3 variants (small protein) causes the disappearance of signals in the spectrum.
However, this experiment was done in a rather unusual manner by fixing p85 at 90 uM and varying the concentration of the
15N labeled SH3 protein. This means SH3 ranges in concentration from 9 uM (1:0.1) to 1.8 mM (1:20) during the titration. It
would reasonably be expected that a 9 uM protein bound to a large complex, even at a somewhat flexible region, would
experience a loss of signal to noise. It appears the authors have not accounted for this and do not have a quantitative way to
consider these very large differences in the 15N labeled protein concentrations. 
3. Cross linking experiments. These are unfortunately prone to non-specific effects and conformational bias. If a
conformation exists with a minor population, a cross link might occur. Such a potential bias might lead to a stronger
conclusion than is justified by the equilibrium arrangement of the complex. 
Overall, the authors have not provided any molecular basis (despite mentioning this a few times) about this mechanism.
They do not have structural evidence and therefore the conclusions are somewhat speculative and unconvincing that the
interaction does not arise from construct variability as a result of binding to a portion of the PR1 domain in the p85 protein. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The SH3 domain is known for low specificity and affinity for binding to the PxxP linear motif. The authors addressed how
Fyn and related SH3 domains activate PI3 kinase and identified a residue (Fyn L112) as a key residue involved in tertiary
interaction, apart from the PxxP binding site. The study involved a variety of research techniques using multiple SH3
domains and their mutants, and the results are convincing. I did not identify major problems with this manuscript and would
recommend publication with minor modifications. 
Minor comments 
1. I assume the SH3 domain of the Src family kinase, such as Fyn or Hck, is inaccessible in the inactive conformation as it is
involved in intramolecular interaction in the closed conformation, and the kinase needs to be in the active conformation so
that the SH3 domain can bind to PI3K. Can the authors describe such signaling pathways that open/activate the Src family
kinase first, then activate PI3K? 
2. It would be easier for readers to read the paper if the order of figure panels is aligned with the manuscript text, so that
readers do not need to go back and forth between different figures. I’d suggest that Fig. 2 C-D may be placed after Fig. 3 A-
B, and Fig. 2E after Fig. 4A.
