REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The contribution by Yang et al. explores the links between sea level and atmospheric CO2 consumption during Quaternary
glacial cycles. 
The topic is interesting, and the article is well-written and well-structured. The scientific question relates to why atmospheric
CO2 levels were 100 ppm lower during glacial phases. 
However, I have two important questions: 
I am not a specialist in Quaternary climate evolution, but it seems to me that 9 ppm out of 100 ppm is very little. Do other
studies confirm this small effect? 
I feel close to a conflict of interest, being one of the designers of GEOCLIM. Nevertheless, I have a question regarding the
methodology. The authors chose the version of the weathering module that is instantaneously at equilibrium. This means
that both regolith thicknesses and the mineralogical profiles of soils are at equilibrium. For weathering profiles, why not.
These should respond quickly to all climate changes. But it seems to me that continental regoliths will take time to
equilibrate, i.e., to stabilize their thickness and mineralogical profile. Simulating the whole in a fully dynamic mode must be
difficult, but it doesn't seem impossible. At the very least, do the authors have any idea, even a vague one, of the impact of
the steady-state assumption on their results? 
I believe that an answer to these two questions is necessary before the article can be accepted for publication.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
This study integrates GEOCLIM modeling with machine learning to explore how sea-level decline and extreme precipitation
enhance silicate weathering carbon sinks in the IPCZ. The interdisciplinary approach is innovative, but methodological gaps
and presentation inconsistencies require substantial revisions to strengthen its scientific rigor and persuasiveness. 
Major Comments
：
 
1
、
The use of a modern-derived runoff coefficient (k≈0.07) for glacial simulations is problematic, as glacial hydrological
conditions (altered precipitation, vegetation, topography) would have changed runoff dynamics. No validation of this
coefficient for glacial climates is provided, and the authors must verify converted runoff against relevant paleo-proxies for the
IPCZ during glacials, along with sensitivity analyses on k. 
2
、
Extrapolating precessional precipitation variability (16.4%) to full glacial periods lacks direct paleoenvironmental
evidence. 
3
、
Critical input data (HadCM3 temperature/precipitation, GEBCO DEM elevation, ERA5 runoff) lack specified temporal and
spatial resolutions, as well as resampling strategies. For instance, the temporal resolution of HadCM3 climate data (e.g.,
whether it is monthly, seasonal, or annual) and the spatial resolution of GEBCO DEM (e.g., grid size) remain unspecified.
Mismatched temporal and spatial resolutions across these datasets may introduce scaling errors, particularly when
integrating climate variables with topographic data to simulate silicate weathering processes in the IPCZ. 
4
、
The authors must justify why the GANs-LSTM-GRU model, which shows significantly lower R² (0.71) than the Random
Forest (0.89), is employed despite its inferior performance. The author should include the unique advantages of their model
in capturing temporal dynamics or generalizing to sparse data that the Random Forest lacks, supported by supplementary
analyses like prediction error comparisons at key intervals.

REVIEWERS' COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
I have carefully read the revised version submitted by Yang et al. 
During the first round of revision, I had suggested comparing the predicted amplitude of silicate weathering alteration
between glacial and interglacial stages with other studies that have addressed this question. This was done satisfactorily. 
The second question concerned the use of GEOCLIM and, more specifically, its dynsoil module in steady-state mode. It
would have been possible to run the model in dynamic mode until it reached equilibrium, and in doing so, directly measure
the time required for the regoliths to stabilize. The authors did not follow this approach and used a simple method that allows
the introduction of a chosen time constant to dampen the equilibrium results. This is acceptable. However, we see that the
weathering response is strongly dampened when the time constant goes from 1,000 to 10,000 years. There are clearly
highly non-linear effects. 
Given this suspected non-linearity, I believe it would be useful to add calculations with time constants of 5,000 and 20,000
years. 
- The first case will make it possible to evaluate the non-linearity of the response between 1,000 and 10,000 years. 
- The second case will allow us to see if the system's response is still evolving. 
If the damping of the oscillations remains similar with a constant of 10,000 or 20,000 years, this will reinforce the validity of
the results. Conversely, if the damping continues beyond 10,000 years, this could call into question the results presented
here. 
A final point: while reading the article, I was surprised by the direct jump from the problem statement to the results. The entire
model description has been relegated to the Supporting Information (SI). I believe it's important to keep a portion of the
model description in the main body of the text to aid readability and flow. 
Ultimately, I think that once corrected to address these last points, the article could be accepted.
