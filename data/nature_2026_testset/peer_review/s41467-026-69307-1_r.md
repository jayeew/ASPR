Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors reported a tandem urea electrosynthesis pathway over PdHx/Cu through a dual spillover of CO* and H*. This
pathway undergoes efficient CO2-to-CO* conversion on PdHx and facile NO3−-to-NO* conversion on Cu. Rapid spillover of
CO* and H* to Cu facilitates key OCNO* formation and protonation. The presented dual spillover is meaningful for urea
electrosynthesis. I recommend that this article can be accepted for publication after addressing the following minor issues. 
1. The source of nitrate as a raw material is not mentioned in the manuscript. If industrial nitrogen fixation and ammonia
production are needed, and then ammonia is oxidized to produce nitric acid, the industrial process of producing urea from
CO2 and ammonia would seem simpler. 
2. All key details about electrochemical testing must be provided. The lack of appropriate description of the experimental
procedures renders all data reported in this manuscript unreliable. 
3. Were other products (e.g., N2, HCOOH and C2) analyzed? 
4. All key electrocatalytic data for PdHx/Cu and Cu must be accompanied by a set of appropriately reproduced
chronoamperograms, which should be used to extract the steady-state current densities to construct polarisation plots. 
5. The authors attempted to verify the source of urea through isotope experiments. Thus, the urea content in the open circuit
should be provided. 
6. In the TEA term, the author is expected to provide a detailed calculation process to ensure the veracity of the data
presented. Should the author take the cost of the electrolyte into account? 
7. The Cdl alone is insufficient to accurately describe the number of catalytically active sites; BET measurements are
necessary for a more reliable assessment. 
8. In the CV measurements, the oxidation peak observed at ~0.27 V vs. RHE for PdHx/Cu could also originate from OH*
adsorption/desorption or metal/hydroxide redox transitions. The authors are encouraged to further investigate these
possibilities. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript submitted by Xia et al. reported a tandem mechanism for urea electrosynthesis based on dual spillover of
CO* and H*, which simultaneously promoted the key C-N intermediate formation and protonation substeps. In situ
characterizations coupled with theoretical calculations demonstrated the proposed mechanism. The proposed conception is
a novel and new approach to solve the fundamental challenges of urea electrosynthesis. Therefore, I will recommend this
work to be published on Nature Communications after some minor revisions. 
1. In the PdHx/Cu system, the dual spillover of CO and H* from PdHx to Cu serves as the key mechanistic pathway. To
further strengthen the evidence supporting this mechanism, additional control experiments are recommended. Specifically,
PdHx-to-Cu CO* spillover is expected to alter the CO adsorption/desorption behavior on Cu. CO-TPD (temperature-
programmed desorption) could be employed to probe these changes, thereby providing direct experimental validation for
CO* spillover. Similarly, if H* spillover occurs, the hydrogen adsorption/desorption kinetics on the Cu side of PdHx/Cu
should differ significantly from those of pure Cu. Comparative studies using techniques such as H2-TPD or electrochemical
hydrogen adsorption/desorption measurements would help confirm this hypothesis. 
2. The authors evaluated electrocatalytic performance across varying NO3− concentrations in Figure S31. However, the
assumption that increased NO3− concentration directly correlates with higher surface coverage of adsorbed N species

requires further validation. Did the authors perform complementary surface-sensitive characterization (e.g., in situ FTIR,
Raman spectroscopy, or electrochemical titration) to quantitatively establish this relationship? 
3. The DFT calculations reveal that PdHx domains facilitate thermodynamically favorable CO2-to-CO* and NO3
⁻
-to-NO*
conversions. Consequently, the potential direct coupling between the generated CO* and NO* intermediates on PdHx
domains should be systematically evaluated, as this pathway may significantly influence the overall reaction mechanism. 
4. While the authors emphasize the contribution of spillovered H on Cu domains to protonation, the possible competing HER
pathway involving this spillovered H remains unaddressed. 
5. The authors use a 400 eV plane-wave cutoff in their DFT calculations. Convergence tests should be conducted and
presented to validate this parameter choice. 
6. The choice of the Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh (2 × 2 × 1) critically influences the accuracy of the computational results;
thus, the authors should provide justification for this selection. 
7. For the completeness of the article, to attract more attention and to increase the readership, the introduction should cite
some of the latest advances in CO2 and NO3− conversion, such as: 10.1038/s41467-025-61069-6;
10.1002/anie.202200723; 10.1038/s41467-024-47765-9. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Revisions to “Dual spillover of carbon monoxide and hydrogen initiates tandem urea electrosynthesis” 
In the present study, Li et al. report the synthesis, characterization, and electrochemical evaluation of PdHx/Cu
electrocatalysts for urea electrosynthesis. The manuscript is supported by a comprehensive suite of physico-chemical
characterizations, in situ electrochemical investigations, control experiments, and theoretical calculations. While the work is
of high quality and addresses a timely and relevant topic, the reviewer has identified critical concerns regarding the
electrochemical methodology and related product quantification protocols. For full transparency, the reviewer notes that their
core expertise lies in electrocatalysis, and as such, the comments provided primarily focus on these aspects. 
In light of the above considerations, the reviewer deems that the manuscript is not publishable at the moment but might be
improved according to the following recommendations. 
Major revisions 
• Urea quantification. As for other branches of research dealing with nitrogen-based reactants, urea electrosynthesis often
suffer from false positives (the recent works of Robert Keller, Plamen Atanassov, Michele Ferri, Oleksandr Voznyy, all
commented on the topic). The use of a single technique for urea quantification is therefore not recommended. In addition,
despite the linear and consistent calibration curve reported by the authors, the actual spectra used for urea quantification in
post-reaction electrolytes are not reported. Adding such data would noticeably improve the reliability of the study. It is also
experience of the reviewer that urea protons do not yield signals (or yield broad ones, of difficult integration) at both acidic
and alkaline pH (possibly because of their fast exchange in solution) when analyzed by 1H-NMR. Considering that urea
electrosynthesis tests carried out in the flow cell used 1 M KOH as electrolyte, the authors should report if they did neutralize
the solution before quantification or if any post-processing of the electrolytes has been necessary; 
• By-products quantification. Despite the sum of the FEs of different products (i.e., the charge balance) of several tests
reaches values around 100%, the authors did not quantify and/or properly excluded the presence of other species. For
example, nitrite ions, which are typically produced when performing NO3RR on Cu-based catalysts. In addition, excluding
the presence of other C-N containing products (e.g., formamide) is paramount to properly assess the product distribution of
the catalysts under study. It is also noted that, for some tests, only the FEs in specific products are reported; 
• Current density and urea electrosynthesis. On line 118, the authors claim that higher current density in the co-presence of
reactants implies higher urea electrosynthesis activity. Actually, higher current densities do not imply a successful C-N
bonding. High current densities may also stem from the formation of individual CO2RR and NO3RR products, without any
formation of urea. In addition, the electrochemical traces reported in Figure S6 and the related caption are not clear. Only two
curves are indeed reported for each catalyst while at least four for each should be reported for a fair comparison (“clean”
electrolyte with Ar flow (HER activity), “clean” electrolyte with CO2 flow (CO2RR + parasitic HER), nitrates-containing
electrtolye with Ar flow (NO3RR + parasitic HER), nitrates-containing electrolyte with CO2 flow (urea electrosynthesis
conditions, CO2RR + NO3RR)); 
• Choice of electrolytes. The study is not consistent in the choice of electrolytes. Preliminary studies are carried out in
bicarbonate-based electrolytes. The same goes for some in-situ measurements. On the other hand, the flow cell testing has
been carried out in 1 M KOH. The difference in pH surely affects the urea electrosynthesis and the generation of
intermediates since, as extensively reported in literature, both NO3RR and CO2RR are heavily affected by the pH (proton
availability) of electrolytes; 
• Choice of anodic reaction in flow cell. It is not clear why the authors decided to implement methanol oxidation at the anode
of the flow cell instead of the typical oxygen evolution reaction. Apart from being a peculiar choice, it also introduces
criticalities related to potential reporting (2 M MeOH solutions might deviate from the typical behavior of aqueous solutions,
thus hampering the reporting of potentials) and, especially, to the membrane stability (see other comments); 
• IR compensation. The authors report that all measurements were compensated by 95% of the system’s ohmic drop. When
the compensation is performed manually—that is, by post-processing the electrochemical data after determining the
uncompensated resistance via EIS—there is no particular reason to avoid full compensation. In contrast, when the correction
is applied in real time (i.e., “on the fly”) using software tools, excessive compensation may lead to oscillations and artifacts in
the recorded data. Extensive literature is available on this matter. The authors should clearly specify which compensation
method was employed and ensure that all data have been properly corrected for the ohmic drop; 
• Supports (GDLs). The authors do not report the kind of GDL on which the electrocatalysts have been deposited. Typically,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

carbon-based GDLs (e.g., Sigracet) are used in these studies. The reviewer would like to point out that such a choice would
be correct for the cathode but not for the anode as carbon-based electrodes are known to undergo fast degradation under the
application of anodic potentials in concentrated alkali; 
• Flow cell design. Arguably the most critical issue. The choice of methanol oxidation at the anode is critical for multiple
reasons (see previous comments). Apart from the reaction itself, the use of methanol poses a serious threat to the stability of
the membrane. Several AEMs are indeed known for being soluble in alcohols and, for example when used in CO2RR to
alcohol processes, they exhibit degradation problems. The use of an AEM also poses crossover problems. Nitrates for
example would tend to electromigrate (and also diffuse, since they are not present in the anolyte) to the anode chamber,
“stealing” reagents for urea electrosynthesis. Similarly, if formate is produced as individual CO2RR product at the cathode, it
might also electromigrate to the anode, hampering the FE calculations of methanol oxidation. The use of “two-gaps” cell
(cathode/catholyte/membrane/anolyte/anode), in which the catholyte is put in direct contact with the gas diffusion electrode,
generally leads to a fast flooding of the cathodic gas chamber, thus suppressing CO2RR activity in favor of HER. The
authors should explain how they avoided flooding during the 200 hours-long tests. Finally, because of its acidic nature, CO2
reacts with the catholyte, forming carbonates. Apart from lowering the overall CO2 utilization of the process, this
phenomenon might lead to a change in the pH of the electrolyte (i.e., KOH turns into a carbonate/bicarbonate solution,
especially if the experiment lasts several hours) and to the formation of salts in the GDE (resulting in the flooding of the
latter); 
• TEA and LCA. Both analyses are significantly simplified and rely on strong assumptions. In the case of the TEA, for
instance, the market price of urea appears to be overestimated—reported at nearly twice its actual value. Moreover, CO2
and nitrate are treated as “free” feedstocks, which overlooks the non-negligible costs associated with their concentration
and/or purification, given that they are typically found in dilute waste streams. The source of the electrolyzer cost estimation
is unclear. It is also not specified whether the term “electrolyzer” refers solely to the stack or to the full system (stack +
balance of plant). Additionally, the assumptions used to determine the CAPEX and OPEX percentages for maintenance and
purification are not sufficiently discussed or supported with references. For a simplified yet more robust assessment of the
process’s economic viability, the authors are encouraged to refer to established methodologies from reputable organizations
(e.g., IRENA). That said, it is also worth noting that, at the current TRL, a full TEA may still be premature. The reviewer
therefore recommends framing these assessments as very preliminary evaluations of the potential profitability and
environmental footprint of urea electrosynthesis. 
Minor revisions 
• Steric hindrance. On line 46, the authors refer to the steric hindrance of the intermediates as one of the limiting factors for
C-N coupling. Despite hindrance surely plays a role, there are other factors being at least as important as steric hindrance to
achieve efficient C-N coupling. For example, adsorption energy, surface coverage ratio of key intermediates at specific
operating potentials, distance between CO2RR and NO3RR active sites. The discussion should therefore emphasize that
multiple factors concur in making C-N challenging; 
• CO stripping voltammetries. On line 113, the authors refer to metallic Pd electrochemical traces which are not reported in
Figure S3; 
• Total FE (charge balance). Throughout the paper, there are examples of FE graphs not reporting the complete product
distribution (for example, Figure 3b and 3e); 
• ECSA. On line 185, the authors discuss ECSA measurements. Actually, despite the use of the double-layer capacitance
method might be meaningful in this paper, the reviewer underlines that such method measures the physical surface area
more than the electrochemically active surface area (check for example the 1990 milestone paper from Trasatti and Petrii).
The discussion should therefore be amended to avoid misleading the reader. The reviewer is also surprised by the values of
the PdHx/Cu and Cu being similar. The decoration of Cu nanosheets with an additional nanostructured moiety is indeed
expected to increase the physical surface area of the sample. Is there any possibility for the main contribution to this
measure to come the support?; 
• Decoupling spillover and interface sites. On line 199, the authors try to decouple the number of interfacial sites and the
spillover-based mechanism they are proposing. Despite the reviewer sees the train of thoughts and the significance of the
experiments reported in Figure S18, it is challenging to decouple such two phenomena. Indeed, the “efficiency” of the
spillover of intermediates is tightly connected to the interface area available. The authors should deepen this discussion to
make it clear to the reader; 
• *CO coverage on Cu. Line 205, the authors discuss on the “limited intrinsic *CO generation capacity” of Cu-based
catalysts. Without going into the details, there are multiple evidences in the literature (and from theoretical calculations) that
Cu exhibits a unique CO2RR selectivity towards beyond-two-electron-reduction products exactly in virtue of its ability to
generate *CO and (i) further transfer electrons to this intermediate (to, for example, methane and methanol) or (ii) foster C-C
binding by *CO “coupling” (to, for example, ethanol and ethylene). Therefore, this claim of the authors might need context
and a proper discussion; 
• Identification of intermediates through MS. Recent literature in CO2RR (from the group of Brian Seger, for example)
suggests to be cautious when using m/z ratios to identify intermediates of electrocatalytic processes. In this specific case,
the m/z ratios of 58 and 44, assumed to be indicative of two different C-N containing intermediates, should be confirmed by
specific control tests. For example, the authors should exclude the possibility that such fragments come from individual
CO2RR and/or NO3RR products (e.g., ethanol, acetaldehyde, N2O); 
• Electrodes fabrication. In the Supporting Information, the authors report to have used Nafion as binder/ionomer in the
preparation of catalytic inks. What is the rationale behind this choice, considering that tests have been always carried out in
near neutral or alkaline environments, using AEMs? The authors should also specify the fabrication method they employed
(the SI only state that the catalyst “was loaded” onto the support); 
• 13C-NMR for urea detection/quantification. The reviewer is concerned about the use of this technique for the detection of
urea. As reported by different groups (see the works of the groups of Robert Keller and Oleksandr Voznyy, for example), the
13C-NMR signals often assigned to urea might be misleading as carbonates/bicarbonates exhibit similar signals in the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

same region; 
• ICP measurements. Despite the graph in Figure S13 indeed reports very low concentrations of Pd and Cu in the
electrolyte, it is impossible to estimate the actual leaching of the catalyst since the volume of electrolytes employed in the
testing is not reported. Considering the low loading of catalysts on the supports, the small surface area of the electrodes, and
the (possibly) large volume of the electrolyte circulated in the electrochemical cell, even low concentrations of metals might
account for a non-negligible leaching. A proper calculation should be carried out to assess the percentage leaching; 
• EIS graphs and model circuit. In Figure S21, the axes of the Nyquist plots are not compliant with the common standards
that apply to electrocatalysis studies. The plots should indeed be squares, with the same scale on both axes. This is to spot
specific slopes that are indicative of peculiar behaviors (for example, a 45° slope in the low frequency region is indicative of
Warburg diffusion). The model circuit employed for experimental data fitting is not commented. Similarly, values for all the
resistances, capacitances, and diffusion elements are reported in Supporting Table 3 but not commented. 
General comments 
• Bosch-Meiser process. On line 32, the authors comment on the energy consumption and emission associated with the
Bosch-Meiser process. Although the process is surely contributing to the environmental footprint of urea, the upstream
production of ammonia (by the Haber-Bosch process) with the related Steam Methane Reforming (SMR, used to generate
the hydrogen needed for nitrogen fixation) are the processes that most impact on the environmental footprint of urea; 
• Over-reduction of nitrates on copper. On line 71, the authors claim that: “[…] metallic Cu is selected as component (ii)
owing to its well-established selectivity in converting NO3− to NO* intermediates while effectively suppressing over-
reduction”. The cited reference (number 18) does not comment specifically on over-reduction. In addition, it is difficult to
understand what the authors mean with such term. The reviewer notes that nitrate ions reduction on copper-based catalysts
may be quite selective towards ammonia (if operated under cathodic enough potential, there is an extensive literature from
Koper’s group on NO3RR), which is the most reduced product obtainable in the process. The authors should clarify what is
meant by over-reduction and how this phenomenon is relevant to the C-N coupling on the catalyst; 
• Spillover of H* species. Despite protonation of C-N containing intermediates being a key step for efficient urea
electrosynthesis, the reviewer wonders how the spillover of H* species might or not be detrimental to the reaction. Such
species are indeed available for CPETs with any of the intermediates available on the surface of the catalysts, possibly
leading to further reduction of C- and/or N-based intermediates, leading to, for example, ammonia (individual NO3RR) and
alcohols/hydrocarbons (individual CO2RR). In other words, how do the authors rationalize the usefulness of H* spillover
(protonating the “right” intermediates) against the possibility of “indiscriminate” CPETs (protonating non C-N containing
intermediates, leading to a scattered product distribution)?; 
• Morphology of the catalyst. Is there any reason behind the choice of the authors to opt for copper nanosheets instead of any
other morphology? Is the choice based on crystal facets exposure or surface area? In addition, what is the rationale behind
the choice of decorating Cu nanostructures with PdHx ones and not viceversa?; 
• Use of the term “Operando”. The reviewer is used to distinguish between the terms “in-situ” and “operando” according to
the following definitions. “In-situ” indicates a measurement carried out while the lab-scale system is operating. “Operando”
indicated a measurement carried out on the actual device. To give an example: running spectroscopy on a three-way
catalyst powder packed in a lab reactor versus monitoring it in an actual muffler on a car driving on the road. Based on these
definitions, the reviewer would define the experiments in this paper as “in-situ”; 
• Cell notation. The notation “PdHx/Cu(−)&PdHx/Cu(+)” is quite odd. The use of “&” for indicating the membrane is not
conventional and the plus/minus signs should be opening and closing the notation. 
• Grammar/syntax check. Line 253-254, the sentence there is not clear, might be a typo; 
• Reference to Pd metallenes. Line 19, Supporting Information, might use a reference for such synthetic procedure; 
• Reference electrode calibration. The calibration carried out is probably accurate but nonetheless cumbersome and prone
to errors. The reviewer suggests to the author the use of commercial reference hydrogen electrodes (for example, HydroFlex
by Gaskatel, which are recommended out of experience, no vested interest); 
• Membrane operated in different environments. On line 94, Supporting Information, the authors should clarify what they
mean with “worked in different electrolyte conditions”; 
• Figure S3. For the sake of clarity and completeness, it is recommended to show the whole voltammetric traces, maybe
magnifying the important features in an inset; 
• Typo in a caption. Line 1013, Supporting Information, the caption reports (d) instead of (f).
