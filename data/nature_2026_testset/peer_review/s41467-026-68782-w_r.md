Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is an interesting study on a 12-month feasibility trial of spinal cord stimulation therapy for gait impairment in Parkinson's
disease. However, there are a few major issues needing attention. 
1. For a typical feasibility trial, the focus should be recruitment rate, attrition rate and etc. However, this study is all about
efficacy as shown in the abstract and the main paper, which is inadequate. 
2. There is no prior sample size calculation. The analysis results are from 6 vs 6 comparisons, which is seriously
underpowered for any meaningful statistical analyses. Therefore the reliability, robustness and believability of the results are
subject to scrutiny. 
3. Recruitment rate (screening, consenting, and etc) and attrition rate were not reported. 
4. The abstract is seriously inadequate. It was not about recruitment and safty at all, but showed efficacy results instead as if
this is a definitive phase III trial. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Terkelsen et al presents the results of a 12-month prospective trial evaluating spinal cord stimulation
applied over the thoracic spinal cord (T7–T11) in 12 patients (14 enrolled). Stimulation was delivered using a microburst
paradigm. The authors assessed improvements in gait and rigidity/bradykinesia (using PIGD and LBG tests), as well as
changes in metabolic activity (PET imaging) and adherence. 
The objectives are clearly described and clinically meaningful. The study protocol had been published previously, ensuring
methodological transparency. The study is well motivated, carefully planned, and well executed. The comparison with the
larger PPMI cohort is well grounded and appreciated. The manuscript is well written, clearly presented, and the few figures
are clear. Nevertheless, additional figures with more detailed data would be welcome, as the limited number and modest
quality of the current figures restricts the reader’s ability to fully appreciate the results. 
Overall, the behavioral results are moderate and warrant cautious interpretation, whereas the metabolic assessments are
novel and potentially valuable to the community. However, the manuscript lacks sufficient depth to substantially advance our
understanding of SCS for gait impairments in PD. In its current form, the behavioral findings add to a growing body of
literature reporting inconsistent effects of thoracic SCS. There is little indication of how these results may inform refinement
of the therapy. More in-depth analyses and discussion would have certainly strengthened the manuscript. 
For example, while the observed metabolic modulations suggest possible supraspinal mechanisms, no link is drawn
between these changes and motor improvements. This limits mechanistic insight and the potential impact of the study for
informing future clinical trials, including the larger multicenter study the authors propose. Although the text notes individual

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

benefits in some participants, no systematic attempt is made to leverage these observations to identify subpopulations most
likely to benefit. A more individualized analysis of metabolic activity in responders, which represents one of the most
compelling aspects of the study, could be highly valuable to guide future directions, define responder profiles, and enhance
translational relevance. 
Specific comments: 
• Stimulation was delivered using a predefined microburst pattern with amplitude set a priori to 50% of the paresthesia
threshold, without optimization or comparison with alternative patterns. This leaves open important questions about
parameter selection and whether this approach is optimal. While microburst stimulation is interesting in that it enables true
blinding, it would be useful to know whether the authors compared it (at least acutely) to other paradigms (e.g., continuous
stimulation), whether patients reported different perceptual experiences, and if so, how these related to outcomes. Were any
muscle contractions (e.g., trunk or abdominal) observed that could have indicated differences across participants? 
• Concretely: The modification of stimulation to sine-wave patterns in two participants after the study period should be be
further discussed, as only three of the five participants who reported meaningful improvement remained on microburst
stimulation. This raises concerns about the robustness and long-term suitability of the chosen stimulation paradigm. 
• Gait assessments are should be presented in greater detail. The behavioral measures used (PIGD and LBG tests) are
relatively coarse and lack objective measures such as electrophysiological, electromyographic, or kinematic data, which are
now standard in many in-lab gait assessments. Such measures could have helped validate the results and reduce
variability. Incorporating finer-grained analyses could also have revealed subtle improvements to inform future studies. It is
unfortunate that the wearable technology, which might have provided these objective metrics, could not ultimately be used. 
• The authors briefly mention individual differences within the microburst group, particularly among younger participants, but
this point is underdeveloped. A more detailed analysis of whether metabolic differences were detectable in this subgroup,
and whether such data could help identify responders, would considerably strengthen the manuscript. 
• To what extent did the oldest patients with Hoehn & Yahr stage III, who generally showed worse outcomes, influence the
overall results? Could the authors draw any conclusions regarding the role of age, disease severity, or dopaminergic
medication levels on either clinical evolution with SCS or metabolic responses? 
• The reported improvement in leg rigidity in the microburst group between 6-month and pre-surgery baselines is of interest,
but since this effect was not significant compared with the sham group, the interpretation is limited. A true post-surgical
baseline would have helped to exclude surgery-related effects. 
• Clarification on self-reported perception and sensory effects would be useful: did all participants report similar sensations
during stimulation? were amplitudes comparable across individuals? 
• The reported metabolic changes are novel and interesting, but the discussion should go further in exploring their
mechanistic significance. For instance, what may these metabolic modulations mean, based on the understasnding of
specific brain circuits in gait function and dysfucntion, or freezing of gait ? The discussion currently feels very clinically
oriented and would benefit from a more science-driven analysis providing reasoning and context for the observed effects. 
• The authors report an adverse event consisting of a transient increase in parkinsonian symptoms following surgery that did
not require medication changes. Was this effect equally distributed across both groups? Could this represent a surgery-
related worsening rather than a therapy-specific effect? 
• How was medication managed during the trial? Were any adjustments made, and if so, did they affect the outcomes? Were
potential effects of L-DOPA accounted for during behavioral and metabolic assessments? Clarification on assessment
timing relative to medication intake, and whether this was comparable across groups, is essential. 
• Patients are reported to have been enrolled with FOG in the ON state, yet results suggest that FOG and balance outcomes
may have favored the sham group. This discrepancy is not addressed. The issue is especially relevant as some participants
who continued stimulation experienced worsening of FOG after stimulation was turned OFF, but the implications of this
finding are not discussed. Should longer stimulation periods be considered to capture benefits? Why did none of the
participants who discontinued stimulation after 6 or 12 months report such changes? Although exploratory and not
statistically significant, these observations may support the idea that long-term SCS is needed to improve gait symptoms in
PD. 
• Self-reported outcomes show significant improvement only at the end of the second arm (6 months for sham and 12 months
for early starters). Since this phase was not blinded (all participants knew they were receiving stimulation), to what extent
could these findings be explained by placebo effects?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Thank you for submitting this very interesting pilot trial on Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) to improve gait impairment in
patients with Parkinson's disease (PD). The use of molecular imaging as biological proof-of-efficacy measure improves the
overall quality of the study. There are a few aspects in the methods and the interpretation of the results that may warrant
further discussion. 
1) According to the methods, the PD patients were tested for [18F]FEOBV PET in their ON state. This is not unusual plus
these patients were tested in relation to their ON freezing of gait, however these same patients were tested in OFF state for
their [18F]FDG PET. Would that change anything? 
2) In the methods, the Authors specify that the MRI was only done at baseline, so the follow-up PET data was co-registered
to the baseline MRI. This could potentially limit the accuracy of the registrations and delineation of regions of interest. This
should be highlighted in the discussion, as a potential limitation. 
3) How long was the timeframe for the recording of clinical, imaging (and also the home wearable) data at any given
timepoint? This information would be useful in the light of the feasibility side of this type of trial. 
4) The results of the [18F]FEOBV PET analysis are somehow surprising in that, at baseline, PD patients with FOG display a
relative increase of cholinergic terminal density in the right frontal areas and right insula compared to PD patients without
FOG; and SCS reduced the levels of tracer uptake in several cortical regions regulating motor activity. While the hypothesis
proposed by the Authors of a compensatory biological mechanism that is “normalised” by SCS makes sense in light of the
present results, previous works on the topic have consistently demonstrated a decrease of cholinergic terminals in several
cortical and subcortical areas in patients with PD and gait impairment (Bohnen et al., Ann Neurol 2019; Chou et al., Mov
Disord 2025). The Authors should consider these insights from the literature in the interpretation of their results. 
5) Similarly for the [18F]FDG results, the decrease of brain metabolism observed in the trial can be ascribed to a general
decrease of FDG uptake in PD patients with freezing of gait, as consistently showed by several past works. From a
molecular imaging perspective, there doesn’t seem to be a difference or a deviation of molecular imaging patterns in the
patients who underwent the SCS (although in a very small cohort) and the overall interpretation of there results should be
weighted according to this. 
6) In the light of the previous two comments, I would also suggest the Authors to include in the discussion whether the
Authors would suggest use of these imaging methods in future trials on SCS. 
Minor comments: 
1) The sham group is made up of all males, could gender differences have an impact on the results? 
2) When reporting the imaging results the Authors write “p=0.000).” I would suggest the Authors to specify better the p value,
for instance by writing p<0.0001.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Thank you for submitting this very interesting pilot trial on Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) to improve 
gait impairment in patients with Parkinson's disease (PD). The use of molecular imaging as biological 
proof-of-efficacy measure improves the overall quality of the study. There are a few aspects in the 
methods and the interpretation of the results that may warrant further discussion. 
 
3.1) According to the methods, the PD patients were tested for [18F]FEOBV PET in their ON state. 
This is not unusual plus these patients were tested in relation to their ON freezing of gait, however 
these same patients were tested in OFF state for their [18F]FDG PET. Would that change anything? 
 
We thank the reviewer for the kind comments on the study. In regard to the first query, [18F]-FDG 
PET was performed after medication withdrawal to replicate the metho dology by Eidelberg and 
colleagues in “The Metabolic Topography of Parkinsonism” in which they propose a disease-related 
network. [18F]-FDG PET performed during peak dopaminergic replacement therapy would, however,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 21 -->

also be very interesting. Recently, Sigurdson and colleagues used a dual-injection method to isolate 
walking-related [18F]-FDG PET patterns which could be very interesting to incorporate in future SCS 
studies.  
 
3.2) In the methods, the Authors specify that the MRI was only done at baseline, so the follow -up 
PET data was co -registered to the baseline MRI. This could potentially limit the accuracy of the 
registrations and delineation of regions of interest. This should be highlighted in the discussion, as a 
potential limitation. 
We agree with the reviewer that repeated MRI would have been preferable. However, the SCS device 
was not compatible and as we did not expect any changes in one year at a level that could impair co-
registration. We have stated the following in the Discussion:  
 
“It should be noted that the follow -up PET scans were co -registered to baseline MRI as the SCS 
device was not compatible with 3T MRI at the time. However, during one-year, we do not expect any 
changes at a level that could impair co -registration, nor did our participants suffer any such acute 
events.”  
 
3.3) How long was the timeframe for the recording of clinical, imaging (and also the home wearable) 
data at any given timepoint? This information would be useful in the light of the feasibility side of 
this type of trial. 
We thank the reviewer for this suggestion and have added the following to the Results:  
 
“Baseline assessments spanned 2-6 weeks due to scanner availability and patient scheduling 
constraints. All follow-up assessments were completed within two weeks, except one completed 
within 18 days.” 
 
 
3.4) The results of the [18F]FEOBV PET analysis are somehow surprising in that, at baseline, PD 
patients with FOG display a relative increase of cholinergic terminal density in the right frontal 
areas and right insula compared to PD patients without FOG; and SCS reduced the levels of tracer 
uptake in several cortical regions regulating motor activity. While the hypothesis proposed by the 
Authors of a compensatory biological mechanism that is “normalised” by SCS makes sense in light 
of the present results, previous works on the topic have consistently demonstrated a decrease of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 22 -->

cholinergic terminals in several cortical and subcortical areas in patients with PD and gait 
impairment (Bohnen et al., Ann Neurol 2019; Chou et al., Mov Disord 2025). The Authors should 
consider these insights from the literature in the interpretation of their results. 
The group at Michigan University has made important contributions in the current knowledge of 
cholinergic system alterations in PD, including the papers referred by the reviewer. However, these 
studies do not report anything on increases. As we were also investigating for possible compensatory 
changes, we specifically looked for areas of increased [ 18F]-FEOBV binding at baseline our gait -
impaired patients. 
Moreover, some methodological differences could also explain the different findings. Our participants 
were enrolled based on the presence of ON FoG and their [18F]-FEOBV images were compared with 
patients who had no FoG during medication withdrawal. Meanwhile, Bohnen and colleagues, 2019, 
defined FoG based on its presence while participants were examined in dopaminergic medication 
OFF state . Chou and colleagues, 2025, found cholinergic deficits in PD patients with levodopa -
unresponsive FoG defined as more FoG episodes in medication-ON than in the medication-OFF state 
compared with levodopa -responsive FoG  suffering PD patients. Thus, while decreases in general 
have been reported in relation to FoG, we are the first to investigate the differences between ON FoG 
and no freezing PD patients.  
Therefore, our study is not directly comparable with the two mentioned studies or any other published 
neuroimaging studies. 
 
3.5) Similarly for the [18F]FDG results, the decrease of brain metabolism observed in the trial can be 
ascribed to a general decrease of FDG uptake in PD patients with freezing of gait, as consistently 
showed by several past works. From a molecular imaging perspective, there doesn ’t seem to be a 
difference or a deviation of molecular imaging patterns in the patients  who underwent the SCS 
(although in a very small cohort) and the overall interpretation of there results should be weighted 
according to this. 
The reviewer states that a general decrease of FDG uptake in PD patients with freezing of gait has as 
consistently showed by several past works. However, to our knowledge, both metabolic increases and 
decreases have been reported in various gait -related regions of PD patients with FOG; Sigurdson et 
al. in Movement Disorders, 2022 (increases or decreases in several ar eas), Zhou et al. in 
Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment 2019 (Increased FDG uptake in the caudate and lentiform 
nucl.), Gallardo et al. in Neurological research 2018 (hypometabolism in motor cortex area), and

Bartels et al. in Movement Disorders 2006 (Caudate metabolism reduced, but putaminal metabolism 
increased). 
Since the significant decrease in FDG uptake from baseline to post SCS in our study is not paralleled 
by a worsening of FOG of gait over the same period, it is unlikely that it is related to the FOG itself.

“It could be argued that the observed metabolic decreases at follow-up could be related to a general
