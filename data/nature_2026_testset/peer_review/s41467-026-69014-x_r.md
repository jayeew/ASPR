Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Fibroblast growth factor 13 (FGF13), also referred to as FGF homologous factor (FHF) 2, is a member of the FGF11
subfamily that is characterized as having sequence similarities to classical FGF receptor (FGFR)-binding FGFs, but
functionally do not bind FGFRs. Similar to other FHFs, FGF13 has been considered a non-secreted protein that lacks an
amino signal and is prominently expressed in developing and mature neurons of the central and peripheral nervous
systems, as well as the heart. The expression of FGF13 is not limited to early embryonic stages and has been shown to
persist in adult tissues. As well, FGF13 is known to localize subcellularly, both within the cytoplasm and the nucleus. In this
study, the authors found that FGF13 was down-regulated in endothelial cells and macrophages in lungs from septic mice
and sepsis patients. They showed that specific knockout of FGF13 in endothelial cells or macrophages reduced acute lung
injury and improved survival in mice with LPS-challenged endotoxic shock. On the other hand, targeted overexpression of
FGF13 was found to aggravate acute lung injury and worsen survival in mice with LPS-challenged endotoxic shock.
Furthermore, they demonstrated that FGF13 appeared to act as a scaffold protein for the TAK1-MEK-ERK signaling axis and
thereby facilitated transmission of the signaling pathway upon LPS stimulation, which promoted HIF-1α-induced aerobic
glycolysis in endothelial cells and macrophages, leading to their pro-inflammatory activation. While this study includes
interesting findings, I have a number of concerns for the authors to consider. 
Major comments are summarized below. 
1) In the Methods section, the authors describe that “Before collection the sample from healthy volunteers and sepsis
patients, the authors provided the human patient consent forms to healthy volunteers and sepsis patients”. It’s really hard to
understand that ‘healthy volunteers’ provided their own lung tissues. In that sense, it’s inconceivable that such a study plan
was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. In addition, patient
information should be more fully and specifically provided. 
2) The authors should mention what was a post-hoc test following ANOVA. 
3) In some animal experiments, an n = 3-4 is not a sufficient experimental number. The insufficient number of samples per
test group makes it clear that the study is greatly underpowered. Indeed, in animal experiments, we empirically learn that
there are great differences between individuals in mice with lethal endotoxic shock. 
4) While LPS plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of sepsis, it is well known that LPS-challenged murine models do not
fully reflect all factors associated with human sepsis. Thus, LPS infusion is unsuitable animal model for sepsis. LPS-
challenged mice should be recognized as an endotoxic shock model but not as a septic model. In this study, the authors
used mice with CLP-induced sepsis, which should have been centrally placed in this study. 
5) It is very hard to understand the reason why the authors used HUVEC for in vitro experiments. This is a very lame
approach. In terms of flow in this study, the authors should have used pulmonary vascular endothelial cells. In the first place,
HUVEC is not so much sensitive to LPS. I doubt it’s true that HUVEC responded to 1 μg/mL of LPS in this study. 
6) Lung histological images, shown in this paper, are somewhat poor. For routine histology, the authors did not probably use
inflation-fixed lungs. Lung histological images are structurally disrupted even in controls. Unfortunately, the readers who are
so familiar with the lung histology may not bear to see the histological images presented in this paper. 
7) The authors conclude that “targeting FGF13 is a potential therapeutic strategy to alleviate septic lung injury”. However, the
authors found that FGF13 was down-regulated in endothelial cells and macrophages in lungs from mice with endotoxic
shock or CLP-induced sepsis and patients suffering from sepsis. Given the down-regulation of FGF13 under endotoxic
shock or sepsis conditions, whether targeting FGF13 is of high clinically therapeutic value is extremely doubtful. 
8) Finally, a major language polishing is required. The manuscript has to definitively use more review for grammar and to
help with readability.

(Remarks to the Author) 
I thank the authors for their extensive and careful responses to both sets of reviewer comments, and for undertaking
substantial revisions and additional experiments. The manuscript is clearly improved in clarity, methodological transparency,
and scope. Below I provide my assessment of how the major issues have been addressed. 
1. Methods, Transparency, and Ethics 
The incorporation of methodological details from the supplemental materials into the main text significantly improves
readability. The corrected and clarified ethics statement, together with inclusion of approval and consent documents,
addresses concerns from both reviewers. 
2. Models of Sepsis and Experimental Design 
The addition of the CLP model is a major improvement that strengthens the translational relevance of the work and
addresses the limitation of relying solely on LPS. Revisions regarding randomization, blinding, housing, and
ARRIVE/MQTiPSS guidelines improve rigor and reporting. 
3. Statistical Analyses 
The re-analysis of data using appropriate statistical approaches is appreciated. I encourage the authors to ensure that figure
legends clearly specify the statistical tests, post-hoc corrections, and sample sizes, as this was a central concern from both
reviewers. The issue of relatively small n (3–4 in some experiments) is partly mitigated by transparency but remains a
limitation that should be explicitly acknowledged. 
4. Cell Models 
Replacing HUVECs with HPMECs is an important correction. The justification for using HEK293 cells for protein–protein
interaction studies is well explained, though it remains a limitation in terms of physiological relevance and should be
discussed explicitly. 
5. Histology 
Reviewer #2’s concerns about poor lung histology images were significant. The authors state that new, higher-quality
images have been provided. If the revised figures indeed meet standard quality, this point is resolved; otherwise, histology
remains a weakness. 
6. Specificity of FGF13 Regulation 
The additional data in myocardial infarction and previous findings in diabetic nephropathy suggest that downregulation of
FGF13 is not specific to sepsis, but may reflect a broader injury/stress signature. This is an important contextualization and
should be framed in the discussion to avoid overstating specificity. 
7. Mechanism and Scope 
The additional experiments on ERK/HIF1α signaling, glycolysis, and cell-type specificity strengthen the mechanistic claims.
The evidence that effects are stimulus- and expression-dependent is convincing. However, conclusions should be
moderated to emphasize context dependence rather than universal application. 
8. Therapeutic Implications 
The paradox that FGF13 is downregulated in sepsis yet contributes to injury is intriguing. The authors provide thoughtful
mechanistic explanations (p53 regulation, enhanced protein interactions despite downregulation). Nevertheless, therapeutic
claims should be tempered, given these complexities and the translational uncertainties. 
9. Presentation and Language 
The manuscript has been substantially revised for clarity and language. The addition of a limitations section and corrections
to references are appreciated. 
Minor Comments – 
Clarifications - in page 5 line 105 please re-word or clarify LPS is a model sterile injury. 
Language – there are still minor problems with editing language: e.g., Line 118 “FGFs critically regulate lung
development…” the phrasing does not make much sense: Do you mean FGF is a critical regulator of lung development OR
FGF’s critical regulation during lung development? Removing redundancies would also help reading e.g. in both no-sepsis
patients and sepsis patients – this could be changed to both non-septic and septic patients. Again – employing a language
editor to go through the manuscript would significantly improve understanding by the reader. 
Overall Assessment 
This manuscript has undergone extensive revision and addresses many of the key methodological and interpretive issues
raised in the first round of review. Remaining limitations include the breadth of claims regarding specificity, the paradox of
downregulation versus pathogenicity, and the reliance on certain cell models.

currently a highly recognized research approach widely applied in numerous high-quality

12. How selective is SCH772984? What are the off-target effects? What is the clinical utility of 
SCH772984?
Response: Thank you for your question. Below is a point-by-point response to your inquiries.
SCH772984 is a highly selective ERK1/2 inhibitor with a unique binding mechanism. 
Structural studies revealed that SCH772984 induces the formation of a previously unknown binding 
pocket between the αC helix and P-loop in ERK1/2 (PMID: 25195011). Its selectivity stems from 
this distinctive binding mode, which involves three key interactions: indazole binding to the hinge 
region, pyrrolidine linker interactions with the conserved active site, and phenyl-pyrimidine 
positioning in the induced P-loop pocket. This creates a highly specific binding profile that 
distinguishes it from other kinase inhibitors. And the slow dissociation kinetics (25-80 minute half-
life) also contributes to its sustained activity. These properties confer high selectivity and low 
nanomolar inhibition (8.3 nM and 2.7 nM for ERK1 (IC 50) and ERK2 (IC 50), respectively) of 
SCH772984 for ERK1/2. 
Regarding SCH772984's off-target effects, this study (PMID: 25195011) demonstrates that its 
inhibitory potency against alternative kinases-including CLK2, DRAK1, and TTK-is dramatically 
reduced, displaying merely 1/70th to 1/40th of the potency observed against its primary ERK1/2 
targets. This pronounced differential in binding affinity highlights the compound's exceptional 
selectivity profile. Furthermore, structural analyses reveal that SCH772984 adopts canonical yet 
distinct Type I binding modes when interacting with off-target kinases such as haspin (IC₅₀=398 nM) 
and JNK1 (IC₅₀=1080 nM), which differs from its unique binding mechanism with ERK1/2.
SCH772984 demonstrates robust inhibition of the ERK signaling pathway, exhibiting 
remarkable efficacy in suppressing the proliferation of tumor cells resistant to BRAF and MEK 
inhibitors in preclinical studies (PMID: 23614898, 28143781). Beyond its promising antineoplastic 
properties, this compound has also shown therapeutic potential in mitigating hepatic ischemia-
reperfusion injury (PMID: 38638379). Despite these encouraging findings, SCH772984 has yet to 
advance to clinical trials due to significant pharmaceutical limitations-specifically its high clearance 
rate and poor membrane permeability—resulting in sub-optimal absorption and diminished 
bioavailability in rodent models (PMID: 30934534). To address these pharmaceutical shortcomings, 
researchers developed MK-8353, an optimized derivative that maintains the therapeutic efficacy

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

while substantially improving drug delivery characteristics. MK-8353 exhibits enhanced oral 
bioavailability across various species, achieves potent inhibition with IC 50 values in the low 
nanomolar range, and importantly, avoids cross-inhibition of MEK1/2. The phase I clinical trial 
(MK-8353-001, NCT01358331) evaluated this compound's safety profile, dosing parameters, and 
preliminary efficacy in 26 patients with metastatic melanoma, colorectal cancer, and other solid 
tumors. Results indicated that MK-8353 was well-tolerated at doses up to 400 mg twice daily, with 
measurable anti-tumor responses observed in three of the fifteen evaluable patients —all of whom 
harbored BRAFV600-mutant melanomas (PMID: 29467321).

Response: We appreciate your thoughtful questions, which raise several points of significant 
scientific importance. Please find our detailed responses below.
The regulatory role of ERK signaling in HIF-1α is well-established in the literature (PMID: 
28928465, 33638620, 37500654, 26579469), thus we did not elaborate extensively on how FGF13 
influences this particular mechanism. Nevertheless, to address your specific concerns, we conducted 
additional experiments to examine this relationship. As is widely recognized in the field, PHD2 
(Prolyl Hydroxylase Domain 2) catalyzes the hydroxylation of specific proline residues in HIF-1α, 
which subsequently targets this transcription factor for degradation via the canonical ubiquitin-
proteasome pathway (PMID: 32853548). Previous research has demonstrated that ERK signaling 
can phosphorylate PHD2 at its Ser125 residue, thereby inhibiting its enzymatic activity. This 
inhibition consequently reduces HIF-1α hydroxylation, ultimately resulting in HIF-1α protein 
accumulation (PMID: 31285371). Our investigations yielded results consistent with this established 
mechanism. Notably, knockdown of Fgf13 produced effects comparable to those observed with

attenuated ERK signaling. Specifically, in both HPMECs and THP-1 cell lines, knockdown of Fgf13
significantly diminished the phosphorylation of PHD2 at Ser125, while concurrently enhancing 
HIF-1α hydroxylation (Fig. R2(A, B)). Furthermore, we observed a marked increase in K48-linked 
polyubiquitination of HIF-1α following Fgf13 knockdown, providing compelling evidence for 
accelerated HIF-1α proteasomal degradation under these conditions (Fig. R2(C, D)).
At the same time, there might be a small misunderstanding here. In fact, we did not propose 
the concept that "FGF13's promotion of ERK signaling occurs on the cytoskeleton." We only
