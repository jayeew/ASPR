Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Capelo et al. present experimental results on the stability of particle-gas mixtures under conditions mimicking that in planet-
forming disks. Such dust-gas instabilities are thought to play prominent roles in modern planet formation theory. However,
experimental studies are needed to verify them. The authors claim the observation of a Kelvin-Helmholtz instability (KHI) in
their experiments. While this interpretation needs to be clarified (see below), the fact that they observe a gas flow being
destabilized by dust-loading is significant in and of itself because this is a central concept in planet formation theory. 
As a non-expert in experiments, I cannot scrutinize the experimental setup and the related data analysis. However, the
presentation of the KHI analyses (Section 4.2) can be improved. It is unclear how the authors map their experiment to the
standard KHI problem of two layers of fluids with different densities and velocities. A cartoon illustration may help. 
1. The author states that the phase velocity of unstable modes is given by (1), which is also the center of mass velocity of the
dust-gas mixture. However, wouldn’t the center of mass velocity be zero without external forces? 
My confusion comes from the notion that in the protoplanetary disks, dust drifts inwards while gas moves out to conserve
angular momentum, giving vcenter = 0. The center of mass azimuthal velocity is non-zero, but dust and gas are supported
mainly by rotation. 
2. I do not understand the rationale behind solving (2) and (3). Is it to obtain the expected steady state of the dust-laden
centerline? Why isn’t there a pressure term in the gas equation? Without any external forces, I would have thought the
relevant dynamical equations are: 
(d/dt)vg + vg.grad(vg) = - (1/rhog)*(dP/dx) - epsilon*(vg - vd)/tf 
(d/dt)vd + vd.grad(vd) = -(vd - vg)/tf 
However, these do not admit a non-trivial, uniform steady state since, in that case, the left-hand side vanishes, implying vd =
vg. Indeed, from Eq (3), if the system reaches steady-state, then vd(i+1) = vd(i), so vd = vg. 
I suggest the authors write down the full governing equations for the dust and gas fluids before explaining how they calculate
steady states. 
3. I assume the analogy with KHI is that the centerline is dust-laden, but the flow above and below it is pure gas. The velocity
difference that goes into the KHI dispersion relation (5) should then be the centerline velocity of the dust-plus-gas mixture
and the gas velocity above or below the centerline. Likewise, the densities that go into (2) should be the total density of the
centerline and the gas flow above and below it. 
However, it is unclear if the authors meant this in (5). This equation can be misinterpreted as using dust and gas velocities
and densities within the same layer, not two layers across space. Some explicit clarification is needed. 
4. Related to the above, the conventional KHI is related to differential velocities in space, while streaming-type instabilities
are related to differential velocities between different components at the same location. A consequence of this distinction is

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

that KHI can still operate in the limit tf -> 0, or a perfectly coupled dust-gas suspension. On the other hand, streaming-type
instabilities require tf strictly greater than zero, or partial coupling. 
Now, the authors find mode frequencies > 300Hz. Together with tf = 0.006s, this gives a dimensionless stopping time > 1.8,
suggesting that dust is partially coupled. It is unclear whether streaming-type instabilities can be ruled out. 
To this end, the authors may want to argue that streaming-type instabilities are inconsistent with their results. Assuming the
only waves in the gas are sound waves, the only streaming-type instability is the acoustic resonant drag instability (e.g.,
Hopkins & Squire, 2018; Magnan et al. 2024). This requires the resonant condition: 
f = kx*|vd - vg| 
to be satisfied, but this would translate to a drift between dust and gas at the sound-speed, which isn’t the case. However,
see Hopkins & Squire for a discussion of non-resonant drag instabilities at subsonic drifts. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the paper "Experimental verification of granular shear-flow instability in the Epstein regime", Capelo et al. report the result
of a parabolic-flight experiment in which KH instability seems to be developed. Instability in the protoplanetary disc is one of
the most essential phenomena in forming planetesimals within a reasonable timescale. However, it is difficult to mimic the
planet-forming environment. Thus, this type of direct experimental approach has not been performed. In this sense, this
paper is quite meaningful. However, the evidence of instability is slightly limited. Although the obtained data are roughly
consistent with the KH instability, fully developed instability cannot be observed. I understand such a long observation is
very hard. This experimental attempt is the first step for this kind of phenomenon. While the content does not contain a
brand-new physical insight (this is a kind of experimental report rather than a scientifically meaningful paper), the challenge
itself might merit publication. If the journal Communications Physics encourages this type of work, this paper should be
published. Anyway, I have some comments that should be considered before the final judgment of this paper. 
1. The raw data should be presented. While the authors conducted PIV measurement, only one analyzed example is shown
in Fig.A1. To evaluate the accuracy of the analysis and measurement, the raw image data examples should be presented.
(Sample movies of the raw data and PIV result might be better.) In addition, image speculation, etc. should also be
mentioned. The velocity time series (analyzed by wavelet method) should also be presented. Showing the raw data is
important for experimental papers. 
2. Method should be mentioned prior to Results. It was very difficult for me to understand the data quality, how to measure
the quantities, the experimental setup, etc. This comment relates to the point #1. The method should be (at least briefly)

3. I do not understand the meaning of Fig.1. The authors insist on sinusoidal oscillation. However, the data do not clearly
show the oscillation. The wavelength and amplitude of two curves are not identical. After this, wavelet analysis is applied.
The spectra are more or less understandable. However, the role of Fig.1 is not clear. Even in Fig.2, the reason for the
intermittent increase of the spectral intensity is not very clear. That has a 10-100 Hz timescale. For the length scale,
lambda=3 cm is mainly used in this paper. However, there are some timescales. The velocity values are also used in Sec.
2.4. A much more detailed explanation for them (and their relations) is necessary. 
4. KH instability usually occurs at the boundary of different flow rates. The situation is different from the mixture of solid
particles and rarefied gas. To consider the two-phase-flow situation, a sufficient amount of particles and gas is necessary. As
mentioned in the paper, particles can be regarded as pressureless gas, perhaps. Anyway, a more reasonable explanation
should be added.

are much more different (10^3 orders...). Perhaps, I misunderstood the definition of density. If so, please clearly explain it. 
-The definitions of epsilon and epsilon_0 are confused. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study aims to experimentally verify the two-fluid approximation, widely used to handle the dust-gas systems in the
planet formation theory, by conducting experiments simulating the dust-gas environment in protoplanetary disks. The current
two-fluid approximation is fundamentally a theoretical framework, and it is difficult to observe the detailed behavior of actual
astronomical two-fluid systems. Therefore, experimental verification like this study is clearly important. 
In this study, wavelet analysis results successfully observed the growth and migration of wave-like patterns and the
tendency for longer wavelengths, which are commonly seen in shear-type instabilities. This manuscript could be the
beginning of a series of studies that will lead the theoretical framework to empirical science.

However, as described in detail below, this paper seems to have some significant problems. Therefore, the reviewer
recommends that this manuscript be published in the journal after sufficient revisions on the following points. 
------------------------------ 
= Major comments = 
1. The manuscript should focus more on the examination of two-fluid behavior directly related to the current experimental
system. 
The entire manuscript should be rewritten to leverage the advantages of a simple pipe-flow-like (or Poiseuille-flow-like)
axisymmetric system without rotation. The current structure seems to address unsolvable problems even if this experiment is
pursued. In particular, repeated references in the manuscript to the streaming instability, where differential rotation is
intrinsically important (or, in RDI terms, resonance with epicyclic motion is intrinsically essential), are misleading to the
readers and do not seem beneficial. I recommend focusing on verifying the validity of the two-fluid approximation and
revising the overall structure. 
----- 
2. I find the current comparison with the KHI (Kelvin-Helmholtz Instability) not insightful. 
The authors seem to be trying to compare the velocity differences between the terminal velocities of dust and gas in a dust-
gas system subjected to Epstein drag and discuss the correspondence with the theory of KHI. However, the reviewer feels
that this line of thoughts is not functioning at all. 
Firstly, the discussions in the cited papers, or the classical dust sedimentation-driven shear instability in the context of the
planet formation, are gas motion instabilities. These KHIs are driven by the velocity difference between the dust-rich gas
layer, whose velocity is altered by dust, and the dust-poor gas layer, which is close to the original gas velocity. Initially, the
comparison should be made between such gas velocities (more precisely, the gas velocity and the dust-gas mixture
velocity), and the instability due to the shear should be examined. (Strangely, in the authors' previous paper [24] on the same
experimental system, this point seems to be correctly argued.) 
Conversely, if discussing the shear instability at the contact surface between granular flow and gas as described in the
current manuscript, a comparison with previous studies for such the situation is necessary (in fact, such experiments and
studies exist). However, given the low dust-to-gas ratio and the short frictional timescale in this study, it is probably
appropriate to interpret the observed dust motion as a result of a gas velocity difference instability (i.e., the gas drag locally
dominates dust motion in the current system). 
Also, the part deriving the steady-state velocities of dust and gas from Eqs. (2)-(3) (if this means giving v_{1}, v_{2} in Eq.(1))
does not make sense to me. The steady-state solution of Eqs. (2)-(3) is only when v_{dust} = v_{gas}, i.e., when dust is
completely carried by gas and moves at the same velocity. This is a natural consequence in a system without external forces
except for the aerodynamic drag. Therefore, there is no velocity difference between dust and gas here, and shear instability
is not driven. 
In addition, the description of the density is incorrect. It would be appropriate to define it in a form like \rho_{1} = \rho_{g},
\rho_{2} = (1+\epsilon)\rho_{g}, i.e, the gas density and the gas-dust mixture density. 
Considering the above discussion, the estimates of dust-to-gas ratio and dust velocity also change, so this is an important
point related to the core of this study. The authors must clear the reviewer's doubts on this point. 
----- 
3. If the dust layer is indeed causing instability as the authors say, from the perspective of verifying the two-fluid
approximation, it is necessary to examine whether the dust layer really feels fluid shear (i.e., whether the continuum
approximation for dust component is valid). 
Estimating how many dust particles are included in the typical wavelength size would help understand this point. 
----- 
4. A comparison with numerical experiments should be made, or discussions from such a perspective should be added. 
Various phenomena that cannot be reproduced in the current experimental system, such as instabilities occurring in rotating
systems, are considered important in the actual planet formation processes. However, even if it is difficult to directly verify
those phenomena, the advantage of this experiment is that it can verify the theoretical assumptions related to the core of
those phenomena. In other words, it is essential to confirm whether the widely used two-fluid equations and the numerical
simulations based on them can predict the results of this experiment. 
Ideally, the authors themselves would conduct numerical experiments, make simulated observations of the results, and
confirm consistency with the experiment. If that is difficult, providing sufficient information for the planet formation community
to reproduce the authors' experiment numerically is desirable.

Author Response: Yes, this is true. The center of mass velocity is zero in the steady

time for the dust and gas in the center of the flow to approach the same velocity, which is less
than the gas velocity in the adjacent dust-free layer. The differential motion that drives the
fluid instability then arises from different velocities between the dusty fluid and the dust-free
