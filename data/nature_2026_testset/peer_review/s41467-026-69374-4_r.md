Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Ultrafast solvent-mediated roaming... 
Su et al 
This paper compares the dissociation dynamics of bromoform in methanol and methyl cyclohexane, polar and non-polar
solvents, using ultrafast time-resolved X-ray solution scattering (TRXSS). Roaming dynamics are found to play an important
role in both, but distinct behavior is seen with methanol undergoing reaction facilitated by roaming, while the methyl
cyclohexane case shows a long-lived intermediate formed by roaming-mediated isomerization. 
Roaming reactions are the subject of considerable ongoing interest with broad implications across many aspects of
chemical reaction dynamics. Roaming reactions in the condensed phase are far less studied as the techniques required are
challenging and the situation far more complex than in the gas phase. This paper represents a spectacular example of how
to meet these challenges. Although the results from diffraction experiments are low--dimensional and underdetermined,
when combined with state-of-the-art theory and careful analysis, deep insight can be obtained with some confidence. 
The quality of the scientific investigation, the analysis, and the clarity of the presentation make this paper eminently suitable
for Nature Comm. I have one concern I think the authors should address. Almost nothing is said about the photoexcitation
process, the excited state lifetime or the decay dynamics. In the SI I find they are modeling the triplet, which I presume is the
relevant excited state, but this should be stated and justified in the main text. Then it seems they are not actually doing any
nonadiaatic dynamics, rather, for the excited state dynamics they sample from configurations on the ground state over the
100 ps of the simulation. Are the AIMD calculations entirely in the triplet state? Clearly the methanolysis reaction is on the
ground electronic state. Some clarity on this point, in the main text as well as the SI, would be helpful. Once the authors
address this point I recommend publication.

Ultrafast solvent-modulated roaming mechanism in bromoform revealed by femtosecond X-ray solution scattering 
Authors: by P. Su et al. 
By combining the results from femtosecond time-resolved x-ray scattering, machine learning, DFT and molecular dynamics,
the authors came to conclusion that CHBr3 undergoes roaming-mediated structural dynamics in both methanol and
methylcyclohexane. To my knowledge, it is the first work (after ref. [3]) which gives support to roaming-mediated structural
dynamics in CHBr3. The significance of this reaction mechanism is very high, and the results are noteworthy and significant.

The manuscript may be publishable, but it requires major revisions. The revised manuscript should be reviewed again. My
detailed comments are below, please see #1-17. 
Overall, the Conclusions (line 290) state that “CHBr3 undergoes roaming-mediated structural dynamics within 150 fs in both
methanol and methylcyclohexane”, but the oscillatory, non-monotonic angle/bond distance behavior (taken to be signatures
of roaming, #9) seems to be present within the first 600 fs (see #17). In the original work on CHBr3, ref. [3] it is a light C atom
of a CHBr2 fragment that roams, but in the manuscript, despite bong angle and bond distance change being similar to what
was described in ref. [3], it seems to be proposed that it is the Br-atom fragment that roams. Br is heavy, and much heavier
that a hydrogen atom roaming of which in formaldehyde in the gas phase was initially suggested by Bowman, so forces to
make it wander should be much, much stronger. This is hard to believe, so the difference C vs. Br should be explained. The
manuscript data clearly show, in my opinion, the formation of CHBr2-Br, which is the isomer (the structure on the isomer
potential) in both methanol and methylcyclohexane, so this primary pathway, which is isomerization, is the same in both
solvent environments, Fig, 2b,d vs. Fig. 3b,d, and Fig. 4a,b. Nevertheless, the Abstract says that “In methanol …it is
solvolysis without isomerization”, lines 35-36. Secondary products are different in methanol as hot CHBr2-Br undergoes a
facile reaction with solvent; this has been well known, #5. Solvent coordination is a key parameter for roaming reactions,
lines 40-41, but the meaning of solvent coordination is unclear (H-bonding with CHBr3?) and no evidence is given. 
Comments 
1. Abstract, lines 35-36 
“In methanol, roaming enhances solute-solvent interactions, leading to solvolysis without isomerization.” This statement
appears further (lines 277-278): “ the compact hydroxyl-rich environment of methanol hinders isomerization and instead
promotes methanolysis”. 
The formation of CHBr2-Br, which is the vibrationally hot structure on the isomer potential in clear both methanol and
methylcyclohexane, Fig, 2b,d vs. Fig. 3b,d, and Fig. 4a,b. It is the formation of the equilibrated ground-state iso-CHBr3 that
the methanol environment ‘hinders’. 
2. Abstract, lines 40-41 
“Our findings establish solvent coordination as a key parameter for roaming reactions, with broad implications for
photochemical outcomes.” However, lines 261-262 state: “Although explicit solvent interactions between CHBr3 and
methanol were not directly modelled…” 
I wonder than how one can state that solvent coordination as a key parameter if explicit solvent interactions were not
modelled? The experimental data in the manuscript are silent regarding solvent coordination, apart from the statement
(based on simulation of the radial distribution function of ground state CHBr3, lines 273-274), that the cage radios in
methanol is significantly smaller than in methylcyclohexane. How this related to solvent coordination being a key parameter
needs to be explained.

line 47 
“As photoactivation is indispensable in Br2 release from CHBr3…”. Is this a unimolecular reaction? Has it been ever
experimentally observed? Reference(s) must be given. 
4. lines 65-68 
“…the lifetime of the iso-CHBr2-Br …. relaxes within a few picoseconds in neat bromoform, ref. [3]”. I do not think that ref. [3]
is relevant for iso-CHBr2-Br relaxation in neat bromoform. Ref. [25] is a poor choice in my opinion because most
observations in that work were due to a Br-atom interacting with the surrounding CHBr3 solvent on a sub-ps time scale.
However, ref. [3], and ref. [26] specifically, are relevant: iso-CHBr3 being metastable in methylcyclohexane and the lifetime
of iso-CHBr3 being within one nanosecond in acetonitrile. Instead of ref. [25], I recommend the authors to refer to the
subsequent paper by Crim and co-workers, J. Phys. Chem. A 2013, 117, 14, 2899
(https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/jp310737d), where the deficiencies of ref. [25] were addressed and the conclusion
became very similar to those in ref. [26]. 
5. lines 78-79 
The authors state that “there is the ultrafast solvent-mediated reaction pathway” (in methanol) “in which the isomer rapidly
reacts with the solvent”, referring to their previous work, ref. [33] and to time-resolved Raman spectroscopy results by Phillips
group, ref. [28]. They continue: “…the mechanistic origin of the contrasting solvent-dependent reactivity remains unsolved.”
However, in my opinion, the mechanistic origin has already been firmly established: isomers of di- and polyhalogenated
alkanes (X = Br, I) readily react with CH3OH via O-H insertion/HX elimination reactions. This reaction may be fast, so that
the formation of the product species is already observed on at 1.5 ps after excitation (ref. [28], CHBr2-Br or iso-CHBr3 in
methanol), or slower, e.g., iso-CHI3 decays with a 740-ps lifetime by reacting with CH3OH via O-H insertion/HI elimination
reactions, ChemPhysChem, 2009, 10, 1895, doi: 10.1002/cphc.200900122, ref. [34] in the current manuscript. Radical
species react via the same reaction but much slower, ref. [28, 34] and other work by Phillips group. So, the mechanistic
origin is established; what is not determined is the iso-CHBr3 lifetime in methanol. Apparently, it is < 1.5 ps.

“…our results reveal that roaming dynamics initiates universally at the beginning of photochemical reaction …” 
This has been shown before, ref. [3,4], and with superior temporal resolution.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents a well-executed and insightful study on solvent-modulated reaction pathways in bromoform,
employing femtosecond X-ray solution scattering (TRXSS) to resolve structural dynamics on ultrafast timescales. The work
lays a solid foundation for further investigations into solvent coordination effects and roaming-mediated reaction channels in
solution-phase chemistry. 
The authors convincingly demonstrate the essential role of solvation in determining reaction mechanisms, motivating the
study of organic solvents through connections to aqueous-phase behavior and atmospheric gas-phase processes,
particularly those involving halogens. 
Furthermore, the manuscript is well structured with the experimental setup described concisely, followed by a detailed
analysis of the results. Key methodological information is provided in the Supporting Information, which enhances
reproducibility and credibility. 
This is a high-quality manuscript with significant insight into ultrafast solvent-modulated reaction dynamics. With minor
clarifications and additions, I strongly support its publication. 
1. Figure 2 is rich in information and instrumental in illustrating the results of the study. Enhancing clarity by explicitly stating
the three distinct pathways (direct dissociation, cage-induced methanolyis of the direct dissociated molecules, and roaming-
mediated methanolysis of short-lived isomers) in the figure caption would aid comprehension. Aligning the color scheme of
the arrows in b) with the colors used in panels c)–e) could further improve visual mapping. 
2. The integration of ATM, GFA, and SVD analyses with AIMD simulations forms a robust analytical framework. The
congruence of timescales and findings across methods, particularly in methanol, demonstrates the strength of this multi-
modal approach. A comparative summary table of results from ATM, GFA, SVD, and AIMD (e.g. associated time constants)
would emphasize methodological convergence and provide readers with a quick reference for cross-validation.

1130 cm-1 and much smaller than in iodine-containing systems (I-atom, SOC 5069 cm-1), which indeed is known to undergo
sub-100 fs ISC.

from which may lead to the delayed population of the T1. 
The methanolysis reaction is on the S0 surface (Section 8 of the SI), which is reached from T1 via T1/S0 intersection. Does
this intersection play a role in methylcyclohexane and when does the iso-form reach the ground electronic state in this
solvent?

Ultrafast solvent-modulated roaming mechanism in bromoform revealed by femtosecond X-ray solution scattering   Authors: by P. Su et al.  By combining the results from femtosecond time-resolved x-ray scattering, machine learning, DFT and molecular dynamics, the authors came to conclusion that CHBr3 undergoes roaming-mediated structural dynamics in both methanol and methylcyclohexane. To my knowledge, it is the first work (after ref. [3]) which gives support to roaming-mediated structural dynamics in CHBr3. The significance of this reaction mechanism is very high, and the results are noteworthy and significant.  The manuscript may be publishable, but it requires major revisions. The revised manuscript should be reviewed again. My detailed comments are below, please see #1-17.  Overall, the Conclusions (line 290) state that “CHBr3 undergoes roaming-mediated structural dynamics within 150 fs in both methanol and methylcyclohexane”, but the oscillatory, non-monotonic angle/bond distance behavior (taken to be signatures of roaming, #9) seems to be present within the first 600 fs (see #17). In the original work on CHBr3, ref. [3] it is a light C atom of a CHBr2 fragment that roams, but in the manuscript, despite bong angle and bond distance change being similar to what was described in ref. [3], it seems to be proposed that it is the Br-atom fragment that roams. Br is heavy, and much heavier that a hydrogen atom roaming of which in formaldehyde in the gas phase was initially suggested by Bowman, so forces to make it wander should be much, much stronger. This is hard to believe, so the difference C vs. Br should be explained. The manuscript data clearly show, in my opinion, the formation of CHBr2-Br, which is the isomer (the structure on the isomer potential) in both methanol and methylcyclohexane, so this primary pathway, which is isomerization, is the same in both solvent environments, Fig, 2b,d vs. Fig. 3b,d, and Fig. 4a,b. Nevertheless, the Abstract says that “In methanol …it is solvolysis without isomerization”, lines 35-36. Secondary products are different in methanol as hot CHBr2-Br undergoes a facile reaction with solvent; this has been well known, #5. Solvent coordination is a key parameter for roaming reactions, lines 40-41, but the meaning of solvent coordination is unclear (H-bonding with CHBr3?) and no evidence is given.   Comments 1. Abstract, lines 35-36 “In methanol, roaming enhances solute-solvent interactions, leading to solvolysis without isomerization.” This statement appears further (lines 277-278): “ the compact hydroxyl-rich environment of methanol hinders isomerization and instead promotes methanolysis”. The formation of CHBr2-Br, which is the vibrationally hot structure on the isomer potential in clear both methanol and methylcyclohexane, Fig, 2b,d vs. Fig. 3b,d, and Fig. 4a,b.
