Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors presented a study that analyzed the mediation role of circulating proteins between multiple air pollutants and 30
diseases in the UKBB cohort. The study is comprehensive in terms of using multiple data sources (e.g., air pollutants,
proteins, disease outcomes, etc.) and using various analytic approaches (e.g., PWAS, survival analyses, functional
enrichments, etc.). 
The following list includes my detailed comments regarding the study design, data, and methodology. 
1. The major concern of the methodology is that in this PWAS-type analysis, there should be careful consideration of
multiple comparisons, as many pairs of exposure-outcomes. The use of a significance level of 0.05 in this manuscript could
lead to large Type I errors. For example, in the following study with multiple disease outcomes, they documented as
“adjusting for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction.” 
a. Wei, Y., Wang, Y., Di, Q., Choirat, C., Wang, Y., Koutrakis, P., Zanobetti, A., Dominici, F. and Schwartz, J.D., 2019. Short
term exposure to fine particulate matter and hospital admission risks and costs in the Medicare population: time stratified,
case crossover study. bmj, 367. 
2. How are the individuals with and without valid proteomic data different in their population characteristics, as well as their
exposure levels to air pollution? 
3. Related to Question 2, how are the results obtained from 49,242 individuals (9.8%) generalizable to the entire UKBB
cohort? In the discussion section, the sentences suggested that the results utilized “data from over 500,000 participants in
the UK Biobank,” which is not entirely accurate, given that the important merit of this paper is on mediation analyses. 
4. It is unclear to me how the air pollutants data were linked to the participants; are they air pollutants between April 4, 2006
and October 1, 2010, or earlier years? How many years, and whether they are multi-year averaged measures? 
5. The specific time periods/orders of air pollution exposures and disease outcomes are unclear to me. For example, is the
air pollution exposure measured before the onset of these diseases? According to the Study Design section, it sounds like
the entire study is cross-sectional, with only one air pollution measurement and disease outcomes for each participant. Is it
possible that the disease onset is actually prior to the air pollution exposure measured, which leads to an important concern
about the validity of the causal link? 
6. The same logic in Question 5 applied to the relationship between air pollutants and proteins and proteins to diseases,
since “this study relies on single time point measurements of proteomic data.” Given that the study was considered a
mediation analysis, the time order of air pollution -> proteins -> disease outcomes seems rather important. 
7. In the mediation analysis, it is unclear what regression techniques were used. The formulation looks like a linear
regression in Line 568. However, this is different from the Cox models used in other analyses. Given that the mediation
analyses also used disease outcomes, which are assumed to be time-to-event. How do the authors adjust for the use of the
regression formulation? 
8. 
In the analyses using Cox models, are they under the assumption that each disease outcome only happened once? How
about repeated measures of outcomes during 4 years? 
9. The use of “exhibited mediation effects in more than two diseases” as one of the inclusion criteria of APPRS sounds ad
hoc. Is it possible that one protein is very important to one disease, yet is being excluded because it failed to meet this
criterion? Also, why not three diseases? 
10. In the development of APPRS, if this procedure followed the development of polygenic risk scores (PRS), the score
should be disease-specific. I found the APPRS is agnostic to multiple diseases. Any reason not to develop a disease-
specific APPRS? 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study explores the biological mechanisms underlying the association between air pollution and disease, with a
particular focus on the mediating role of circulating proteins. Leveraging large-scale data from the UK Biobank, the authors
identified 30 diseases significantly associated with air pollution exposure and uncovered 1,089 pollution-associated

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

proteins, among which 296 were found to significantly mediate disease risk. These mediating proteins were enriched in
immune-related pathways and exhibited disease-specific patterns, including MAPK signaling, innate immunity, carbohydrate
metabolism, and respiratory cell proliferation. Based on these findings, the authors developed a novel Air Pollution Protein
Risk Score (APPRS), which demonstrated strong predictive capacity for disease risk in both the UK Biobank and two
external validation cohorts. Moreover, the integration of APPRS into baseline models yielded improved prediction
performance. This work highlights the central role of circulating proteins in mediating the health impacts of air pollution and
introduces APPRS as a promising tool for personalized risk assessment and precision public health strategies. The study is
well-conceived, methodologically sound, and clearly written. However, I believe the manuscript would benefit from
addressing the following concerns prior to acceptance: 
(1)Given that the temporal dynamics of exposure can vary significantly across diseases, the authors should clarify how the
exposure window for air pollution was selected. Was a disease-specific sensitive window considered, or was a uniform
window applied to all outcomes? 
(2) In the Introduction section, the authors are encouraged to summarize existing methodologies that integrate air pollution
exposure with biological markers such as proteins. This context would help readers understand how APPRS builds upon or
advances previous approaches. 
(3) While APPRS showed overall improvement in prediction, the gains appear modest for many diseases. The authors
should discuss the limitations of APPRS more explicitly, especially in terms of its discriminatory power and practical
applicability. 
(4) Although the statistical mediation analysis provides a screening tool to identify candidate proteins, further evidence is
needed to support their biological plausibility. The authors are encouraged to elaborate on whether there is experimental or
literature-based support for the mediating roles of the highlighted proteins.

We thank the reviewer for these insightful questions regarding the construction 
and interpretation of the APPRS.  Below, we provide detailed point -by-point

(Q1) “How could you make sure the effects from different proteins are not overlap 
and can be additive?” 
The APPRS is constructed under the assumption that proteins contribute additively 
and independently  to the risk of air pollution -related diseases. This is a common 
approach in composite risk score construction (e.g., polygenic risk scores , PRS) [12], 
especially when the underlying biological mechanisms are complex and potentially 
non-linear.  
While protein pathways may overlap biologically, our g oal was not to model 
mechanistic dependencies, but rather to aggregate statistically estimated mediation 
effects (ACMEs) of individual proteins that represent their independent contributions 
to disease risk via air pollution exposure. Furthermore, the proteins included in APPRS 
were selected based on stringent significance and consistency criteria (ACME FDR < 
0.05 and mediation proportion > 4%), which helps minimize redundancy and enhance 
robustness in the final score.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

(Q2) “Why only include 65 proteins?” 
We included 65 proteins to construct the APPRS based on their mediation 
performance across multiple diseases. The inclusion criteria were:  a) FDR < 0.05 for 
both the ACME and total effect in at least two or more AP index -disease mediation 
models; b) Mediation proportion > 4%. These thresholds were designed to ensure both 
the statistical significance and biological relevance of the mediators. The final list of 65 
proteins thus represents a robust subset with reproducible mediation signals across 
diseases. 
(Q3) “How could you get ACME?” 
The calculation of ACME (Average Causal Mediation Effect) for each pollutant -
protein-disease triplet strictly follows the causal mediation analysis framework 
proposed by [13], implemented in the “mediation” R package. Each ACME was derived 
from the following steps: 
 An Exposure–Mediator model estimating the effect of air pollution exposure 
on protein expression:  
Protein = α0 + α1⋅Pollution + ε1 
 An Exposure –Mediator–Outcome model assess ing the effects of both 
pollution and protein mediator on disease outcome: 
Disease = β0 + β1⋅Pollution + β2⋅Protein + ε2 
 The ACME for each protein represent s the indirect effect of air pollution on 
disease risk mediated through that specific protein, calculated as: 
ACME = α1⋅β2 
To account for potential sampling variability and ensure the robustness of the 
mediation effect estimates, we performed bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to 
estimate 95% confidence intervals for the ACME values.

(Q2) “Figure 4 shows that many values are negative — what does a negative 
APPRS value represent?” 
The APPRS is a continuous, relative risk sco re that quantifies an individual ’s 
protein-mediated susceptibility to air pollution-associated diseases. A negative APPRS 
value indicates lower -than-average molecular susceptibility relative to the study 
population, and a positive value suggests higher sus ceptibility. The score is not an 
absolute risk measure, but rather reflects cumulative weighted protein signatures 
relevant to pollution-related disease burden. 
(Q3) “Does the negativity come from the direction of the ACME coefficients or 
from the protein expression values?” 
The negative APPRS values arise primarily from individual variation in protein 
expression levels, rather than from the direction of the ACME coefficients. All proteins 
included in the APPRS construction had positive mediation effects  (Supplementary

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 15 -->

Table 11) and were directionally consistent in mediating increased disease risk 
associated with air pollution exposure. Therefore, the sign of the APPRS is determined 
by the individual's relative expression of these proteins after standardization (z-scoring), 
not by conflicting directions in mediation weights. 
(Q4) “When calculating the weighted sum of protein expression levels, how do 
you account for the fact that some proteins may have positive ones while others have 
negative ones? Wouldn ’t t his cause offsetting effects between positive and negative 
contributions?” 
The positive and negative values in protein expression arise from the z -score 
normalization of protein levels across the population. Negative values indicate below-
average expressio n, and positive values indicate above -average expression.  In the
