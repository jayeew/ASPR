Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an innovative strategy for enhancing room-temperature phosphorescence (RTP) in organic
materials via the construction of rigid ionic-bonding networks. The authors employed alkyl chains functionalized with
quaternary ammonium groups as universal ionic hosts, effectively addressing the molecular compatibility issue in
conventional host–guest systems. With a high degree of experimental rigor and theoretical support, the study demonstrates
long-lived phosphorescence (up to 572.27 ms), wide color tunability, and practical applications in information encryption.
Some issues should be addressed as follows: 
1. Experimental procedure requires some more details. Critical experimental conditions are not sufficiently detailed. For
reproducibility, please expand the Materials and Methods or Supplementary Information accordingly. 
2. Figure 2e uses a different vertical axis scale or unit compared to other subfigures (e.g., 2a, 2f), which may mislead readers
when comparing emission intensities. 
3. Novelty needs clearer distinction from prior work. While the integration of ionic bonding and external heavy atom effect is
promising, similar concepts have been reported recently. The manuscript would benefit from a more explicit comparison with
these studies to highlight its unique contributions. 
4. UV photostability under prolonged excitation was not examined. These are important for practical applications and should
be addressed. 
5. Mass spectrometry data is missing for newly synthesized compounds and should be included. Several newly synthesized
molecules are central to the study. While NMR and single-crystal analyses are reported, molecular weight confirmation via
mass spectrometry (e.g., ESI-MS or MALDI-TOF) is not provided. For organic phosphorescent materials, confirming
molecular integrity is essential for structural verification and subsequent photophysical correlation. It is strongly
recommended to include high-resolution MS data for all key intermediates and final products in the Supplementary
Information to ensure the completeness of molecular characterization.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a novel and practical strategy for the development of RTP materials. The authors innovatively
employ ionic alkyl chain-based molecules as hosts and introduce quaternary ammonium groups to guest chromophores,
enabling a facile host–guest doping approach. This method effectively overcomes the common limitations associated with
precise host–guest molecular compatibility, offering a more universal and accessible platform for RTP material construction.
The manuscript is well-structured, the experimental results are convincing, and the mechanistic insights are clearly
articulated. The synergistic effect of host–guest doping and ionic interactions represents a significant conceptual
advancement in the design of non-conjugated RTP systems. Given the originality and potential impact of this work, I strongly

suppression of non-radiative decay and triplet state stabilization, it is essential to include the photoluminescence and
phosphorescence spectra of the pure host materials under identical measurement conditions.

Reviewer report: 
The manuscript by Tu et al. investigates the creation of afterglow materials aimed at extending triplet lifetimes to several
hundred milliseconds. The authors synthesized ionic alkyl chain molecules as hosts for ionic phosphorescent materials,
incorporating quaternary ammonium groups into the chromophores. By modifying isolated chromophores, they achieved
phosphorescence colors ranging from blue to orange red, with lifetimes reaching 572.27 ms. They assert that matching the
alkyl chain in the ionic host–guest system provides a rigid, ordered environment. Combined with the external heavy atom
effect, this arrangement stabilizes chromophores for prolonged phosphorescence. The strategy, they claim, relies on the
synergistic effects of host-guest doping and ionic bonding networks to enhance triplet stability. While the concept of ionic
bonding-mediated afterglow enhancement is established, the efficiency of other host–guest systems already exceed one
second. This limits the novelty of their findings. Based on the design approach and reported performance, this work does not
meet the standards required for publication in this journal. The topic may be more appropriate for a publication focused on
supramolecular materials. The authors would need to address significant concerns before the manuscript could be
considered suitable for its intended venue. 
1. Recent literature on ionic bond-mediated room-temperature phosphorescence (RTP) must be discussed and
appropriately cited. The reported RTP lifetime is less impressive when compared with published studies involving host–
guest systems. The author should reference relevant works demonstrating superior afterglow performance, such as the ionic
polymer matrix (https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202108025), ion-regulated RTP (Chem. Sci., 2024, 15, 4222–4237), and H-
bonding interaction studies (https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jpclett.4c03707). 
2. The introduction should include a discussion on lp–π and π–π interactions, citing examples from J. Phys. Chem. C 2019,
123, 22104–22103, and J. Phys. Chem. Lett., 2018, 9, 3808–3813. 
3. PL studies of new guests in different hosts, ranging from traditional oligomers to polymeric systems, should be conducted
for performance comparison. 
4. The energy gap of the sole emitter and any variation with host-guest combinations should be reported. The discussion
must address the orbital nature of the states, spin-orbit coupling, quantum yield of intersystem crossing, and rates of non-
radiative processes to substantiate claims. 
5. The rationale for chain matching as a means of enhancing triplet lifetimes requires clear explanation. 
6. Crystal structures should be provided, along with PL studies on crystals for comparison with powder samples. 
7. XRD patterns for both powder and crystal samples must be included, with differences related to PL behavior discussed. 
8. The minimal lifetime improvement upon increasing emitter concentration (27.85 ms to 48.50 ms) should be analysed.
Additional PL studies on varied hosts, as noted earlier, are recommended. 
9. HPLC and elemental analyses of new materials must be presented to confirm purity. 
10. In Supplementary Figure 40, the phosphorescence spectra of 4BrNapHBr in dilute solution exhibit multiple peaks, which
should be clarified.

Comment #3: Novelty needs clearer distinction from prior work. While the integration of ionic bonding 
and external heavy atom effect is promising, similar concepts have been reported recently. The manuscript 
would benefit from a more explicit comparison with these studies to highlight its unique contributions. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising the point regarding novelty. While the general concept of 
ionic stabilization has indeed been reported, our work establishes a fundamentally different design strategy. 
Rather than relying on simple ionic salts or rigid crystalline hosts, we deliberately engineered alkyl chain–
matched ionic host–guest systems. In this study, we introduce a versatile and modular approach for creating 
high-performance organic RTP materials using a universal ionic host-guest doping method. In this method, 
non-conjugated alkyl chains act as the host matrix. These chains naturally possess higher singlet and triplet 
energy levels than conjugated guest phosphorescent chromophores, effectively preventing reverse energy 
transfer. Furthermore, unlike the weak hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions typical in traditional 
host-guest systems, the consecutive ionic bonds in this work form ionic -bonding networks, providing 
notable advantages in preventing molecular motion and promoting the external he avy atom effect. 
Interestingly, most previous studies achieved this effect by directly adding heavy atoms, while this work 
accomplishes it through the matching of host-guest alkyl chains, demonstrating a different mechanism. Not 
only are the ionic -bonding networks formed by ionic nodes ( Fig. S1a and S1b ), but the quaternary 
ammonium alkyl chain matching principle in the host/guest structures (Fig. S1c) is also crucial in inhibiting 
non-radiative transitions of the chromophores, thereby enabling phosphoresce nce. This precise chain -
length matching creates an ordered and confined ionic microenvironment, which effectively stabilizes 
chromophores and minimizes nonradiative losses.  Importantly, the observed performance arises from a 
synergistic mechanism: ionic confinement suppresses vibrational dissipation, while the external heavy atom 
effect accelerates intersystem crossing. This dual regulation not only extends the triplet lifetime to 572 ms 
but also enables broad color tunability from blue to orange -red. Our c ontribution addresses a 
complementary challenge: to achieve color -tunable, solution-processable, and structurally modular ionic 
afterglow materials. We therefore believe our work represents a clear conceptual and methodological 
advance, providing a new sup ramolecular strategy with broad implications . The relevant sections of the 
revised manuscript include these discussions, and we hope the reviewer will find them acceptable.

Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for the thoughtful comments regarding the novelty of our 
ionic strategy. We would like to clarify that our work goes beyond the general concept of ionic stabilization 
and establishes a new design logic for afterglow materials. In previous studies, ionic interactions were 
mostly introduced in a rather auxiliary manner, where simple salts or crystalline matrices 
offered some degree of rigidification but lacked precise structural control  (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 
2025, 147, 11098; Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2025, 64, e202415042; Adv. Mater. 2025, 37, 2503074; Nat. Commun. 
2024, 15, 7149; Adv. Mater. 2024, 36, 2410739; etc.). In contrast, our system deliberately integrates alkyl 
chain engineering into the ionic host–guest framework. By carefully matching the alkyl chain length of host 
and guest, we created an ordered ionic microenvironment that enforces chromophore alignment, 
minimizes vibrational dissipation, and provides a unique level of spatial confinement not observed in earlier 
systems. Moreover, the novelty of our approach lies in the synergistic mechanism: the external heavy atom 
effect alone cannot account for the obse rved performance, but when combined with  the ionic-bonding 
network, the two processes mutually reinforce each other —heavy atom modulation accelerates 
intersystem crossing, while ionic confinement stabilizes the triplet excitons . This dual regulation is what 
enables both long lifetimes and broad spectral tunability. In fact, by modifying isolated ionic chromophores 
with quaternary ammonium groups, we successfully achieved multicolor phosphorescence spanning from 
blue to orange -red with lifetimes up to 572 ms. Compared with earlier ionic systems, which generally 
displayed single-color afterglow, our strategy provides a versatile platform with clear generalizability. 
We recognize that certain crystalline or polymeric host –guest systems can exhibit lifetimes beyond 
one second; however, such systems often prioritize lifetime extension at the cost of tunability and 
modularity. Additionally, phosphorescence lifetime is strongly related to the intrinsic properties 
of chromophores. For example, as shown in Figure S2, NapHBr in dilute solution (1 × 10 -5 M) at 77 K 
exhibited the phosphorescence peak at 548 nm  with a phosphorescence lifetime of 1.49 ms. The 
phosphorescence performance of the prepared doped material, DNBr,  is significantly enhanced by the 
construction of ionic-bonding networks. The phosphorescence lifetime can reach up to 48.50 ms, exhibiting 
a 32-fold enhancement compared with that of Nap HBr in MeOH at 77 K. A Chromophore with a short 
intrinsic lifetime (in dilute solution at 77 K) can achieve an extension of the phosphorescence lifetime via 
this strategy. These results indicate that our proposal plays a crucial role in enhancing phosphore scence. 
Our contribution, therefore, addresses a different but equally important challenge: to achieve structurally

Comment #5: The rationale for chain matching as a means of enhancing triplet lifetimes requires clear 
explanation. 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s comments. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, the rationale 
for chain matching lies in its ability to promote the formation of highly ordered ionic -bonding networks 
that rigidify the microenvironment and sustain long -lived triplet excitons. Specifically,  strong ionic 
interactions among the ionized hosts drive the assembly of a head -to-head cross-linked network, where 
bromine atoms in the alkyl chains act as ionic nodes to stabilize the structure. Within this network, guest 
molecules are further immobilized by C–H···Br (3.029 Å) and C–H···O (2.516 and 2.585 Å) interactions, 
which effectively suppress non-radiative motions of the chromophores. A critical factor is the near-perfect 
length matching of the flexible n-hexyl chains between host (8.859 Å) and gues t (8.825 Å) molecules 
(Supplementary Fig. S1c). This precise alignment enables seamless integration of chromophores into the 
host’s ionic framework, strengthening both ionic bonding and external heavy -atom interactions with Br ⁻ 
ions. When the alkyl chain lengths are mismatched, guests fail to embed within the host lattice, leading to 
weakened interactions and significantly reduced phosphorescence. By contrast, in the matched system 
(DNBr), chain complementarity ensures a well-ordered ionic microenvironment and efficient host–guest 
coupling, thereby delivering superior triplet lifetimes (Supplementary Fig. 41).

Comment # 10: In Supplementary Figure 58, the phosphorescence spectra of 4BrNapHBr in dilute 
solution exhibit multiple peaks, which should be clarified. 
Response: We gratefully acknowledge the reviewer’s comments. At 77 K, molecular motions , including 
vibrations and rotations, were effectively suppressed, enabling the observation of multiple emission peaks 
from the chromophores of 4BrNapHBr. These peaks arise from radiative transitions among different 
vibrational sublevels within the lowest triplet excited state. As shown in Figure S8, the radiative transition 
spectra of phosphorescent materials can be analyzed based on the Franck-Condon principle (J. Phys. Chem. 
1990, 94, 4420). Distinct vibrational progressions typically accompany the radiative decay of an organic 
phosphorescent material. Ideally, when the T 1 state exhibits a nearly identical structure to the S 0 state 
(molecular vibronic coupling is negligible), the resulting emission spectrum is very narrow, with only a 0-0 
vibration peak (Fig. S8, left). An increase in structural differences between the S 0 and T1 states can induce 
broadening of the emission spectrum (Fig. S8, middle), which is attributed to the involvement of vibrational 
progressions separated by characteristic vibrational frequencies . For vibrational modes that are strongly 
coupled to structural changes, the corresponding vibrational progressions may even lead to multiple 
emission peaks  (Fig. S8, right). Notably, owing to charge redistribution in the excited state, structural 
changes of the material are inevitable . Hence, the phosphorescence spectra of 4BrNapHBr in dilute 
solution exhibit multiple peaks.
