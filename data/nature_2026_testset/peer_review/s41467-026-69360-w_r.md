Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigated SARS-CoV-2 infection at different key timepoints during pregnancy in Syrian hamsters. 
The
authors showed that maternal infection leads to intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) due to placental inflammation without
any vertical transmission. Vaccination and anticoagulant therapies were found to protect the placenta and improve fetal
outcomes, highlighting their importance in maternal/pregnancy care. This study is thorough and well conducted and
addresses an important topic with significant implications for maternal and fetal health during SARS-CoV2 infection. The
article is clearly written, with a logical flow, thorough explanations of the results and adequate discussion with a section on
the limitation of the work. Overall, I believe this manuscript is well-suited for publication pending minor revisions. 
General comments: 
I understand the need for the authors to perform their analysis based on days post-infection (dpi). However, dpi will
obviously vary depending on when the infection occurred and the length of exposure will vary. Considering that the
placenta, unlike maternal organs, is still growing during pregnancy and its function evolves, could terminal c-section not
have been conducted at the same dpi for each timepoints of infection to address is the placental susceptibility/response to
infection? This might be beyond the scope of the work and could be addressed in the discussion. 
Minor comments: 
Introduction: Short, concise and well structured. The authors could add more details on human placental pathology studies
following SARS-CoV-2 infection considering that there are several published studies and their work has a fair amount of
histopathology analysis. This would offer context for interpreting the findings from the hamster placenta. 
Methods: The manuscript is well written and provides sufficient methodological detail to allow for experimental
reproducibility. Was fetal/placental sex considered as a variable? Given that females tend to be lighter than males, this could
be a relevant factor, particularly if there are discrepancies in survival rates among the pups. 
I understand that the vaccine used is YF-S0* but in Fig.5 and elsewhere in the text they write YF17D-vectored. Which might
not be adequate since YF-S0* is an updated construct of YF17D. 
Should the “Study Approval” section be placed before the “Animal” section at line 683? 
Results: The results are well structured, and the inclusion of the therapeutic intervention significantly strengthens the study. I
also appreciated the addition of the PCA analysis, which effectively highlights distinct molecular signatures and helps
explain the observed heterogeneity.

- Line 192-194, does the authors have any data supporting this statement: “suggestive of a compensatory adaptation in

Title: COVID-19-related inflammation of the placenta impedes fetal development in pregnant hamsters 
Summary/Key Results: The introduction sets the stage well, establishing that, while SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated
with increased disease severity in expectant mothers as well as fetal complications such as compromised placental
insufficiency/hypoxic injury, mechanisms of SARS-CoV-2 induced maternal and fetal pathology are poorly understood.
Additionally, vaccine hesitancy remains common among pregnant women. 
Here, authors explored the effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on pregnancy in hamsters, which is an appropriate animal
model for this study. Hamsters are naturally susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 infection, exhibiting similar pulmonary pathology to
human COVID-19 disease. Furthermore, hamsters and humans have some similarities in overall placental anatomical
structure- specifically hemochorial placentation. But, in contrast to the human hemomonochorial placental interface
(characterized by deep trophoblast invasion of the maternal uterus), the labyrinthine rodent placenta generally exhibits a
hemotrichorial interface with multiple trophoblast layers. Deeper trophoblast invasion in hamsters (vs. mice), coupled with
the fact that individual pups carried by one dam are multichorionic with separate placentas and umbilical cords, makes the
hamster well-suited for studying pregnancy-related pathologies. 
To assess the effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy, after timed mating and confirmed insemination,
anesthetized female Syrian hamsters were a) inoculated intranasally with SARS-CoV-2 (VOC Alpha; B.1.1.7) at various key
stages of pregnancy: insemination (E0.5), implantation (E4.5), placentation (E7.5), and a fully formed placenta (E10.5); or b)
non-inoculated pregnant dams were anesthetized at matched gestational time points as controls. Pups were surgically
delivered before the onset of labor (E15.5). Viral RNA and infectious viral particles in the lungs, placenta and fetal liver
(vRNA only) were quantified (via RT-qPCR and TCID50 assay, respectively) on the day of surgical delivery (E15.5), and
pulmonary transcript levels of cytokines Ip10, Mx2, Ifnγ, Il6, Tnfα, and Il1β (previously shown to be upregulated in the lungs
after SARS-CoV-2 infection) were assessed (via RT-qPCR). Tissues (maternal lung from all groups and placenta from the
E4.5 and E10.5 inoculation group) were evaluated and scored histologically. Next, the authors quantified transcript levels of
markers for immune cells, including NK cells (Klrg1, Cd94), CD4

Taken together, the authors conclude from the above that SARS-CoV-2 infection of pregnant dams frequently results in a
transient placental infection without direct transplacental/vertical transmission, but with risk of fetal IUGR, likely secondary to
pathological alterations in the placenta (placentitis), characterized by pronounced fibrin deposition and tissue remodeling of
the decidua, and persistent inflammation hallmarked by elevated Ip10 (Cxcl10), Il6, and Il10 expression in the placentas of
affected pups. Regardless of the health status of the dam (acute versus resolving pneumonia) and the timing of infection,
pregnancy outcomes following SARS-CoV-2 infection in hamsters are heterogeneous, likely worsened more by a sustained
innate immune response than by placental infection or vertical transmission. 
Since SARS-CoV-2 is known to cause systemic coagulopathy and placentitis in pregnant women, and significant placental
pathology, including fibrosis and thrombosis, was observed in this hamster model, the authors next evaluated whether low
molecular weight heparin (enoxaparin- an anticoagulant used to prevent thromboembolism in pregnancy) could mitigate
adverse pregnancy outcomes in pregnant SARS-CoV-2 infected hamsters, with focus on the E4.5 group (which exhibited
the most distinct adverse effects on fetal outcomes). Post-exposure 3μg/g enoxaparin was administered subcutaneously
once daily to dams between 3dpi (E7.5) and 7dpi (E14.5), followed by a similar experimental design/assays to those
described above. Finally, subsequent to evaluation/monitoring of seroconversion kinetics in pregnant hamsters challenged
with B.1.1.7, two cohorts of dams were vaccinated with YF-S0* (safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy previously established
by this group) in a prime-boost regimen prior to conception (along with naïve controls) and challenge at E4.5 or E10.5.
Seroconversion to consistently high levels of spike-specific IgG in these immunized animals prior to infection was confirmed
by flow cytometry. 
Overall, administration of low molecular weight heparin (enoxaparin) improved pregnancy outcomes and averted placental
malfunction in SARS-CoV-2 infected hamsters: 
• Enoxaparin treatment resulted in significantly increased fetal and placental weights (vs. 
untreated controls), without affecting litter size 
• Histological analysis of placentas from infected dams showed improved pathology scores following 
enoxaparin treatment, characterized by reduced JZ/labyrinth congestion, fibrosis, and necrosis (no 
measurable impact on the relative proportions of placental zones) 
• Enoxaparin treatment did not exert any antiviral effect (oral swab vRNA levels remained similar to 
controls) and did not enhance the overall health of the dams- they continued to lose weight 
throughout infection and gained significantly less cumulative weight (vs. non-infected dams)

REVIEWER COMMENTS 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript investigated SARS-CoV-2 infection at different key timepoints during 
pregnancy in Syrian hamsters. The authors showed that maternal infection leads to 
intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) due to placental inflammation without any vertical 
transmission. Vaccination and anticoagulant therapies were found to protect the placenta and 
improve fetal outcomes, highlighting their importance in maternal/pregnancy care. This study 
is thorough and well conducted and addresses an important topic with significant implications 
for maternal and fetal health during SARS-CoV2 infection. The article is clearly written, with a 
logical flow, thorough explanations of the results and adequate discussion with a section on 
the limitation of the work. Overall, I believe this manuscript is well-suited for publication 
pending minor revisions. 
General comments: 
I understand the need for the authors to perform their analysis based on days post-infection 
(dpi). However, dpi will obviously vary depending on when the infection occurred and the 
length of exposure will vary. Considering that the placenta, unlike maternal organs, is still 
growing during pregnancy and its function evolves, could terminal c-section not have been 
conducted at the same dpi for each timepoints of infection to address is the placental 
susceptibility/response to infection? This might be beyond the scope of the work and could be 
addressed in the discussion. 
R/ Indeed, this is an interesting point and can be considered a natural continuation of 
this work . However, placental morphology in hamsters has not yet been fully 
characterized. We are currently generating a temporal and spatial 3D map to define 
the anatomical and developmental features of the hamster placenta as we did before 
for mice ( De Clercq PNAS 2019, PMID: 31249139). Building on this, we plan to 
investigate placental development and its response to infection in follow -up studies. 
As noted, this falls beyond the scope of the present manuscript as now also addressed 
in the discussion (lines 591-599).

human placental pathology studies following SARS-CoV-2 infection, considering that there are 
several published studies and their work has a fair amount of histopathology analysis. This

Title: COVID-19-related inflammation of the placenta impedes fetal development in pregnant

Summary/Key Results: The introduction sets the stage well, establishing that, while SARS-
CoV-2 infection is associated with increased disease severity in expectant mothers as well as 
fetal complications such as compromised placental insufficiency/hypoxic injury, mechanisms 
of SARS-CoV-2 induced maternal and fetal pathology are poorly understood. Additionally, 
vaccine hesitancy remains common among pregnant women. 
Here, authors explored the effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on pregnancy in hamsters, which 
is an appropriate animal model for this study. Hamsters are naturally susceptible to SARS-
CoV-2 infection, exhibiting similar pulmonary pathology to human COVID-19 disease. 
Furthermore, hamsters and humans have some similarities in overall placental anatomical 
structure- specifically hemochorial placentation. But, in contrast to the human 
hemomonochorial placental interface (characterized by deep trophoblast invasion of the 
maternal uterus), the labyrinthine rodent placenta generally exhibits a hemotrichorial interface 
with multiple trophoblast layers. Deeper trophoblast invasion in hamsters (vs. mice), coupled 
with the fact that individual pups carried by one dam are multichorionic with separate placentas 
and umbilical cords, makes the hamster well-suited for studying pregnancy-related 
pathologies. 
To assess the effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy, after timed mating and 
confirmed insemination, anesthetized female Syrian hamsters were a) inoculated intranasally 
with SARS-CoV-2 (VOC Alpha; B.1.1.7) at various key stages of pregnancy: insemination 
(E0.5), implantation (E4.5), placentation (E7.5), and a fully formed placenta (E10.5); or b) non-
inoculated pregnant dams were anesthetized at matched gestational time points as controls. 
Pups were surgically delivered before the onset of labor (E15.5). Viral RNA and infectious viral 
particles in the lungs, placenta and fetal liver (vRNA only) were quantified (via RT-qPCR and 
TCID50 assay, respectively) on the day of surgical delivery (E15.5), and pulmonary transcript 
levels of cytokines Ip10, Mx2, Ifnγ, Il6, Tnfα, and Il1β (previously shown to be upregulated in 
the lungs after SARS-CoV-2 infection) were assessed (via RT-qPCR). Tissues (maternal lung 
from all groups and placenta from the E4.5 and E10.5 inoculation group) were evaluated and 
scored histologically. Next, the authors quantified transcript levels of markers for immune cells, 
including NK cells (Klrg1, Cd94), CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, genes associated with type I, II, and 
III IFN responses (Mx2, Ip10, and IL28, respectively), and cytokines IL6 and IL10 (via RT-
aPCR) in order to investigate whether these correlated with the presence of placental vRNA. 
Since individual mother-placenta-offspring triads revealed heterogeneity, the authors also 
utilized Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to see whether a specific molecular signature 
correlated with the heterogeneity observed in placental infection and IUGR.
