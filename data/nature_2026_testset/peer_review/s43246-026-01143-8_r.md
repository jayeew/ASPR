Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript entitled “Controlled Oxygen Vacancy Electrode Reservoir for Robust WO3-based Memory Devices”,
submitted by the authors, focuses on strategies for enhancing the performance of memristor-based neuromorphic devices. In
particular, it systematically investigates how the concentration of oxygen vacancies in ITO affects the device characteristics
of memristors with an ITO/WOx/TiN structure. The approach, which the authors term COVER, assigns two functions to the
ITO top electrode: serving both as a reservoir of oxygen ions and as a current collector. This is achieved by controlling the
transport number of ITO, which essentially behaves as a mixed conductor of oxide ions and electrons. In doing so, they
succeed in reducing the operating voltage and improving endurance, thereby distinguishing their method from conventional
memristor designs that employ stacked structures of oxygen-reservoir layers and resistive-switching layers. Thus, the
significance of this study lies in the fact that its findings are not limited to the specific ITO/WOx/TiN system but are potentially
extendable to a wide range of ion-driven systems. 
The mechanism underlying the improved electrical characteristics achieved by COVER is explained through thorough
analyses (based on KPFM, UPS, STEM-EDS, XPS, RBS, Operand Raman and PL). From my personal perspective, I am
particularly interested in how the oxygen partial pressure during ITO deposition influences various neuromorphic properties
such as LTP/LTD, PPF, and STDP. Nevertheless, from the standpoint that “at the very least, reliable endurance is essential”
in neuromorphic hardware, the strategy of focusing on the 5% oxygen partial pressure—which exhibited the most stable
SET/RESET endurance—appears to be a valid one. 
From these perspectives, the manuscript meets the high standards required by “Communications Materials”, and I am
inclined to recommend it for publication, provided that the authors address the following minor comments. 
Comment 1 
Figures 2a–c present conductance modulation related to long-term memory (i.e., LTP/LTD characteristics), while Figure 4e
shows short-term memory behavior via PPF. Why is this device capable of exhibiting seemingly opposite long-term and
short-term memory under the same writing voltage (2 V)? A brief explanation would be helpful to avoid confusing the reader. 
Comment 2 
The PPF characteristics shown in Figure 2e exhibit two distinct relaxation times. An explanation of the physical mechanisms
corresponding to each would be desirable. 
Comment 3 
The operando Raman and PL analyses presented in Figures 4e–h during SET/RESET cycling indicate the fundamental
endurance limits of this approach (as the authors attribute to limitations in oxygen recyclability). Is there any possibility of
overcoming such limitations? 
Comment 4 
The value of this work lies largely in the fact that the proposed method is, in principle, broadly applicable beyond the specific
ITO/WOx/TiN system. Could this strategy also be extended to three-terminal memristive devices such as electrochemical
transistors?(e.g. Neuromorph. Comput. Eng. 3, 034008 (2023).) If so, mentioning this potential would further enhance the
impact of the manuscript. 
Comment 5 
The short-term memory behavior demonstrated here, such as the PPF characteristics, appears highly promising for use in
reservoir computing (e.g. Adv. Electron. Mater. 10, 2400625 (2024).), a form of neuromorphic computation that has attracted
considerable attention in recent years. Because reservoir computing corresponds directly to the “nonlinear and parallel data
processing” and “small-data scenarios in edge computing” mentioned by the authors in the introduction, it would be valuable
to briefly note its potential applicability as one example of future directions for this system.

Review Report to COMMSMAT-25-0793-T 
The manuscript entitled “Controlled Oxygen Vacancy Electrode Reservoir for Robust WO3-based Memory Devices” by Yuan
et al. provides a comprehensive discussion of the structural, electrical, and synaptic behaviors of WO3-based RRAM
devices employing ITO as the top electrode. The authors demonstrate the feasibility of resistive switching with stable
endurance as well as neuromorphic functionalities, such as analog plasticity and spike-timing-dependent plasticity. 
However, in its present form, the manuscript suffers from a lack of sufficient novelty within the RRAM research field. While
the data are solid and the experiments are carefully performed, the central concept of oxygen vacancy modulation in oxide-
based memristors has already been extensively reported. The contribution of the present work therefore appears
incremental rather than fundamentally innovative. 
My comments are listed below: 
1. The use of ITO as the top electrode raises concerns regarding CMOS compatibility. While ITO is widely recognized as a
transparent conductive oxide (TCO), its integration into standard CMOS back-end processes remains highly challenging.
The manuscript seems to overstate the feasibility of direct CMOS integration with ITO, and the authors should provide a
more realistic discussion of its limitations and possible alternatives. 
2. Regarding device characterization, the devices presented are not patterned, which limits their relevance for practical
RRAM applications. Without proper device patterning, it is difficult to unambiguously distinguish the resistive switching
behavior originating from the active switching layer versus contributions from the common electrode. Furthermore, the
reported operating current on the order of ~mA is excessively high for real-world memory applications, where low-power
operation is critical. The authors should clarify these limitations and discuss possible strategies to reduce current levels. 
3. The device performance is attributed to filamentary-type conduction; however, the manuscript does not sufficiently
address the critical role of the ITO/WO
₃
 interface in determining the resistive switching behavior. Since the interfacial
energetics and oxygen exchange at this boundary strongly influence filament formation and stability, a more detailed
analysis or discussion of interfacial effects is necessary to support the proposed mechanism. In addition, the synaptic
behaviors shown (PPF, PPD, STDP) are important demonstrations, yet the performance is comparable to existing reports.
The authors should discuss how their device performance (endurance, state linearity, retention) compares quantitatively with
state-of-the-art synaptic memristors. 
4. Although the manuscript provides detailed information on device fabrication and electrical measurements, the novelty of
the work remains questionable. In the current state of the field, RRAM devices are often integrated with transistors in a 1T1R
configuration for embedded memory applications. It is therefore unclear why the authors chose this particular material
combination (ITO/WO
₃
/TiN) and simple two-terminal device structure, and how it advances beyond existing approaches. A
clearer justification of material selection and device design strategy is needed to establish the significance of this work.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
I sincerely appreciate the detailed effort of the authors and acknowledge the work they have presented. However, after
carefully reviewing the manuscript and considering the current state of the art in this field, I regret to say that the study does
not demonstrate sufficient novelty to merit publication in Communication Materials. 
The authors argue: 
“Scientific novelty in materials science often arises not from discovering an entirely new phenomenon, but from achieving
deterministic control over a known mechanism to overcome longstanding performance bottlenecks. In this regard, we
respectfully emphasize that our work offers a distinct and significant advancement over prior ITO-based memristor studies.” 
While this principle is valid, the present work does not convincingly establish novelty even in terms of deterministic control.
Comparable approaches have already been reported, for example: 
1. ACS Appl. Mater. Interfaces (2018, 10, 25, 21445–21450) 
2. Nanoscale (2020, 12, 9024–9031) 
Merely altering the oxygen vacancy content of the electrode layer, in my view, does not constitute a sufficiently novel
contribution—either mechanistically or in terms of device performance. 
The study could have been more compelling had it focused on the nature of the conductive filament. While the indirect
evidence provided is acceptable, it does not advance novelty. Operando microscopy (though admittedly beyond the scope
here) would have offered deeper insights. Furthermore, without theoretical validation, the proposed reversible oxygen
exchange between the ITO and WOx layers remains speculative. It is possible, but at present, it is not substantiated.

Reviewer # 2 The manuscript titled ‘Controlled Oxygen Vacancy Electrode Reservoir for Robust WO3-based Memory Devices’ discusses the use of non-stoichiometric ITO as a functional electrode, where apart from its use as an electrode, the oxygen vacancies present in it act as a prime source for the formation of oxygen vacancies and conductive filament in the oxide layer (WO3). Depending upon the concentration of oxygen vacancies, various parameters (forming voltage, endurance) have shown variation. The work presents a unique approach of achieving switching in WO3 via modulation of electrode stoichiometry; however, several key issues needs further justification: 1. Utilisation of an electrode with an oxygen reservoir to control the resistive switching (RS) properties of metal oxide thin films is not an entirely new concept. In fact utilisation of ITO as an oxygen reservoir has been reported earlier (e.g. Appl. Phys. Lett. 111, 041601 (2017) https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4995411 ; IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON ELECTRON DEVICES, VOL. 66, NO. 3, MARCH 2019; https://doi.org/10.1109/TED.2019.2895079 )In this work, the authors have extended similar concept by changing the stoichiometry of the ITO layer which allows certain control over the RS properties. This does not make the present work highly novel. Response: We thank the reviewer for highlighting these prior works. It is true that some previous studies have employed ITO electrodes as oxygen reservoirs in memristive devices. We have now cited these works, and we acknowledge that they laid important groundwork. However, our approach differs in several key aspects, which we believe constitute a significant advancement over those. Firstly, the prior works did not systematically tune the ITO’s oxygen vacancy concentration. They used ITO in a fixed condition, without exploring how varying its non-stoichiometry would impact device behavior. In our work, we deliberately deposit ITO under a range of oxygen partial pressures (0%–40%) and thereby achieve quantitative control over its defect chemistry. This allows us to draw direct correlations between the ITO’s stoichiometry and memristor performance, which was not addressed previously. By contrast, in the first study, for example, the ITO composition was kept constant. Forming-free switching was reported but relatively poor endurance (on the order of 10 cycles)4 was found. We suspect that the limited endurance was due to an unoptimized ITO stoichiometry and degradation of the top contact in that device. In our devices, by optimizing the ITO oxygen content, we achieve >104 cycles, which is a substantial improvement in performance. Secondly, our device demonstrates a broad spectrum of neuromorphic functions (multi-level analog switching, long-term potentiation/depression over hundreds of cycles, short-term facilitation/depression, STDP, etc.), whereas the earlier reservoir-type devices did not explore or achieve these synaptic functionalities. For example, the second paper used ITO as one electrode but did not demonstrate any analog or short-term plasticity effects. The focus was on digital memory aspects, and the device required an additional interface layer rather than using the electrode itself as a tunable reservoir5. In contrast, we show that by carefully tuning the electrode’s defect chemistry alone, one can obtain device behavior that spans both non-volatile memory and volatile synaptic response, all in a simple stack. This level of neuromorphic performance (e.g., high endurance analog weight updates and biologically relevant short-term dynamics) was not present in those earlier studies.

The previous works did not provide such analysis linking the reservoir electrode’s chemistry to the device physics. The authors found evidence of indium-rich filaments forming, effectively turning the SiOx into an electronic conductor with indium participating. We considered whether something similar could occur in our ITO/WO3/TiN devices, but there are several reasons why an indium filament is highly unlikely here. WO3 is an oxide that can easily support oxygen vacancies7, so forming a conductive path via oxygen vacancy aggregation is much easier energetically than driving a large metal cation (In3+) through the lattice. WO3 readily accommodates sub-stoichiometry, whereas SiO2 is a network covalent insulator with no native oxygen vacancies8. If the SiOx device is forced to switch, metal filament formation can be one of the only options. In WO3, an oxygen vacancy filament is the natural outcome under bias, and our electrical characteristics (non-volatile bipolar switching with continuous analog modulation) are typical of oxygen-vacancy-driven filamentary rather than metallic conductive bridging behavior. Also, the device geometry and field requirements make indium migration impractical in our case. Our WO3 layer is ~150 nm thick, five times thicker than the SiOx in the referenced device. Pushing indium ions through such a thickness would be exceedingly difficult. Moreover, in our stack, only the top electrode contains indium; the bottom electrode is TiN (which contains no indium). Even if some indium were to diffuse out of the ITO top electrode, it would not find a continuous supply or pathway from the bottom side to complete a metallic filament through the entire film. This asymmetry contrasts with the symmetric ITO/SiO2/ITO structure, where indium could potentially diffuse from both electrodes toward the center. The In elemental map showed no detectable indium penetration into the WO3 layer beyond the immediate ITO/WO3 interface. There was no indication of any indium-rich filamentary region extending into the WO3; the indium signal remained confined to the ITO electrode region, exactly as in a fresh device. This observation supports our claim that the filament is composed of oxygen vacancies in WO3, not metallic indium. From a theoretical standpoint, one can note that oxygen ions (O2-) in oxides are relatively mobile under electric field at room temperature (especially in an oxide like WO3 that has multiple oxidation states), whereas indium cations are much larger and less mobile. The activation energy for oxygen vacancy migration in WO3 is ~1 eV9, whereas indium (larger cation) diffusion in an oxide lattice may require breaking and reforming bonds in a way that likely has a substantially higher barrier. In contrast, the KPFM results show an opposite trend. Please explain. Response: This is an important point, and we appreciate the reviewer for drawing attention to it. At first glance, there does appear to be a discrepancy: ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) shows that increasing the oxygen content in ITO (making it more insulating) raises the ITO work function, whereas our Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements show that an ITO electrode deposited with higher oxygen content yields a larger contact potential difference (CPD) relative to WO3, which initially seems counter-intuitive if one thinks in terms of work function alone. We have carefully analyzed this and realized that the key is to recognize that UPS and KPFM probe different aspects of the system under different conditions. We have expanded our discussion in the manuscript to clarify this. Using UPS, we measured the absolute work function of ITO films in vacuum. UPS reveals that oxygen-rich ITO (i.e., deposited with higher O2%) has a higher work function than oxygen-poor ITO. This is expected because adding oxygen reduces the carrier (electron) concentration in ITO (fewer oxygen vacancies means fewer donors), which shifts the Fermi level lower and thus increases the work function10. KPFM, on the other hand, measures the CPD between a conductive AFM tip (here Pt-coated) and the sample’s local surface region (Figure R4a). If one were measuring two metals in ideal conditions, the CPD directly reflects the work function difference. However, in our case, the tip is scanning over a multilayer device surface (ITO/WO3 or WO3 regions). An important factor, band bending, should be considered. Because of this, we did not express the results by absolute KPFM potential in the original manuscript, but the potential difference between the WO3 and the ITO/WO3 junction.  We revised the band diagram (old Figure S10 in the original manuscript) and included here as Figure R4b, and explicitly sketched Figure R4c for clearer comparison. ITO is a degenerate semiconductor where the Fermi level falls within the conduction band. When ITO and WO3 form a junction, their Fermi levels equilibrate, causing band bending in the WO3 near the interface. Compared to oxygen-poor ITO, oxygen-rich ITO has a higher work function (measured by UPS), it will tend to pull oxygen vacancies (or equivalently, electrons) from WO3, creating a more upward band bending (depletion) in the WO3 near the interface13. This effectively increases the potential of the WO3 surface relative to the ITO11. In other word, KPFM is effectively measuring the potential barrier (i.e., Schottky barrier) in this ITO/WO3 interface12. When the KPFM tip scans from the WO3 region to the ITO/WO3 region, it senses that the WO3 surface is at a higher potential (due to the depletion region) relative to the ITO. Thus, the CPD is bigger, indicating a larger Schottky barrier,	𝜙$%, between the ITO surface and the WO3 surface. Then, 𝑉%& is the built-in bias resulted by the difference in work function of the two layers. With more oxygen in ITO, UPS showed a higher work function, and the Fermi level is lower, so 𝑉%& is smaller (as the WO3 work function is unchanged).

Effectively, the increase of oxygen content in ITO has two effects: (i) it reduces the built-in potential due to intrinsic work function increase; and (ii) it increases the barrier height due to larger band bending. Oxygen-rich ITO and the corresponding depleted WO3 surface might adsorb more polar species or have different surface states, potentially increasing the CPD difference. For endurance, as we found by Raman/PL that a minor irreversible accumulation of oxygen vacancies existed, the endurance (number of cycles to failure) can be thought of in a simplified way as how many oxygen vacancies can be reversibly shuffled before the reservoir is depleted. A higher concentration of oxygen vacancies in the ITO (oxygen-poor ITO) means the ITO can accept more oxygen ions from WO3 without “filling up”. This delays the point at which the exchange becomes irreversible. In a rough analogy, one could imagine endurance ≈ (oxygen vacancy capacity of reservoir) / (irreversible oxygen vacancies per cycle). Oxygen-poor ITO starts with a large capacity of vacancies, so each cycle’s worth of oxygen exchange is a small fraction of the total, yielding many cycles before failure. Oxygen-rich ITO has fewer vacancies initially, so after fewer cycles the ITO cannot take more oxygen, leading to earlier failure. This is exactly what we observed: the most oxygen-poor ITO (5% or 10% O2 sputtered) gave the best endurance, while oxygen-rich ITO (30% or 40% O2) had shorter endurance. Additionally, if we increase the ITO thickness, we also increase the absolute reservoir of oxygen vacancies (more volume of ITO, hence more sites for oxygen). So a thicker ITO should similarly improve endurance, which we already noted as one mitigation strategy in response to comment 3. After we SET the device into LRS and then removing bias, the filament is metastable and could gradually oxidize, which would increase its resistance over time (LRS decay). If the ITO electrode is oxygen-rich, it has oxygen readily available at the interface that could diffuse into WO3 and recombine with vacancies in the filament, causing the filament to partially heal over time. This would degrade LRS retention (the device’s resistance would drop over time towards HRS). On the other hand, if the ITO is oxygen-poor, the filament will actually tend to hold onto oxygen vacancies. Therefore, we expect better LRS retention with oxygen-poor ITO (filament remains intact longer), and poorer LRS retention with oxygen-rich ITO (filament more quickly “heals” when unbiased). In contrast, an oxygen-rich ITO would tend to not absorb any oxygen from WO3, which would lead to a more stable broken-filament. In summary, all evidence indicates that our devices operate via an oxygen-vacancy-driven filament in the WO3 layer, and the ITO electrode’s stoichiometry primarily influences the formation/dissolution dynamics of this filament. We agree that directly visualizing an oxygen vacancy filament or performing a full theoretical simulation would provide additional insight; as noted, these avenues (e.g., operando imaging) would constitute a substantial investigation of their own and are suggested as future work.
