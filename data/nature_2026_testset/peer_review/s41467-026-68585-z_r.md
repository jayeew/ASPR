Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript titled "Disruption of Iron Homeostasis Sensitizes Pancreatic Cancer to Irreversible Electroporation" offers
valuable insights into pancreatic cancer therapy using a triple-combination strategy (IRE, MOF-Fe, and M-C20U4V). In
particular, the authors synthesized C20U4V, a novel PROTAC, to target FTH1. Overall, the work is well-executed, but a few
additions and clarifications are necessary prior to publication. The authors are requested to carefully address the following
comments to improve the manuscript. 
MAJOR CONCERNS 
1. In Figure 2, the experiments do not clearly demonstrate that MOF-Fe induces ferroptosis. 
1-1) In Figure 2D, the correlation (R-value) between FTH1 expression and the AUCs of three chemicals (Erastin, 1S,3RSL-
3, and ML162) ranges between 0.114 and 0.152. Are these values truly meaningful? Please discuss their significance. 
1-2) In Figure 2E, the authors show data from two cell lines transfected with an FTH1 vector. What is the difference between
#1 and #2? The distinction is unclear and appears illogical. 
1-3) In Figures 2F and 2H, FTH1 was more effectively suppressed in FTH1KD#2 than in FTH1KD#3. Please use the KD#2
cell line for consistency. Additionally, even without treatment, the FTH1KD#3 group exhibited elevated ROS levels. This
finding is unclear—please explain why this cell line was used. 
1-4) To strengthen the biological significance of FTH1 in Figure 2, the reviewer suggests performing rescue experiments.
The authors should restore FTH1 expression in the KD cell lines using the FTH1 vector, then treat these cells with MOF-Fe. 
2. In Figure 4, the novel PROTAC C20U4V requires further characterization. 
2-1) Please confirm the localization or cellular uptake of C20U4V in pancreatic cancer cell lines. 
2-2) To clarify the role of C20U4V in targeting FTH1, the authors should assess FTH1 protein levels following C20U4V
treatment in FTH1-overexpressing (FTH1OE) cell lines. 
3. In contrast to the enhanced tumor retention of MOF-Fe observed in Figure 2M, M-C20U4V did not show improved
retention following IRE treatment in Figure 7H. Please discuss this discrepancy. 
4. In Figure 8, the relationship between the triple-combination treatment and anti-tumor immune responses is unclear.
Please specify the main immunological factors or immune cell types involved. Additionally, IFN-γ expression should be
analyzed in the other treatment groups in Figure 8J. 
MINOR CONCERNS 
1. The authors should verify that all figure labels correspond accurately with the manuscript text, including those in the
supplementary figures. 
2. In Supplementary Figures 2c–e, please explain the rationale and biological relevance of using DFO. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author)

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

In this study the authors develop a strategy to degrade FTH1 in pancreatic cancer cells via an arachidonic-acid-derived
PROTAC. They argue that this sensitizes the cells to ferroptosis, allowing the eradication of persister cells after irreversible
electroporation treatment. This study is timely, interesting and well-performed. This reviewer is favourable that this
manuscript should be considered for publication, but recommends that the authors consider the following points to
strengthen their findings. 
Major points : 
(1) The authors talk about ferroptosis without citing the key original references, and this oversight should be corrected (Dixon
et al. Cell. 2012 May 25;149(5):1060-72, Cell Metab. 2008 Sep;8(3):237-48). 
(2) Some Western blots look odd and the authors should check the raw data if everything is correctly labelled, and if loading
controls are on the same gels or not, i.e. sample loading controls or sample processing controls. For instance Fig. 3i: why is
the last band of FTH1 pointing down, whereas the actin points up? Was the band inversed? Another example Fig 5i: The
band widths of NRF2 and actin are not the same and look like from different gels. Figure 7e: why are FTH1 bands pointing
down, whereas actin is pointing up (very visible in the last band on the right). This reviewer did not check all the blots, but
recommends to check throughout to avoid any mistakes that can happen during Figure preparation. 
(3) A recent paper showed that ferroptosis can be induced by targeting and activating lysosomal iron in PDAC and other
cancers and specifically kill iron-rich cells (Nature. 2025 May 7. doi: 10.1038/s41586-025-08974-4). This work is directly
relevant to this manuscript and the authors should consider putting their work and this work in perspective in the introduction
or discussion. This work also describes ferroptosis initiation in lysosomes, where the chemical environment and iron
abundance favour the Fenton reaction. This should be discussed in the introductory paragraph between references 14 and
16. 
(4) Two key papers showed that iron overload on persister cancer cells characterizes these cells, and can be exploited
therapeutically. Even though these studies were on breast cancer and ovarian cancer, they are directly relevant for this work
here (Oncogene. 2017 Jul 20;36(29):4089-4099, Nat Chem. 2017 Oct;9(10):1025-1033) 
(5) It is true that ferritin can act as an iron storage protein, but only in its oligomeric 24-mer form. Ferritin also has other
functions and properties, and this might be interesting to discuss for the authors in light of their work (for a comprehensive
review see: Metallomics. 2021 May 12;13(5):mfab021). 
(6) Lines 103-108: The authors describe experiments where they used sublethal doses of PFE and then talk about
ferroptosis inhibitors not rescuing cell viability. The design of these experiments makes no sense by definition, as lethality
describes death. So it is not a sublethal dose of PFE? Also, unless the authors identify the specific cell death mechanism,
they cannot claim that it is not ferroptosis by using one condition and concentration of Ferrostatin or Liproxstatin. They
should establish concentration curves, with several time points and concentrations, and also use tocopherol, a strong anti-
oxidant, that is the best ferroptosis inhibitor known to-date. This part of the study needs some serious revisiting (I suggest to
look at the experimental details of the Conrad, Friedman-Angeli and Rodriguez labs, to get inspired how best design these
experiments). 
(7) BODIPY-C11 does not measure lipid oxidation, but it is a surrogate or proxy to infer lipid oxidation. To show lipid
oxidation, the authors should perform lipidomics. 
(8) The authors present small heat maps of cherry-picked genes in their RNA-seq data. Whereas this reviewer understands
that presenting omics data is challenging, this is a highly biased approach. Bubble plots of GO pathways could be a good
way to start with a non-biased analysis. For instance, do the authors identify GO for ferroptosis in their PEF or MOF-Fe
experiments? (Please look at these papers for an idea of how to present RNA-seq data: Nature. 2023 May;617(7960):386-
394, Nature. 2025 May 7. doi: 10.1038/s41586-025-08974-4). 
(9) Line 217-218. Mitochondrial alteration is also indicative of other cell death modalities like apoptosis (review: Nat Rev Mol
Cell Biol. 2020 Feb;21(2):85-100). Even ferrotposis inducers can induce non-ferroptotic non-apoptotic cell death in some cell
types (Cancer Discov. 2022 Mar 1;12(3):774-791). Maybe the authors could be more careful with these semantics? 
(10) Since the authors claim that FTH1 downregulation sensitizes the cells to ferrptosis, they should test if these cells are
mores sensible to ferroptosis inducers like erastin/ RSL3 etc. 
Minor points: 
(1) Flow cytometry data histograms, the y-axes are not labelled throughout. This is normalized to the mode? Please indicate
scale and annotate the axes. 
(2) The authors write peroxidation throughout, whereas iron and hydrogen peroxide can generate hydroxyl and hydroperoxyl
radicals, that can lead to peroxidation and oxidation. It is better to talk about lipid oxidation rather than lipid peroxidation,
even if this semantic mistake is used aplenty in the literature. 
(3) Line 114. Significantly instead of significant. 
(4) Line 173/174: very interesting result. This goes hand in hand with the finding that tumors are higher in iron content
compared to healthy tissue from human biopsies (Nature. 2025 May 7. doi: 10.1038/s41586-025-08974-4).

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a highly innovative study aiming to improve the efficacy of pulse electrical fields (PEF) by inducing ferroptosis. 
The
first layer of innovation is a fast-release iron nanoparticle (MOF) only modestly delayed growth of subcutaneous KPC-
derived tumors. 
The data that resistance to this iron therapy was driven by increased expression of ferritin are compelling,
including genetic knockout studies FTH1. 
The next and even greater innovation was the development of a PROTAC
targeting fatty acids, based on rationale that FTH1 is known to bind multiple fatty acids. 
The winner (C20U4V) was based on
arachidonic acid (AA) and chosen on the basis of growth inhibition in vitro. 
It is confusing that C20U4V increases FTH on its
own in proteomic studies in vitro. 
It is only when given in combination with MOF that C20U4V decreases FTH relative to
FTH. The combo treatment increases FTH in multiple experiments compared to control or IRE alone. 
A potential explanation
is offered that this may be due to effects of AA, but it seems inappropriate to refer to this as an “FTH1-targeting” PROTAC,
when it clearly affects multiple proteins. 
The efficacy of the PROTAC for degrading FTH is not extremely high (Fig 5i)—1.3-
fold by proteomics in vitro and FTH1 was not significantly decreased (p=0.07) in treated tumors in vivo (relative to combo of
PEF + MOF without PROTAC). 
Despite this, there is impressive growth delay with the triple combination in subcutaneous
tumor model and survival improvement in an orthotopic model. 
My other significant criticism is the use of sublethal PEF. 
It is understandable why this is necessary for in vitro studies,
because it is too easy to kill cell in vitro, and you won’t have any cells to study if you use lethal PEF. 
However, the conditions
for intentionally sublethal PEF are very different than what are used clinically for IRE (or what have been used in mouse
models of IRE). 
How do the effects of IRE on ferroptosis-associated proteins compare to sublethal PEF? 
Would there still be
synergy? 
If perhaps this is not feasible to do for manuscript, this limitation needs to be acknowledged. 
A sign that the
conditions used are perhaps insufficient is that “IRE” in this study (which should perhaps be referred to as either PEF or
incomplete IRE or “reversible electroporation”) did not increase uptake of M-C20U4V in tumors. 
Finally, the reasons for “resistance” to IRE are many and complex, many are likely technical or anatomical related to
incomplete physical ablation. 
While mutations in apoptosis genes might contribute, this is an unnecessary over-
simplification of the issue. 
It is certainly true that local recurrence rates are higher than we would like, but the statement that
“the rate of local recurrence is higher than other ablative therapies” is not supported by data. 
Two studies were referenced
that had amongst the highest recurrence rates in the literature but are not representative of the field, particularly open
surgical IRE, and did not compare IRE to other forms of ablation, which have equally high, if not higher rates of local
recurrence for pancreatic cancer. 
This statement needs to be amended/softened. 
Minor things is that there are too many non-standard, undefined abbreviations in graphic abstract. 
Reviewer #6 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Review 
This manuscript by Linrong Li, Shihao Su et al. presents an novel study investigating a strategy to enhance the efficacy of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

irreversible electroporation (IRE) in primarily pancreatic cancer by sensitizing residual tumour cells to ferroptosis. During
IRE, residual viable cancer cells can remain a significant cause of tumour recurrence. The authors demonstrate that iron-
based metal-organic framework nanoparticles (MOF-Fe) can suppress recurrence by inducing iron overload and
subsequently ferroptosis; however, this effect is attenuated by IRE-induced upregulation of ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1). To
overcome this limitation, the authors employ an arachidonic acid-derived PROTAC, C20U4V, to selectively degrade FTH1,
thereby amplifying ferroptotic activity in both pancreatic cancer cell lines and organoids. Encapsulation of C20U4V within a
ROS-responsive micelles (called M-C20U4V) enables in vivo delivery, and in combination with MOF-Fe, they show
enhanced ferroptosis and therapeutic efficacy in murine models. This study is conceptually novel, supported by
experimental data, and of clear translational relevance. While a few minor points requiring clarification, the manuscript is of
sound quality and in my opinion would be suited publication in Nature Communications. 
Comments 
Sublethal PEF Induces Anti-Ferroptotic Gene Expression 
As shown pulsed electric fields (PEF) upregulate anti-ferroptotic genes in pancreatic cancer cells. However, in Figure 1B,
immunohistochemical staining of 4-Hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) in KPC tumours appears non-significant. It may be more
appropriate to include this panel as a supplementary figure, especially since the subsequent panels (PANC-1 data) clearly
demonstrate significant effects. 
Figure Labeling and Misreferencing in the Text: 
There are multiple inconsistencies between figure panels and their citations in the text: 
Figure 1C is incorrectly labelled as Figure 1B. 
Figures 1I–K are referred to in the text as Figures 1E–G. 
Figure 1D in the text corresponds to Figure 1H. 
Figure 1E in the text is Figure 1I. 
Figure 1H in the text refers to Figure 1L. 
Figures 1J and 1K are not referenced in the text at all. They appear to be extensions of earlier panels and may benefit from
reordering to align with their corresponding experiments and improve overall clarity. 
Anti-Ferroptotic Gene Upregulation and Ferroptosis Resistance 
The demonstration that PANC-1 cells upregulate anti-ferroptosis/antioxidative stress genes post-PEF is convincing. The
data showing that Ferrostatin-1 (Ferr-1) and Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1) do not restore viability suggests that PEF does not
sensitize cells to ferroptosis. However, a useful addition could be to investigate whether PEF-treated cells gain resistance to
ferroptosis. For example, treating both PEF and non-PEF cells with erastin and comparing their sensitivities could yield
informative insights about sensitisation/desensitisation of model systems to ferroptosis post electroporation. 
Arachidonic Acid-Derived PROTACs Targeting FTH1 
C20U4V is the chosen molecule for FTH1 degradation. However, C20U5V also appears to have very similar activity. What
was the rationale for selecting C20U4V over C20U5V? Furthermore, why was C20U5V excluded from the CCK-8 assay? I
imagine that the EPA will have a different interactome to AA and therefore it would be interesting to perform some of the later
experiments with MOF-FE and PEF to determine what difference are present between compounds. 
Although the degradation is shown to be VHL-dependent, arachidonic acid (AA) is not likely to be inherently selective for
FTH1. It would therefore strengthen the conclusion to demonstrate that FTH1 degradation is a result of specific binding of
VHL and FTH1 rather than a by-product of a loss of stability of FTH1 through non specific targeting of C20U4V. A suggested
approach is a competition assay, where FTH1 is pre-saturated with (S,S,S)-C20U4V prior to treatment with C20U4V to
confirm target specificity. 
A pull-down assay combined with Mass spectrometry could establish to us that C20U4V is binding to FTH1 and also could
help define the interactome of the arachidonic acid and further support target engagement of C20U4V. 
A DC
₅₀
 titration assay for C20U4V would also be beneficial to quantify compound potency and the optimal therapeutic
window of C20U4V. 
Micelle-Loaded C20U4V (M-C20U4V) 
In Figure 5, degradation kinetics for C20U4V are measured at 12 hours, whereas M-C20U4V is measured at 48 hours. For
proper comparison, these should be performed at the same timepoint. In the supplementary file they do perform a 48 Hour
titration of C20U4V and show that it is no longer capable of degradation, is this due to stimulation of FTH1 expression of
FTH1 or that they’re compound isn’t potent? Could the authors clarify this 
Are they authors able to treat at even higher concentrations given the low level of activity or are they constrained by solubility
of the molecule? 
Dual Treatment with MOF-Fe and M-C20U4V in vivo 
There are some referencing issues in this section: 
Figure 8 is mistakenly cited as Figure 6 in the text. 
Figure 8K should say M-C20U4V, not C20U4V. 
Conclusion 
This study presents a promising strategy for enhancing ferroptotic cancer therapy following IRE via targeted degradation of
FTH1 using a PUFA-based PROTAC. The results are compelling, but improving figure organization, correcting some
references, and clarifying compound selection and specificity would be advisable.

This manuscript by Linrong Li, Shihao Su et al. presents an interesting and novel study investigating a strategy to enhance
the efficacy of irreversible electroporation (IRE) in pancreatic cancer through sensitisation of residual tumour cells to
ferroptosis using a PROTAC-based approach. During IRE, residual viable cancer cells can remain, representing a
significant cause of tumour recurrence. The authors demonstrate that iron-based metal–organic framework nanoparticles
(MOF-Fe) can suppress recurrence by inducing iron overload and subsequently ferroptosis; however, this effect is
attenuated by IRE-induced upregulation of ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1). To overcome this limitation, the authors employ an

evaluation of this paper, I am satisfied with the corrections and additional experiments performed in response to my previous

₅₀

The inclusion of the competition assay clearly demonstrates that C20U4V degradation activity depends on the binding of the

intravenously through tail vein. IRE ablated a substantial portion of tumor cells and reduced tumor 
interstitial pressure, which may have facilitated MOF-Fe retention within the tumor . Tumor uptake of M -
C20U4V, on the other han d, is affected by the micelles’  pharmacodynamic properties as well as the 
function of intratumoral vasculature. 
 IRE and low-intensity reversible electroporation exert distinct effects on vasculatures [3]. Significant 
apoptosis of endothelial cells and microvessel thromboses w ere observed in tumors at 30 min after IRE 
[4]. IRE also disrupt ed blood-brain barrier [5]. Reve rsible electroporation, on the other, induced a 
temporary “vasculature lock” that is often resolved within 48 h [6]. The vasculature lock could be divided 
into two phases. During the first phase, electroporation induced a reflexory vasoconstriction of afferent 
arterioles mediated by the sympathetic nervous system. During the second phase, the increase in vascular 
permeability triggered fluid leakage from the vascular lumen into the adjacent interstitial space of the tumor. 
The subsequent elevation of tumor interstitial pressure, along with the reduction in intravascular pressure, 
ultimately converged to cause vasculature collapse. 
 These early studies suggested that electroporation may have dichotomous effects on tumor uptake of 
nanoparticles. On the one hand, increase of vasculature permeability facilitated nanoparticle leakage into 
the interstitial space, and thus favor ed their tumor uptake. On the other hand, IRE -induced thromboses 
and reversible electroporation -induced vasculature lock may hinder tumor blood perfusion, and 
consequently was unfavorable for the tumor uptake of nanoparticles.  
 Several studies have investigated the effects of IRE on the tumor uptake of chemotherapy drugs or 
nanoparticles (summarized in the table below). Bhutiani et al discovered that IRE enhanced gemcitabine 
delivery to the zone of reversible electroporation in pancreatic tumor models [7]. Peng et al. reported that 
the combination of microsecond and nanosecond PEF increased nano-gel uptake in murine colon tumor 
models [8]. We previously showed that tumor uptake of fluorescence-labeled dextran was elevated on day 
4 post-IRE [9].  
 We noticed that the sequence of electroporation and nanoparticle injection was different in the current 
study: M-20U4V was injected 4 h before electroporation, while nanoparticles/gemcitabine was injected 
after electroporation in the other three studies. Such discrepancy in schedule might have shifted the peak 
of plasma concentration-time curve of M -C20U4V relative to the behavior change of tumor vasculature . 
No improvement of M-C20U4V retention was observed as a result. Further experiments will be performed
