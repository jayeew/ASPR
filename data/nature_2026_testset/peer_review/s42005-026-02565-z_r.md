Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The work by Kuchkin, V. M. et al, gives a regularized theory for micromagnetism, which allows for including Bloch points,
whose magnetization structure possesses singularities causing several problems in continuum theories. Particularly, the
work modifies the domain in which the order parameter lies, expanding it from the S2-model to an S3-model, keeping the
usual constraints for micromagnetism. The work is exceptionally well-written, very clear, and I think that could help to bridge
a gap regarding the proper micromagnetic description of Bloch points. However, before recommending this work for
publication, the authors should address the following points: 
1) I am afraid of the behaviour of the magnetostatic energy. It is essentially non-local in the coordinates (because of the
Green function or Kernel), so it would be beneficial for the readers if the authors go deeper into how the extended
micromagnetic theory looks in such an energy term. 
2) Mathematically, the theory allows for the inclusion of the parameter a_3, which, as the authors stated, could be set to zero
(by setting \epsilon = 0). Is this parameter meaningful in some respect? What are the consequences of setting \epsilon\neq
0? 
3) Regarding the collective coordinate approach: How is the position of the corresponding BC captured in Fig 2?. It should
be mentioned because, in principle, it could affect the actual BP position. 
4)The authors claim that the S3 micromagnetic model, when including a singularity, should be better compared with a
quantum atomistic model. Can the authors provide such a comparison, at least qualitatively? 
5)Is the extended micromagnetic theory capable of capturing all the well-known features of the S2 model, even for systems
without singularities? Specifically, the Walker breakdown for domain walls? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents a new extension of the classical micromagnetic theory to the case of the spatially-dependent and
variable-in-length order parameter vector. The authors extended the previously proposed approach to describe
magnetization via \mathbb{S}^3 sphere-vector to dynamics and, by construction of the equations of motion and Thiele
equations, demonstrated a good stability of numerical solutions of Bloch point motion. While the technical side of the work
looks valid and interesting, I would like to clarify several physical points before recommending it for publication in
Communications Physics. 
The first straightforward question to the model that appears is, what is the advantage of \mathbb{S}^3 sphere over a ball B =
{ \vec{r}; r \le 1} ? At some point, probably, the result should be equivalent, however, a reader may miss some deeper
reasons for the choice of the proposed way. 
I am confused that the authors call \nu_4 as the fictitious component while it serves as a formally physical quantity in the
energy expression. And, generally speaking, by its relation to the length of the order parameter, should also be constrained
by the symmetry of the particular magnet. Still, its physical meaning and role in the analysis are hidden. In particular, the
exchange energy density (4) seems to reach a minimum at \nu_4 = 0. Then it’s not clear how the variable length of the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

magnetization vector can be developed? Is it just a dynamic effect, while the statics needs a constraint n_0(0) = 0 as given in
Supplemental Material I? 
For a “classical” LLG equation, two possible symmetries of torques have a clear physical meaning: precession and
dissipation. On the first sight, the third torque vector h in Eq. (8) which is quadratic by the effective field \beta, should be
responsible for the change of length of magnetization. But then the authors skip it because of smallness, while all effects are
handled by the torques p and d, probably because they also include \nu_4. For me it sounds like a hint that one may choose
a better basis of torques where each of the vectors will have a unique responsibility. 
My major issue with the understanding of the further potential of the presented theory is related to Discussion part of the
manuscript. The major part of modern magnetism is based on the symmetry considerations and establishment of the
relations between different approaches. Despite 1.5-page size of the discussion, I missed this point. The classical “fixed-
length spin” models, when transformed into continuous description, basically deal with the spatial average over a volume
that is larger than the interatomic distance and smaller than the characteristic length scales. Thus, I do not envision any
issues with the correspondence to either classical or quantum spins. Also, I’m not fully convinced that LLB and LLBar have
major physical disadvantages in comparison with the presented theory. The constraint |\vec{n}| \le 1, it is rather a
mathematical adaptation of the fact that far below any phase transition point, one should make a power series of the free
energy over directional cosines of the order parameter rather than over its magnitude itself. In real materials, the saturation
magnetization as a function of temperature is not even necessarily monotonic. While the LLBar equation stands aside by
intrinsic physics, I would expect that there should be a connection of the presented approach with the LLB equation, e.g., via
specific functional dependency of the longitudinal relaxation constant with \kappa from Eq. (4). The presence (or absence) of
such a connection would be important to demonstrate, e.g., by providing both dynamic equations in the same variables and
analyzing similarities and differences. 
Reading about Thiele approach, I missed the Ansatz used for the calculations. To my belief, more detailed analytics
presented in the manuscript can strength it, otherwise it leaves a taste that the regularized equations cannot be handled
analytically and should be processed only numerically. 
Finally, I wonder if there is any relation between the parameter space of \vec{\nu} with a possibility to get a finite Hopf index?
Several technical remarks: 
- In the introduction, the authors provide a number of references regarding experiments (Refs [22-47]) and theory (Refs [48-
91]). I would recommend to dedicate a paragraph or so to provide a bit of insight into what has been done there. In particular,
some of those references, like Ref. 56 also considers variable magnetization length in statics and dynamics, it would be
instructive for a reader to provide a comparison of their and the present approaches. 
- After Eq. (1), the authors write “is the demagnetizing field (or dipole-dipole) interaction”. Although the only far-field part of
the dipole-dipole interaction is equivalent to the magnetostatic energy, while the near-field part contributes to the
magnetocrystalline anisotropy, see, e.g. Akhiezer et al, Spin waves, North-Holland (1968). 
- In the paragraph before Eq. (12), the authors write “solution of the Thiele equation can be considered an exact result toward
which numerical simulations should converge in the steady-state limit.” This statement is misleading because the Thiele
equation is Ansatz-based and technically cannot provide the estimate of accuracy in a self-consistent way. A common way
is the opposite: one performs full-scale simulations or other fully numerical solutions and then verifies the Ansatz and
assumed degrees of freedom of this Ansatz by comparing with numerics. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper presents a theoretical model for magnetization dynamics which can smoothly describe the motion of Bloch points.
The authors use a micromagnetic energy functional extended to account for changes in the length of the magnetization to
derive a modified version of the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation. It is demonstrated that the generalized equation of motion
corrects numerical problems with the original Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation in the vicinity of Bloch points where the length
of the magnetization has to vanish due to topological constraints. The numerical solution of the modified equation
(implemented in the micromagnetic solver Mumax3) shows favourable agreement with the solution of the Thiele equation
derived analytically from the same equation of motion. 
The structure and dynamics of non-collinear spin textures has attracted considerable attention over the past decades.
Micromagnetic simulations represent the most popular numerical approach for studying such structures. However,
micromagnetics is based on the assumption of the magnetization direction varying smoothly on length scales which are
considerably larger than the atomic distances, and this approximation breaks down for textures containing Bloch points
where the magnetization vanishes, causing numerical instabilities. The method proposed by the authors enables a
numerically more stable description of such structures while preserving the general formalism of the micromagnetic
framework. Therefore, the paper is expected to attract the interest of a broad community utilizing micromagnetic simulations
for making theoretical predictions or for explaining experimental results. I have two main concerns with the manuscript: First,
the derivation of the modified equation of motion is presented purely in mathematical terms, and the physical motivation
remains obscure. Second, in my opinion the Discussions section should be considerably reworked. After these revisions,
the manuscript may be suitable for publication in Communications Physics.

My main points of criticism are the following: 
1. The form of Eq. (5) appears to be determined purely from mathematical principles, namely that the equation of motion
must conserve the length of the vector \nu. Because of this, the physical meaning of the different terms in this equation is
unclear. In contrast, the conventional Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation is not based only on the conservation of the
magnetization length, since that would allow any linear combination of the precession and dissipation terms in the equation.
The motivation for this specific choice of terms is that the precession term may be derived from the quantum-mechanical
equation of motion for the spin, while the damping term allows for a change in the energy in the spin system via spin-orbit
coupling, and can generally be assumed to be much weaker than the precession term. In my opinion, it would be useful to
introduce similar physical arguments for the terms in the generalized equation. This is particularly true for the h term which
does not have an analogue in the conventional equation; therefore, it is not clear under which conditions it may be neglected
as is done in the manuscript. 
2. The physical motivation of the generalized energy expression in Eq. (4) could also be elaborated upon. First, the gradient
term appears to be invariant under any O(4) rotation performed in the space of the order parameter \nu, but it is not clear
whether this is required by physical principles or is an arbitrary choice. The invariance of the conventional energy
expression under rotations of the order parameter follows from the exchange term being isotropic in spin directions, but since
in the generalized model the role of the \nu_{4} component is different from the other three components, it may also be
exempted from such a rotational symmetry. 
Second, the value of the \kappa parameter in the same equation appears to be very hard to estimate. Although the authors
discuss this point in detail and even comment on the possibility of performing experiments that could help in determining its
value, it would at least be necessary to discuss how the value of \kappa influences the simulation results in the manuscript.
The value of \kappa most likely has an effect on the equilibrium structure of the non-collinear textures, as well as on the
direction and the velocity of their movement. Currently, only results for a fixed value of \kappa are presented, and it is not
discussed why this value differs by a large factor between the two different simulated systems (\kappa=7.9 nm and 42 nm,
respectively). 
3. The Discussions section is expected to contain a detailed discussion of the results presented in the manuscript and their
potential impact on future studies. However, starting from the subtitle "Classical spin lattice models", the authors mainly
focus on discussing alternative models of magnetic systems in a not particularly impartial way. Many of the statements in this
part could be considered rather controversial. In my opinion, this type of analysis is not suited for an original research article.
The authors are of course welcome to stimulate the exchange of ideas on this topic, but this could be more appropriately
done by splitting this part of the manuscript and expanding it into a separate perspective-type article. 
Other minor comments: 
4. In the fourth paragraph on page 1, the authors cite an excessive number of previous works on Bloch points. Despite this,
from this paragraph it does not become really clear what types of problems the treatment of Bloch points in the conventional
micromagnetic framework causes, and this can be only understood from the results presented later in the manuscript. I
would suggest to specify the problem more precisely here, possibly supported by a few earlier publications where such
problems have been observed instead of a huge number of previous works which might only be loosely related to the main
point of the manuscript. 
5. In the first paragraph of the Results section on page 2, the definition of the dipolar interaction term should be extended by
the meaning of the B_{d} field. Currently, the dipolar and the Zeeman terms formally have almost equivalent definitions,
although they influence the magnetic structures differently. 
6. In the second paragraph of the Results section on page 2, it should be defined what a "hedgehog-like spin texture" is,
since this is currently only presented in Supplementary Section I, without referring to its hedgehog-like nature. 
7. In the third paragraph on page 4, it is written that "Here, we restrict our analysis to small torques and focus on the near-
equilibrium regime, where linear theory is applicable." I suggest to rephrase this sentence, since the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert
equation is nonlinear. 
8. In the fourth paragraph on page 4, it is stated that "Moreover, the solution of the Thiele equation can be considered an
exact result toward which numerical simulations should converge in the steady-state limit." In my opinion, this statement
should also be rephrased. The Thiele equation is not exact, since it is derived under the assumption that the spin structure is
not deformed by the forces acting on it. For example, for very strong driving currents, this is no longer a valid approximation,
and the Thiele equation cannot accurately describe the motion of textures even if a steady state is reached. 
9. In the caption of Fig. 2, "regularized S^{2}-model" should probably read "regularized S^{3}-model". 
10. In the fourth paragraph on page 6, it is written that "simulations based on the S^{2}-model [in Figs. 2e and f] do not exhibit
convergence with increasing mesh density". This is not really clear from the figure: simulations are presented for only three
mesh sizes, so the convergence is hard to judge, but for the two larger mesh sizes the data points appear to be rather close
to each other. 
11. In the first paragraph of the Methods section on page 8, it is written that "To stabilize skyrmion and chiral bobber, we

apply an external field of 0.7D^{2}/2AM, which corresponds to the equilibrium period of spin-spiral L_{e} = L_{D}." I could not
find the definition of L_{e} in the text. It may be connected to the exchange length denoted by L_{c} in the first paragraph of
the Discussions section on page 7, but that does not depend on the external field as implied by the sentence. 
12. In the line above Eq. (2) on page 1 of the Supplementary Material, "continuous" should be replaced by "smooth" when
introducing the Taylor series. 
13. In Eq. (19) of the Supplementary Material, \dot{n}_{z} should be replaced by \dot{\nu}_{3} as introduced in the line above
the equation. 
14. In Supplementary Sec. V, Eq. (36) is derived under the assumption that the velocity lies in the xy plane. However, the
velocity is obtained as a solution of the Thiele equation, so it cannot be assumed beforehand that it will lie in the plane. The
derivation would be more straightforward to follow if it was shown that when certain components of the gyrovector and the
dissipation tensor in the general form of Eq. (38) vanish, then the velocity will lie in the plane.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors replied to the comments of the Reviewers exhaustively, and revised the manuscript accordingly. In my opinion,
they successfully addressed the concerns connected to the presented results. However, I think the Discussion section, in
particular the subsections "Classical spin lattice models" and "Temperature in micromagnetism", still requires considerable
revision. Apparently, the authors interpreted my previous comments regarding these parts that I find them unnecessary,
while actually I think the present formulation is inappropriate: they should be rewritten and extended such that they present a
more balanced view on and a detailed comparison of different approaches, and accurately represent the state-of-the-art in
the literature. Most of these issues have been laid out by Reviewer 2 in the previous review, but I reformulate them in the
following. I find the revision of this part of the manuscript important before being able to give a final recommendation
concerning publication. 
The main points are the following: 
1. The main result of the manuscript is the realization that standard micromagnetic simulations cannot correctly describe the
dynamics of structures containing Bloch points, and providing a regularization of the simulations solving this specific
problem. This is generally correctly reflected in the text. However, the subsection "Classical spin lattice models" seems to
offer a different perspective: (i) other approaches to magnetization dynamics are "simplified"; (ii) they suffer from numerous
(but in part unspecified) problems; and (iii) since the regularized equation solves the problem of Bloch point dynamics, it is
implied that it has solved all problems of the previous methods. 
Concerning point (i), like in other areas of physics, the different approaches to magnetization dynamics are not really
competing, rather complementary; that is, they can be applied on different length and time scales. More general models have
an increased computational complexity, which restricts their applications to small systems and short time scales if the results
are meant to be obtained in a reasonable amount of simulation time; then there are approaches involving more
approximations that are only suited to a subset of problems, but provide a solution within a shorter time frame. The authors
seem to be aware of this context when they write that "However, the complexity of these models limits their applicability to
only the simplest systems. In practice, we often have to rely on simplified models.", but I would suggest to rephrase these
sentences by avoiding references to simplification. In the present context, I even find the application of these terms factually
incorrect: quantum and classical atomistic models are impractical for treating large systems not because they "use a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

simplified 
Hamiltonian" or represent a "next level of simplification", but precisely because they are more general and thus
computationally more complex than micromagnetic models. The micromagnetic approach cannot treat, e.g., quantum spin
liquids or various types of antiferromagnetic order described by the lattice models, and this is not a technical but rather a
fundamental limitation due to them being more specialized. Furthermore, the sentence "classical atomistic spin models, […]
[are] generally regarded as a useful but simplified approximation" clearly represents an opinion without any scientific
arguments, and is consequently inappropriate in the present context. It reads similar to arguing that the many-body
Schrödinger equation is "simplified", because it is highly impractical for describing the collision between two macroscopic
bodies. 
Concerning point (ii), the sentence mentioned above about "a useful but simplified approximation" or "do not overcome the
fundamental limitations discussed above", do not provide much insight into what these many limitations are. One of these
explicitly mentioned in the text is that atomistic models are better suited for smaller systems, but this is inherent to the
complementary nature of the methods as mentioned above. Rather than simply stating that "real magnetic systems typically
contain billions of spins", the authors should specify in what types of problems they are interested in in the present context.
Other researchers may have a different interpretation of a "real magnetic system": quantum computers only contain a handful
of logical qubits corresponding to spin 1/2 particles, while macroscopic permanent magnets used for providing large
magnetic fields contain orders of magnitudes more spins than mentioned above, and neither of these are well suited to
micromagnetic simulations. Multiscale models are specifically designed to bridge this gap between the length scales, and
have an extensive literature which I encourage the authors to discuss in more detail; currently, they only cite Ref. [135] which
apparently also treats Bloch point dynamics specifically, and this suffices for them to conclude that "Due to their inherent
complexity, such methods remain less thoroughly examined in terms of agreement with experimental observations and may
introduce additional artifacts at the borders between the regions described by different Hamiltonians." The other issue the
authors specifically mention is that classical atomistic models use magnetic moments of a fixed length. Probably the
implication is that classical atomistic models cannot describe Bloch points; however, Bloch points do not occur in atomistic
models in the first place, since they require the magnetization to be defined continuously in space, rather than on a discrete
lattice. 
For point (iii), the authors state that the "regularized micromagnetic model [...] avoids [the] inherent limitations" of lattice
models. This is certainly true concerning the fixed length of the spin, but the regularization does not address inherent
limitations of the micromagnetic model, namely that it is designed for treating ferromagnetic systems primarily on the
micrometre length scale. In particular, "it is more appropriate to compare our model with such quantum atomistic models"
appears to be an overstatement, since any classical model is incapable of treating, e.g., quantum paramagnets or
entanglement. Even where such a comparison would be possible, namely how the expectation value of the spin length is
reduced in the vicinity of the Bloch points, the authors do not provide a direct comparison, since their approach is
phenomenological in nature. Although the authors discussed this question in more detail in their previous work in Ref. [96]
and in their reply to Reviewer 1, it would be useful to elaborate upon the short statements currently found in the manuscript
along these lines. For example, would it be possible to fit the value of the phenomenological parameter \kappa to quantum
simulations? 
2. In the subsection "Temperature in micromagnetism", the authors perform a comparison with other generalizations of the
standard micromagnetic approach, namely the Landau-Lifshitz-Baryakhtar and the Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch equations. Due to
the similar length and time scales involved, the comparison is more meaningful here. However, the authors here mainly refer
to early publications in Refs. [92] and [93], while they do not address the advances in these methods made since then; see,
e.g., the reviews V. G. Baryakhtar et al., Low Temp. Phys. 39, 993 (2013) or U. Atxitia et al., J. Phys. D: Appl. Phys. 50,
033003 (2017), which also cover multiscale modeling and comparisons with experiments. Furthermore, a meaningful
comparison would require presenting the solution to the same problem using the different approaches, preferably also
including the analytical solution or experimental data as a reliable benchmark, and pointing out the advantages and
disadvantages of the various approaches. Without such a detailed study, stating that "there is no clear advantage in
employing the Landau energy term and LLB or LLBar dynamics for modeling Bloch point behavior" appears unjustified. 
Apparently, one of the issues the authors raise is that standard micromagnetic theory is "athermal", and they treat this as a
feature that cannot be avoided, restricting the simulations to low temperatures. However, the Landau-Lifshitz-Baryakhtar and
Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch approaches consider this as a limitation, which they try to overcome by including thermal fluctuations,
making them applicable up to the Curie temperature. Despite these alternative approaches covering wider temperature
ranges, the authors conclude that they are limited to a "narrow parameter range". 
The authors also mention that the length of the dimensionless order parameter in these approaches may become larger than
one due to thermal fluctuations at certain points in space and time, and interpret this such that "the model permits unphysical
increases in the magnetization magnitude beyond saturation". However, the saturation magnetization is not a fundamental
limiting value of the local value of the magnetization, but rather represents the thermal expectation value of the spins inside a
small volume. This is why it has a strong temperature dependence and vanishes at the Curie temperature, which would not
be expected from a mathematical constraint independent of temperature. The Landau theory of the phase transition does not
treat the order parameter as a normalized quantity in this region specifically because the changes in its magnitude are also
relevant. In this context, the theoretical approach need not necessarily enforce a limit on the length of the instantaneous
value of the magnetization at a single point in space which cannot be measured, rather only on its expectation value which
is an observable quantity; this is indeed the approach followed by the Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch equation, in particular. 
It is also mentioned that "there is no established method for incorporating external torques into the LLB or LLBar equations

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

while preserving the constraint |n|\le 1". While this may be correct in this form since these methods do not satisfy this
constraint as discussed above, there has certainly been efforts to incorporate spin torques in these models; see, e.g., C.
Schieback et al., Phys. Rev. B 80, 214403 (2009). 
The authors also state that "the Thiele equation [...] can be derived from the regularized LLG framework proposed 
here, but not from the LLB or LLBar equations". First, while I could not find previous publications on the derivation of the
Thiele equation with the alternative methods, I see no fundamental limitation that would prohibit it; the Thiele equation
excludes thermal fluctuations which avoids the issue raised by the authors concerning the magnetization exceeding its
saturation value. Second, it is not clear why this could be considered an advantage. Even if the Thiele equation is treated as
a benchmark (which is debatable since it involves more approximations than the approaches which are compared against it,
as pointed out by Reviewer 2 and myself in the previous review), the natural question is whether the results of the methods
agree with the Thiele equation (which, in the case of the LLB and LLBar equations, no results are provided for or against in
the present manuscript), not whether the equation itself can be derived from the formalism. 
Finally, the authors return to the point that the regularized approach "offers a more consistent and physically 
grounded framework, particularly for systems where the magnetization length varies due to quantum effects rather than
thermal fluctuations". As discussed above, the comparison of the present method to quantum spin models is limited and only
qualitative. It is also worth mentioning that the Landau-Lifshitz-Bloch equation was actually derived from a quantum-
mechanical approach (see D. A. Garanin, Physica 172, 470 (1991)), so it is not clear why it would be completely incapable
of treating quantum effects. 
Other comments: 
3. I am not convinced by the derivation in Sec. II of the Supplemental Material. In short, I have the impression that the authors
confound constraints on the direction of h (orthogonality relations) with constraints on the length of h (how it scales with the
effective field \beta). In particular, the authors set out to construct h such that it is perpendicular to the vectors \nu, p and d. In
Eq. (7), they provide the most general form of h that is already perpendicular to \nu and d (since d is in the plane spanned by
\nu and \beta), while being not parallel to p. Importantly, although \beta appears in a linear form on the right-hand side of Eq.
(7), this does not imply that h scales linearly with \beta, since the coefficients c_{1}-c_{3} may have any functional
dependence on \beta which is not denoted explicitly in this equation, and does not affect orthogonality. Then when checking
orthogonality to p, they derive the condition "\beta^{T}A\beta=0, which leads to a necessary and sufficient condition A^{T}=-
A." I do not understand what results in the conclusion that A must be antisymmetric. If the relation has to hold for any \beta,
that implies A=0, which technically satisfies A^{T}=-A but is a stronger constraint. However, after the further steps in Eqs.
(11) and (12), this would imply that it is impossible to construct any vector h, linear in \beta or not, that is simultaneously
perpendicular to \nu, p and d. This is obviously a contradiction, since the authors provide the natural choice of h=\nu \times
p\times d. I suspect that the problem is that \beta^{T}A\beta=0 does not have to hold for any \beta vector, since this equation
was already derived under assuming a certain direction for \beta. In this case, the equation only implies that A cannot be a
strictly positive definite matrix. Concerning the scaling, choosing h=|\beta|\nu\times p/|p| \times d/|d| would give a vector that
satisfies the orthogonality conditions while its length is linear in |\beta|, and it is not clear to me why this term cannot be
allowed in the equation of motion. 
4. In my previous report, I suggested adding to the Introduction what kinds of problems Bloch points introduce in the
conventional micromagnetic formalism. The authors replied that this problem was not really covered in the literature
previously. Since the problem was realized in this work, then it could be added to the last paragraph of the Introduction
instead where the manuscript is summarized. In my opinion, this would be important for understanding why the
regularization is required. It is written in the fourth paragraph of the Introduction on page 1 that "The obstacle lies in the
divergence of the effective field in the magnetization dynamics equation when a BP is present", but I doubt that this gives a
full picture, since this would also prohibit describing static Bloch points with micromagnetic theory. Apparently the problem
arises during the dynamics of Bloch points, which may get pinned between discretization cells, which I agree with the
authors is an artefact of the standard micromagnetic approach. 
5. In the second paragraph of the subsection "Current-induced soliton dynamics" on page 6, the structures are identified as
"a skyrmion tube [Fig. 2c], chiral bobber [110] [Fig. 2d], and dipole string [107] [Fig. 2e]". This does not seem to agree with
the current panel labels, where these structures appear in panels a, b and c. The same panel labels are repeated in the first
paragraph of the Methods section on page 9.
