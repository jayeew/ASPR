Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled ‘Iterative discovery of potent polymeric antibiotics via multi-stage and multi-task learning against
antimicrobial resistance’ describes a framework (PolyCLOVER) to discover by Deep Learning application novel potent and
selective antimicrobial polymers that mimic the properties of Host Defence / Antimicrobial Peptides (HDPs / AMPs), among
combinatorial libraries. The validity of this framework is demonstrated through the successful identification of 3 candidates
from a library of approximately 100,000 poly(β-amino ester)s, which are subsequently evaluated, validated in vitro and in
vivo, in mono and combination therapy studies, as antibiotic candidates and adjuvant antibiotic carriers, respectively. 
The significance of these results is justified by the structural complexity that can be displayed by antimicrobial polymers,
which are of interest to a broad community of researchers and for a number of important applications. They can present a
broad diversity and fill a vast chemical space, while being peptidomimetics of AMPs, which have complex mechanisms of
action and structure-activity relationships already. As a result, the traditional trial-and-error approach for the identification of
novel candidates is particularly ineffective. In addition, model learning for antimicrobial polymers is complicated by other
factors such as data availability and standardisation, as well as molecular representation, as explained by the authors. The
successful identification by PolyCLOVER of 3 potent and selective candidates from ternary copolymers based on a poly-
amino ester backbone in 20 days, is therefore significant. 
The manuscript is well written and presented and discloses a comprehensive, well designed and executed study, including
two in vivo models. As polymer antibiotics are more commonly developed as single agents (antibiotic candidates), the ability
of the poly(β-amino ester)s identified in the present work to self-assemble into stable nanoparticles for the effective delivery
of a classical antibiotic that synergise the two agents appears to extend the development of these polymers to a higher level
than generally performed and to address an additional challenge as self-assembly of antimicrobial polymers can in fact
result in a loss of antimicrobial activity.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents an innovative polymeric platform for the autonomous exploration of polymeric antibiotics,
leveraging a self-supervised deep learning approach that integrates graph-based molecular representations, active learning,
and high-throughput synthesis to explore large chemical spaces without reliance on pre-existing datasets. Experimentally,
the study focuses on the systemic efficacy of self-assembled nanoparticle polymers (SANPs), including in pneumonia and
intraperitoneal infection models. Specifically, it describes the development of PolyCLOVER, a deep learning framework that
integrates multi-stage self-supervised learning, active learning, and high-throughput synthesis to navigate the vast and
structurally complex chemical space of host defense peptide–mimetic polymeric antibiotics. By applying PolyCLOVER to a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

combinatorial library of ~100,000 poly(β-amino ester)s, the authors identified three lead compounds that self-assemble into
stable nanoparticles (SANPs) with potent antibacterial activity against multidrug-resistant S. aureus and A. baumannii, as
well as adjuvant properties that restore bacterial sensitivity to penicillin G. The in vivo efficacy of these SANPs, both as
monotherapies and in combination with conventional antibiotics, underscores the potential of this AI–experimental
integration to accelerate antibacterial discovery in the absence of large pre-existing datasets. While the concept is
compelling and the integration of AI-driven molecular design with in vivo validation is of high interest, several key areas
require clarification and expansion to enable a thorough peer review and to strengthen the scientific rigor and reproducibility
of the work. 
Comments 
Machine Learning Methods: The ML/AI methodology in the current form is difficult to follow and lacks sufficient detail for
reproducibility and thorough peer review. 
• The manuscript does not explicitly state what molecular features were used to train the models. While Tables S5 and S6
appear to contain some feature-related information, it remains unclear whether these are the only features employed. This
should be explicitly clarified in the Methods section. 
• Tables S5 and S6 are not referenced in the main text. All supplementary tables should be clearly cited and contextualized
where relevant. 
• Table S7 lists the hyperparameter search space, but the final hyperparameter values chosen for each model are not
reported. These are essential for reproducibility and should be included. 
• The authors should provide a clear, structured description of the entire ML workflow, including: 
o Data preprocessing steps 
o Feature selection/engineering 
o Model architectures 
o Training and validation strategies 
o Performance evaluation metrics and thresholds 
This structured reporting will enable assessment of methodological rigor and facilitate replication or adaptation by other
researchers. 
Experimental Concerns: 
1. Toxicity assay relevance (Figure S17, panel B): The cell line toxicity assay does not specify which cell lines were tested.
For a pneumonia model, inclusion of toxicity data from lung epithelial cell lines would be more relevant. This should be
addressed in either the current dataset or with additional experiments. 
2. Toxicity markers in vivo (MRSA peritonitis model): In the mouse model of lethal MRSA peritonitis, standard toxicity
markers such as serum alanine transaminase (ALT) and kidney function assays should be included to provide a more
complete safety profile. 
3. Generalizability of PolyCLOVER platform: To demonstrate that PolyCLOVER can be applied more broadly to polymeric
antibiotic discovery, the authors should include at least one example using a chemical framework or backbone other than
poly(β-amino ester)s. 
4. Activity against bacterial persistence phenotypes: Testing SANPs against biofilms and persister cells would help
establish their potential against challenging infection states and broaden their translational relevance.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a machine learning–guided, iterative framework for the discovery of polymeric antibiotics,
leveraging a multi-stage, multi-task learning design to jointly predict antibacterial and hemolytic activities. By integrating
computational predictions with experimental feedback, the authors establish a closed-loop discovery cycle that accelerates
candidate identification. Using this approach, they identified polymeric antibiotics with potent activity against resistant
bacterial strains, with wet-lab validation confirming that several candidates outperformed existing comparators in efficacy
while maintaining acceptable safety profiles. Overall, the manuscript is clear and easy to follow. I only have some minor
comments: 
1. Transductive limitation: The authors acknowledge that the method design (e.g., the ratio node and the pretraining strategy)
makes the approach kind of transductive. I think this is a major limitation of the work as it prevents direct application of the
model to other polymers. I suggest that the authors more explicitly state this constraint in the manuscript so that readers
clearly understand the current scope and applicability of the method. 
2. Greedy acquisition strategy (Page 8, line 234): The authors mention that a “greedy acquisition strategy was employed to
further exploit the most promising candidates,” but the description is rather brief. Please elaborate a bit more about this. 
3. Ratio embedding: The manuscript does not clearly explain how the ratio embedding is implemented. Was it based on a
learnable sinusoidal encoding, or another approach?

From a machine learning perspective, we directly modeled the relationship between the input 
feeding ratios (combined with the chemical structures) and the antibacterial and hemolytic 
properties. This design enables the model to automatically learn the systematic biases or kinetic 
effects presen t in the experimental data. In other words, the model is trained to learn the 
correspondence between feeding ratios (combined with the chemical structures) and observed 
biological activity, and during inference, it predicts activity under the same type of inputs (feeding 
ratios)—ensuring that the training and prediction domains are aligned. Thus, the model effectively 
captures the relationship between input conditions (feeding ratios + structures) and measured 
properties, even if the actual ratios are not explicitly known. 
Moreover, even if the actual monomer ratios of the synthesized candidates were precisely 
characterized and used for training, such information would not be available for unlabeled samples 
during prediction. In practice, we only know the fe eding ratios when selecting new candidates for 
synthesis, and there is no reliable inverse mapping from actual composition back to the feeding 
conditions. Therefore, modeling based on actual ratios —while potentially more accurate during 
training—would not translate into a practically useful tool for guiding optimization.

optimization of antibacterial polymers, where it is essential to balance efficiency and accuracy. In 
this context, detailed structural characterization would not be cost-effective, compatible with a high-
throughput workflow, or necessary for achieving the goals of this study. At the same time, 
considering the broad synthetic accessibility of poly( β-amino ester)s and the precedent set by 
previous studies that have employed them to construct libraries 1,2, we accepted a certain degree of 
deviation between the actual ratio and the feeding ratio to accommodate the practical requirements. 
Our current structure + feeding ratio modeling approach provides a practical, consistent, and data-
driven framework for predicting polymer activity under high-throughput experiment settings.

We sincerely thank the reviewer for this insightful comment and for stimulating discussion on 
the mechanisms underlying the restored susceptibility of MRSA to penicillin G. As the reviewer 
correctly notes, similar observations have been reported in the literature, where cationic polymers, 
peptides, or nanoparticles with membrane -disruptive properti es were shown to potentiate the 
activity of conventional antibiotics. However, to the best of our knowledge, a definitive molecular-
level explanation for this phenomenon remains elusive, and we hope the reviewer will appreciate 
the difficulty of establishi ng such a mechanism with certainty. For instance, in the study cited by 
the reviewer (DOI: 10.1021/acsinfecdis.0c00016) 3, cationic nanoparticles were shown to restore 
MRSA susceptibility to oxacillin, and the authors observed mutations in the ebh gene encoding the 
hyperosmolarity resistance protein Ebh across multiple isolates. Likewise , Williams et al. 4 (DOI: 
10.1021/acscentsci.4c01973) demonstrated that polyacrylamide copolymers with membrane -
disruptive effects enhanced the efficac y of rifampicin, penicillin, and vancomycin against E. coli, 
and their interpretation favored a model in which copolymer -induced membrane perturbation 
facilitated antibiotic penetration. 
We also found the reviewer ’s reference to Mobashery ’s seminal work 5 (DOI: 
10.1021/ja0434376) particularly inspiring. In this study, the key acylation step (1 → 2) was shown 
to depend on both the substrate concentration and the acylation rate constant k2 (as illustrated in the 
schematic pathway below). In light of this, we now appreciate that two complementary mechanisms 
may be possible in our system. On the one hand, SANP nanocomposites carrying penicillin G may

enrich the antibiotic at the m embrane interface, effectively increasing its local concentration and 
thereby driving the acylation reaction forward, which was the basis of our original hypothesis. On 
the other hand, membrane interactions, particularly those involving destabilization, could create an 
environment rich in NAM/NAG fragments from cell wall turnover, which may in turn promote 
conformational changes in PBP2a. Such changes could facilitate the opening of the active site and 
increase k2, thereby enhancing access of β-lactam antibiotics. We have now incorporated this latter 
possibility into the revised manuscript as a plausible alternative mechanism. 
 
Accordingly, we have revised the corresponding section in the main text to clarify this 
point: “Based on the above findings, we speculate that, due to the cationic and hydrophobic 
properties, the SANP nanocomposites can act as membrane -disruptive carriers that preferentially 
accumulate and integrate into bacterial membranes 56. This process leads to p assive enrichment of 
PG at the membrane interface, thereby increasing its local concentration. At the same time, the 
membrane-disruptive effect of SANPs may generate additional cell wall fragments in the 
surrounding environment, which could kinetically facilitate the acylation of the active-site serine of 
PBP2a by β-lactams57, ultimately overcoming the low-affinity resistance mechanism.” 
Finally, regarding the stability of penicillin G when encapsulated in SANP nanocomposites, 
we agree that this is an impor tant consideration. The nucleophilic ring -opening of β-lactams by 
amines typically requires harsh catalytic conditions (strongly acidic or basic media) 6. Under the 
relatively neutral conditions of our experiments (pH ≈ 7), such reactions are unlikely to proceed to 
a significant extent. To experimentally validate this, we mixed penicillin G with a model amine 
monomer structurally analogous to the repeating unit of the identified SANPs (N,N -dimethyl 
ethylenediamine) under neutral pH and analyzed the mixture by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The NMR 
spectra confirmed that no detectable reaction occurred under these conditions. In contrast, when pH 
= 11, the NMR spectrum exhibited multiple additional peaks with irregular integrations, indicating
