Reviewers' comments: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript addresses variations in local deformation of a nanocrystalline gold sample using 4D-STEM (2-D mapping of
2-D diffraction patterns). 
1. 4D-STEM for materials characterization is enjoying a great deal of interest. 
However, there are different ways in which the
technique is implemented. 
The method used in the current study, namely precession enhanced electron diffraction (PED) for
crystal orientation mapping (COM), has been in use for more than fifteen years and therefore it is neither new nor novel. 
PED
mapping for determination of grain boundary character is also not new. 
Nor are the use of PED orientation mapping for
elastic strain determination and the mapping of heterogenous strains new. 
2. The number of grains in the sample studied is small (24 grains), and the study was not repeated with other samples.
Hence, a great deal of the text is devoted to detailing the orientation of each grain and how these orientations change, such
as by grain rotations, as the sample is strained in-situ. 
The mapping of the heterogeneity of strain in the grains and near the
boundaries is interesting but not surprising. 
3. Although the sample is strained in the elastic regime, the authors acknowledge that the residual strains present from the
initial sample preparation (DC sputter deposition and post deposition annealing) impact the deformation behavior. They then
attribute this behavior to dislocations and their movement. The von Mises elastic strains are computed. However, the
dislocation densities are not quantified, and, as a result, the detailed discussions and conclusions regarding correlations or
lack therefore of are based on R-squared values that are in the range of 0.2-0.6, and therefore fail to be convincing. 
4. The methodology for determination of the geometrically necessary dislocation (GND) densities using the Nye Tensor was
reported by Leff et al., Ultramicroscopy153, 9(2015). Leff et al. also showed that the use of PED mapping can allow
determination of statistically significant dislocation (SSD) densities in addition to the GND densities. The approach of Leff et
al. should be used for these Au samples to quantify both the residual dislocation densities as well as the evolution of these
densities during in-situ straining. 
Once this is done and more samples and grains are analyzed it may be possible to arrive at
a quantitative description of the deformation behavior of nanocrystalline samples. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors present their work on investigating the local deformation mechanisms and microstructure influences during in
situ TEM deformation of a Au thin film using a scanning diffraction-based approach. The results are interesting and the
technique development is very impressive. The approach provides insight into the elastic-stage deformation of Au thin films
that is not accessible using other techniques and the results broadly provide insight into the deformation of nanocrystalline
materials. I have only a few minor issues with the paper: 
The reported trends in the paper linking different quantities such as grain rotation and Von Mises stress are extremely weak.
I would not rely on an R^2 value of 0.345 to prove the authors' hypothesis about the influence of strain fields on grain
rotation. The correlation is very tenuous at best. This is true of most of the correlations that the authors discuss. 
All of the evidence of dislocation activity presented by the authors is indirect. Do the authors have direct evidence of
dislocation activity/stored dislocations in the pre-strained films (either from TEM imaging accompanying the 4D STEM
analysis or from virtual BF/DF images)? This would significantly strengthen their arguments. 
Overall, this is a strong paper that provides new insight into the deformation of nanocrystalline materials and introduces a
new combination of techniques that will be useful in future applications. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author):

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

The submitted manuscript outlines the results and analysis following an experiment combining precession-assisted ACOM
with in-situ straining of a nanocrystalline Au film. The main findings/claims of the authors are that grain rotation was
observed in the 'elastic regime' of loading and that elastic strains are partitioned heterogeneously across grains for the entire
duration of loading. As the authors outline, the combination of in-situ loading and ACOM to obtain full-field 2D orientation
and strain maps has been shown in previously published papers to detailed characterization of deformation mechanisms. 
The authors claim to have observed "strong grain rotations up to more than 6° occur already in the elastic regime" (138) and
reference similar observations in published studies. I have a few questions regarding this claim and suggestions for
additional descriptions/figures to assist in interpreting the results. 
1) Typically grain rotation has been observed to occur simultaneously with grain boundary motion. This was the case for the
Mompiou and Legros paper cited in the manuscript and is well described in the broader literature (see Thomas et al Nat
Comm 2017 as a good starting point). Firstly, how did the authors identify the location of grain boundaries, and were the
locations tracked between straining steps? I ask primarily because some of the grain boundaries look very
intriguing/surprising including the quadruple junctions among grains 5/6/10/11 and 9/11/13/14. These are atypical of other
sputtered fcc microstructures I've seen and I think are noteworthy. If no GB migration was observed, how does this contrast
with previous descriptions of rotation/migration being a coupled behavior for similar materials. I think a more lengthy
description of this in the discussion is warranted. 
2) Grain rotation is measured by turning the three Euler rotation angles into a single rotation angle around a specific vector,
but not much more information regarding this is provided. Is the same direction/vector used for each pixel/grain? Figures
2g/k/o/s show the scalar 'rotation angle' but no sense for which angle the rotation is tending toward. In the text, for example in
line 131, specific directions of rotation are described, but this is impossible to verify and be persuaded of with the provided
data. I highly recommend a presentation similar to the cited Mompiou and Legros paper where angle rotations are
represented via pole figures or, alternatively, Rottman and Hemker Acta Mat 2017 who represent grain rotations via IPF.
This would provide a detailed perspective of the sense of rotation of grains relative to one another. 
3) Lastly, the claim of elastic rotations more than 6° seems only to be true largely for grains that exhibit ambiguous
orientation solutions to their diffraction pattern (e.g. grains 3, 17, 18, 21, 22) as evidenced by the multiple orientation
solutions (i.e. colors) within each grain. I suggest an alternate indexing approach (e.g. dictionary indexing) that can better
distinguish between ambiguous 'solutions' or removing ambiguous grains from the overall data and revising quantitative
claims accordingly. 
The authors utilize their ACOM data to calculate elastic strains over the course of the loading experiment and observe some
trends associated with grain size etc (Figure 5). It is interesting that the authors observe grain rotation to occur during the
'elastic portion' of the straining yet also attribute grain rotation to dislocation motion and diffusion in the Discussion (253-
274). If the rotation measurements in the elastic regime are accurate (see above comments/questions), do the authors
believe that they are due to diffusive and dislocation-mediated processes? Some additional clarity in the discussion would
be appreciated. 
Lastly, while using Schmid factor and elastic modulus of grains to interpret elastic strain partitioning common, there has
been substantial development in our ability to understand how/why elastic strains are partitioned heterogeneously in
polycrystalline materials in recent years using x-ray techniques. See for example the work of Matt Miller's group
(https://doi.org/10.1177/0309324713492325). Perhaps the authors will find a review/comparison between their techniques
and x-ray methodologies developed elsewhere fruitful.

The manuscript addresses variations in local deformation of a nanocrystalline gold 
sample using 4D-STEM (2-D mapping of 2-D diffraction patterns). 
Thank you very much for the valuable feedback. Based on the given comments, the 
manuscript was improved in line with the stated suggestions. Below, all the provided 
comments are answered in detail. 
1. 4D-STEM for materials characterization is enjoying a great deal of interest. However, 
there are different ways in which the technique is implemented. The method used in the 
current study, namely precession enhanced electron diffraction (PED) for crystal 
orientation mapping (COM), has been in use for more than fifteen years and therefore 
it is neither new nor novel. PED mapping for determination of grain boundary character 
is also not new. Nor are the use of PED orientation mapping for elastic strain 
determination and the mapping of heterogenous strains new. 
We agree with the reviewer’s comment that PED has been extensively used for crystal 
orientation mapping in polycrystalline materials and has also proven to  enhance the 
precision of elastic strain determination in single-crystalline materials. The novel aspect 
of our study is the application of strain mapping algorithms to polycrystalline materials. 
Traditionally, strain mapping is limited to grains oriented along specific, low -index 
zone axes. Here, by harvesting locally resolved information on the crystal orientation 
acquired through AC OM, we can determine elastic strains across different grains 
independent of crystal orientation. The results obtained by this new method/algorithm 
allow for a more holistic picture of the deformation process  by providing nanoscale 
strain information. 
2. The number of grains in the sample studied is small (24 grains), and the study was 
not repeated with other samples. Hence, a great deal of the text is devoted to detailing 
the orientation of each grain and how these orientations change, such as by grain 
rotations, as the sample is strained in -situ. The mapping of the heterogeneity of strain 
in the grains and near the boundaries is interesting but not surprising. 
To address this comment, a second in -situ tensile test was performed on an Au film 
annealed at a  higher temperature. The annealing resulted in a drastic decrease of the 
initial dislocation density, which is directly shown via bright-field STEM images.  
3. Although the sample is strained in the elastic regime, the authors acknowledge that 
the residual strains present from the initial sample preparation (DC sputter deposition

and post deposition annealing) impact the deformation behavior. They then attribute 
this behavior to dislocations and their movement. The von Mises elastic strains are 
computed. However, the dislocation densities are not quantified, and, as a result, the 
detailed discussions and conclusions regarding correlations or lack therefore of are 
based on R -squared values that are in the range of 0.2 -0.6, and therefore fail to be 
convincing. 
The concern regarding the strength of correlation was addressed by performing a 
second tensile test on a higher annealed Au thin film. Annealing at 410 °C resulted in 
a microstructure with individual dislocations visible in the STEM image (in the 
supplemental information ). This second tensile test showed vastly different 
deformation characteristics , with pronounced deformation twinning and strongly 
reduced grain rotations up to around 2  °. These results provide proof for the claimed 
mechanism of grain rotation accompanied by the initial dislocations in the first tensile 
test. Furthermore, the dislocation densities were also quantified via GNDs as further 
discussed in the next point.

nanocrystalline samples. 
We thank the reviewer for pointing to the methodology of Leff et al. (Ultramicroscopy 
153, 9 (2015)) and fully agree that their Nye‑tensor‑based analysis, combined with PED 
mapping, is powerful for quantifying both GND and SSD densities when the probed 
volume contains a statistically representative ensemble of dislocations. We calculated 
GND densities and correlated them with the grain rotation, providing a more 
quantitative discussion. The results show that the claimed correlation between initial 
dislocation density and grain rotation is even stronger for this analysis.  However, it 
should also be pointed out that  in the present work , the effective spatial resolution of 
our PED‑based strain/orientation mapping is on the order of 2 nm . At this resolution, 
the probed volume frequently contains a single dislocation in these nanocrystalline Au 
grains. Under these conditions , the GND densit y becomes strongly dependent on the 
arbitrarily chosen averaging length.

To determine the correlations between grain rotation  and other parameters like GND, 
D-4, etc., a mean scaler value of orientation change is used. To obtain this value, not all 
orientations within each grain were used ; rather, a filtering technique was applied. A 
vector median approach was employed to arrive at a first mean orientation of the grain.

subsequently, a new mean orientation was determined. This extra step ensures that a 
few outliers/wrong indexations do not falsify the determined grain rotation.

We gratefully thank the reviewer for pointing to the work of Schuren et al. (The Journal 
of Strain Analysis for Engineering Design  49(1),33-50(2013)) on estimating the 
uncertainty in lattice strain measurements through X-ray techniques. They clearly show 
that the uncertainty in measured strain by this technique depends not only on the 
number of diffracting crystals but also on the strain variation between these crystals. 
Contrary to X-ray scattering, which provides an average over a large number of grains, 
4D-STEM provides nanoscale lattice information originating from within individual 
crystals. This directly rules out the origins of uncertainty studied by the Miller group, 
and enables us to analyze these strain fluctuations with nanometer resolution. With the 
methodology presented in this work, we are able to measure not only differences in 
lattice strain between di fferent grains but also strain variations within individual 
nanocrystals.
