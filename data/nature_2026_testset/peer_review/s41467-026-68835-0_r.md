experimental rigor, and conceptual novelty demonstrated in this work fully justifies publication in Nature Communications

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The reviewed paper focus on the development and characterization of a TiNi-based shape-memory alloy that exhibits
significant elastocaloric effects with quite good reproducibility during a very large number of cycles through its stress-
induced martensitic transformation. 
To design this material, the authors have synergically combined the two strategies recently proposed to improve
elastocaloric properties of shape-memory materials. The former is based on the growth of precipitates with suitable
morphology that permits smoothing out the elastic interaction between martensitic variants that gives rise to a significant
decrease of the transition hysteresis. The second is based on tailoring an appropriate texture causing the transition to
progress continuously as the applied stress increases. Overall, the paper is well written and well organized and the material
developed has been very well characterized using numerous experimental techniques. These results provide a good
understanding of the combined influence of both precipitates and texture on the transition properties. 
My negative viewpoint about the paper refers to the fact that due to the designed microstructure, the transition takes place
over a very wide range of stresses. The consequence of this fact is that a large stress must be applied to induce the entire
transition and take advantage of the whole transformation latent heat, which is at the origin of the large elastocaloric
response of the material. In the case of the present work a stress of 900 MPa is required. While this value is considered as a
{\it low driving stress} by the authors, a simple calculation indicates that for a sample of 12 mm^2 (as the one used in the
experiments) a mechanical force larger than 10^4 N (more than one tonne!) is required to induce the whole transformation. 
In
my opinion, this 
makes the material of little interest 
for practical applications of refrigeration. Therefore, although the material
designed in this work is interesting and indeed represents a step forward in the development of new elastocaloric materials,
it is still far from being suitable to be used efficiently in new refrigeration devices that can replace current ones based on
vapor compression technology. The authors should clearly indicate that much more efficient materials will be needed before
the elastocaloric effect can be used in commercial refrigeration applications. 
In many places along the text the material is specified with the formula TiNi_{51}. This could be understood as a material
with a composition such that for every 51 atoms of Ni there is 1 atom of Ti (which, as far as I know, is not a shape-memory
material). In fact, I suppose that the composition should be 49 at. % Ti and 51 at % Ni. Therefore, I suggest that 
instead of
TiNi_{51} the material be specified as Ti_{49}Ni_{51}. 
The authors indicate that the measured temperature decrease induced by adiabatic release of stress, increases from -21.8 K
to -15.1 K in the first 2 x10^6 cycles and only increases by 0.2 K in the next 8x10^6 cycles. While this is correct, a simple
inspection of Figure 3b suggests a continuous evolution with cycling that is faster than the one described by a logarithmic
law. Can this evolution be explained in terms of changes of the microstructure? 
In conclusion, I think that after a revision that takes into account the points raised above, the paper could 
deserve publication
in Nature Communications.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have corrected the terminology of continuous phase transformation. However, overall, the practical significance
of this work is limited as the material requires quite high stress (900 MPa). The so-claimed new transformation behaviour
and mechanism are in fact not distinct from traditional NiTi. The mechanistic interpretation on the cyclic stability remains
rather superficial and is just based on speculative claims. I still do not believe this manuscript reach a novelty and academic
depth for publication of nature communication. The key comments are as below: 
1. The authors still claim that the transformation mechanism in the textured Ti
₄₉
Ni
₅₁
 alloy differs in both macroscopic
manifestation and microscopic origin from those reported in conventional TiNi alloys. This is not true at all. Macroscopically
(in Fig. 2(c)), the textured Ti
₄₉
Ni
₅₁
 still exhibits a stress-strain curve typical of conventional NiTi under compression. The so-
claimed progressive transformation that proceeds over a wide stress interval (600 – 900 MPa) is not true, since the
transformation of conventional NiTi occurs in a similar stress interval under compression. The DIC images in Fig. 4 clearly
show inhomogeneous strain fields, which means macroscopically inhomogeneous transformation. The hysteresis of the
textured Ti
₄₉
Ni
₅₁
 is smaller, but this does not constitute a novelty. Microscopically, the heterogeneous martensite nucleation
at precipitates is well established both in experiment and in theory. To me, the only novelty is that the authors used Ti
₄
Ni
₂
O
precipitates that were rarely reported before. But this improvement is just incremental. 
2. The authors attributed the high cyclic stability to 4 points, which I believe is not convincing or new. I comment each point
below: 
(i) Variant confinement reduces interface length and mobility per event. 
The authors claimed that precipitate-regulated single-variant domains are shorter, more strongly pinned, and less mobile.
Each interface contributes less to plastic slip, reducing the overall dislocation multiplication rate. In my opinion, however,
although variant confinement reduces the length of each individual interface, it significant increases the total number of
interfaces or the total volume fraction of the interface, which may increase the total amount of dislocation accumulated. 
(ii) Precipitates suppress dislocation motion near interfaces. 
The precipitation strengthening is a well-documented mechanism. 
(iii) Distributed heterogeneous nucleation alleviates stress concentration. 
I highly doubt this interpretation. From microelasticitity theory of phase transformation (AG Khachaturyan, Theory of structural
transformations in solids, 2013), the martensite should exhibit the highest degree of stress concentration at the nucleation
stage (i.e. single variant stage). The stress concentration is minimized only when martensite develops a twinned structure at
the later growth up stage. This is because at the early stage, the interface energy dominates so that the single-variant
martensite adopts a sphere-like morphology to reduce the interface energy, at the cost of high elastic strain energy (thus high
stress concentration). At the later stage, the interface energy becomes less important, and the martensite will be self-twinned
to reduce the elastic strain energy (thus low stress concentration). The authors’ claim is contractionary to this classic theory
and is speculative in nature (no experiment evidence supports this). 
(iv) Early-cycle defect accumulation saturates, then stabilizes transformation. 
My comment: this defect saturation is a well-documented mechanism.

Reviewer #3:
The authors report a textured TiNi51 alloy that exhibits a large adiabatic temperature 
change of -15.9 K after 10 million compressive cycles. The alloy was fabricated using 
directional solidification and consists of highly textured columnar B2 grains and high-
density Ti₄Ni₂O precipitates. The authors claim that their alloy exhibits continuous and 
homogeneous phase transformation, different from conventional TiNi alloys showing 
strain localization and Lüders-like banding. This continuous transformation mechanism 
was interpreted as the origin of the observed good transformation reversibility and high 
fatigue life. In my opinion, although the reported “-15.9 K after 10 million compressive 
cycles” is interesting, the interpretations are rather superficial, full of inconsistence, 
errors and misuse of basic concepts. I do not think this paper merits publication in 
Nature Communication. Detailed comments are given below:
Authors’ response:
We thank the reviewer for the careful and critical reading of our manuscript and for the 
detailed comments. We regret that some of our original wording produced ambiguity or 
overstated inferences. We have carefully revised the manuscript to improve precision 
in terminology, to supply additional experimental evidence where requested, and to 
temper mechanistic claims so they exactly match the supporting data. Below we 
respond point-by-point to each of the reviewer’s concerns and list the exact changes 
made in the revised manuscript.
1. The authors claim that their textured TiNi51 alloy shows different transformation 
mechanism from conventional TiNi alloys. However, this is not true at all. Under 
compression mode, conventional NiTi alloy also shows homogeneous deformation at 
the macroscale (see C. Elibol et al., MSEA, 2015) and continuous changes of diffraction 
peak intensities at the microscale (see R. Vaidyanathan et al., Acta Materialia, 1999). 
The macroscopic strain localization of conventional NiTi alloy only appear under 
tension mode (see P. Sittner et al., JMPS, 2005: 1719-1746).
Authors’ response:
We appreciate this important observation. We fully agree that transformation behavior 
in NiTi is sensitive to loading mode and experimental conditions, and that some prior 
studies report relatively uniform macroscopic responses under compression (e.g., Elibol 
et al., MSEA, 2015; Sittner et al., JMPS, 2005). Nonetheless, several later studies have 
also documented strain localization under compression (Bimber et al., Mater. Sci. Eng. 
A, 674 (2016) 125–134; Baghbaderani et al., Proceedings of the ASME 2020 15th 
International Manufacturing Science and Engineering Conference, 2020, 
V001T01A019.), indicating that this phenomenon is not restricted to tension.

The authors report a textured TiNi51 alloy that exhibits a large adiabatic temperature change 
of -15.9 K after 10 million compressive cycles. The alloy was fabricated using directional 
solidification and consists of highly textured columnar B2 grains and high-density Ti₄Ni₂O 
precipitates. The authors claim that their alloy exhibits continuous and homogeneous phase 
transformation, different from conventional TiNi alloys showing strain localization and 
Lüders-like banding. This continuous transformation mechanism was interpreted as the origin 
of the observed good transformation reversibility and high fatigue life.  In my opinion, 
although the reported “-15.9 K after 10 million compressive cycles” is interesting, the 
interpretations are rather superficial, full of inconsistence, errors and misuse of basic 
concepts. I do not think this paper merits publication in Nature Communication. Detailed 
comments are given below:  
1. The authors claim that their textured TiNi51 alloy shows different transformation 
mechanism from conventional TiNi alloys. However, this is not true at all. Under 
compression mode, conventional NiTi alloy also shows homogeneous deformation at the 
macroscale (see C. Elibol et al., MSEA, 2015) and continuous changes of diffraction peak 
intensities at the microscale (see R. Vaidyanathan et al., Acta Materialia, 1999). The 
macroscopic strain localization of conventional NiTi alloy only appear under tension 
mode (see P. Sittner et al., JMPS, 2005: 1719-1746).  
 
2. The authors conceptually mixed the continuous phase transformation with the first-order 
phase transformation that proceeds through continuous growth/shrinkage of the product 
phase. By thermodynamic definition, continuous phase transformation refers to second 
(or higher) order phase transformation and involves no coexistence of two phases and no 
hysteresis. An example for continuous-type phase transformation of SMAs can be found 
in (H. Chen et al., Nature Materials, 2020). For the textured TiNi51 alloy, however, its 
phase transformation obviously involves two-phase coexistence, as evidenced by in-situ 
XRD and TEM results in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5. It cannot be called continuous phase 
transformation. Mixing these two basic concepts largely weakens the quality of the work. 
 
3. It is true that continuous (second-order) phase transformation is highly reversible due to 
absence of phase interfaces. However, since the transformation of the textured TiNi51 
alloy is indeed not continuous (second-order) one, it is very misleading to conclude that 
the alloy exhibits high reversibility (or cyclic stability) because it shows continuous phase 
transformation. This misleading conclusion should be removed throughout the paper.  
 
4. At Page 8, the authors claim that “Subsequent growth of the B19′ martensite is confined 
into a single-variant morphology (Fig. 5), as opposed to the conventional multi-twinned 
structure 61. This controlled nucleation and confined growth underpin the observed 
continuous SIMT, which is highly reversible and stable over ultra-high fatigue cycles.” I 
wonder how the controlled nucleation and confined growth of martensite is beneficial to 
cyclic stability?? It is well known that the cyclic degradation of NiTi originates from 
dislocation activities driven by the high internal stress near the phase interface (see 
D.M. Norfleet et al., Acta Mater., 2009; T. Simon, et al., Acta Mater., 2010). This high

internal stress is generated by the lattice misfit between the B2 and B19’ phases and 
should persist no matter whether the phase interfaces move in a controlled or uncontrolled 
manner. Thus, it is quite unclear to me how the controlled growth of martensite (i.e. 
controlled movement of the phase interface) can lead to high cyclic stability. 
 
5. At Page 8, the authors claim that “the present alloy exhibits a continuous transformation 
behavior characterized by uniform transformation strain (Fig. 4–5). This is enabled by 
homogeneous martensite nucleation at B2/Ti₄Ni₂O interfaces and regulated by interfacial 
strain fields.” However, the strain distribution in Fig. 4 is not uniform at all. There are 
noticeable strain variations across the sample. Moreover, the martensite nucleation of the 
sample is not homogeneous but heterogeneous, nothing different from conventional NiTi.  
Homogeneous nucleation refers to the nucleation aided by random thermal fluctuations. It 
usually occurs in solidification with small nucleation barriers but seldom in solid-state 
phase transformation involving lattice structural changes (see AG Khachaturyan, Theory 
of structural transformations in solids, 2013).  What the authors shown in Fig. 5 is in fact 
heterogeneous nucleation, which is a type of nucleation aided by structural defects or 
chemical inhomogeneity.   
 
 
6. At Page 8, the authors claim that the high fatigue life is due to a mutual texture, which 
acts as a "reinforced concrete" architecture in delaying crack initiation and propagation. 
However, in absence of the “reinforced concrete” architecture, conventional NiTi alloy 
can even survive more than 108 PT cycles (see D. Liang et al., Applied Materials Today, 
2022) under compression. It is generally believed that the main reason for the high fatigue 
life is the compressive loading mode, which results in a low stress intensity factor and 
thus a low crack growth rate. The inherent high fatigue life under compression is the main 
reason why this loading mode is widely used in elastocaloric cooling (see H. Hou et al., 
MRS Bulletin, 2018). If the authors insist "reinforced concrete" architecture is the 
primary cause of high fatigue life, they should conduct fatigue test on a sample without 
"reinforced concrete" architecture for comparison. 
 
7. There is inconsistency in the interpretation of the fatigue life. In the abstract, the high 
fatigue life is attributed to continuous phase transformation. In the main text (see 
comment 6), however, it is attributed to a mutual texture. It seems that the authors 
themselves are not sure of the exact reason for the high fatigue life.  
 
8. Why the textured TiNi51 exhibits better cyclic stability when compressed along SD than 
along the transverse direction? At page 7, the authors attribute this to the strengthening 
effect and the columnar morphology of the Ti₄Ni₂O precipitates. However, this 
interpretation is unsatisfactory. Do the precipitates provide stronger strengthening effect 
and consequently better cyclic stability along SD?? Experimental evidence for the 
anisotropy in the precipitation strengthening is lacking. In addition, the evidence for the 
columnar morphology in Fig. 1a is ambiguous. The elongated etch pits in Fig. 1a is more

9. At page 7, the author claims that the larger superelastic strain along the SD indicates a 
higher degree of stress-induced martensitic transformability, thereby amplifying the 
elastocaloric response. However, using the absolute strain level to quantify the 
transformation degree is inaccurate because even under 100% transformation, the strain 
along the SD and transverse direction may differ much. I suggest to use the ratio of the 
measured strain to theoretical averaged transformation strain instead. The theoretical 
averaged transformation strains of the sample for both the SD and transverse directions 
can be calculated under either Reuss or V oigt approximations by using the inverse pole 
figures for the two directions and the lattice-level transformation strain tensor. This 
calculation can be readily carried out using software packages such as MTEX in Matlab.
