Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript examines the postnatal development of the brain’s structural connectomes from birth to childhood and their
relationship with neurobiological signatures. By combining connectome mapping, machine learning, and neurobiological
analysis, the study provides novel insights into the maturation of the human brain’s structural topology. While the manuscript
is well-structured, several areas could benefit from refinement. Here are my comments, which I hope are useful to improve
the manuscript.  
Major comments: 
1. The Methods section is very concise. Since Nature Communications doesn’t impose word limits in the Methods section,
sufficient details should be provided in the manuscript rather than in the supplementary material. 
2. The sample size appears limited. While this study examined development from birth to 8 years of age, it included only 114
cross-sectional subjects in the primary analyses. Given the rapid pace of brain development during early infancy, whether
this sample size sufficiently captures true developmental trajectories remains uncertain. The study utilized BCP as a
validation set, but why wasn't BCP combined with existing data to enhance the sample size? Additionally, the authors might
consider incorporating the HCP-Development dataset to strengthen their findings. 
3. The fitting method for the major findings appears limited to linear and exponential models. Do these two predetermined
models adequately capture the developmental trajectories? Previous fMRI studies have frequently employed more complex
models including log-linear, quadratic, and cubic fittings. Considering recently proposed approaches like GAMLSS or GAM
models, what advantages justify retaining these conventional modeling approaches? 
4. In the Results section, the authors should provide basic methodological descriptions and references for key metrics. For
instance, the presentation of normalized local/global efficiency in the section "Global maturation of network efficiency and
targeted resilience in the structural connectome" is particularly confusing. I could not find any explanation of how these
normalized LE/GE measures were calculated, either in the Methods section or supplementary information. Additionally, the
normalized GE results appear to be missing from Figure 1B. 
5. The rationale for using the AAL-500 uniform-region atlas requires clarification. Regarding the sensitivity analysis, is "AAL-
80" a typographical error that should read "AAL-90"? While the results have been validated across two AAL-derived atlases,
the stability of findings should be further examined using alternative parcellation schemes to demonstrate robustness across
different anatomical definitions. And the usage of adult atlas in infants and children need to be further discussed. 
6. Additional methodological details should be provided for the late childhood network topology prediction analysis (Section:
"Heterogeneous maturation in communication efficiency between primary and transmodal association cortices"). Based on
the current description, I cannot assess whether the prediction results might be affected by data leakage. Specifically, if the
8-year data were included in the model fitting process, this could potentially bias the estimation of growth rates. 
7. Applying the concept of flexibility into structural connectomes is very novel. Again, more method details and references
should be provided. I guess this is still associated with the Multi-layer network analysis proposed by Mucha? 
8. The authors should consider including a visual representation of the integrated sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis in the
Results section titled "Regional topological growths are associated with multiscale neurobiological hierarchies along the
sensorimotor-association axis." 
Minor comments: 
1. The reporting of statistical p-values appears inconsistent throughout the manuscript, with some values labeled as raw p-

values (?) and others as FDR-corrected. For improved clarity and scientific rigor, the authors should maintain consistent
labeling of all statistical values, clearly indicating whether reported p-values represent raw or corrected values. 
2. How were the targeted hubs determined in the Results section titled "Global maturation of network efficiency and targeted
resilience in the structural connectome"? 
3. When estimation the LE/GE, any threshold was applied in the connecomes matrices? 
4. In the Discussion section, it would be valuable to compare and contrast the developmental patterns observed in these
structural connectomes with existing findings on functional connectome development. Highlighting both the convergences
and divergences between structural and functional network maturation could provide important insights into the brain's
developmental organization.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper the authors perform a series of analyses to explore structural network topology in a small cross-sectional
sample (n=114) aged between 2 months and 8 years. Structural networks are constructed using diffusion MRI, defined using
deterministic tractography between 500 cortical nodes. Overall, the authors find that measures of network topology (different
forms of efficiency, small-worldness, modularity) develop over time differently in primary sensory cortex compared to higher
order association cortex. As noted in the Introduction, this follows the well-established hierarchical maturation of cerebral
white matter. As expected, primary/association differences correlate with several other measures of cortical structure and
function that vary along the same axis. The authors demonstrate that network topology changes over time are dependent on
FA and RD of the underlying white matter tracts. The main results largely replicate findings that have been described
elsewhere (e.g.: Hagmann, PNAS, 2010; Khundrakpam, Cerebral Cortex, 2012; Wierenga, Human Brain Mapping, 2017;
Chen, Frontiers Human Neuroscience, 2013; Yap, PLoS ONE, 2011; Huang, Cerebral Cortex, 2013; Baum, Current Biology,
2017), though with an age range beginning close to birth. Many related analyses are presented, with a multitude of different
cross-validation schemes (2F-CV, LOOCV, 5-fold stratified CV) and often without sufficient justification, resulting in
somewhat diminishing returns. Several sensitivity analyses are a welcome addition, including assessment of head motion
(low as participants were sedated for scans), different parcellation schemes and, importantly, replication of many of the core
findings (across a partial age range) in an independent, longitudinal cohort with difference acquisition and processing. 
Specific comments are listed below: 
1) The authors refer to changes in network efficiency as ‘growth rates’ which seems like a misnomer. Consider using
‘change’ and/or ‘rate of change’ instead. Similarly, descriptions of changes in efficiency as ‘exponential growth’ are
misleading; while formed from an exponential model, the term ‘exponential increase’ commonly refers to a continuing
increase in growth rate with age, rather than a decrease in growth rate as is observed here. Finally, the authors should
consider including tables to show the model terms for each node/region. 
2) Figures 1 and 2 focus on changes in node efficiency over time, revealing differences in growth rate between sensory and
association areas. It would be helpful to illustrate node efficiency at different time points as well (this is shown underlying the
hub analysis in Figure 3, but is not referred to in the corresponding text). It is important to understand not just the growth rates
but also the final state of node efficiency. Is node efficiency higher or lower in association areas at birth, or in childhood?
Based on the two plots, efficiency is much higher in association areas to start with? Does this imply maturation is faster in
association areas compared to sensory? How does that align with knowns patterns of white matter development? 
3) After calculating rates of change in efficiency across network node, the authors fit an SVR model to predict final node
efficiency from rate of change. They partition nodes into two sets: association (with mostly nonlinear change) and sensory
(within mostly linear). The data are arranged into a node x age matrix (though it is not clear how many age points are
defined) and used to predict efficiency at 8 year. This model doesn’t work well in the primary sensory nodes, probably
because the rates are derived from a linear model, so will be equal for all age points. It’s difficult to see the added value of
this analysis. 
4) The authors include network sparsity and brain size as covariates in their models to correct for the large differences in
head size across the cohort. Are the effects of these variables on the nodal metrics linear? 
5) The replication in the BCP is a nice demonstration that differences between primary sensory and association cortex are
reliably detected. In Fig S11, the authors should consider only plotting the overlapping age ranges (0-2.5y) for borth cohorts. 
6) On line 122: “in contrast with the stable normalized global efficiency (Figure 1B).” Does this statement mean that global
efficiency does not increase over age when normalised against random networks? If not, the authors should consider
reporting all metrics normalised in this way to remove artefactual differences due to network construction/sparsity. 
7) Line 129: “(e.g., global efficiency: become only 1/9 of the max rate after the age of 3.4 years)”. Please clarify this
statement. 
8) In Fig 2A: Change in efficiency are shown for two regions (SOG and SFGdor.R). I assume these refer to anatomical
regions? How are they mapped onto the 500 node subdivision? Which nodes in the brain images are shown in the plots?

9) The sliding window analysis takes an unorthodox approach, averaging 15 subjects together, then removing the youngest,
adding the next oldest and repeating. This might make more sense if the distribution over ages was uniform, which it is not.
There are very few scans between 3 and 5, meaning these windows contain a much wider age range that at either end of the
distribution. This time window also aligns with major transitions shown in Fig S6. Can the authors comment on this effect?
Similarly, can the authors justify the inclusion of the clustering analysis in Fig 3C and S3? Presumably, clustering a

“Materials and Methods 
Paediatric participants and data acquisition. The primary dataset comprised two subsets. Subset 
1 included 114 typically developing children (50 males and 64 females; mean age: 3.31± 2.41

acquisition details are provided in SI-1. The replication dataset included two subsets with 
longitudinal scans (the BCP subset and the EBDS-2 subset), details can be found in SI-4.6.  
Diffusion MRI preprocessing and network reconstruction. For the single-shell dMRI images 
(the primary Subset 1 and the replication EBDS-2 subset), image processing included eddy 
current and small head motion correction, tensor fitting and estimation of diffusion tensor 
imaging-derived metrics (i.e., FA and diffusivities). The structural network was built with fibres 
reconstructed from brute-force deterministic tractography 1 as the network edges and with 500 
similar-sized cortical regions subdivided from the AAL atlas as network nodes. We defined the 
number of streamlines between two regions as the edge weight. For the multi-shell dMRI images 
(the primary Subset 2 and the replication BCP subset), a different WM connectome 
reconstruction pipeline was employed. We first used the preprocessing in QSIprep, which 
included EPI distortion correction, eddy current correction and head motion correction2. Then, a 
set of age-specific brain templates3 were employed to warp brain parcellations into individual 
brain. For fibre reconstruction, we employed the iFOD2 probabilistic tracking algorithm with 
anatomical constraints (ACT) and estimated streamline weights using the command tcksift2 2. A 
scaling coefficient (μ-factor) was estimated to normalize the total contribution of streamline 
weights into the FOD-derived fibre density4. Further details are provided in SI-2. 
Graph theoretical modelling. Global and regional network metrics were quantified via graph 
theoretical modelling. For the global metrics, we calculated the global efficiency (Eglob), local 
efficiency (Eloc), small-world parameters (λ, γ and σ) and network robustness 5. For a network 
graph, the global efficiency measures the parallel information transfer capacity by calculating the 
average of the reciprocals of the shortest paths. The local efficiency represents how efficient the 
communication is among the first neighbours. The small-world parameters include three indices. 
The normalized global efficiency (λ) and normalized local efficiency (γ) measure the ratio 
between real network efficiency and the mean of that in 100 matched random networks. These 
two measurements can be summarized into a quantitative metric, small-worldness,𝜎 = 𝛾/𝜆, 
which is typically greater than 1 for small-world networks 6,7. Thus, a small-world network not 
only has a higher local interconnectivity, but also has an approximately equivalent shortest path 
length compared with random networks. For network robustness, we assessed how the size of the 
largest connected component (LCC) of the network changed as nodes were progressively 
removed, either randomly (random failures) or by descending nodal strength (targeted attacks). 
The robustness was further defined as the area under the resulting LCC–node removal curve, 
with a larger area indicating greater network resilience. 
For the regional metrics, we quantified the regional efficiency, regional hubs and regional 
module. Notably, the developmental reconfiguration of hubs and modular distribution was 
quantified using a sliding window design 8 . Specifically, to prevent site effects, we first sorted 
114 children (Subset 1 of the Discovery Dataset) in increasing age order and defined the first 15 
children as the first subgroup and the mean age within the subgroup. Next, we generate the 
remaining 99 subgroups by continuously replacing the youngest child within the group with the 
child outside the group. Network hubs were defined as the top 10% of nodes in terms of nodal 
efficiency separately for each age subgroup. A common module affiliation of each node across 
all age subgroups was identified by a multilayer module detection approach 9. Specifically, we 
linked each pair of neighbouring age subgroups to create a multilayer network with an intralayer 
resolution (γ) of 1 and an interlayer strength (ω) of 0.5. These two parameters were determined

by evaluating the balance between sensitivity to age-related changes and module reliability. 
Then, the heuristic Louvain algorithm 10 was used for the whole multilayer network. Finally, we 
calculated the modular flexibility of each node by tracking the change in module affiliations over 
sliding windows for all nodes (More details are provided in SI-2.4.).  
Mapping the developmental curves of the network metrics. To fit the differential growth curves 
of all network metrics, both linear and saturating exponential models were tested, with sex and 
brain size included as covariates. We used the AIC 11 and selected the model with the smallest 
AIC value as the optimal model. The rate of change for a given network metric was defined as 
the differential coefficient of the optimal fitting curve. We further defined the critical growth 
phase of each node by identifying the age at which the nodal efficiency reached 8/9 of its final 
value. The mean rate of change during the critical growth phase was calculated as the 
representative growth rate of each node. Of note, considering the sensitivity of the fitting models 
for measuring rates of change in nodal efficiency, we validated the curve fitting with a 
generalized additive model (GAM). Thin plate regression splines were used for the smoothing 
basis, and the residual estimates of the maximum likelihood (REML) method were used to 
estimate the smoothing parameter. More details are provided in SI-3.1. 
Predicting regional maturation. Support vector regression (SVR) with a linear kernel was 
performed with a two-fold cross-validation (2F‒CV) strategy. We set the fitted nodal efficiency 
at 8 years as the prediction vector (𝑁𝑟𝑒𝑔𝑖𝑜𝑛𝑠 ×  1) and used the rates of change in node efficiency 
at each age point (uniformly sampled at 100 time points between 0 and 7.5 years) as features 
(that is, the matrix 𝑁𝑟𝑒𝑔𝑖𝑜𝑛𝑠 ×  𝑁𝑎𝑔𝑒 𝑝𝑜𝑖𝑛𝑡𝑠 ). To avoid data leakage, we re-estimated the growth 
curves after removing individuals older than age 7.5. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient 
between the predicted values and the observed nodal efficiency of the testing samples was 
reported as the prediction accuracy. Notably, brain nodes were separated into two classes 
(association: including AC1, AC2, and INS; and primary: PM, PS, PSS, and LIM), and we 
performed prediction analyses within each class. We identified the contribution weights by 
estimating the prediction accuracy of age-specific subfeatures in an additional LOOCV analysis. 
Further details are provided in SI-3.2. 
Neuron-map analysis on the relationships between nodal topological maturation and 
multiscale neurobiological hierarchies. We adopted 11 cortical feature maps which represent 
diverse fundamental hierarchies of the brain 12. These maps include anatomical hierarchies 
(cortical myelination map, cortical thickness map and externopyramidization), functional 
hierarchies (functional gradient map and neuro-synth map), evolution-related hierarchies

sensorimotor‒association axis maps. They represent diverse fundamental hierarchies of the brain

primary and association cortices. The neuro-map tool 13 was further employed to estimate their 
spatial correlations with the nodal topological maturation maps by transforming the volume-
based features into surface space. The significance levels of these spatial correlations were 
corrected using “spin test” null models. Further details are provided in SI-3.4.

R: We sincerely thank the reviewer for raising this important point regarding sample size and for 
providing us with the opportunity to improve the quality of our work.  
In our original manuscript, we used the BCP cohort as an independent validation set (Replication 
Dataset 1) rather than combining it with our primary Chinese cohort (Subset 1 of the Discovery 
Dataset) for developmental curve fitting. This approach was based on two considerations. First, 
using an independent dataset for validation allows for a more robust replication of our main 
findings. Second, the BCP dataset contains dMRI scans only up to 2.5 years of age. 
Incorporating such a limited age range into our developmental curve fitting across entire 0–8 
year span could systematically bias the estimation of rate of change at pivotal age points14,15. 
Regarding the HCP-D dataset, it includes only a very small number (17 individuals) of dMRI 
scans from children aged 5 to 8-year, which is why it was not included in our original analysis. 
Nonetheless, we agree with the reviewer that a larger sample size is essential for robustly 
estimating white matter network development. To address this concern, we have incorporated 
two new datasets in this revised manuscript.  
(i) A new Chinese pediatric cohort (Subset 2 of the Discovery Dataset): This dataset 
includes high quality, multi-shell dMRI scans from 107 children (53 males, 54 females; mean 
age: 4.36± 1.78 years; range: 0.58–7.1 years), with relatively uniform age sampling across the 
range of 0.58–7.1 years old. This dataset included the following scan parameters: number of 
diffusion weighting directions = 8, 16, 32, 64, b=500, 1000, 2000, 3000 s/mm2 , two opposite 
phase-encoding directions, 1.7 mm isotropic resolution. Two analyses were further performed: a) 
fitting curves by combining the original 0–8-year-old cohort and the new cohort (Main results); 
b) fitting curves independently in the new dataset. Of note, to minimize potential multicenter 
effects in the combined fitting analysis, we included only the b=1000 volumes for white matter 
network construction and modeled the site label as the random effect in the statistic model. In 
both analyses, the results consistently support our original conclusions. We have updated the 
main results by the combined fitting analysis throughout the manuscript accordingly. The 
independent fitting of Subset 1 and Subset 2 were arranged into the cross-validation section in 
the supplementary materials. 
(ii) A new Replication Dataset 2: We additionally used a publicly available longitudinal 
dataset of 97 children aged 0 -6 years (EBDS-2)16 to make a further independent validation. 
Although this dataset only contains sparse age sampling points (only 5 separate integer age

Meanwhile, we also want to clarify the aim of normalization by random network models for 
global topological attributes. Randomly rewired networks preserve the original sparsity and 
network degree distribution but randomly shuffle edges between nodes. Normalizing the 
global/local efficiency of the real network by these random networks provides a measure of 
whether the whole network is optimized in information integration/segregation as a small-world 
organization6,7: a network not only has a higher local interconnectivity, but also has an 
approximately equivalent shortest path length compared with random networks. Such 
normalization approaches are rarely used for nodal measurements. Because the random rewired 
edges for an individual node fluctuate substantially and are thus unstable6,7. We have added this 
interpretation into the Method section as follows. 
“[lines 649-654] 
The normalized global efficiency (λ) and normalized local efficiency (γ) measure the ratio 
between real network efficiency and the mean of that in 100 matched random networks. These 
two measurements can be summarized into a quantitative metric, small-worldness,𝜎 = 𝛾/𝜆, 
which is typically greater than 1 for small-world networks 6,7. Thus, a small-world network not 
only has a higher local interconnectivity, but also has an approximately equivalent shortest path 
length compared with random networks.”
