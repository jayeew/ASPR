Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work reports a significant breakthrough in the synthesis of high-nuclearity titanium metal-organic polyhedra (Ti-MOPs).
The authors have not only synthesized a record-high nuclearity Ti-MOP but, more importantly, unveiled a novel 'pathway
intervention' strategy with broad implications for the field of supramolecular assembly. I think this is an excellent and
impactful work that can be published in Nature Communications after minor revision. 
1. The authors propose a compelling mechanism where the differential kinetics between Ti4+ and Ni2+ ions control the
assembly pathway. To strengthen the generalizability of this concept, could the authors comment on the selection criteria for
the kinetic trap metal ion? Specifically, what is the rationale for choosing Ni2+ over other moderately labile ions (e.g., Co or
Cu)? 
2. The authors state that the sample was activated at 30 °C under vacuum to evaluate porosity. Please provide the
experimental basis for the selection of activation temperature. Comparative gas sorption data should be included to
substantiate that 30 °C is optimal for achieving complete activation and maximum porosity, as opposed to higher
temperatures, which may induce pore collapse or degradation. 
3. Please double-check the consistency of compound labels (e.g., FIR-151, FIR-152, FIR-153) and the corresponding
intermediate numbers (Intermediate 1 and Intermediate 2) throughout all figures and the main text for clarity.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports the synthesis, structural characterization, and further functionalization of a Ti24-based metal-organic
polyhedron (MOP), designated as FIR-151. A notable strength of this work lies in the intriguing structure of Ti24-MOP, which
resembles that of the well-known molecular "tennis ball"—a hydrogen-bonded capsule. The authors also make a valuable
contribution by clearly demonstrating that non-coordinating additives can exert a profound influence on the assembly
structures during synthesis; leveraging these additives, they successfully captured two intermediates and proposed a
logically reasonable assembly mechanism for FIR-151. Furthermore, the experimental data provided comprehensively
characterize the material’s physicochemical properties, and the manuscript is generally well-organized. However, several
issues requiring revision were identified. The manuscript is recommended for publication in Nature Communications after
addressing the following suggestions. 
1. Page 4, line 82, the authors describe FIR-151 crystals as “yellow rhombic dodecahedral” and cite Supplementary Fig. 1 in
support. However, this Supplementary Fig. 1 (an SEM image) only exhibits the morphology (rhombic dodecahedral shape)
of the crystals, not their color (yellow). In fact, the photograph in Fig. 5 clearly shows the FIR-151 crystals with the perfect
yellow rhombic dodecahedral appearance, making it the more appropriate reference for the authors’ description.
Additionally, the caption of Supplementary Fig. 1 contains a minor error: the term “images” should be revised to “image” (as
the figure only includes one SEM image).

(Remarks to the Author) 
This study stands as a perfect example of how to combine structural innovation with profound mechanistic elucidation. The
successful synthesis of the Ti24 MOP with truncated octahedron topology is itself a remarkable achievement, given the

modification further enhances the practical impact of these findings. Therefore, I would like to recommend the manuscript for
publication in Nature Communications, after addressing the following revisions. 
Comment 1. Was the 'pathway intervention' strategy attempted with other transition metal ions? If so, please summarize the
outcomes in the supplementary information. Were similar intermediates observed, or did other metals lead to different
products or no modulation at all? 
Comment 2. It is crucial to confirm the structural integrity of Ti24 MOP after gas sorption. Please provide the PXRD data of
FIR-151 after gas adsorption in the supplementary information will strongly demonstrate its stability for potential applications.
Comment 3. In Fig. 5b-d, please clearly indicate in the figure captions that the non-covalent interactions between MOPs
(such as hydrogen bonds or π-π interactions) are highlighted with green dotted lines. This clarification is crucial for guiding
readers in interpreting crystal stacking diagrams. 
Comment 4. In Supplementary Tables S1 and S3, please provide the complete chemical names for all ligand abbreviations
to ensure clarity and thereby enhance the accessibility and utility of the presented data.

Response to Reviewer #1 
This work reports a significant breakthrough in the synthesis of high-nuclearity titanium metal-
organic polyhedra (Ti-MOPs). The authors have not only synthesized a record-high nuclearity 
Ti-MOP but, more importantly, unveiled a novel 'pathway intervention' strategy with broad 
implications for the field of supramolecular assembly. I think this is an excellent and impactful 
work that can be published in Nature Communications after minor revision. 
1. The authors propose a compelling mechanism where the differential kinetics between Ti 4+ 
and Ni2+ ions control the assembly pathway. To strengthen the generalizability of this concept, 
could the authors comment on the selection criteria for the kinetic trap metal ion? Specifically, 
what is the rationale for choosing Ni2+ over other moderately labile ions (e.g., Co or Cu)? 
Responses: The reviewer raises a fundamental point regarding our choice of metal ion, which 
allows us to elaborate on the design principles behind the kinetic trap. The choice of Ni 2+ was 
indeed deliberate, based on its unique positioning in what we might call the "coordination 
lability landscape". 
While seve ral borderline acids like Co 2+ or Cu 2+ could in principle interact with the 
squarate ligand, our working hypothesis was that Ni 2+ would provide the optimal kinetic 
profile—its ligand exchange rate is slow enough to form metastable complexes that can be 
crystallized, yet sufficiently dynamic to allow incorporation during the Ti 4+-driven assembly. 
This creates the perfect conditions for intervention without a complete pathway shutdown. 
The control experiments the reviewer might be wondering about strongly sup port this 
reasoning. When we substituted Cu2+ or Co2+ under otherwise identical conditions, the reaction 
proceeded directly to the final Ti24 MOP without any observable intermediate trapping. These 
"negative" results are actually quite telling: 
With Co2+, we believe its faster ligand exchange kinetics prevent the formation of a stable 
enough trap to halt the assembly process. For Cu 2+, while it shares the borderline acid 
classification, its tendency toward Jahn -Teller distortion leads to preferred geometrie s that 
appear incompatible with the specific coordination environments required in our intermediate 
structures. 
So in essence, Ni 2+ emerged as the unique candidate that provided just the right 
combination of moderate lability, geometric flexibility, and binding affinity to make the kinetic 
trapping strategy work. This understanding gives us a valuable set of criteria to consider when 
extending this approach to other metal-organic systems. 
 
2. The authors state that the sample was activated at 30 °C under vacuum to evaluate porosity. 
Please provide the experimental basis for the selection of activation temperature. Comparative 
gas sorpt ion data should be included to substantiate that 30 °C is optimal for achieving 
complete activation and maximum porosity, as opposed to higher temperatures, which may 
induce pore collapse or degradation. 
Responses: Thank you for the reviewers' suggestions. The selection of 30 °C as the activation 
temperature for FIR-151 is not random. We screened different activation temperatures from 30 
to 120 °C and found that as the activation temperature increased, the adsorption uptakes 
gradually decreased, possibly du e to pore collapse caused by higher temperatures 
(Supplementary Figure S24a). Similarly, we also compared the TGA results of as-synthesized, 
after CH3CN exchange, and activated at 30 °C for FIR-151 (Supplementary Figure S24b). This 
comparison further confi rmed that 30 °C is the optimal activation temperature for this

compound.

activation temperatures. (b) TG curves of as -synthesized, after CH 3CN exchanged, and 
activated (30 °C) FIR-151. 
 
3. Please double-check the consistency of compound labels (e.g., FIR-151, FIR-152, FIR-153) 
and the corresponding intermediate numbers (Intermediate 1 and Intermediate 2) throughout 
all figures and the main text for clarity. 
Responses: Thanks very much for the kind suggestions. We have carefully reviewed and 
unified all compound labels and the corresponding intermediate numbers across the text and

> In regards to all characterization, I would like to highlight that the presence of proton signals

Response: We thank the reviewer for raising these important points. Both the purity and the  
solution-phase behavior of FIR -154 and FIR-155 have been re -examined through re-analysis 
of the original batches, additional purification, and measurements under solvent conditions 
representative of our experimental workflow. 
(1) Impurity signals in the original ¹H NMR spectra 
We agree that the earlier spectra displayed weak resonances assignable to residual carboxylic 
acids and acetate. These species originate from surface -adsorbed or lattice -included guests 
associated with the ligand -exchange synthesis, rather than from the MOP framework itself. 
This interpretation aligns with the single -crystal XRD data, which shows complete ligand 
exchange at the cage surface. 
To isolate the framework signals exclusively, the original batches were subjected to a strict 
multi-cycle washing protocol using anhydrous CH ₃CN, a non -coordinating solvent that 
removes guest molecules without perturbing the coordination structure. 
The updated ¹H NMR spectra (Supplementary Figs. 39–40) show complete disappearance of 
all previously noted impurity peaks while all framework resonances remain unchanged. No 
new signals appear after purification. This confirms that the earlier features originated from

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 31 -->

removable guest species rather than structural defects, as the framework signatures are 
unaffected by the washing process. 
(2) Signals 4 and 5 and solution-phase behavior 
The reviewer’s observation regarding signals 4 and 5 is well taken. Our re -analysis indicates 
that their appearance in the initial spectra was caused by the use of pure DMSO -d₆, a strongly 
coordinating solvent capable of inducing minor, reversible perturbations in metal–carboxylate 
environments. Such perturbations do not involve irreversible ligand dissociation. 
To assess the species present under conditions relevant to our study, we re-recorded the spectra 
in CD ₃CN containing only minimal DMSO -d₆ for solubility —the solvent environment 
employed in our synthesis, crystallization, and SAXS experiments. 
In this representative solvent system, signals 4 and 5 are absent. Only the resonances expected 
for the intact cages are observed, and no signals corresponding to free ligand or decomposition 
products are detected. 
These observations show that: 
 The features observed in pure DMSO arise from solvent -induced coordination 
perturbations rather than structural dissociation. 
 Under all chemically relevant conditions used in this work, the MOPs display 
spectroscopic signatures fully consistent with intact cages. 
 No evidence for ligand liberation or framework decomposition was detected under any 
of the conditions relevant to our workflow. 
Therefore, the new results support the conclusions that: 
 FIR-154 and FIR -155 are structurally pure; the initial impurity peaks were due to 
removable guest species. 
 The cages exhibit stable, intact spectroscop ic signatures in the CD ₃CN/DMSO-d₆ 
environment central to synthesis and characterization. 
 The earlier concerning signals were artifacts specific to pure DMSO and not 
representative of the materials’ behavior under the experimental conditions used in this 
study. 
The revised manuscript includes the updated spectra and corresponding clarifications. We 
appreciate the reviewer’s comments, which led to a clearer and more rigorous characterization.
