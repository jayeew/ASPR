Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Panah et al describe the results of a feasibility study of fecal microbial transplant in 18 Adults with
Anorexia Nervosa. This was an open label, non-randomized trial and demonstrates the feasibility of a larger FMT trial. 
This was a single treatment study with two stools samples. 
While 80% of enrolled subjects completed the trial, the 4
dropouts failed to provide the second stool sample. In a larger placebo control trial, would the design be the same, a single
treatment with two stool collections? (although the stool collection is less important than clinical measures as an outcome).
Outside of C difficile treatment, most FMT studies have more frequent treatments, and more treatment usually correlates with
better outcomes. 
If a larger trial design would involve more treatments, does this feasibility study address dropout and
adverse events that would be observed in such a trial? 
From the description of the metagenomic analysis, the microbiome data was limited to the analysis of metagenome
assembled genomes (MAGs). The following comments are based on the methods used as understood from the methods as
described. 
An important missing detail is the thresholds of completeness and contamination that were used to define MAGS
as this does vary between studies. 
It seems that the taxonomic analysis was limited to the species and coverage of the
MAGs and this is significant limitation. 
Many genomes are present in metagenomic data that are not present at sufficient
depth to assemble MAGs and these would be considered absent from those samples in the methods as described. 
Using a
short read based taxonomy (such as MetaPhlAn4) are more sensitive and would be an improvement by the MAG based
approach. 
Regardless species level analysis (particularly with shallow profiling such as MAGs as described) are insufficient
to predict engraftment and the authors should avoid conclusions about this. 
The authors should also not conclude presence
and absence of specific taxa based on MAG analysis. 
Prokka is no longer a recommended annotation tool for microbial genomes or MAGs. Even the developer of the software
recommends not using it. Bakta or RAST would be two common alternatives that will generally give better gene prediction
and annotation. 
The functional analysis, it did appear that the data was restricted to MAGs. If this was done on all the assembled data it
should be clearly stated and if there was a contig size threshold applied (to minimize artifacts due to partial genes). Using
clustering approaches to define functional groups (such a linclst) does reduce the ability to distinguish engraftment. The
clustering thresholds are critical to understand the analysis but this was not mentioned. 
As these are set by the user, it is not
sufficient to reference previous the software or paper, and these values should be included in the methods. If as described,
all of the analysis was based on MAG data, it means most of the sequenced data is unused. 
Since there was no obvious clinical response (unlikely from a single FMT and short term follow-up) the definition of
responders and “likelihood of FMT success” are based primarily in the Bray -Curtis distances. 
The fact that some patient
microbiomes become more donor-like is not evidence of engraftment (and none of the subjects get that close to the donor). A
good control for Fig 2D would be to plot the distance between the two donors and the distance between the two subjects (w0
and w1) to the donor they did not receive. 
If the change in the microbiome reflects improve transit time (and consequently
changes in the microbiome towards a more “healthy one” this distance might be donor “independent”. 
The results in Fig 1 are quite striking (although self reported). 
This might be a proxy for clinical response and it would be
interesting to stratify the subjects for comparing microbiome data and other measured variables (fig 4). Ofcourse we do not
know if this may represent normal weekly fluctuations in this patient population or a placebo response to choosing to be in
the trial. 
For a small study with no control group and the chosen analysis approached restricted to MAGs, the focus on the

microbiome is not justified and the conclusions are overstated. 
Minor comments 
There were do details of donor selection and screening for pathogens which is standard in the field. 
This is not mention in
the paper or in the Study Protocol provided in the supplementary material. 
Figure 2A-C, this is a very small data set for violin plots and not very useful here (these are useful for seeing distributions in
large datasets). 
With the subject samples (w0, w1) the individual data points are larger obscured and there is no real
structure. 
The change in the Bristol Stool scores reporter in Fig 1 was one of the only ‘clinical’ findings. Considering that the Bristol
Stool Index was a self reported, a more quantitative measure stool consistency would have been useful. The authors could
consider measuring water content of the samples (not an onerous task for a small study). 
In the limitations, the authors could comment on the possibility of a placebo effect. 
This is common in disorders of the gut-
brain axis and other eating disorders, although not to my knowledge for AN. 
Because this is open label, and the patients are
self selected, this could be a confounding factor. 
The quality of supplementary figures are low and the font very small, the figures could be made larger and more legible. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper “Investigating the Impact of a Single Fecal Microbiome Transplantation 
to 18 Adults with Anorexia Nervosa - A Feasibility Study” reports a series of single dose FMT in individuals with anorexia
nervosa exploring both the efficacy of the transfer and some data on psychological outcomes (although they correctly state
that they were not powered to see differences). 
This paper adds to a growing body of literature related to the gut microbiota (GM) in anorexia nervosa and here a case series
of FMT delivered via frozen capsule or enema (patient’s choice). Overall, the authors demonstrate feasibility and
acceptability with patients generally choosing frozen capsule administration and an 81% completion rate. I find myself
wishing that they had just taken a few extra steps to make this a more definitive feasibility study. As conducted several
important questions remain unanswered and unanswerable with the data they have collected. 
Although the paper is billed as a feasibility study, the authors give one sentence of feasibility-type information on line 241-
243 (“Most participants preferred oral FMT, completed all study requirements, and were satisfied with administration method;
those using capsules found them easy to swallow and were open to future doses”), however, no additional feasibility data
were provided. 
Inclusion and exclusion criteria are not entirely clear from the paper. The protocol is somewhat helpful, but this information
needs to be clearer in the manuscript. They state: 
"69 volunteers expressed interest in participating. Of these, 19 were excluded based on a pre-interview assessing age, sex,
and current BMI. Fifty completed the EDI-3 and our participation questionnaire and further 28, were not included as they did
not meet our inclusion criteria (BMI, age, probiotics, antibiotics, laxative abuse, intercurrent disease)." 
I would like to see how many individuals were excluded for each reason. 
Presenting the study as a feasibility study can explain why they opted for a single FMT administration. However, it seems
like a disappointing missed opportunity not to have collected subsequent stool samples to see how long the observed
changes lasted. In fact there is no commentary on the importance of determining how long any changes toward donor
composition last. In the absence of any subsequent measurements, the actual value of the single fecal transfer is unclear. 
I am also unclear why they collected the second fecal sample a week later and then completed questionnaires at one-month
follow-up. 
They used the Bristol Stool Scale to self-report stool consistency and transit time. Although use of the Bristol Stool Scale is
adequate for estimating stool consistency, it is an imperfect measure of gut transit time. Are the authors aware of any
literature validating it against more conventional gastric transit time measures? If not, this should be listed as a weakness (in
addition to the general weakness of relying on self-report). 
No information on selection of donors was provided in the manuscript. In the protocol there is additional information, but it is
not sufficient. They state: 
“Donor identification and FMT material: Through our established procedures and guidelines for FMT in C. diff treatments at
Hvidovre Hospital, we will, as before, recruit and screen donors via either via the voluntary blood donor system/ blood and
tissue transplant service or among already established donors at Hvidovre Hospital.” 
Without knowing their standard screening procedures, we do not know if the donors had histories of eating disorders or other
psychopathology that could affect GM. Donor selection should be mentioned in the article.

In addition it is unclear why Donor 1 was n=3 and Donor 2 was n=10). 
Figure 2A: the donor2 spread is quite massive. They describe a significant difference in chao1 between w0 and w1, but the
figure is not convincing. 
Plus why only plot Donor 2 in Figure 5? 
The authors report “notable trends” in their data, which if corrected for multiple comparisons would not at all be significant.
Could the authors please comment on the potential clinical significance of these trends. If they are not clinically significant,
then they should not be reported as trends. 
"Notable trends included a two-fold reduction in estradiol levels across participants, though this did not reach statistical
significance (OR = 0.51, CI: 0.26–1.01, p = 0.054). Insulin levels showed a modest 10% increase overall (OR = 1.1, CI: 1.00–
1.21, p = 0.047), while PYY levels in responders exhibited a significant increase (OR = 1.22, CI: 1.02–1.46, p = 0.037)." 
The authors highlight gmGUS as a key hypothesis in the introduction but then brush over it in the results (219-227). If
something is explicitly mentioned as a hypothesis, the results should also be explicitly described in text, not just relying on a
figure). 
Interesting that the authors mention: 
“Future studies should measure stool energy content, such as through bomb calorimetry, to determine if decreased transit
time leads to energy loss, especially in these sensitive patients.” 
Accordingly, I recommend that they cite a recent paper on this topic PMID: 39569450. 
Minor points: 
Abstract: It is odd that they chose body image distortion as a defining feature of AN as this is not required to be present for a
diagnosis. Later, in the text, they also refer to “disturbed body image,” which is an odd phrase. 
Spelling error: Bristol Stoll (Stool) and line 37 of introduction starts with a misplaced “Of”.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I have been asked to assess whether the authors have adequately dealt with reviewer #1's comments. 
I commend the authors for largely addressing the technical issues raised, however there are several points that have not ben
adequately addressed. 
1. The authors still overstate the reported changes in stool consistency. On P4 the authors state "The change reflects a
significant decrease in estimated gut transit time...", which is simply incorrect when fecal moisture content ( a more objective
measure) did not change. 
2. As already stated the term responders should not be used based upon Bray Curtis and not clinical response. It is
misleading and incorrect to use responders in this way. I suggest you consider instead :greater similarity to donor
microbiomes". 
3. The authors state that "these numbers (of participants) suggest good interest, when they do not. Recruiting 22 subjects in
9 months suggests that any larger RCT will likely need to includer multiple centers to achieve adequate recruitment.
Recruitment of anorexia nervosa trial patients for clinical trials has previously been found to be challenging. 
4. Whilst the supplementary figures labels have been made more readable, all of the figure labels in the manuscript are
almost unreadable. Of note Figure 4 X axis labels run over each other and are completely unreadable. The X and Y axes of
Fig 4 should be symmetrical. As presented there is an illusion created of a far steeper gradient of the regression lines than
should exist.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
- Although the authors added in a few feasibility metrics, they still excuse the small sample size as a "feasibility" matter
without reporting much on data. More specifically, they report the following in the discussion without presenting any data
elsewhere: "Most participants preferred oral FMT, completed all study requirements, and were satisfied with the

administration method; those using capsules found them easy to swallow and were open to future doses". If the authors are
going to claim this is a feasibility study, it must be treated as such in structure. The readers cannot be expected to take their
word for it. 
- There is no evidence of engraftment. 
- The authors use of the term "responder" is specific to being "a reduction of at least 1.5 standard deviations in Bray-Curtis
dissimilarity metrics". I worry that the use of responders later in the manuscript, for example in the blood samples results
section, can misdirect a reader to forget the absolute definition of the term. 
- The most striking findings are in Figure 1, but this is all based on self-report. this limitation must be highlighted further. It is
additionally complicated by the fact that there were no changes in fecal moisture content (in which the authors also omit
freeze/thaw/storage protocols that may impact these results)
