Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments to the Authors: 
The manuscript by Xing et al. investigates the reasons behind the varying objective response rates and pathological
complete response rates observed in breast cancer patients treated with either Paclitaxel (PTX) or Nab-Paclitaxel (Nab-
PTX). The authors employ a multi-faceted approach, including meta-analysis of patient data, immunostaining of patient-
derived breast cancers, RNA sequencing, several mouse models of metastasis, and in vitro co-culture assays. They found
that a TREM2+ macrophage population was upregulated in patient and mouse tumors upon treatment with PTX, but not with
Nab-PTX. The authors build upon previous work by other groups and, using genetic and gene silencing techniques,
demonstrate that decreased expression of TREM2 in mice decreases tumor growth and metastasis. Mechanistically, the
authors find that PTX increases the expression of FGF2 in tumor cells which, in turn, signals to macrophages to activate
ERK1/2 signaling, increasing the expression of the transcription factor EGR1, and driving TREM2 expression. Using a
cytokine array of macrophage conditioned media, the authors find TREM2+ macrophages differentially upregulate Serpin
E1, which they speculate could drive tumor cell metastasis. 
This study is consistent with many previous reports of TREM2+ macrophages/tumor tissue correlating with poor prognosis in
patients and functionally increasing tumor growth and decreasing overall survival in mouse studies. The study nicely
extends the authors’ previous work, demonstrating that PTX and Nab-PTX treatments cause distinct immune cell alterations
in the tumor microenvironment and provides an explanation for the differences in patient outcomes. While many sections are
difficult to read, the study is interesting and could have therapeutic value. In general, the study is well-executed, with
complementary approaches used to test their hypotheses. However, several gaps need to be addressed to completely
substantiate their conclusions regarding the signaling between the tumor cells and macrophages and the mechanism of how
TREM2+ macrophages drive tumor cell metastasis. Moreover, larger patient cohorts and multivariate analyses need to be
used to make definitive conclusions about the relationship of TREM2+ macrophages and metastases in patients. 
Major Concerns: 
1. The authors use Trem2 directed ASOs to knockdown expression of TREM2 in vivo as a second model. While the use of a
second model is appreciated, the authors must confirm knockdown of Trem2 in vivo, within the macrophages of the tumor.
The authors have shown FACS analysis to identify a TREM2+F4/80+CD11b+ population in tumors in Figure 2N, which
could be used to confirm knockdown. 
2. The authors should confirm the PTX-driven signaling between tumor cells and macrophages that drives the increase in
the TREM2+ macrophage population (and increased metastasis) is FGF dependent. This is the major connection between
tumor cells and macrophages the authors propose is driving the PTX-induced metastasis. PTX treatment induces a wide
range of inflammatory signals and it is important to determine how much of the increased TREM2+ macrophage
compartment is due to FGF alone. In vivo the authors could orthotopically inject FGF2 knockdown tumor cells (or treat with
an FGF inhibitor) and treat with PTX. In vitro, the authors could use conditioned media from the PTX treated tumor cells and
supplement with or without an FGFR inhibitor (or knockdown FGF2 in tumor cells) when added to macrophages. 
3. The conditioned media experiments in Figure 7H-I should be repeated using the TREM2-/- macrophages. This
experiment is needed to confirm that PTX is mediating its pro-invasion/migration effects through TREM2 expressing
macrophages. 
4. A major finding of this study is that TREM2+/+ macrophages display increased expression of Serpin E1 compared to
TREM2-/- macrophages. The authors suggest that this could be the mechanism of how PTX induces increased metastasis
compared to Nab-PTX. It is difficult to understand the connection between TREM2 expression and Serpin E1. Is TREM2
driving the expression of Serpin E1? Are these proteins just co-expressed in inflammatory macrophages? The +/-TREM2

BMDMs used in the experiments in Figure 7 could represent different macrophage subtypes and therefore would have
differing expression of many genes beyond TREM2. Furthermore, PTX did not upregulate TREM2 expression in
macrophages in Figure 5A-C and the authors showed that FGF2 instead mediated the upregulation of TREM2. However,
PTX does upregulate Serpin E1 in macrophages (Figure S15A). This suggests that TREM2 and Serpin E1 expression may
not be related at all. Several experiments are needed to substantiate these conclusions. 
a. The authors should test whether PTX or Nab-PTX treatment upregulate Serpin E1 expression in macrophages and if this
upregulation is FGF dependent, in vivo. Is Serpin E1 upregulated in the RNAseq patient dataset from Figure 1C? 
b. The authors should test if macrophage expression of Serpin E1 is required for the PTX-driven pro-metastatic effects, in
vivo. 
c. The authors should test the connection between TREM2+ macrophages and Serpin E1 expression in response to PTX.
The authors could use the TREM2-/- mice and co-inject TREM2-/- Serpine1-overexpressing macrophages with tumor cells,
similar to the experiment in Figure 3I. 
5. Using only tissue from only 5 patients who develop metastasis and 5 who did not, to determine the association of
TREM2+ macrophages with metastasis is meaningless. This needs to be done on a much larger cohort. Additionally, the
authors should include multivariate analyses. 
6. More information should be provided regarding tumor and patients’ characteristics in Table S4 and S5. Some important
variables such as tumor subtype, body-mass index, and menopausal are missing. Further, in Table S4 it seems that all but
one patient received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Why was it important to include one patient who was treated in the
adjuvant setting? It is noted that the limitation regarding the number of patient samples used to determine the increase in
TREM2+ macrophages needs to be expanded in future studies. It would be also important to perform multivariable analyses
stratified by tumor subtype and including critical covariates such as tumor size and grade, number of positive lymph nodes,
patients age and body mass index to determine the true effect of PTX on TREM2+ macrophages. 
Minor Concerns: 
1. There are many unclear sentences throughout the paper, including the title of the paper, figure titles, and in the
introduction and results. A major editorial revision is required to improve the understanding. 
2. In all quantifications of staining, the authors describe changes in the proportion of cells, e.g. lines 153-155, “The PTX
group showed a significant increase in the proportion of detected TRME2+ macrophages in tumor sections … (Figures 1G,
1H, S5B, S5C).” This type of measurement throughout the paper should be better described. There is no description of how
tissues were analyzed in the methods or legends. In the matching Figure S5B, the y-axis is “Percentage,” is this a
percentage of all cells in the tissue? If so, the representative images are misleading, as the quantification in Figure S5B
shows ~30% of the cells should be CD68+, while the corresponding image in Figure 1G shows CD68+ cells to make up less
than 1% of the cell population. This is an issue throughout the manuscript. 
3. Many of the representative stained tissue sections (and the magnified portions) are not of high quality. It appears that
some are in necrotic areas, some are at the edge of the tissue where tissue-edge effects appear to have occurred (non-
specific staining), and some are in areas where the tissue appears to be torn. These areas should be excluded from any
staining quantification. Did the authors exclude these areas and how was this done? Examples in the main figures: 
a. Necrotic: 1G (Control), 1H (Control and Nab-PTX), 2H (DMSO) 
b. Tissue edge/torn tissue: 2H (PTX), 5R (PBS and Nab-PTX) 
4. In Figure 1D, the authors profile the different macrophage populations before and after patients were treated with PTX or
Nab-PTX. It is interesting that the macrophage profile for the pre-treated patients in the two groups look very different, when
ideally for experimental purposes they would have had similar macrophage profiles pre-treatment. The striking differences
hint that the “pre-PTX” patients were already predisposed to have a strong response to the chemotherapy insult, possibly
due to the expanded IL-1B population, while the patients in the “pre-Nab-PTX” group have no IL-1B population before
treatment. Do the authors know how the treatment of these patients was decided (PTX vs. Nab-PTX)? Perhaps the authors
could discuss these differences, as these differences also could explain why these patients perform worse with PTX, given
they may be more prone to strong responses to any chemotherapy insults to begin with. 
5. The H&E staining of the lungs used to measure metastasis throughout the manuscript appears to have no or extremely
faint hematoxylin staining and the lungs do not seem to be fixed properly. This makes it difficult to identify early-stage
metastases. In addition, the representative H&E-stained images are very blurry. Moreover, the authors should also show
lower magnification images so the reader can see the whole lung lobes. 
6. It is surprising that there are zero (and rarely one) metastases when the mice are treated with DMSO or PBS in the highly
metastatic models used (4T1, PyMT, Py8119). DMSO treated mice are shown to die starting at ~19 days and have 0%
survival after 30 days in Figure 4F. What are these mice dying from, if not metastatic lesions? 
7. Instead of “metastasis,” lines 300-301 should say Trem2+/+ BMDMs significantly promoted Py8119 cell “migration and
invasion” compared to Trem2-/- BMDMs in vitro, as the authors are not measuring metastasis in vitro. 
8. Is macrophage viability in the tumors dependent on TREM2 expression? This would be important to test as in other
inflammatory models (Yan Cui 2024 PMID 38849370), TREM2 deficiency promoted macrophage apoptosis. Depletion of
immunosuppressive macrophages is well known to decrease tumor growth and metastasis in breast cancer. If the knockout
or inhibitor of TREM2 is causing macrophage death, the authors should parse out the effects they are seeing that are from
macrophage depletion verses targeting TREM2 downstream signaling. 
9. The authors state that Serpin E1, HGF, CCL3, and CXCL2 are increased in the CM of TREM2+ BMDM in Figures 7J and
S15A. However, Figure S15A does not show a CM experiment with TREM2+ BMDMs, but instead shows that PTX only
increases the mRNA expression of Serpin E1 in RAW macrophages, and significantly decreases the expression of HGF,
CCL3, and CXCL2. Can the authors explain this finding? It also makes the experiments where HGF, CCL3, and CXCL2 are
added to tumor cells in Figure 7K-M (and their inclusion in the model figure 7N) irrelevant if TREM2+ macrophages aren’t
upregulating these factors in this system. 
10. Many experimental details are missing from the methods section. What antibodies were used? How were
immunofluorescence images analyzed? How was the in vivo imaging performed (looks to be an IVIS)? What drugs,
inhibitors, and cytokines were used, how were they reconstituted, and what were the concentrations used?

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigates why paclitaxel appears less effective than albumin-bound nab-paclitaxel in breast cancer,
focusing on the role of TREM2-expressing (immunosuppressive) tumor

associated macrophages (TAMs). First of all, they
perform a meta-analysis of ~6,000 patients showing significantly higher ORR and pCR with Nab-PTX versus PTX. Using
single-cell RNA-sequencing and IF on patient samples, the authors find that only PTX (and not Nab-PTX) increases the
proportion of TREM2+ TAMs in breast tumors. The authors used several mouse models to show that, in parallel with tumor
growth suppression, PTX treatment increases TREM2+ TAMs and promotes lung metastasis, compared to Nab-PTX. They
further performed functional experiments to demonstrate that TREM2 is required for this effect: in Trem2
⁻
/
⁻
 mice, PTX no
longer increases lung metastasis, and the co-injection of TREM2-competent macrophages with cancer cells (in Trem2
⁻
/
⁻
hosts) drives tumor metastasis, whereas Trem2
⁻
/
⁻
 macrophages do not. Mechanistically, the authors show that PTX-treated
tumor cells secrete FGF2 (via the ATF3 transcription factor), which activates ERK/EGR1 in TAMs to upregulate TREM2 and
secrete EMT-promoting cytokines, driving tumor cell migration and invasion. Finally, the authors show that therapeutic
targeting of TREM2 with antisense oligonucleotides against Trem2 combined with PTX reduces lung metastases and
improves survival in mice compared to PTX alone. 
This study provides an innovative mechanistic insight into how PTX may induce innate immune programming and
dissemination. The findings that PTX uniquely expands an immunosuppressive TREM2+ TAM population via an ATF3–
FGF2–EGR1 pathway and that this TAM population promotes EMT and lung metastases are original and mechanistically
detailed. The results were validated across multiple systems - patient samples (scRNA-seq and IF), mouse models (4T1,
Py8119, MMTV-PyMT), and in vitro mechanistic studies, using a comprehensive methodology (ATAC-seq, ChIP-qPCR,
cytokine arrays, functional assays). The work has some potential for clinical impact, suggesting that combining PTX with
TREM2-targeted agents could improve patient outcomes. 
That being said, we have several concerns and comments regarding this manuscript. 
Major: 
1) In the primary tumor from the mouse models, there was no significant difference between tumor growth curves on PTX
versus nab-PTX, so it seems that the key difference in the apparent superior efficacy of nab-PTX, as shown in this study, lies
only in the prevention of dissemination to the lung, rather than enhancing tumor kill. Authors should temper their claims
accordingly. 
2) In the meta-analysis, they interpret the better efficacy of nab-PTX based on 11 studies with ORR and 7 with pCR data.
Why is the pCR also superior for nab-PTX? Authors should discuss this. Also, there is considerable heterogeneity among
the 11 studies with ORR data (I²=70%). It is unclear whether all studies were directly comparable (e.g. early or advanced
stage, patient populations, lines of therapy, dosing). Maybe the authors should limit their meta-analysis to advanced setting
and PFS in patients with metastases. 
The authors specify that TREM2 expression in the tumor was correlated with worse OS in patients after chemotherapy
(N=144 vs 57), but they should give more details on this analysis and the cohort. 
3) Also, the authors should discuss why there was no significant difference in tumor growth between PTX and DMSO in the
TREM2-/- mice. Is TREM2 needed for PTX cytotoxicity? 
4) In the scRNAseq analysis of patient samples, the authors show increased expression of TREM2 after PTX treatment in
most TAM clusters, including those classically known as having pro-inflammatory, T-cell recruiting, and anti-tumor
phenotypes. However, no further results are provided regarding other changes in their phenotypes, transcriptional programs,
and cellular networks in parallel with TREM2 overexpression, post-treatment with PTX. It would be particularly interesting to
evaluate whether these macrophages have a reduced capacity to recruit T cells, present antigens, or undergo other changes
in the cross-talk with tumor and immune cell compartments. 
Also, do they influence differently the transcriptional program of
other cell types (CAFs, T cells, tumor cells, DC, mast cells) to induce other immune-suppressive or metastatic-promoting
programs? 
Without this information, the analysis of the role of PTX in the immune reprogramming remains largely incomplete. 
Moreover, the authors make reference in their discussion to their previous studies where they have found that PTX treatment
was associated with higher levels of suppressive TAMs and Tregs and reduced the expansion of CXCL13+ to compromise
the response to ICB. They conclude that TREM2 “plays a pivotal role” in response to checkpoint immunotherapy. However,
without an analysis of the impact of TREM2 overexpression on the TME beyond the promotion of EMT and lung metastasis,

it is difficult to assume its role in response to immunotherapy. 
Finally, the test (in Fig. 1E) was done comparing the expression of TREM2 across all macrophages. This is not correct, as
macrophages from a given patient are expected to be more similar than those from other patients. It may be that the effect is
due to a single patient. 
The test should be corrected to take the patient into account, for instance, by comparing the proportion of TREM2+ in each
patient. The critical point is that the experimental unit is the patient, not the cell. 
5) The analyses on patient samples are done on small numbers (although acknowledged as a limitation). Considering inter-
patient variability, the results should be validated in larger cohorts, and the single-cell comparisons between gene
expression in different groups should be done at the sample level (and not cell level) to exclude that the observed
differences are due to sample-specific characteristics. 
6) It’s not clear whether the authors searched or not for the presence of metastases in other organs in mice. 
7) Authors talk about TREM2 expression on tissue-resident macrophages (and indeed, it is overexpressed in FOLR2+ and
LYVE1 TAMs), but then they also talk about them being bone marrow derived and found in PB – discuss more, are these
TAMs resident and only found in the primary tumor or they can also circulate in PB? 
8) Not clear if in mouse models in scRNAseq TREM is also upregulated after PTX. It seems it is the case only in one cluster
of TAM – macrophage Fcrls, but again, at the cell level, and the p-values are not clear in all figures. Also, it’s not clear if the
p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons. 
9) Some comparisons show n=3 biological replicates and may benefit from power analysis justification for these
experiments. 
10) The explanation for why PTX but not Nab-PTX affects ATF3 chromatin accessibility remains incomplete. The albumin-
binding difference alone seems insufficient to explain such distinct epigenetic effects. Also, while the study explains PTX's
pro-metastatic effects, the protective mechanism of Nab-PTX beyond "lack of ATF3 activation" could be elaborated. 
11) While TREM2 ASO shows promise in mice, the path to clinical implementation needs discussion, including potential
combination with existing therapies and patient selection strategies. 
12) As the authors mentioned in their limitations, while the focus on FGF2 is well-justified, the potential contribution of other
upregulated cytokines (e.g., GDF15) may deserve brief exploration. 
13) Some figure legends lack details or have minor errors (e.g., fig S3, I suppose it should be “sensitivity analysis for pCR”,
not ORR). 
14) The manuscript is generally well-written, but a few corrections would help readability. Spelling errors (e.g. “pervious”
instead of “previous”, “the observed PTX-promoted of lung metastasis”) 
should be fixed in several places. Abbreviations like
“ASO” should be defined on first use. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Xing et al. provide a detailed molecular dissection of the differential effects of paclitaxel (PTX) and Nab-
paclitaxel (Nab-PTX) in breast cancer, focusing on the induction of TREM2
⁺
 macrophages and their role in lung metastasis.
The central claim is that PTX — but not Nab-PTX — activates the ATF3–FGF2 axis in tumor cells, leading to paracrine
activation of the ERK/EGR1–TREM2 pathway in macrophages and the establishment of a pro-metastatic immune
microenvironment. 
The experimental work is thorough, and the authors employ patient samples, meta-analysis, mouse models, single-cell
RNA-seq, and proteomic approaches. However, despite the amount of data, the study reinforces a clinical observation that is
already well accepted: Nab-paclitaxel performs better than paclitaxel in many breast cancer settings. The mechanistic
insight is interesting, but it is unclear whether these findings will impact future therapeutic strategies, as no gain-of-function
or additive strategy is suggested to improve Nab-PTX efficacy. The work focuses on why PTX is worse, not on how to make
anything better. 
Specific comments: 
• Figure 2N: The quantification of TREM2
⁺
 macrophages by flow cytometry lacks rigor. The gating strategy is unclear, and
no proper negative control (e.g., FMO or isotype) is shown. The increase is modest and may not reflect a biologically distinct
population. 
• Figure 2L: The lung metastatic burden in the PyMT model is surprisingly low. As this model is known to be highly
metastatic, a longer follow-up (beyond two weeks) would strengthen the conclusions and provide more informative
quantification. 
• Trem2 knockout data (Figure 3): The data convincingly show that PTX-induced metastasis is TREM2-dependent.

However, a key control is missing: whether Nab-PTX retains its efficacy in Trem2
⁻
/
⁻
 mice. This would clarify whether Nab-
PTX avoids the TREM2 axis altogether or whether it might still benefit from its inhibition. 
• Figure 5: The FGF2–TREM2 connection is supported by CM transfer and neutralization experiments, but additional
specificity controls would be valuable. For example, does FGF2 neutralization also reduce EGR1 induction in
macrophages? The link is plausible, but more direct data would reinforce the proposed cascade. 
• ATAC-seq analysis (Figure 6H): The observation that PTX increases chromatin accessibility at the ATF3 locus is
interesting but needs more context. Are these changes unique to ATF3, or do other stress-response genes also show
enhanced accessibility? Some comparative enrichment analysis could support the specificity of this finding. 
• Figure 7: The authors identify Serpin E1, HGF, CCL3 and CXCL2 as EMT-promoting cytokines downstream of TREM2
⁺
macrophages. It would be informative to know whether TREM2 expression correlates with EMT markers in clinical samples
— the multiplex IF shows a trend, but quantification is needed.

Point-by-Point Responses to Reviewer Comments 
We sincerely thank the reviewers for their insightful comments and constructive 
suggestions. In this revised revision, we have addressed all concerns raised by 
reviewers. A detailed, point-by-point response to reviewers’ comments is provided 
below.
Response to Reviewer 1
The manuscript by Xing et al. investigates the reasons behind the varying objective 
response rates and pathological complete response rates observed in breast cancer 
patients treated with either Paclitaxel (PTX) or Nab-Paclitaxel (Nab-PTX). The authors 
employ a multi-faceted approach, including meta-analysis of patient data, 
immunostaining of patient-derived breast cancers, RNA sequencing, several mouse 
models of metastasis, and in vitro  co-culture assays. They found that a TREM2 +
macrophage population was upregulated in patient and mouse tumors upon treatment 
with PTX, but not with Nab-PTX. The authors build upon previous work by other 
groups and, using genetic and gene silencing techniques, demonstrate that decreased 
expression of TREM2 in mice decreases tumor growth and metastasis. Mechanistically, 
the authors find that PTX increases the expression of FGF2 in tumor cells which, in 
turn, signals to macrophages to activate ERK1/2 signaling, increasing the expression of 
the transcription factor EGR1, and driving TREM2 expression. Using a cytokine array 
of macrophage conditioned media, the authors find TREM2 + macrophages 
differentially upregulate Serpin E1, which they speculate could drive tumor cell 
metastasis.
This study is consistent with many previous reports of TREM2 + macrophages/tumor 
tissue correlating with poor prognosis in patients and functionally increasing tumor 
growth and decreasing overall survival in mouse studies. The study nicely extends the 
authors’ previous work, demonstrating that PTX and Nab-PTX treatments cause distinct

immune cell alterations in the tumor microenvironment and provides an explanation for 
the differences in patient outcomes. While many sections are difficult to read, the study 
is interesting and could have therapeutic value. In general, the study is well-executed, 
with complementary approaches used to test their hypotheses. However, several gaps 
need to be addressed to completely substantiate their conclusions regarding the 
signaling between the tumor cells and macrophages and the mechanism of how 
TREM2+ macrophages drive tumor cell metastasis. Moreover, larger patient cohorts 
and multivariate analyses need to be used to make definitive conclusions about the 
relationship of TREM2+ macrophages and metastases in patients.
Major Concerns:
Comment 1.  The authors use Trem2 directed ASOs to knockdown expression of

Response to Reviewer 2
This manuscript investigates why paclitaxel appears less effective than albumin-bound 
nab-paclitaxel in breast cancer, focusing on the role of TREM2-expressing 
(immunosuppressive) tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs). First of all, they perform 
a meta-analysis of ~6,000 patients showing significantly higher ORR and pCR with 
Nab-PTX versus PTX. Using single-cell RNA-sequencing and IF on patient samples, 
the authors find that only PTX (and not Nab-PTX) increases the proportion of TREM2+
TAMs in breast tumors. The authors used several mouse models to show that, in parallel 
with tumor growth suppression, PTX treatment increases TREM2+ TAMs and promotes 
lung metastasis, compared to Nab-PTX. They further performed functional experiments 
to demonstrate that TREM2 is required for this effect: in Trem2⁻/⁻ mice, PTX no longer 
increases lung metastasis, and the co-injection of TREM2-competent macrophages with 
cancer cells (in Trem2⁻/⁻ hosts) drives tumor metastasis, whereas Trem2⁻/⁻ 
macrophages do not. Mechanistically, the authors show that PTX-treated tumor cells 
secrete FGF2 (via the ATF3 transcription factor), which activates ERK/EGR1 in TAMs 
to upregulate TREM2 and secrete EMT-promoting cytokines, driving tumor cell 
migration and invasion. Finally, the authors show that therapeutic targeting of TREM2 
with antisense oligonucleotides against Trem2 combined with PTX reduces lung 
metastases and improves survival in mice compared to PTX alone.
This study provides an innovative mechanistic insight into how PTX may induce innate 
immune programming and dissemination. The findings that PTX uniquely expands an 
immunosuppressive TREM2 + TAM population via an ATF3–FGF2–EGR1 pathway 
and that this TAM population promotes EMT and lung metastases are original and 
mechanistically detailed. The results were validated across multiple systems - patient 
samples (scRNA-seq and IF), mouse models (4T1, Py8119, MMTV-PyMT), and in 
vitro mechanistic studies, using a comprehensive methodology (ATAC-seq, ChIP-
qPCR, cytokine arrays, functional assays). The work has some potential for clinical 
impact, suggesting that combining PTX with TREM2-targeted agents could improve 
patient outcomes.
