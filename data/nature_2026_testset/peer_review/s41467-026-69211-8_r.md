activity and regioselectivity of a steroid-hydroxylating P450 enzyme (P450stri). The authors employed a multi-stage strategy:

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The article reports the hydroxylation of testosterone using a new self-sufficient P450 enzyme from a streptomyces ‘P450stri’.
The enzyme is a member of the well characterised CYP102 family. The enzyme was engineered to improve the selectivity of
testosterone hydroxylation to favor hydroxylation at C15. The mutation of a phenylalanine to an alanine enabled this reaction
using just the heme domain and hydrogen peroxide. This is in agreement with what has been reported previously for similar
CYP102 enzymes. 
Overall, it is a thorough piece of work using known strategies and methods. Most of the work is well done and presented.
The work is well referenced, and these are up to date. It is on the whole well written, but some aspects need clarification
before publication. My major comment on the outcomes would be that the authors focus on the high peroxide stability of this
enzyme compared to others. This is interesting but doesn’t translate into improved levels of steroid product per enzyme
molecule (total turnover numbers appro 175-250 compared to >1000 already reported for other P450 steroid peroxygenase
systems). Noteworthy results are the high stability of the enzyme to peroxide and the good selectivity for 15-hydroxysteroid
formation. There is a lot of useful data but impact may be limited by the availability of whole cell systems to do effective
hydroxylation and cofactor regeneration and the low turnover numbers of the peroxygenase versus other reported systems
and low number of steroids used in the study.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study focuses on the engineering of a newly discovered cytochrome P450 enzyme, P450stri from Streptomyces
triculaminicus, into a peroxygenase for the selective hydroxylation of steroids. The researchers aimed to overcome
limitations of current P450 peroxygenases, such as poor H2O2 tolerance, to create a cost-effective biocatalytic system for
generating pharmaceutically valuable steroid compounds. 
The study identified P450stri as a novel self-sufficient P450 with native steroid hydroxylation activity, capable of converting
testosterone into three hydroxylated products (C-15β, C-16β, and C-2β) using NADH. However, similar to other P450s, its
application was hindered by reliance on the expensive NAD(P)H cofactor. To address this, P450stri was engineered into a
peroxygenase (M1) by mutations(F88A) and M4 by F88A/L27F, enabling it to utilize cost-effective H2O2 as an oxidant. 
The manuscript showed an enzyme engineering for practical applications in steroid modification. However, plenty of such
bioengineering works have already been published (for different P450 members), and therefore, I am not sure whether it is
novel enough to be published in Nature Communications. My other concern is that mechanistic insights are based on the
Alphafold models, which provide a poor confidence score for the Metalloenzymes. In fact, in Alpha fold reconstructed
models, the position of heme-porphyrin is never predicted. Therefore, structural information based on such poor facts is only
speculative. Authors must provide experimental structures (Crystal structure) for a mechanistic insight. The MD simulations
are not up to mark and seem to be using incorrect models. My other concerns are as follows: 
1) On Page 8, line 157, the authors say that the Alphafold was used to generate the 3D structure of the mutant, and then
molecular docking with the substrate was performed to study the selectivity. Furthermore, the authors claim that after the
mutation, M1, the distance of the C15 from the heme center decreased from 12 to 7 angs. Such a claim is preliminary and
may be inaccurate for two reasons: 1) Alphafold is not recommended for the metalloenzyme, and therefore, the confidence
score of an Alphafold-generated structure may be incomplete. 2) Molecular docking is not a tool for conformational study;
conformational study must be discussed after the MD simulations. For the second case, I see the authors have performed the
MD simulations, but on page 8, claims are made based on docking, and therefore, it is inaccurate. 
2) Figure 3B, the distances are misleading. Figure caption says that distances are from the TST and heme center. Note that
heme center ( center of mass of heme) may not be the heme iron and substrate's center of mass may not be the C15.
Therefore, such a distance plot is completely misleading. 
3) Figure 3 C and D. How does the enhanced water population play a role in peroxidase activity? It is well known that during
peroxidase activity, catalytic cycles don't need 2e and 2 protons as in the mono-oxygenase. In such a case, the role of water
becomes less important. Therefore, I am curious to know the mechanism if water populations increase. 
4) It seems that the authors have performed all MD simulations in the resting state. This is an inaccurate approach. In the
presence of the H2O2 ( peroxide shunt pathways), H2O2 coordinates with the distal heme iron, forming Fe-H2O2, which
ultimately converted to Cpd I. Therefore, MD simulation must be performed in Heme-Fe-H2O2. The parameterization of
heme-H2O2 should be done properly, which is not done here. 
Since most of the following results are based on incorrect computational model (that is the resting state without H2O2
coordinated to Heme-iron), I am not sure about their validity.

1. For the response to Comment 3 of Reviewer 1, I still cannot understand the significance of the experiment to examine the
CO binding ability of M1 in the presence of hydrogen peroxide. From the revised manusript, it seems that the author intends

monooxygenases and peroxidases (please carefully review the literature). Secondly, the experimental itself also has its
shortcomings. In the absence of substrate, heme will be bleached by hydrogen peroxide. It is likely that this situation leads to
the inability to detect the binding of CO.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
The article reports the hydroxylation of testosterone using a new self -sufficient P450 
enzyme from a streptomyces ‘P450stri’. The enzyme is a member of the well 
characterized CYP102 family. The enzyme was engineered to improve the selectivity 
of testosterone hydroxylation to favor hydroxylation at C15. The mutation of a 
phenylalanine to an alanine enabled this reaction using just the heme domain and 
hydrogen peroxide. This is in agreement with what has been reported previously for 
similar CYP102 enzymes.  
Overall, it is a thorough piece of work using known strategies and methods. Most of 
the work is well done and presented. The work is well referenced, and these are up to 
date. It is on the whole well written, but some aspects need clarification before 
publication. 
My major comment on the outcomes would be that the authors focus on the high 
peroxide stability of this enzyme compared to others. This is interesting but doesn’t 
translate into improved levels of steroid product per enzyme molecule (total turnover 
numbers appro. 175-250 compared to >1000 already reported for other P450 steroid 
peroxygenase systems). Noteworthy results are the high stability of the enzyme to 
peroxide and the good selectivity for 15 -hydroxysteroid formation. There is a lot of 
useful data but impact may be limited by the availability of whole cell systems to do 
effective hydroxylation and cofactor regeneration and the low turnover numbers of the 
peroxygenase versus other reported systems and low number of steroids used in the 
study.
