Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors studied the co-expression patterns and mutation burden of various cell clusters with advancing age in human.
They have used two datasets of scRNA seq data to perform these analyses and validated it using computational methods
and data from bulk RNA seq. Understanding the effect of age is crucial to studying the advancement of multiple lung
diseases in human. There has been a constant effort to understand the role of cellular senescence in this mechanism and
the current manuscript is another step in that direction. Use of scRNA sequencing (scRNA seq) data to determine the
mutational burden is a unique way of utilizing the information obtained through scRNA seq, but has its weaknesses as
pointed out by the authors. In general, the study is a good attempt at unravelling some interesting effects of age on the lung
tissue, but the scientific implications of these findings are not discussed in the manuscript, which is a major drawback in
interpretation of the results. 
Overall, the study provides a rich, multi-omic framework for understanding cellular aging in the human lung. Its strengths lie
in the comprehensive, high-resolution analysis of cellular aging and somatic mutations, as well as the novel findings related
to AT2 cells and capillary endothelial cells. However, there are challenges linked to the limitations of single-cell RNA-seq,
incomplete mutation data, and inconsistencies in senescence gene expression, which suggest areas for further investigation
and validation. To improve the study, the authors should consider integrating temporal dynamics, exploring a broader range
of lung cell types, and refining mutation-calling methods. These enhancements could provide a more robust understanding
of lung aging and its associated diseases, offering valuable insights into the complex biological processes underlying lung
aging. 
This reviewer has identified the following shortcomings in the manuscript 
Major Comments: 
1. The authors provide little to no information with regards to the clinical characteristics of the donor lungs. Various
publications have suggested that the biological age of an individual is drastically varied than the chronological age.
Furthermore, sex-based differences are expected to be observed in the tissue aging process. It will thus be important to
include some details about the biological age of the study subjects with discussion about the sex-based changes observed
with aging. 
2. The study does not fully explore the temporal and spatial dynamics of lung aging. A more detailed analysis of how aging
progresses across different lung regions (e.g., upper vs. lower lung) or over multiple time points would provide deeper
insights into the spatial and temporal aspects of lung aging. This would enhance the understanding of how aging unfolds in
different areas of the lung and across its complex tissue architecture. The authors should consider incorporating such an
analysis or addressing this aspect in the manuscript. 
3. The study finds no significant correlation between senescence markers and age, which contradicts some previous
studies. Given the central role of cellular senescence in aging, the authors should provide more context for these findings
and discuss potential reasons for the discrepancy. Further exploration of alternative cellular aging mechanisms and a more
detailed investigation into cell-specific senescence pathways could help clarify the role of senescence in lung aging. It
would also be valuable for the authors to analyze the single-cell data to investigate whether different senescence pathways
are activated in distinct lung cell types, as suggested by the observed heterogeneity in their findings. 
4. The type of cells identified from single cell analyses vastly depend upon the region of the lung being sampled. Do the
authors have information about the lung regions used for this study, the duration of cryopreservation and whether the lungs
were perfused before cryopreservation. All these factors may affect the transcriptional make up of individual cell types and

needs to be discussed in the manuscript. 
5. It is not clear to this reviewer how scRNA seq data could be validated through bulk sequenced data. A more detailed
explanation of these comparisons in the Methods section would be important to make better conclusions. The authors
should explicitly address the inherent limitations of single-cell RNA sequencing, such as sparsity and biases in transcript
coverage. Although the authors validate the findings with bulk RNA-seq, a more detailed discussion of how these biases
might affect the accuracy of gene expression profiling in specific cell types could provide greater transparency. 
6. The method used to call somatic mutations from single-cell RNA-seq data should be reconsidered. Due to the lower
sequencing depth of RNA-seq compared to DNA sequencing, there is an increased risk of false negatives or inaccurate
mutation detection. To address this limitation, incorporating DNA sequencing would help confirm somatic mutations and
validate findings across a broader range of cell types, especially those with lower representation in the dataset. The study
would benefit from discussing this limitation and considering additional strategies to improve mutation detection accuracy.
The authors show that the mutational burden is highest amongst gCap and AT2 cells. Could it be possible that this is due to
higher proportions of these cells identified through scRNA analyses. What is the %FDR for the findings. Are these mutations
leading to loss of function, or are they linked to specific molecular pathways that influence lung health and disease? An
analysis of mutation hotspots and functional consequences would add depth to the findings. 
7. The study mentions that lung aging is dyssynchronous, with specific cell types (e.g., AT2 cells and capillary endothelial
cells) undergoing more pronounced changes. However, the study could delve deeper into how aging may vary not just
across cell types, but also across individuals. Factors like environmental exposures, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle
might contribute to the observed heterogeneity, and this should be discussed more thoroughly. 
8. The observation of two distinct AT2 subpopulations based on the SPC expression is interesting and has been supported
by study done by various groups previously. The authors show that aged lungs have a higher proportion of Spclow AT2 cells
which are believed to be responsible for regenerative and proliferative properties of alveolar epithelium. This seems quite
contradictory considering repair mechanisms in the lung epithelium are usually affected with age. 
9. While the study provides valuable insights into AT2 cells and capillary cells, other lung cell types could be briefly included
in the analysis, particularly those involved in immune response or fibroblasts, which play important roles in aging and fibrotic
diseases. The inclusion of additional cell types might reveal a more comprehensive picture of lung aging and the role of
different cell populations. 
10. A more detailed discussion of statistical methods and sensitivity analysis would be helpful to ensure that the observed
differences are statistically significant and not due to technical artifacts. For example, how robust are the identified aging
signatures across different methods of normalization or analysis? A more thorough sensitivity analysis could ensure the
reliability of the findings. 
11. While the study measures transcriptional entropy and noise as a proxy for aging, more detailed explanations of how
these metrics relate to the functional loss of lung cells would be useful. What are the specific consequences of increased
transcriptional noise and entropy for lung function, and how might these metrics be used to predict age-related lung
diseases? 
12. The study could highlight the role of inflammatory genes in their dataset in the context of aging, especially given the
crucial role of inflammation in age-related diseases. Incorporating the potential involvement of the senescence-associated
secretory phenotype (SASP) or changes in immune cells during aging could provide additional insights. The authors should
clearly state what the single-cell dataset revealed in terms of inflammatory genes in both young and aged datasets,
identifying which key genes were significantly expressed. This would enhance the understanding of the aging process and
its links to inflammation, providing a more comprehensive view of the molecular mechanisms involved. 
Minor Comments 
1. This study does not fully explore the role of epigenetic changes and environmental factors (e.g., smoking, pollution) in the
aging process. The authors could strengthen the study by incorporating an analysis of how these factors may influence the
observed transcriptional changes. Additionally, investigating whether specific environmental exposures could accelerate or
modify aging signatures in lung cells would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the aging process and its
interaction with external influences. 
2. The method used to identify senescence signatures in this study relied on previously published gene lists. However,
these lists may not fully reflect the cell-type-specific mechanisms of cellular senescence in the aged human lung. The
authors should clarify the limitations of relying on these pre-existing gene lists and discuss how they might expand or refine
their senescence gene sets to better capture lung-specific senescence mechanisms. 
3. Figure 2C: The top and bottom panels are not very clear. The color bar is not shown in the figure. 
4. Figure 3A: The data plotted on the Y-axis is actual count, normalized count or overall expression of these genes in the
lung tissue. It will help if the scale of gene expression in the figures is uniform. 
5. Figure 5E: the correlation coefficient must be included in the graph. 
6. Figure 6A: What is the color coding for UMAP at the right. 
7. Figure 3G: scale bar is missing. 
8. Manuscript needs to be revised for grammatical errors.

(Remarks to the Author) 
De Man et al. integrated their own and publicy available single-cell RNA sequencing data to create a human aging lung

atlas, capturing patterns of transcriptional changes and mutation accumulation. Using both bulk and single-cell data, they
find that aging affects cell types heterogeneously and suggest that these differential effects may be stochastically driven.
Overall, this paper provides an interesting set of novel molecular data on the aging human lung, which could provide a
valuable resource. However, while overall well-written, it is not always clear. This is especially true for the abstract, which is
unfocused and unclear, even for a specialized audience. For example, what is exactly meant by “dyssynchronous”? Different
lung cell types? Different cells within one lung cell type? In the abstract, the main conclusions drown in too many features,
none of which are clarified very well. Things become clearer in the results section. I suggest re-writing the abstract.
Throughout the manuscript the statistical significance of the findings is often not clear, for example, the results in Fig. 3E, F
and H. I suggest a separate statistics section in Methods. I have the following specific comments. 
Comments 
• The authors do not consistently define young vs. aged throughout the paper (for example, Fig. 1A). The only section where
these terms were defined was in the “Immunohistochemistry” methods section. Maybe a supplementary table with the
subjects would be useful. 
• Including cells from individuals aged 10-20 may skew results due to developmental effects, as observed differential
expression may be due to a ceasing of development rather than a progression of aging. 
• On page 9, line 8, the authors report a somatic mutation burden of 93.6 mutations/MB in AT1 cells, 79.1 mutations/MB in
alveolar macrophages, and 62.1 mutations/MB in general capillary cells. This is extremely high and much higher than
reported by others, i.e., typically less than 1 SNV per Mb. Due to transcription-coupled repair the SNV frequency the authors
found should be even lower than that. Their reported mutation rates may be a typo because the log mutation burden in Fig
5A and Supplemental Figure 4A suggest a much lower mutation rate. 
• The authors also analyzed the somatic mutation data from bulk RNA (GTEx study by Garcia-Nieto et al). The authors
should mention that these are clonally amplified somatic mutations, so not the same as what they analyze at the single-cell
level. 
• The fact that there are genes whose expression correlates with a high mutation burden could suggest a regulated response
to the number of mutations. However, this is difficult to imagine due to the stochastic nature of somatic mutations. Instead, it
seems more likely that the observed differentially expressed genes are a response to the source of high mutation burden,
possibly higher exposure to mutagens in their environment. This is likely because the authors look at mutation burden in
individual samples rather than individual cells and because we see an upregulation of DNA damage repair genes in high
mutation burden samples. 
• It’s unclear if increasing entropy was defined as decreasing KL divergence from a uniform distribution. 
• The authors state that increasing transcriptional entropy measures transcriptional stochasticity or dysregulation.
Decreasing KL divergence of the observed vs. a uniform distribution only indicates that the expression of genes is becoming
more uniform, which can be driven by either coordinated or stochastic forces. More work may be necessary to prove that the
more uniform expression of genes is a result of increasing stochasticity or loss of regulation. 
• It’s unclear if the calculation of transcriptional noise included all genes or if they followed the method outlined in Angelidis
et al. and only selected genes with a low coefficient of variation. 
• The authors suggest that somatic mutation accumulation may contribute to the induction of senescence phenotypes. While
I agree that such a causal relationship cannot be ruled out, it seems more likely that high mutation frequencies correlate with
high levels of DNA damage, which is known to induce senescence as part of the DNA damage response. Mutations occur
as a consequence of DNA repair or replication errors. 
• It was stated that “strong overlap was observed across known senescent gene lists” (pg11, line 13). However, the
referenced figure 5B doesn’t correspond to this statement.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the process of aging, the lung structure and function undergo extensive changes that are closely associated with the onset
and progression of various pulmonary diseases. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying
pulmonary cellular changes during aging will not only enhance our understanding of age-related diseases but also provide
new perspectives for their prevention and treatment. The authors conducted a joint analysis of bulk RNA-seq and single-cell
RNA sequencing data to investigate the changes in lung cells, transcriptomes, and genomes during aging. The overall
logical structure of the manuscript is relatively clear, but the analysis of the results lacks depth, the age groupings are
unclear, and the differences in the results are not significant enough.

Major: 
1, The authors define the young group as ages 44-53 and the elderly group as ages 66-78. However, the dataset includes
samples from a wide age range (11-80 years), evenly distributed across ages, without any explanation as to why samples
from other age groups were excluded. Some experiments, such as the analysis of the "relationship between age and
mutation burden," utilized samples from all age ranges, while others focused only on the young and elderly groups (e.g., cell
type proportions, differential gene expression, etc.). 
2, In Figure 1B, DC cells are labeled as lymphoid-like cells, but according to the reference data (GSE136831, Adams TS et
al., 2020), DC cells are typically annotated as myeloid cells. Additionally, in Figure 1C, the distinction between these cells
and B cells is unclear, and typical marker genes (e.g., CD11c) are not shown to support this classification. 
3, The differential gene expression analysis only compares differences between cell types and samples, but lacks functional
analysis of these differentially expressed genes. I encourage the authors to further explore the potential biological roles of
these genes in the aging process. 
4, Some trends in gene expression (e.g., in Figure 4B, 5B, and 5C, left subplots) are not easily concluded from heatmaps
alone. Similarly, comparison plots of expression distributions (e.g., Figure 4E, 6C) are difficult to differentiate. I recommend
providing more quantitative differential analysis results, such as log fold change, p-values, etc. Furthermore, Figure 4B does
not show all 408 differentially expressed genes, only a subset, and this should be clarified. 
5, In Figures 5B and 5C, gene expression changes associated with somatic mutation burden are observed, but no significant
age-related transcriptomic changes are detected. This result may warrant further discussion, especially in relation to aging-
related transcriptional changes. 
6, In several results, the authors compared their data with other studies (e.g., GSE136831) but did not discuss these
comparisons in detail. If there is high consistency between the two datasets, the differences in data sources may not be as
important. I suggest the authors explore the consistency of the data and its impact on the study’s conclusions. 
7, The authors analyzed gene expression, mutation rates, entropy noise, and aging markers in lung tissue during aging, but
only presented the analysis results. If these indicators could be jointly assessed to infer biological age or the degree of
aging, it could have clinical significance for diagnosis and disease prevention.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have made substantial revisions and have addressed many of the concerns raised in the initial review in this
revised manuscript by Ruben et al. The addition of donor demographic information expanded methodological details, and a
more comprehensive discussion of aging-related pathways is appreciated and strengthens the overall manuscript. However,

Below are the comments outlined that still require attention. 
1. Authors rely exclusively on chronological age and transcriptional entropy for age modeling, without incorporating a direct
biological aging measure, such as epigenetic clocks, such as Horvath or PhenoAge. No justification is provided for this
omission. Given the increasing emphasis on biological age as a more relevant measure of tissue aging, the authors should
either integrate such a metric if available or explicitly acknowledge its absence and discuss potential implications for their
conclusions. 
2. No sensitivity analysis is provided to demonstrate the robustness of differential expression results across different
normalization or batch correction methods such as SCTransform vs log-normalization. A supplementary table comparing
gene overlap and correlation of effect sizes between methods would strengthen confidence in the reproducibility of the
findings. 
3. Authors now report on somatic mutation burden, but do not provide the breakdown of synonymous vs. nonsynonymous
mutations, and do not include a functional enrichment analysis of the mutated genes. Without this, the biological significance
of the reported pattern of mutations remains largely speculative, and it would take very little work to run a basic classification
and nominal pathway enrichment, which would improve this part of the study. 
4. Authors acknowledge a lack of spatial resolution, but do not include any discussion on how spatial transcriptomics could
be applied to study regional heterogeneity in lung aging. A brief outlook paragraph on this point would strengthen the
discussion section and signal future directions.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors calculate somatic mutation burden for each cell type using 10X single-cell RNA-seq data. While the overall
trends (i.e. epithelial cells and gCap cells having the greatest somatic mutation burden) may be informative, the raw values
should be interpreted with caution. Because 10X library prep strongly biases sequencing toward the 5' end of the transcripts,
mutation burden is extrapolated from a very limited portion of the genome. Moreover, variants can only be confidently
detected at highly expressed loci. 
The reported rates of 3.1-7.2 mutations per Mb extrapolate to 10K+ mutations per cell. The sequenced regions are likely to
have a lower mutation burden than other parts of the genome due to transcription-coupled repair so, if their rates are
accurate, the per-cell mutation burden is likely to be even higher. This stands in contrast to previous literature measuring
per-lung cell mutation burden with whole-genome sequencing. Both Yoshida et al Nature 2020 and Huang et al Nature
Genetics 2022 find an average per-cell mutation burden in the 1000s rather than 10K+. 
Overall, this study offers valuable new insights and serves as a great resource. However, the mutation burden calculated
single-cell RNA-seq data should be regarded as preliminary and not definitive measures.
