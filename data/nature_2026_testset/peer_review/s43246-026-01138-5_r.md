Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors report the pressure–temperature phase diagram of the van der Waals material NiBr2 using ac susceptibility
measurements, supported by ab initio calculations. The manuscript is generally well written; however, there are several
points regarding the presentation and analysis of the data that require clarification. Major revisions may be needed before
consideration. 
In the calculations, the authors describe a pressure-induced change in the ground state from HM to AFM. It would
significantly strengthen the manuscript to include a plot of the calculated total energy versus pressure (if such an analysis is
feasible), clearly showing that above a certain pressure, the AFM state becomes energetically preferred. Moreover, the
critical values of J3/J1, J2’/J1, and K_U for the HM–AFM transition shown in Fig. 4(c) appear to be arbitrarily chosen. A more
systematic demonstration is needed—such as presenting how the ground state energy evolves as a function of these
exchange parameters—to substantiate the determination of the phase boundary. 
The authors raise the possibility that defects contribute to the observed growing trend in TN1 under pressure. What is the
value of TN1 after decompression? If defect formation is indeed responsible, one would expect TN1 for a fully
decompressed sample to remain higher than that of a pristine sample. Clarification and experimental evidence on this point
would be valuable. 
The manuscript predicts an opposite trend in J3/J1 under pressure compared to Ref [26]. However, there is no discussion
offered regarding this discrepancy. Since the basal exchange interactions are considered crucial to the experimental
findings, a discussion on the difference and its possible origins should be included. A discussion should also be given
regarding the discrepancy between the observed decreasing TN2 under pressure in the manuscript and the predicted
increasing TN2 under pressure in Ref [26]. Can authors predict the pressure dependence of TN2 quantitatively? 
Overall, the authors’ experimental observations contrast with existing theoretical predictions, which is intriguing for the field
of van der Waals magnets. However, the current version of the manuscript lacks strong theoretical support for these findings.
Other comments: 
The abbreviation "vdW" should be defined in full at its first appearance in the manuscript. 
In the Introduction (lines 51–53), a reference is needed for the claimed threefold theoretical increase of TN2 at 15 GPa. 
In Fig. 1, the heat capacity and ac susceptibility data should be distinguished more clearly, for example, by using different
colours (and using the same colour for labels) or arrows. 
The left panel of Fig. 2(a) appears to have mislabelled pressure values. 
The caption of Fig. 2 states, “The maxima of dχ/dT and d²χ/dT² identify values of TN1and TN2.” This is confusing. Are TN1
and TN2 identified, respectively, by the maxima in the first and second temperature derivatives? Please clarify. Additionally,
in the right panel of Fig. 2(a), do the thin lines represent the second derivative? 
Fig. 3 appears redundant with part of Fig. 4. I recommend either deleting Fig. 3 or removing the redundant schematic figures
from Fig. 4 to avoid repetition.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
1. It is not clear what the main goals and findings of the paper are. Is it the increase of the 
transition temperature TN1? If so, the statement that this enhancement is “unique” needs to 
be put into better context. As the authors themselves state, an increase of the ordering 
temperature meets the general expectation and is therefore not surprising. While they list 
two counterexamples (CrI 3, CrBr 3), it is i mportant to also list examples for which an 
increase is observed. Note that even for CrI3, an initial increase of the ordering temperature 
in the few GPa range was observed. If the enhancement is particularly large compared to 
other compounds, it should be  explicitly discussed. In the second part of the paper, the 
authors discuss in great length the suppression of T N2 with pressure. If this is the main 
message of the paper, it should be noted that it is not novel, since the earlier studies (Ref. 
21) already  point this out. Why are these results significant, apart from the possible 
inconsistency with previous studies? In fact, the inconsistency is also not very clear. In the 
cited Ref. 21, only two data points exist for TN1 for very low pressures below 5 kbar  (0.5 
GPa). Based on these, the authors drew a straight line as an extrapolation to higher pressure. 
Looking at the present phase diagram in Figure 2, the previous low -pressure data is 
consistent within the error bars with the present results.  
We sincerel y thank you for this critical observation, which has prompted us to clarify the presentation and 
significance of our findings substantially. We fully agree that our original manuscript did not adequately articulate 
the true novelty. Upon reflection, we rea lize we presented T N1 enhancement and T N2 suppression as separate 
phenomena, when the genuine discovery is their simultaneous occurrence with unprecedented magnitudes, 
combined with mechanistic insights from isostructural comparison. 
 
The novelty of our work lies in three interconnected phenomena that occur simultaneously. First, we find that the 
AFM Néel temperature (T N1) increases at about +20 K/GPa, making it the highest positive pressure coefficient 
observed for any antiferromagnetic(AFM) transition in van der Waals (vdW) materials —far surpassing all 
literature values by factors of 2 to 4 (MnPS₃: +6.7 [1], FePS₃: +3.6[2], NiI₂: +13 K/GPa[3]). As requested, we 
note that this enhancement is common in vdW materials (MnPS₃, FePS₃, NiI₂), confirming your  observation that 
the increases generally meet expectations. However, the key point about NiBr₂'s enhancement is not just that it 
occurs, but that its magnitude is the highest ever recorded. 
 
Second, the helimagnetic phase (T N2) is simultaneously suppressed at 0.8 GPa, making it the steepest negative 
pressure coefficient among vdW materials. This dual-opposite response—both extreme values in one compound—
is unprecedented. Most vdW materials exhibit monotonic behaviour; none show simultaneous extrem e positive 
and negative reactions. Regarding your point about CrI₃'s initial increase , CrI₃ exhibits an enhancement at low 
pressure, followed by suppression at higher pressures, with a sign reversal between the regimes. NiBr₂ behaves 
differently; both phases respond monotonically without sign change throughout 0 -3 GPa, indicating a different 
physical mechanism. 
 
Third, and most importantly for distinguishing our work from Ref [3], we compared isostructural NiI₂ under the 
same experimental conditions. Despite sharing the same crystal structure (CdCl₂ type, R -3m, triangular lattice), 
NiI₂ shows an increase in both phases with pressure (TN1: ~+13, TN2: ~+9.4 K/GPa(up to 7 GPa before vanishing 
instantly)). This comparison of identical structures with opposite behaviors demonstrates that the interlayer 
exchange parameter 𝑗2
′ not the crystal structure, controls the pressure response. Regarding Ref. [4] reported TN2 
suppression qualitatively but was limited to two TN1 data points below 0.5 GPa. Our systematic study from 0 to 3 
GPa provides a complete pressure -temperature phase diagram. Notably, the isostructural comparison uniquely 
reveals that the evolution of 𝑗2
′ with pressure drives opposing phase responses, a mechanistic insight that cannot 
be derived from Ref.[4] results alone. 
 
A recent DFT study predicted that TN1 should increase from 23 K to 60 K at 5 GPa [5]. Both these values appear 
to be almost twice smaller than in the experiment. Our calculation has led to significantly different values of 
exchange interactions, and the agreement with experiment is very good at ambient pressur e, while for high 
pressure it is within the expected margin of 30%. Furthermore, the study[5] did not examine the pressure-induced 
transition from HM to AFML, all our calculation results on this question are novel. Our work validates the findings 
of Ref.[4], reveals the underlying mechanism and provides corrected exchange parameters that theoretical models 
must incorporate for accurate pressure predictions in competing magnetic systems.

2. The analysis of the model and its conclusions unfortunately, lack clarity. The authors 
propose a Heisenberg spin model for triangular lattice layers, with intra - and interlayer 
interactions, but fail to compare their approach with previous studies of similar models. A 
brief discussion of previous publications, summarizing which couplings are most significant 
for the model would be very helpful.  
We thank the referee for his suggestion to review previous work more in detail. One of the initial studies 
for this type of model was already conducted in a general way by [6](cited in our original manuscript 
as [26] and briefly discussed under the Hamiltonian). This work shows the basic con ditions for the 
formation of helimagnetic order based on mean -field theory, valid without interlayer interactions, |𝑗3| 
> |𝑗1|/4. The case of a negligible 𝑗2 and a significant interlayer interaction ( 𝑗′), as in our case, was, 
however, not the primary focus of that work.  
There is a number of vdW transition -metal halides crystallizing in the CdCl₂ -type structure with a 
similar magnetic structure [7]. From the related studies, the recent work on NiI₂ (cited as [19] in our 
manuscript) appears to be highly  relevant for our problem. Here, high -pressure experiments revealed 
that the third-nearest intralayer exchange ( 𝑗3) and second -nearest interlayer exchange  (𝑗2
′) play a 
crucial role in driving pressure -induced magnetic transformations from helimagnetic to collinear 
antiferromagnetic [3]. In line with these findings, our results for NiBr₂ indicate that while 𝑗1 remains 
the leading intralayer exchange, the second-nearest interl ayer coupling ( 𝑗2
′) becomes increasingly 
important under pressure, enhancing the stability of the collinear antiferromagnetic state and driving 
the suppression of the helical phase. The most important difference in this work originates from the 
strongly d ifferent 𝑗3/𝑗1 ratio in NiI₂ and NiBr₂, which provides the possibility to shift the HM/AFM 
transition by interlayer coupling. This fact we also highlight in our text. 
Another recent first-principles work on the closely related compound NiI₂ has shown that heavy iodine 
ligands produce strong ligand spin–orbit coupling (SOC), which induces sizable anisotropic exchanges 
(Kitaev-like terms) and biquadratic couplings that ar e necessary to reproduce the experimentally 
observed propagation direction and canting of the helical state [8]. However, the magnitude of ligand-
induced anisotropic exchange depends strongly on the ligand SOC. Bromine produces a much weaker 
ligand-SOC–induced anisotropic exchange.  In our DFT mapping for NiBr₂ (including SOC), the 
extracted anisotropic terms are below our numerical uncertainty and are small relative to the isotropic 
exchange scale and to the Kitaev amplitudes reported for NiI₂. We therefore adopt the isotropic + 
interlayer + single -ion-anisotropy model as the minimal model for NiBr₂. We have now modified the 
text to reflect all these findings (added paragraphs 2nd and 3rd last in the Introduction). 
 
3. It is not clear how the values of exchange couplings were chosen for the simulation results, 
and some of these decisions appear somewhat arbitrary. The authors should better guide the 
reader through their logic for choosing the values of exchange couplings and for deciding 
which parameters to hold fixed or vary.  
Most values of exchange couplings were calculated from first principles; only for the discussion 
of the role of parameters, we varied values of selected exchange interactions (as shown in Fig. 
4d and 4e). Since this was probably not clearly formulated, we updated the text. 
 
4. In addition, they should explain their conclusions more carefully and argue how robust they 
are. For example, they suggest that a "mere decrease of j3/j1 could therefore explain the 
decrease of TN2 within the simplest model". However, later they state that this ratio changes 
"only slightly" with pressure and is "not sufficient to explain the decrease of TN2". It is, in 
fact, unclear, why the ratio of j3/j1 determines the transition temperature. The transition 
temperature is mostly determined by absolute sizes of J's rather than specific ratios.  
The ratio 𝑗3/𝑗1 determines the tendency of the system towards choosing the helimagnetic ordering. The 
underlying principle is explained in the cited Ref. 33. Basically, it is the competition between these two 
interactions that prefer different types of ordering. In the case of the HM ground state, a transition 
appears at an elevated temperature. You are right that 𝑗3/𝑗1 alone does not determine the critical 
temperature, but a decrease in this ratio w ould lower the T N2. We have added extra details on this 
problem.

6. In the context of the discussion of the model, it may also be questioned why the values were 
fixed for the first scale at 2GPa, while the other two have values fixed at 0GPa? These 
should be made consistent to be easily comparable, or it should be made clear in the text 
why these values were chose n, to avoid misinterpretation. In line 165, it was stated that 
these values were fixed at 1 GPa, which is inconsistent with the Fig. 4 caption.  
The parameters were intentionally fixed at different pressures to qualitatively explore how specific 
interactions influence the stability of the magnetic phases under varying conditions. Our goal was to 
test whether the helical magnetic (HM) phase could be stabilized at higher pressures by selectively 
tuning key parameters. We agree with the referee that these different choices could be confusing; 
therefore, we have decided to show only values for the fixed case of 0.5GPa in the revised version. This 
analysis also provides insight into why the HM phase in NiBr₂ disappears near 1 GPa, unlike in NiI₂, 
where it persists up to around 7 GPa. 
 
7. In addition, one can ask what is known about the exchange parameters at ambient pressure 
(e.g., from existing neutron scattering data) and how those compare to their model 
parameters at ambient pressure. In total, a more careful literature comparison is absolutely 
necessary to judge the novelty of the conclusions here.  
We have conducted a thorough literature review of available experimental data on NiBr₂, 
particularly neutron scattering studies that directly determine exchange parameters, and added the 
reference to the current manuscript. Our ab initio calculations of exchange parameters at ambient 
pressure show a reasonable agreement with experimentally determined parameters from inelastic 
neutron scattering (INS) measureme nts available in the literature. Specifically, our calculated
