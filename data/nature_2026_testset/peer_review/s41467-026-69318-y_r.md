Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a well-developed human gut-brain-vascular (hGBV) platform designed to model the complex
bidirectional interactions between the gut, blood vessels, and the brain. The platform successfully incorporates human-
derived cells, demonstrating potential utility for elucidating disease mechanisms and therapeutic screenings related to gut-
brain axis disorders. However, addressing the following points would substantially enhance the manuscript: 
General Comments 
• The choice of statistical methods is incorrect for several analyses. Specifically, parametric tests (such as Student's t-test
and ANOVA) should not be used when sample sizes are as small as n=3. For instance, the Mann-Whitney U test, not the
Student's t-test, should be applied in Figure 2l. Additionally, unless sample size is large enough to assume normal
distribution (n>20), normality tests should be performed; if data do not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests are
required. Also, for unequal variances between groups, Welch's t-test or Brown-Forsythe ANOVA should be performed
instead of Student’s t-test or standard ANOVA. 
• In all bar graphs, individual data points should be clearly shown along with mean ± SD. 
• Cytokine profiles should be compared with statistical analysis. Increasing the sample size to statistically compare cytokine
changes is required. 
Specific Comments by Figure 
Figure 1: 
• Figure 1e aims to show cross-sectional villi; however, current images do not clearly demonstrate polarity. It is strongly
recommended to provide a representative 3D image clearly showing polarity. If possible, quantitative polarity analyses
should be provided to strengthen the evidence. 
Figure 2: 
• Figure 2a shows significant background noise for ViL-1 staining. Image quality must be improved. 
• Figure 2b also has unclear TUJ1 signals. Additionally, the arrow labeled as "astrocyte end-feet" inaccurately points to
BMEC only, without clearly visible astrocytes. Proper labeling and representative images are required. 
• Figure 2e: We recommend the authors to perform live-cell calcium imaging in order to assess neuronal activity. 
• Figures 2g, h, i: Methods for quantification of gut thickness, surface area, and uptake capacity must be clearly explained.
The gut surface area should be quantified through full-chip imaging and 3D rendering software. Clarification on the exact
quantification method for gut uptake capacity is necessary. 
Figure 3 and 4: 
• Statistical comparisons are necessary for Figure 3b and Figures 4b,c. If groups with n=3 are included, a Kruskal-Wallis test
and subsequent appropriate posthoc tests must be performed. 
Supplementary Figures: 
• Supplementary Figure 3b could benefit from larger-area Z-stack imaging to better illustrate polarity. 
• Supplementary Figure 4i, labeled as astrocyte end-feet, requires more definitive evidence showing 
direct interactions
between astrocytes and BMEC, beyond GFAP staining alone. 
• Supplementary Figure 4j could better represent the 3D structure through a clearer reconstruction image. 
Additional Critical Comments 
1. Helicobacter pylori primarily reside in the stomach; hence, using conditioned media from H. pylori in a colon cell line
(Caco-2) model seems inappropriate. Either a stomach cell line-based model should be used or conditioned media from
bacteria commonly residing in the colon should be considered. 
2. In physiological conditions, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) is composed of direct physical interactions between BMEC and

astrocytes without a significant gap. The current model has a 100 µm-thick microchannel separating these cells, which is not
physiologically relevant. Clarification and justification from the authors regarding this issue are necessary. 
3. While demonstrating barrier integrity, the gut also functions to transport nutrients to the brain. To show the functional
connectivity of the GBV-on-a-chip, authors could demonstrate that specific nutrients (e.g., glutamate) can pass through the
gut and vascular compartments to reach neurons in the brain compartment, activating neuronal function validated via
calcium imaging. Fluorescence imaging or quantitative evidence could show the functional connectivity of current model. 
4. The physiological difference between gut and BBB integrity should be represented. BBB is inherently tighter, allowing
very limited permeability compared to the gut barrier. Statistically comparing fluorescence intensity of Dextran from the gut
compartment to vascular compartment and from the vascular compartment to the brain compartment would validate the
physiological accuracy of the model. 
5. The BBB naturally comprises BMEC, astrocytes, pericytes, and some neurites. The current model omits pericytes; thus,
authors must either include this cell type in their chip or provide a thorough justification for this omission. 
6. The mechanism underlying cytokine secretion following inflammatory stimuli is intriguing. Identifying the specific cell type
responsible for cytokine production via techniques such as single-cell RNA sequencing would significantly enhance the
manuscript. 
Minor Comments 
1. Figure 1d: Dotted lines extend unnecessarily below the figure; these should be revised. 
2. Individual fluorescence channel images (separated by each light source/channel) should be additionally provided in
supplementary figures. 
Addressing these comments will significantly improve the manuscript, ensuring a robust, physiologically relevant, and
statistically accurate presentation of the findings. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
A more extensive understanding of microbiome-gut-brain axis signalling requires more sophisticated in vitro models. To this
end, there is much interest in using organ-on-a-chip technology. This current report focuses on the validation of a 3D human
gut-brain vascular (GBV) model via a modular microfluidic chip. The authors successfully replicated communication via a
compartment reminiscent of the intestinal epithelium and a compartment reminiscent of endothelial-astrocyte interactions in
the brain. Key outcomes include a demonstration that responses to microbial factors could be detected, including brain-gut
and gut-brain signaling pathways. 
I agree with the authors that this approach is an important research objective and the data reported is very encouraging. It is
particularly noteworthy that the model captures the bidirectional nature of gut-brain axis signalling. I have the following
comments and queries: 
(1) The rationale underpinning some of the experimental decisions are a little unclear, an example being the use of
conditioned media from Helicobacter bacteria. My understanding is that this species is more commonly found in the human
gastric antrum than lower in the gut. Do the authors have results from strains more representative of the colonic ecosystem? 
(2) It is also unclear what components of the Helicobacter conditioned media are responsible for the observations reported.
An important addition to a revised submission would be an analysis of the conditioned media to better understand the
microbial factors responsible for disrupting gut-brain axis signalling. 
(3) It also seems as some aspects of quality control from simpler in vitro models such as TEER measurements and
confirmation of cell viability are omitted (e.g. MTT assay). Given it is largely paracellular permeability that is assessed, it
would also be good to have a TEER readout of combined paracellular and transcellular aspects of integrity. Are these or
alternative options available to evaluate cell viability in these microfluidic compartments available? 
(4) In the introduction, the authors mentioned that animal models do not capture human-specific mechanisms of gut-brain
communication. Could they elaborate on this point, particular in terms of how this model circumvents such a limitation? 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Tran, Jeong and collegues, designed a 3D gut-brain-vascular platform to recapitulate the dynamic
interaction between these organs. In particular, they have demonstrated that their platform can mimic bidirectional
communication between brain and gut, finding that degeneration and disruption of vascular barrier can occur. They
modelled brain degeneration (Alzheimer and Parkinson) and vascular inflammation suggesting the great potential of this
platform with extensive immunofluorescence and permeability quantification. 
While this 3D model could potentially have several applications for a broad range of neurodegenerative and gastrointestinal
disorders, and it is potentially interesting for the readers of this journal. Minor revision is needed to substantiate their claims. 
In the case that the following experiments are not feasible, largely discuss the limitation of the platform and narrow down the
capability of this platform within the specific experimental limitations assay and openly discuss future developments to
address these needs.
