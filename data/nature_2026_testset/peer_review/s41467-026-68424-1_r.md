Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is an interesting study showing that EGFR-mutant, EGFR-inhibitor-resistant non-small cell lung cancer cells (NSCLC)
are hypersensitive to a small molecule compound, MCB-613. Using an elegant comparison with an inactive structural
analog (C151-0598) and click chemistry, combined with proteomic and genomic screens, the authors identify Keap1 as a
target for MCB-613, and further show that the double Michael acceptor functionality of MCB-613 acts as a molecular bridge,
causing an oligomerization in Keap1, and that this is important for conferring sensitization. Surprisingly, the thiol reactivity of
MCB-613 was low. Instead, the compound was found to require K97 in Keap1 for Keap1 oligomerization. They propose that
Keap1 oligomerization in increases ROS production, causing ATF4/CHOP-mediated cell death. 
The authors should consider the following points: 
• Fig.3b: Why does compound treatment cause an increase in fluorescence at the lower temperature, and a slight decreases
at the higher temperatures? 
• Fig 3c and Suppl. Fig. 3b: the V5-tagged Keap1 appears mote thermostable than the endogenous Keap1. What could be
the reason? It will be good to show the corresponding immunoblot for the endogenous Keap1, which is quantified in Suppl.
Fig. 3b. Is the thermal stability of the dimer affected by the concentration of MCB-613? 
• Fig. 4f: In addition to oligomerization, MCB-613 seems to cause an overall increase of Keap1. This is in contrast to the
endogenous Keap1 monomer, which appears to decrease (e.g. Fig. 3d). What could be the reason for this difference? 
• Does MCB-613 react with K97 in each monomer of dimeric Keap1? How do the authors envision formation of the higher
molecular weight species? What happens to these species? 
• The levels of Nrf2 are similar in cells expressing Keap1-WT and Keap1-K97A mutant. Does the level of Nrf2 change in
Keap1-K97A mutant expressing cells treated with MCB-613? 
• The authors propose that Keap1 oligomerization by MCB-613 leads to ROS induction. How does this happen? Which is
the cellular source(s) of ROS under these conditions? Is the generation of ROS caused by MCB-613 treatment really due to
Keap1 oligomerization? Or, is it an independent (from Keap1) effect of MCB-613. In Fig. 5j, the authors show nicely that
Keap1 depletion prevents the increase in ATF4 caused by MCB-613. Does Keap1 depletion also prevent the increase in
ROS caused by MCB-613 in the experiment shown in Fig. 5f? If so, this will provide a better link among Keap1
oligomerization, ROS and ATF4. If not, it will suggest that the increase in ATF4 is not due to Keap1 oligomerization and the
increase in the levels of ROS may be an independent effect of the compound. 
• Why does the sensitization occur only in EGFR inhibitor-resistant cells? Could GCN2 be involved? 
• The authors suggest that MCB-613 may cause general proteotoxic stress. Do they see activation of the heat shock
response? Knowing this may help to establish/test for selectivity. 
• Lines 238- 242: The authors state: “Although prior reports have shown that structurally diverse electrophiles can
oligomerize KEAP1, MCB-613 seems to be unique in that it induces the formation of a complex which persists even in the
face of stringent reducing conditions, suggesting that it does not arise as a result of non-covalent protein-protein interactions
or disulfide bridges”. This mechanism is not unique to MCB-613. For example, methylglyoxal has been shown to modify
Keap1 by forming a covalent methylimidazole crosslink between proximal cysteine and arginine residues, which also
persists under reducing conditions (Bollong et al. Nature 2018; 562: 600-604). 
• Lines 343- 345: The authors state: “In the presence of oxidative or electrophilic stress, however, KEAP1 dissociates from
substrates and allows for their rapid cellular accumulation.” This is a common misconception. Neither oxidants, nor

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

electrophiles cause dissociation of Nrf2 from Keap1; only protein-protein interaction inhibitors do this (e.g. see Horie et al.
Commun Biol. 2021 May 14;4(1):576; Baird et al. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2013 Sep 17;110(38):15259-64). The
oxidant/electrophile-mediated modification of Keap1 disables its substrate adaptor function, allowing accumulation of newly
synthesized Nrf2. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study Bassil et al. describe their investigations of EGFR-therapy resistant cell lines with hypersensitivity to compound
MCB-613 in a KEAP1-dependent manner. Initially, based on a compound library of 2,100 molecules they found this
molecule acting as a covalent, lysine-addressing binder of KEAP1, which leads to the dimerization of two KEAP1 monomers
and subsequent cellular dysfunctions causing ATF4/CHOP-dependent cell death. It’s noteworthy that this effect is especially
observable in mutated cell lines or xenografts and apparently to a lesser amount to wildtype EGFR, which is of high interest
for follow-up therapies for current EGFR related cancer treatments. 
Their biological examinations and discussion are of high relevance and generally well structured and conclusive. The
discussion of MCB-613 as proof-of-concept molecule for deeper understanding of the relationship between
KEAP1/NFE2L2/CUL3 and EGFR inhibitor resistance is fair, as long as the compound is not stated as a druglike molecule
due to it’s PAINS-like structure and missing PK data. 
Some major and minor issues questions should be addressed (major revision): 
1) Is the structure of ARM-143 correctly drawn? (Supp. Fig. 3d.) 
2) The structure of MCB-613 is not druglike and has structural elements that resemble common PAINS. Pleiotropic effects
are likely as well. Although the authors mention limited selectivity and non-ideal PK properties of the compound class, it
should be pointed out more clearly throughout the manuscript that this is not a druglike molecule. 
Interestingly, there is a certain structural similarity to curcumin. Are the authors sure that MCB-613 does not have similar
PAINS effects in in-vitro and in-vivo assays (see e.g. DOI: 10.1021/acs.jmedchem.6b00975)? 
3) The authors investigate the binding site of MCB-613 within the KEAP1 protein with a HA-clover system and sequential
elongation of the peptides. Activity can is traced back to an amino acid between residues 90-100, and ultimately K97.
Replacement of this lysine with a non-isostructural alanine (K97A) leads to reduced oligomerization when treated with MCB-
613. 
Could the authors perform a mass labeling experiment to proof the covalency of the investigated interaction? Their applied
method sounds plausible, but the final argument is missing here. 
4) This study needs to be accompanied by lysine and cysteine reactivity assays. As is common best practice, the reactivity of
all relevant compounds i.e. 
MCB-613, C151-0598 and ARM-143 needs to be quantified in GSH assays vs a common
reference compound (see e.g. doi 10.1039/9781839160745-00069 by London and colleagues). Such data would also be
essential to support the claim made on page 15 (“…suggesting that this molecule might be less promiscuous and thiol-
reactive than initially assumed”). As the compounds are supposed to react with lysine an analogous Lys-reactivity assay
(see e.g. doi 10.1039/C7OB02028G) should also be conducted. 
5) The use of N-acetylcystein as ROS Scavenger is interesting in this context. Couldn't NAC quench the covalent warhead? 
6) It is surprising that very little cysteine modification has been observed in chemoprotomics experiments. Could it be that
these compounds react with cysteine in a reversible covalent manner making the interaction difficult to capture? The authors
should assess this by experiments with model thiols. 
7) Is there clear evidence that the presumed reaction with lysine proceeds though a double Michael addition? Reversible
covalent imine formation followed by Michael addition may also be a potential mechanism. 
8) Other studies that describe MCB-613 (partially cited here as well) traced back the effect of EGFR-resistance on TP53
gain-of-function mutations (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-03599-w, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41698-024-00557-2) and
also described it e.g., for curcumin with high structural similarities (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbrc.2023.08.016).) or outlined a
relationship to the steroid receptor coactivator (10.1016/j.ccell.2015.07.005), and subsequential the generation of ROS. Can
the authors cross check and provide evidence, that their observed effects in EGFR TKI resistant cell lines do not
(also/partially/completely) stem from other correlations? This would be of high importance since the KEAP1-dependency
phenomenon of EGFR-mutant NSCLCs remains fragmentated, as mentioned by the authors. 
9) The authors should depict the chemical structures of the KEAP1 inhibitors used for validation in Supplementary Figure 4. 
10) The structures included in the 2,100 compound commercial screening library should be added as supplementary
information as SMILES strings

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The work by Bassil et al. clearly demonstrates that EGFR-driven NSCLC cell lines with developed resistance to EGFR
inhibitors show preferential sensitivity to the compound MCB-613. They also outline the molecular mechanisms through
which this sensitisation occurs which involves drug-induced dimerisation of KEAP1 and associated IRS. The data are of
very high quality, the authors perform adequate rescue experiements for the observed phenotypes and their conclusions are
adequately supported by the results presented. 
The paper is very well written and clear and the figures very well presented. 
The data are of great interest, both from a biological point of view and from a clinical perspective as the authors propose a
novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of TKI-resistant EGFR-driven NSCLC. Hence, further translation of their findings
could be transformative. 
It is my opinion that the manuscript is acceptable for publication in its current state, but I would suggest that the authors make
the following minor amendments: 
- Fig 2g: Statistical assessment of the difference between the curves would be required. If the differences between consitions
are not significant, the authors should clarify this in the text by stating that they are discussing "non-significant trends". The
possible absence of statistical significance in this precise experiment is not damaging to the rest of the manuscript, and
should therefore not be a concern to recognise if such. 
- Fig 4f: Could the authors suggest an explanation for where the residual dimerisation might be coming from in the K97A +
MCB-613 condition? Mention/explanation of this in the Discussion section of the manuscript would be welcome. 
- Line 350-353: Further to the point that the authors are making here and also directly relevant to TKI resistance in EGFR-
driven NSCLC, a manuscript by Roy et al. in Cell Discovery in 2016 described decreased NRF2 activity and in GSH levels
being associated with resistance to first-generation EGFR TKIs. Citing this paper here would tie up their reasoning more
tightly with the resistance phenotype that they investigate.

REVIEWER COMMENTS 
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This is an interesting study showing that EGFR-mutant, EGFR-inhibitor-resistant non-small 
cell lung cancer cells (NSCLC) are hypersensitive to a small molecule compound, MCB-
613. Using an elegant comparison with an inactive structural analog (C151-0598) and click 
chemistry, combined with proteomic and genomic screens, the authors identify Keap1 as a 
target for MCB-613, and further show that the double Michael acceptor functionality of 
MCB-613 acts as a molecular bridge, causing an oligomerization in Keap1, and that this is 
important for conferring sensitization. Surprisingly, the thiol reactivity of MCB-613 was low. 
Instead, the compound was found to require K97 in Keap1 for Keap1 oligomerization. They 
propose that Keap1 oligomerization in increases ROS production, causing ATF4/CHOP-
mediated cell death. 
 
The authors should consider the following points: 
• Fig.3b: Why does compound treatment cause an increase in fluorescence at the lower 
temperature, and a slight decreases at the higher temperatures? 
Although not necessarily intuitive, this pattern is commonly observed in thermal shift assays 
and results from fluorescence quenching due to protein aggregation at high temperatures.  
In our case, MCB-613 binds purified KEAP1 and destabilizes it, which exposed hydrophobic 
residues. The fluorescent probe binds to these residues and fluoresces, and the machine 
detects that fluorescence. Once the curve peaks and the protein has fully melt ed, 
disorganized proteins aggregate. This obscures the hydrophobic residue s from the probe, 
leading to a loss of signal. Since the conditions with the highest concentrations of MCB-613 
are the most destabilized, they are the first to quench and lose signal once they cross the 
peak.  
 
• Fig 3c and Suppl. Fig. 3b: the V5 -tagged Keap1 appears mote thermostable than the 
endogenous Keap1. What could be the reason? It will be good to show the corresponding 
immunoblot for the endogenous Keap1, which is quantified in Suppl. Fig. 3b. Is the the rmal 
stability of the dimer affected by the concentration of MCB-613?  
At the reviewer’s suggestion, we have included the immunoblot for endogenous KEAP1 as 
Supplementary Figure 3c. With respect to the thermostability of endogenous vs. V5 -tagged 
KEAP1, there are a variety of possible explanations, as there several differences  between 
these assays. In the first place, the use of an ectopic promoter for V5-KEAP1 expression may

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

result in different amounts of total KEAP1 protein in these two experiments. The use of 
different antibodies (anti-V5 vs anti-KEAP1) may also account for differences. The presence 
of a V5 tag, though small, may influence thermostability. With respect to the thermostability 
of dimers at varying concentrations, the majority of assays directed against the dimer in 
question were run at 2 µM doses of MCB -613. While the question of thermostability and 
MCB-613 concentration is interesting, we believe it is ultimately outside of the scope of the 
present investigation. 
 
• Fig. 4f: In addition to oligomerization, MCB -613 seems to cause an overall increase of 
Keap1. This is in contrast to the endogenous Keap1 monomer, which appears to decrease 
(e.g. Fig. 3d). What could be the reason for this difference? 
We thank the reviewer for highlighting this difference. Although we cannot be certain about 
the exact cause, we agree with the reviewer’s emphasis on the differences in transcriptional 
control between these two assays. With respect to the KEAP1 monomer in Fig. 3d, for 
example, protein expression is ultimately under control of an endogenous promoter which 
is responsive to changing cellular conditions, and it is possible that proteotoxic stress 
related to KEAP1 oligomerization may feed  back to inhibit further  transcription and protein 
expression. By contrast, the protein levels in Fig. 4f reflect a GFP -tagged protein which is 
driven off of an ectopic promoter which is not responsive to cellular feedback. Additionally, 
the blots in Fig. 3d were detected using an anti -KEAP1 antibody, while those in Fig. 4f were 
detected using anti-GFP antibody. Differential epitope exposure, especially in the setting of 
dimerization and/or oligomerization, may also account for some of these differences.   
 
• Does MCB -613 react with K97 in each monomer of dimeric Keap1? How do the authors 
envision formation of the higher molecular weight species? What happens to these species? 
We thank the reviewer for the question, and agree that there would be value in investigating 
the exact, step-by-step mechanism involved in KEAP1 oligomerization by MCB-613. As now 
discussed in the revised version of the manuscript (lines 345-348, 444-446), however, we 
cannot definitively claim that MCB-613 interacts directly with KEAP1 at the K97 residues. In 
order to support that claim, we would need to demonstrate direct engagement of the K97 
residue by MCB -613 via e.g. lysine ABPP and MS, but our attem pts to do so were 
unsuccessful based on current limitations of the technology. Our data clearly demonstrate, 
however, that K97 is required for KEAP1 oligomerization by MCB -613, whether or not it is 
directly engaged.

As for the formation of higher molecular weight species, we speculate that, if K97 is directly 
engaged by MCB -613, then MCB -613 may even act to cause “daisy chains” of KEAP1 
monomers which form higher order oligomers. 
 
• The levels of Nrf2 are similar in cells expressing Keap1-WT and Keap1-K97A mutant. Does 
the level of Nrf2 change in Keap1-K97A mutant expressing cells treated with MCB-613? 
We thank the reviewer for the question. We have performed this study, and have included 
the results in a revised version of Supplementary Figure 6 (Supplementary Fig. 6 e). As this 
study shows, WZR12 cells which overexpress WT KEAP1 show a greater accumulation of 
NRF2 than do cells which overexpress KEAP1 K97A. The residual NRF2 accumulation seen 
in the latter setting is presumably due to the presence of endogenous wild-type KEAP1. 
 
• The authors propose that Keap1 oligomerization by MCB-613 leads to ROS induction. How 
does this happen? Which is the cellular source(s) of ROS under these conditions? Is the 
generation of ROS caused by MCB-613 treatment really due to Keap1 oligomerization? Or, is 
it an independent (from Keap1) effect of MCB -613. In Fig. 5j, the authors show nicely that 
Keap1 depletion prevents the increase in ATF4 caused by MCB -613. Does Keap1 depletion 
also prevent the increase in ROS caused by MCB -613 in the experiment shown in Fig. 5f? If 
so, this will provide a better link among Keap1 oligomerization, ROS and ATF4. If not, it will 
suggest that the increase in ATF4 is not due to Keap1 oligomerization and the increase in the 
levels of ROS may be an independent effect of the compound. 
We thank the reviewer for the proposed experiment, and agree that a demonstration of the 
KEAP1 dependence of MCB-induced ROS would further enhance the claims presented in the 
study. We have performed this study, and included the results in Supplementary Figure 7b, 
which clearly shows that the loss of KEAP1 abrogates ROS accumulation after treatment 
with MCB-613, demonstrating that this effect is dependent upon KEAP1.  
 
• Why does the sensitization occur only in EGFR inhibitor-resistant cells? Could GCN2 be 
involved? 
We thank the reviewer for the question. As detailed in lines 389-412, we believe that EGFR-
inhibitor-resistant cells are sensitized to MCB -613 by their high levels of ATF4 and CHOP , 
which arise as a consequence of the acquisition of drug resistance. The reviewer is correct 
to point out that GCN2 is one of the kinases which  phosphorylates eIF2a and drives ATF4 
expression. In data which we have not included in the present manuscript (due chiefly to 
space constraints), we have knocked out each of the four kina ses which feed into the ISR:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

GCN2, PERK, PKR, and HRI. None of these knockouts was sufficient to rescue the effect of 
MCB-613. This may be because there are overlapping inputs which would require 
combinatorial knockouts of some combination of these kinases, or because there is an 
alternative mechanism at play. We believe that further investigation of this question, while 
certainly interesting, falls outside of the scope of the present manuscript.  
 
• The authors suggest that MCB-613 may cause general proteotoxic stress. Do they see 
activation of the heat shock response? Knowing this may help to establish/test for 
selectivity. 
At the reviewer’s suggestion, we have incorporated data demonstrating that MCB-613 does 
not induce activation of the heat shock response. Specifically, we tested PC9, GR4, and 
WZR12 cells against standard doses of MCB -613 over multiple timepoints. As expected, 
MCB-613 selectively induced ATF4 expression in the drug-resistant derivatives, but not in the 
parental cells. However, no increase in HSP60, HSP70, or HSP90 was observed in any of the 
cell lines. These data are now included in Supplementary Figure 6g. 
 
• Lines 238- 242: The authors state: “Although prior reports have shown that structurally 
diverse electrophiles can oligomerize KEAP1, MCB-613 seems to be unique in that it 
induces the formation of a complex which persists even in the face of stringent reducing 
conditions, suggesting that it does not arise as a result of non-covalent protein-protein 
interactions or disulfide bridges” . This mechanism is not unique to MCB-613. For example, 
methylglyoxal has been shown to modify Keap1 by forming a covalent methylimidazole 
crosslink between proximal cysteine and arginine residues, which also persists under 
reducing conditions (Bollong et al. Nature 2018; 562: 600-604). 
We thank the reviewer for this correction, and we have amended the manuscript to reflect 
these details (revised manuscript lines 250-251). 
 
• Lines 343- 345: The authors state: “In the presence of oxidative or electrophilic stress, 
however, KEAP1 dissociates from substrates and allows for their rapid cellular 
accumulation. ” This is a common misconception. Neither oxidants, nor electrophiles 
cause dissociation of Nrf2 from Keap1; only protein-protein interaction inhibitors do this 
(e.g. see Horie et al. Commun Biol. 2021 May 14;4(1):576; Baird et al. Proc Natl Acad Sci U 
S A. 2013 Sep 17;110(38):15259-64). The oxidant/electrophile-mediated modification of 
Keap1 disables its substrate adaptor function, allowing accumulation of newly synthesized 
Nrf2.

4) This study needs to be accompanied by lysine and cysteine reactivity assays. As is 
common best practice, the reactivity of all relevant compounds i.e. MCB -613, C151-0598

We appreciate this suggestion by the reviewer, and agree that these studies are important 
for the evaluation of MCB-613. We have addressed this concern in the following ways: 1) we 
have tested MCB-613 in a standard GSH reactivity assay, in which the BTK inhibitor ibrutinib 
was included as a standard control/reference compound. This assay, which is now 
mentioned in lines 298-301 in the text and included as Supplementary Figure 5a, revealed 
that MCB -613 is highly cysteine -reactive and rapidly labels GSH. 2) We have also tested 
MCB-613 in a lysine reactivity assay using N-acetyl-L-lysine methyl ester with a model NHS-

Supplementary Figure 6a, revealed that MCB-613 shows high lysine reactivity.

MCB-613. This result is expected, as the loss of the dienone structure signifi cantly reduces 
the conjugation of the molecule and, therefore, its reactivity. Despite this expected decrease

labels MCB -613 and therefore functions as an effective con trol for MCB -613-induced 
dimer/oligomerization.  
C151-0598, though tested, is not included in the figure panels. Due to solubility issues with 
this compound, we were unable to obtain meaningful results from the cysteine and lysine 
reactivity assays.
