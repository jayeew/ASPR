Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript introduces GARDEN, a spatial transcriptomics framework aimed at identifying rare or subtle pathogenic cell
populations through dynamic graph attention and spatial contrastive learning. I found this paper to be very comprehensive
and ambitious. The authors present over 10 experiments spanning multiple data modalities (e.g., transcriptomics and
chromatin), tissue types, platforms (e.g., Visium, STARmap, Xenium), and species, as well as diverse downstream tasks
such as spatial domain detection, 3D registration, and disease mapping. This scope is impressive and reflects significant
effort and engineering. 
Overall, I am positive about the work. The method appears generalizable, and the results suggest consistent performance
gains across benchmarks. I particularly appreciate the authors’ clear emphasis on GARDEN’s ability to uncover rare, subtle,
or spatially dispersed regions that may be missed by other methods, which will be the innovation and how this method
stands out among many methods with a similar aim. 
However, my main concern lies precisely in this claimed innovation. While the paper provides numerous case studies that
show detection of small or rare regions, it remains unclear whether the model design truly has a built-in advantage for this
task. The method architecture, as presented, does not contain a clearly defined mechanism that is uniquely suited for
identifying rare or spatially sparse populations. Features like dynamic attention and contrastive learning could plausibly
help, but the manuscript stops short of explaining how these elements directly support rare-cell identification, versus general
clustering improvement. 
Moreover, much of the evidence is visual or anecdotal, specific examples where GARDEN finds rare regions missed by
others. While this case-based validation is helpful, it risks overemphasizing positive instances, especially in the absence of
quantitative evaluations tailored to rare cell detection. It would greatly strengthen the manuscript if the authors could either: 
Provide additional ablation or synthetic experiments designed to test rare-region recovery explicitly, or clearly explain which
architectural elements in GARDEN are theoretically or empirically responsible for improved rare-cell detection. 
Besides, there are a few other major comments detained in below (many of them are centering around this innovation claim):
* I’m not entirely sure how the authors define “rare cell populations” or “small spatial regions.” Are you referring to regions
that differ subtly in gene expression from surrounding areas and are therefore harder to separate? Or do you expect a
substantial difference in expression, despite the group being small in size? Or is the definition of “rare” driven purely by
biological context (e.g., rare cell types known from prior knowledge)?

described around Figure 1c (top of page 6) seems to correspond to Figure 1a(ii) rather than 1c. Also, where is Fig 1f?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors propose a dynamic graph attention encoder-based method named GARDEN for identifying rare pathogenic cell
populations from spatial omics data. They demonstrate the superior performance of GARDEN across various platforms,
including spatial ATAC-seq data. The method appears promising. However, I have the following major concerns that need to
be addressed: 
1) As part of the benchmarking analysis, I suggest that the authors first present quantitative evaluation metrics to assess the
accuracy and efficiency of GARDEN in spatial domain identification, especially using datasets with known annotations. In
the case of the Slide-seqV2 dataset, the annotations are described, but the ground truth remains unclear. This lack of
reference makes it difficult to objectively evaluate the accuracy of GARDEN’s predictions. 
2) Related to point 1, the authors should provide more quantitative evidence that the identified spatial domains are
biologically meaningful. The current use of only two marker genes is insufficient to substantiate the biological relevance of
the clustering results. 
3) In Fig. 1b, the red arrow appears to represent two distinct biological interpretations simultaneously, which may lead to
confusion or misinterpretation. The authors should revise the figure to avoid ambiguity. 
4) In Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4, results for GARDEN are missing from the bar plots. Additionally, other methods such as
GraphST and SpaGCN seem to exhibit better performance. 
5) In Fig. 2j, the cluster names are not consistent with those in Fig. 2h. Please verify and ensure consistent cluster naming
across Fig. 2c–j. 
6) On page 9, “compaired GARDEN” should be corrected to “compared GARDEN”. 
7) In Fig. 3g, the top three marker genes alone do not convincingly support the accuracy of GARDEN’s cluster annotations. It
appears that some cells in clusters 0 and 1 may be misclassified. Further validation is needed. 
8) In Fig. 5g and h, Seurat seems to produce more accurate annotations based on marker gene distribution. The authors
should comment on this and provide an explanation for GARDEN’s performance in these cases. 
9) As widely known, the performance of deep learning models can vary with random initialization. The authors are
encouraged to include robustness analysis under different random seeds to validate the stability of GARDEN.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I believe the authors’ revision has significantly strengthened the manuscript. I now have just a few remaining comments
aimed at improving clarity and avoiding potential confusion for readers: 
While I understand that GARDEN is an unsupervised method, and that cluster colors are assigned independently across
slices, it would still be helpful to use more consistent or intuitively aligned colors when visualizing spatial domains across
consecutive sections. For example, in Figure R1, the orange color in GARDEN’s results refers to central regions in some
slices, but to peripheral areas in others. This inconsistency makes it more difficult to compare slices visually and might lead
to the incorrect impression that the slices were jointly analyzed but resulted in inconsistent or mismatched domains,
especially when the same color legend is shared by multiple slices. Another example appears in Figure 6b, where
inconsistent color assignments between adjacent samples could make it harder to interpret domain correspondence. While
this may seem cosmetic, it can inadvertently impact the perceived coherence of the model’s output. Even a brief note in the
figure captions or methods section clarifying that color assignments are independent per slice (unless otherwise specified)
would help avoid this confusion. 
The authors have provided a GitHub repository with a Python package and tutorial materials. However, it would be more
helpful to include clearer guidance on how to use the tutorials, such as setup instructions, and the sequence in which to run
the scripts or notebooks. Additionally, to enhance usability and reproducibility, I strongly encourage the authors to include
code for reproducing some of the key results or figures presented in the manuscript, ideally using publicly available datasets.
This would greatly assist users in validating the method and applying it to their own data.

1. Architectural Mechanisms Supporting Rare-Cell Detection in GARDEN. GARDEN’s architecture integrates sev-
eral key components that collectively enhance sensitivity for detecting rare or spatially sparse cell populations. These
components enable effective feature representation, model spatial context, and amplify low-abundance signals, all crucial
capabilities for capturing rare cell types. We describe the design and functionality of each component below, and the
following section provides ablation studies that quantitatively validate each component’s individual and joint contributions
to GARDEN’s overall performance.
• Dynamic Graph Attention Mechanism . GARDEN employs a novel dynamic graph attention mechanism that
adaptively adjusts attention weights among spatial transcriptomic spots based on their contextual relationships.
This design accommodates local variations and heterogeneous signals within complex tissue environments, high-
lighting important local features while preserving the global tissue structure. For identifying rare cell types, the
dynamic attention mechanism focuses and amplifies weak signals from small or sparsely distributed cell popula-
tions, preventing these rare signals from being overshadowed by dominant cell groups. This adaptive weighting not
only improves sensitivity to rare populations but also enhances interpretability by revealing biologically meaningful
spatial organization and gene-driven domain formation. In practice, GARDEN’s attention mechanism excels at iden-
tifying major cell populations and, importantly, also detects and validates rare cellular subpopulations, providing a
powerful tool for dissecting the diversity and function of tissue microenvironments.
• Spatially-aware Graph Fusion Contrastive Model . Conventional spatial domain identification methods can be
vulnerable to noise-induced artifacts that mimic spurious spatial patterns. GARDEN addresses this with a spatially-
aware graph fusion contrastive learning scheme that merges multi-scale spatial graphs within an adversarial training
framework. This approach amplifies meaningful local differences while preserving broader spatial context, effectively
isolating genuine microenvironmental variation from random noise. Notably, during training GARDEN simulates ar-
tificial “niche perturbations” to explicitly teach the model to distinguish true spatial heterogeneity from synthetic or
noisy artifacts. Together, the dynamic graph attention and spatial contrastive modules enable GARDEN to reli-
ably uncover subtle tissue structures. These synergistic components are especially crucial for detecting rare cell
populations, as they help the model avoid being misled by noise and instead focus on true low-abundance signal
patterns.
2. Rare-focused quantitative evaluation. To move beyond qualitative, case-based evidence, we performed two new
sets of quantitative experiments to rigorously evaluate GARDEN’s ability to recover rare-cell regions under controlled
conditions. First, we generated synthetic spatial transcriptomics datasets that systematically vary two key biological fac-
tors: (1) the abundance of rare cell populations (ranging from extremely low to moderate frequency) and (2) the degree
of spatial continuity of the rare cells (from highly clustered, spatially coherent rare cells to sparsely and discontinuously
scattered ones). These synthetic datasets cover a diverse spectrum of realistic scenarios in which rare cell types might
appear, enabling a rigorous, controlled evaluation of each method’s performance under different conditions. Using these
benchmarks, we found that GARDEN consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baseline methods in accurately identifying
rare cell populations across all simulated settings.

2. Comprehensive Benchmarking of GARDEN for Rare-Cell Detection and Scalability. We evaluated GARDEN and
baseline methods on controlled synthetic datasets designed to capture diverse rare-cell scenarios. Using scCube (1), we
generated 15 spatial transcriptomics datasets based on a human squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) single-cell RNA-seq
reference (2), systematically varying the Rare Cell Proportion (RCP: 0.5%, 1%, 3%) and spatial continuity (Delta: 5, 15,
30, 50, 200). B cells, naturally rare (<1%), were used as the rare-cell population. Delta values ranged from highly dis-
persed to strongly clustered, thereby representing a wide spectrum of tissue architectures (Supplementary Fig. 1a).
Using these datasets, we compared GARDEN with state-of-the-art approaches, including GraphST, STAGATE, SEDR,
CellCharter, and BANKSY , and evaluated rare-cell recovery using Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), F1 score, and
Cohen’s Kappa. Across all 15 scenarios, GARDEN consistently outperformed competing methods (Supplementary Figs.
1b and 2). At an RCP of 3%, SEDR and BANKSY performed relatively better, whereas GraphST, STAGATE, and CellChar-
ter frequently merged rare and non-rare regions, resulting in high false-positive rates. Although SEDR and BANKSY
reduced such misclassifications, they still failed to accurately capture dispersed background spots. Under ultra-rare con-
ditions (RCP = 1% and 0.5%), baseline methods were unable to distinguish rare-cell regions from background and often
produced artificial spatial domains. In contrast, GARDEN maintained high detection fidelity, accurately reconstructing
spatially coherent rare-cell clusters and achieving substantially higher MCC, F1 score, and Cohen’s Kappa. These results
underscore GARDEN’s strong sensitivity and robustness under extreme data imbalance.
To further investigate the sources of GARDEN’s performance gains, we conducted ablation studies on the same simu-
lated datasets to evaluate the contributions of key components in GARDEN. We focused on two modules: the dynamic
graph attention mechanism and the spatially-aware graph fusion contrastive learning module. Six model variants were
constructed by removing or simplifying these modules. The complete GARDEN model consistently achieved the high-
est MCC, F1 score, and Cohen’s Kappa (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). The dynamic attention
mechanism was crucial for preserving spatial relationships within dispersed rare-cell clusters, while contrastive learning
enhanced robustness under severe class imbalance. Neither module alone achieved comparable results, demonstrating
that the synergy between dynamic graph attention and spatially-aware graph fusion contrastive learning is essential for
accurate rare-cell detection while maintaining tissue topology.

detection of rare cell populations.

artifacts through multi-scale graph fusion and adversarial perturbation during training. This synergistic design embodies

2. I’m not entirely sure how the authors define “rare cell populations” or “small spatial regions.” Are you referring to regions
that differ subtly in gene expression from surrounding areas and are therefore harder to separate? Or do you expect a
substantial difference in expression, despite thegroup being small in size? Or is the definition of “rare” driven purely by
biological context (e.g., rare cell types known from prior knowledge)?
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. In our study, we adopt an integrative definition of “rarity”
that combines transcriptional distinctiveness, functional/spatial context, and prior biological knowledge. In practice, a “rare
cell population” or “small spatial region” in our analysis must satisfy criterion (1) and at least one of criteria (2) or (3): (1)
Expression-based rarity, meaning a small cluster of cells whose gene expression profile is unusually divergent from its
neighbors; (2) Functional/spatial corroboration, meaning that the cluster’s gene signature aligns with a coherent biological
program or anatomical microdomain; and (3) Previously known rare cell type, meaning correspondence to a cell subtype
documented in the literature as low-frequency. These criteria are complementary: criterion (1) ensures transcriptional
distinctiveness, while criteria (2) and (3) provide orthogonal biological or contextual support. This combined strategy
ensures that a candidate rare domain is not simply a stochastic outlier but reflects a reproducible biological signal. Our
approach follows recent spatial- and single-cell studies that emphasize both transcriptional and contextual evidence when
identifying rare populations (Xu et al., 2024 (3); Wang et al., 2024 (4); Christopher et al., 2023 (5)).
• 1. Expression-based rarity (subtle or strong signatures): First, we require that a rare cluster exhibit a distinctive
transcriptional signature relative to its surroundings. This can take two forms. In one case, the cluster may express
one or a few marker genes at markedly different levels than nearby cells (a “clear-cut” signature). In another
case, the cluster’s overall profile may differ in many genes by modest but coordinated amounts, yielding a unique
transcriptomic fingerprint even if no single gene is highly specific. Both scenarios are recognized in the rare-cell
literature: Christopher et al. note that “rare cell types present a particular challenge that is magnified when they
are defined by differentially expressing a small number of genes” (5), and Xu et al. describe methods (e.g. FiRE,
RaceID (6, 7)) that score individual cells by how much they deviate from global gene expression patterns. In line
with these approaches, we flag small clusters whose expression patterns stand out in the high-dimensional space,
such as unique markers or transcriptional profiles — yet comprises only a very small number of cells or occupies
a minimal tissue area (e.g., <3% of the dataset (4)). By analogy, even a handful of spatially adjacent cells can be
considered “rare” if their gene program is consistently distinct. Importantly, we require such signatures to involve
multiple genes or coherent modules, not single-gene noise. This aligns with Shaffer et al. (8), who identified rare
cells in homogeneous populations through stable, multi-gene expression modules.
• 2. Functionally/spatially corroborated rare regions: Second, we require that candidate rare clusters make
biological sense in space. Specifically, we test whether the cluster’s marker genes or pathways point to a coherent
function or an anatomical niche. Spatial transcriptomics data are known to organize into higher-order “domains”
that reflect tissue physiology (9); thus an authentic rare cluster should map onto a plausible tissue substructure (for
example, a perivascular niche, a glandular microenvironment, or a developmental zone) or be enriched for specific
biological processes. In practice we perform gene set enrichment (or reference-atlas mapping) on each cluster’s
signature: if the top genes are all related to, for instance, immune activation or neuronal development, this reinforces
that the cluster is a genuine cell type rather than random noise. This principle is exemplified by Yuan et al. (9), who
Xinming Zhang | GARDEN bioRχiv | 11

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 16 -->

showed that their algorithm could “uncover previously overlooked spatial domains that exhibit strong associations
with brain aging” by focusing on reproducible, functionally coherent gene patterns. Likewise, in our analysis we
compare novel clusters to known atlases (e.g. Allen Brain or other organ reference maps) when available, and only
label a region as rare if it forms a spatially contiguous domain with a biologically interpretable signature. In short, we
insist on multimodal evidence (gene expression and spatial location) so that a “rare” domain aligns with established
anatomy or function rather than arising from random scatter.
• 3. Known rare cell types: Third, in defining rare cell types, we incorporate prior knowledge of low-frequency cell
populations from the literature. Many tissues contain cell subtypes that have been independently characterized as
“rare” (for example, certain interneuron subtypes in brain, tuft cells in epithelium, specific stromal or immune subsets,
etc.). When our clustering recovers a small population matching the markers of a known rare cell type, we annotate
it accordingly rather than treating it as a novel anomaly. This cross-check with published atlases adds confidence:
e.g. Wang et al. (3) cite multiple examples where their method rediscovered established rare subpopulations – such
as “unique subpopulations of rare bipolar cells” in mouse retina and an intermediate B-cell state in lymph node –
demonstrating that literature-grounded validation is feasible (4). In our work we similarly refer to prior single-cell and
spatial studies: if a cluster expresses, for instance, a set of genes previously attributed to a known rare endothelial
subtype or developing cell state, we interpret the finding as confirming that subtype. This prevents mislabeling of
trivial variants as novel ‘rare’ types when they are already documented.
We emphasize that all three criteria are applied judiciously to avoid false positives. In particular, we distinguish true rare
signals from technical artifacts by demanding multi-gene and spatial consistency. In other words, a putative rare domain
must pass internal quality checks: it should appear in multiple adjacent spots or replicate samples, involve co-expression
of several marker genes, and survive batch correction. This is analogous to the “Rarity” framework of Christopher et
al., which balances sensitivity to rare clusters against controlling false discoveries (5). We avoid considering single-spot
dropouts or solitary outlier cells as genuine rare populations. By combining expression evidence with functional annotation
and literature support, we ensure that identified rare clusters are biologically meaningful (not random noise).
In summary, our notion of “rarity” is deliberately integrative. We capture both transcriptional rarity (at either extreme of
expression divergence) and contextual rarity (unique niche or pre-defined low-frequency type), as supported by recent
literature in spatial genomics. This multi-tiered definition is meant to align with current practice: rare cell population
discovery always involves verifying that a small cluster’s signature is robust and biologically grounded (3–5). In this
way, we clarify that by “rare” we do not mean mere random variation, but rather a small group of cells distinguished by
reproducible transcriptomic and biological features, whether or not those features are subtle.

5. After reviewing Supplementary Figure S2, I have a concern: Does GARDEN tend to over-segment small domains? For
example, in slice 151507, regions like Layer 2 and Layer 4 appear larger in the GARDEN predictions than in the ground

Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. In particular, we acknowledge that in original
Supplementary Figure S2 (slice 151507) the predicted regions for Layer 2 and Layer 4 were noticeably larger than the
ground truth annotations. This observation revealed that our model had over-predicted the extent of these fine-grained
cortical layers, which is an important issue that needed to be addressed. We appreciate the reviewer’s critique, as it
prompted us to refine our model for greater accuracy on these delicate structures.
To investigate this issue, we closely examined the root causes of the over-segmentation. Spatial transcriptomics data
from the cerebral cortex inherently exhibit a hierarchical laminar structure, and this is especially pronounced in datasets
generated by low-spatial-resolution platforms such as 10x Visium. Under such low-resolution conditions, each spatial
spot often spans multiple adjacent cortical layers, resulting in gene expression profiles with substantial spatial blurring
and cellular heterogeneity. Consequently, constructing a spatial graph purely based on physical proximity can lead the
model to fragment semantically continuous cortical regions into multiple sub-clusters or erroneously amplify small but
homogeneous domains, undermining the biological continuity of the tissue structure.
Existing methods have recognized this challenge and often treat low-resolution cortical data as a special case, incor-
porating custom strategies outside of their general frameworks. For example, Dong et al. proposed STAGATE, which
introduces a “cell-type-aware module” that refines the spatial graph using gene expression–driven clustering, alleviating
topological distortions caused by insufficient resolution (12). Similarly, Zhang et al. proposed CeLEry, which for cortical
datasets like DLPFC replaces the standard loss function with a rank-consistent logistic regression loss (13). This
specialized loss penalizes predictions that violate the known hierarchical ordering of cortical layers, thereby preserving
the laminar structure at the model level. Inspired by these approaches, we redesigned our model to account for the
unique characteristics of low-resolution cortical data. In addition to the original spatial adjacency graph, we constructed
a semantic similarity graph that links regions with similar gene expression profiles. We then incorporated an additive
attention mechanism to adaptively fuse these two “views” (spatial adjacency and semantic similarity) within a dual-graph
framework. This design balances local spatial topology with global transcriptional consistency: on one hand, it preserves
the structural continuity of spatially contiguous regions, and on the other hand, it brings together semantically similar
yet spatially dispersed regions. As a result, the refined GARDEN model mitigates the over-segmentation caused by
over-reliance on local spatial information, improving both the biological coherence of clusters and the spatial continuity of
cortical domains.

7. Figure 3b raises another concern: how can we be confident that GARDEN is not over-detecting false “rare regions”?
For instance, the LSX_HY_MB_HB region (in pink) appears split across multiple predicted clusters in GARDEN’s output.
How can one determine whether these additional segments are meaningful or artifacts?
Response: We thank the reviewer for the valuable comment. In response to the first question about how to ensure
that GARDEN does not over-detect false “rare regions”, we have implemented multiple rigorous safeguards and cross-
validation strategies across both the method design and validation phases. These measures align with our definition of
rare cells (see response to Comment 2) and emphasize three key criteria: distinct gene expression patterns, contiguous
spatial distribution, and biological interpretability. Taken together, these criteria maximize the reliability, specificity, and
biological plausibility of the rare regions identified by GARDEN.
• 1. Expression-based rarity and spatial adjacency constraints: We consider a region “rare” only if it satisfies
two conditions. First, the cells must be spatially adjacent, ensuring that any signal arises from one or more spatially
contiguous clusters of cells in the tissue rather than from isolated outliers or random noise. Second, these cells
must share a coordinated, multi-gene expression signature that consistently sets them apart from the surrounding
tissue. By requiring both spatial proximity and a robust expression pattern across multiple genes, GARDEN explic-
itly excludes spurious single-gene fluctuations and ensures that identified rarity reflects meaningful transcriptional
divergence.
• 2. Consistency with expert annotations: In datasets with detailed anatomical annotations (for example, the
fine-grained subdivision of the LSX_HY_MB_HB region), the rare regions identified by GARDEN strongly align with
independently curated structures. This agreement with expert-labeled anatomy serves as critical external validation,
indicating that GARDEN captures true biological heterogeneity rather than generating algorithmic artifacts.
• 3. Functional enrichment supporting biological plausibility: To ensure that GARDEN’s findings remain inter-
pretable and trustworthy even in the absence of ground-truth anatomical references, we rely on functional enrich-
ment as a core validation criterion. Specifically, the gene programs associated with GARDEN-detected rare regions
are consistently and significantly enriched for coherent biological pathways—such as region-specific neurodevel-
opmental regulators, stress-response programs, or localized signaling cascades. These enrichments are not only
statistically robust but also functionally meaningful, strongly suggesting that the identified rare regions represent
genuine biological states involved in development, homeostasis, or environmental response, rather than artifacts of
technical noise or random transcriptional variation.
We now address the reviewer’s second concern regarding the biological meaning of the spatial domains identified by GAR-
DEN in the LSX_HY_MB_HB region. GARDEN is fundamentally an unsupervised spatial clustering method designed to
uncover intrinsic, data-driven structures rather than to enforce alignment with coarse pre-defined annotations. Therefore,
finding multiple spatial clusters within the LSX_HY_MB_HB region should not be viewed as "over-segmentation". Instead,
it likely reflects genuine biological heterogeneity in this brain region. To support this interpretation, we performed experi-
mental validations, which are described in detail in Supplementary Note 11 and reproduced below.

Supplementary Note 12: Biological Validation of GARDEN Clustering for Domain 0 and Domain 1. First, we
acknowledge that spatial continuity can lead to gradual transcriptional transitions—a common biological phenomenon in
tissue microenvironments. Neighboring cell populations often exist in intermediate or transitional states, so some degree
of mixed gene expression at cluster boundaries is not unexpected. In the revised manuscript, we now explicitly note this
point and emphasize that the observed boundary ambiguity likely reflects genuine biological continuity rather than any
flaw in our clustering approach.
To more formally assess the biological validity of GARDEN clustering, we examined the correspondence between the
unsupervised domains and the original cell-type annotations (scgnIN and MSN). We visualized this relationship using a
Sankey diagram (left panel of Supplementary Fig. 26a), which illustrates the association between Domain 0, Domain
1, and their annotated cell types. Consistent with expectations, Domain 0 is predominantly composed of scgnIN cells,
whereas Domain 1 is primarily enriched for MSN cells. Notably, we observed a subset of cells within Domain 0 that were
originally annotated as MSN, accounting for 27% of all cells in Domain 0. These MSN-annotated cells are predominantly
localized to the spatial interface between MSN and scgnIN regions (right panel of Supplementary Fig. 26a). In fact, scgnIN
and MSN cells are not strictly segregated into two mutually exclusive territories; instead, they exhibit local intermixing.
This spatial intermingling reasonably explains the gradual transition observed at the domain boundary in the clustering
results. The limited overlap may reflect an intermediate transcriptional state between the two neuronal subtypes, thereby
supporting a model in which neuronal phenotypes transition gradually across space rather than being sharply delineated.
Furthermore, we compared the gene expression signatures of the GARDEN-derived domains to those of the original cell
types. We identified the top differentially expressed genes separating Domain 0 and Domain 1 (using Wilcoxon rank-sum

interneurons, is one of the top markers in Domain 0. This high concordance strongly supports the biological validity of
our domain definitions, indicating that GARDEN successfully recapitulates the known molecular differences between the
scgnIN and MSN cell populations.

trajectories), resulting in inherently blurred transcriptional boundaries. Thus, the difficulty in completely segregating these
