Review Comments for 
A Wideband Tunable, Nonreciprocal Bandpass Filter Using Magnetostatic Surface Waves with Zero Static Power
Consumption 
The manuscript presents an innovative demonstration of nonreciprocal bandpass filters implemented on thick (18 µm) YIG
films grown on GGG substrates. The reported results are impressive and highlight significant advancements in
magnetostatic wave-based filtering. However, the manuscript would benefit from a more thorough analysis of certain design
choices. The paper explores several aspects of the device, including YIG thickness, transducer design (meander vs. straight
lines), waveguide geometry, and surface planarization using BCB. While the rationale behind BCB planarization and the
use of meander line transducers is well-articulated, the selection of YIG thickness and geometry—and their impact on device
performance—require further elaboration. Below are specific comments and suggestions: 
1. The selection of YIG thickness for the study needs to be explained with a more detailed analysis of its effect on non-
reciprocity. The reference [30] cited in the manuscript details the non-reciprocity dependence as a function of the ratio of film
thickness and wavelength. Was the thickness of 18 µm selected to keep these dependencies in mind? 
2. Most of the analysis is only shown for 3 µm and 18 µm films. It would be beneficial to demonstrate the effects of varying
YIG thickness from 3 to 18 µm or even higher and explain why 18 µm was chosen. 
3. Since the authors claim that most of the performance metrics of the filter improve as we increase the thickness, is there no
trade-off? Moreover, reference [27] provided in the manuscript suggests that the absorbed power (which I assume the
authors relate to coupling) doesn’t significantly change from 1 µm to 3 µm YIG. Can the author elaborate on this? 
4. Perhaps the most impressive aspect of this manuscript is the non-reciprocity, which is achieved using a dual-hexagon
shape of YIG that is 18 µm thick. Can the authors quantify how much the thickness of the YIG is helping? For example, if we
use the same dual-hexagon shape on a 3 µm YIG, what level of non-reciprocity can we expect? Additionally, can the author
detail how they arrived at this specific geometry and if it is optimal in terms of isolator performance? Have the authors tried
other geometries? 
5. The main manuscript mostly uses 18 µm but the fab images in the supplementary shows 15 µm which results in some
confusion. 
6. It is stated that a triangular YIG boundary prevents MSSW propagation on the bottom surface, but the mechanism is
unclear. Could the authors elaborate on why a triangular YIG boundary is more effective at suppressing backside reflections
compared to other boundary geometries?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors made a critical advancement in fabrication, specifically a benzocyclobutene planarization process, that enabled
the development of a 18 µm thick YIG filter tunable from 4 GHz to 17.7 GHz. In addition to the broad tuning range, the filter
also demonstrates pronounced nonreciprocity, which expands the application space of this technology within RF frontends.
In addition to the device demonstration, the authors implemented integrated magnetic bias circuity, a critical step forward
toward practical system integration. While this team previously demonstrated a similar technology, specifically a 3 µm thick
YIG tunable filter, again with incorporated magnetic bias circuitry, the authors made a significant step forward advancing
performance on both fronts. With respect to the filter the significantly thicker YIG film improved the power handling, filter
selectivity, and bandwidth, while enabling the observed RF transmission nonreciprocity. In addition, the magnetic bias
circuitry was designed to be more compact (reduced volume from 1.68 to 1.07 cm3) with a larger magnetic field range (up to
5700 Gauss). The authors presented significant and comprehensive characterization of all aspects of the nonreciprocal,
tunable filter and those results fully supported their findings. While related to their earlier work, this represents a major
improvement enabled by a new fabrication method that allows for thicker YIG films than previously possible. This
breakthrough not only benefits RF front-end applications but is also relevant to the broader YIG research community,
including spintronics applications. Since this benzocyclobutene planarization process was central to enabling this device, I
was happy to find that in the methods section the details of the fabrication were listed explicitly, such that others could exploit
this approach in their own research. I also really enjoyed the final figure #6 which contrasts competing technologies with this
study. I believe this work has high technical merit, and represents a significant step forward toward compact, low power
nonreciprocal tunable filters based on YIG, and therefore should be accepted for publication. There should be more work
like this—where the focus isn’t just on a scientific demonstration, but on translating state-of-the-art magnetic technologies
into real-world applications. This kind of research helps bridge the gap between novel materials science and practical RF
systems, making the case for why advanced magnetics matter beyond the lab and serves a purpose.

1. The selection of YIG thickness for the study needs to be explained with a more detailed analysis

Answer to the reviewer:  
We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment regarding the selection of YIG thickness and 
its relation to nonreciprocity. The 15–18 µm YIG film used in this study was not chosen based on 
an optimization process but rather due to commercial YIG availability constraints. This thickness 
range represents the thickest high-quality YIG-on-GGG films currently available from commercial 
vendors, in contrast to the much more common 3 µm films. 
Nevertheless, the observed enhancement in nonreciprocity for the 15–18 µm films aligns well with 
the theoretical understanding described in reference [30], where nonreciprocity is shown to 
increase with the ratio of film thickness to magnetostatic surface wave (MSSW) wavelength. 
Although the thickness was not specifically selected to satisfy this condition, our experimental 
results are consistent with this dependence. As also noted in [30], we expect that even greater 
nonreciprocity could be achieved in future devices using thicker YIG films, should such materials 
become available.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

2. Most of the analysis is only shown for 3 µm and 18 µm films. It would be beneficial to 
demonstrate the effects of varying YIG thickness from 3 to 18 µm or even higher and explain why 
18 µm was chosen. 
Answer to the reviewer:  
We appreciate the reviewer’s valuable comment regarding the exploration of YIG film thickness 
variation. In our current study, we focused on 3 µm and 15-18 µm YIG films primarily due to the 
high materials cost and limited commercial availability of intermediate thicknesses. Each wafer 
costs over $20,000, which makes it financially impractical to include multiple additional 
thicknesses within this work. Moreover, despite our efforts to procure YIG films with intermediate 
thicknesses, the manufacturer (MTI corporation) was unable to provide YIG-on-GGG wafers 
between 3 µm and 18 µm. 
Nevertheless, this study systematically investigates the influence of YIG thickness through both 
experimental comparison (3 µm vs. 18 µm) and theoretical analysis. We examined several key 
parameters affected by thickness, including dispersion characteristics, power handling capability, 
nonreciprocity, linearity, and propagation loss. These analyses collectively offer a framework that 
can be extended to predict and optimize the performance of YIG filters with intermediate or even 
greater thicknesses in future work.

3. Since the authors claim that most of the performance metrics of the filter improve as we increase 
the thickness, is there no trade-off? Moreover, reference [27] provided in the manuscript suggests 
that the absorbed power (which I assume the authors relate to coupling) doesn’t significantly 
change from 1 µm to 3 µm YIG. Can the author elaborate on this? 
Answer to the reviewer:  
We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comments and the opportunity to clarify this point. In the 
study by V. Castel et al. [27], the absorbed power did not appear to increase significantly with YIG 
film thickness primarily because the measurements were performed near the saturation level of the 
available RF power. Nevertheless, their results did show that the magnitude of the absorbed power 
increased with frequency below the saturation point, which is consistent with the fact that absorbed 
power scales with the effective volume of YIG interacting with the microwave field. Therefore, in 
general, the coupling efficiency (or absorption efficiency) is expected to increase with YIG film 
thickness, as a thicker film provides a larger interaction volume for MSSW excitation. 
Regarding the trade-offs associated with increasing YIG thickness, several important challenges 
arise. As discussed in detail in this study, the primary limitation is the increased difficulty in 
microfabrication, which we addressed through the proposed BCB planarization process.  
Additionally, demagnetization effects become more pronounced in thicker films. As shown in 
Supplementary Note #6 (comparison of YIG filters with dual-waveguide and single-waveguide 
designs), stronger demagnetizing fields in thicker YIG reduces the effective channel width and 
central internal field, degrading the uniformity of the MSSW mode. The integration of two parallel 
waveguides into a single, wider structure partially mitigates this issue. 
Another observed trade-off is that increasing YIG thickness raises the minimum operating 
frequency of the tunable filter. In this work, the minimum frequency with acceptable insertion loss 
is approximately 4 GHz, which is higher than the ~3.3 GHz achieved in previous works using 
3 µm thick YIG films [R1-3-1]. The underlying mechanism for this frequency shift remains under 
investigation and will be explored in future studies. 
[R1-3-1] X. Du et al., "Frequency tunable magnetostatic wave filters with zero static power

4. Perhaps the most impressive aspect of this manuscript is the non-reciprocity, which is achieved 
using a dual-hexagon shape of YIG that is 18 µm thick. Can the authors quantify how much the 
thickness of the YIG is helping? For example, if we use the same dual-hexagon shape on a 3 µm 
YIG, what level of non-reciprocity can we expect? Additionally, can the author detail how they 
arrived at this specific geometry and if it is optimal in terms of isolator performance? Have the 
authors tried other geometries? 
Answer to the reviewer:  
We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comments and recognition of the significance of the 
observed nonreciprocity. To quantify the effect of YIG thickness on nonreciprocity, we compared 
the performance of meander-line transducer filters fabricated on 3 µm and 18 µm YIG films. As 
shown in Figure 1-4-1 (Fig. 3c in the main text), both filters exhibit distinct passbands in the 
forward transmission (S 12). However, in Figure 1-4-2 (Supplementary Figure 11), the backward 
transmission (S21) differs markedly: the 3 µm filter shows less than 5 dB attenuation, whereas the 
18 µm filter exhibits strong suppression, indicating pronounced nonreciprocal behavior. 
The difference arises from the underlying propagation mechanisms. In the 3 µm-thick YIG filter, 
the device functions similarly to a resonator, where the two straight edges act as reflective 
boundaries [R1-4-1, R1-4-2]. The magnetostatic surface waves (MSSWs) are reflected back and 
forth between the edges, forming standing or circulating wave patterns that yield nearly reciprocal 
transmission. 
In contrast, the 18 µm-thick YIG filter no longer supports such resonant circulation. The spin 
waves excited at one port propagate primarily in a single direction and experience minimal 
reflection. One key factor is the increased sidewall roughness of the thicker YIG film, as visible 
in the optical micrographs (Fig. 2c and Fig. 2d). The roughened edges, which result from the 
greater etching depth required for thick YIG, act as scattering centers that suppress spin-wave 
reflection. This phenomenon is consistent with previous studies that used rough and lossy edge 
terminations [R1-4-3, R1-4-4, R1-4-5, R1-4-6], nonuniform magnetic fields [R1-4-7] , or 
additional spin wave absorbers [R1-4-8, R1-4-9, R1-4-5] to minimize reflection and enhance 
nonreciprocity. Furthermore, increasing the film thickness enhances surface confinement and 
unidirectionality of MSSWs on a single surface, further amplifying nonreciprocal transmission. 
Regarding the geometry, we appreciate the reviewer’s question. While the dual-hexagon geometry 
used here effectively promotes unidirectional propagation, it has not been fully optimized. Other 
geometries may also enhance nonreciprocity, such as rotated YIG slabs [R1-4-10], or tapered 
edges (cut at an oblique angle to the propagation direction to scatter reflected waves) [R1-4-11 and 
R1-4-12]. These alternatives will be investigated in future work to identify optimal designs for 
isolator performance.

6. It is stated that a triangular YIG boundary prevents MSSW propagation on the bottom surface, 
but the mechanism is unclear. Could the authors elaborate on why a triangular YIG boundary is 
more effective at suppressing backside reflections compared to other boundary geometries? 
Answer to the reviewer:  
We thank the reviewer for the comments regarding the role of the triangular YIG boundary in 
enhancing nonreciprocity. The triangular boundary improves nonreciprocal behavior through two 
primary mechanisms. 
First, the oblique edges of the triangular YIG structure suppress standing-wave formation by 
scattering the propagating magnetostatic surface waves (MSSW) into directions that follow the 
magnetostatic backward volume wave (MSBVW) condition. The MSSWs propagate in-plane and 
perpendicular to the magnetic field, whereas the MSBVWs correspond to magnetostatic waves 
propagating in-plane but parallel to the magnetic field, typically at lower frequencies than MSSW. 
Because the scattered waves lie within the stopband of the MSBVW, they rapidly decay, leading 
to energy dissipation along the oblique boundary and the absence of standing-wave resonances. 
Similar techniques have been demonstrated in rotated YIG slabs [R1-6-1], which showed 
improved MSSW nonreciprocity by suppressing reflections, or tapered edges cut at an oblique 
angle to the propagation direction [R1-6-2 and R1-6-3]. 
Second, within the triangular regions, much of the YIG material lies near the etched sidewalls 
around two ends. The increased roughness of these sidewalls introduces additional damping and 
attenuation of the MSSWs, thereby preventing reflection toward the bottom interface and 
suppressing wave circulation within the structure. This serves as an additional loss mechanism that 
further strengthens unidirectional propagation. 
Moreover, the triangular YIG boundaries help reduce coupling to higher-order width modes, 
mitigating spurious modes below the passband, as illustrated in Fig. 4e and utilized in prior 
magnetostatic-wave studies [R1-6-4].  
Overall, the hexagonal device geometry effectively promotes unidirectional propagation and 
suppresses higher-order width mode spurs. The nonreciprocal MSSWs cannot circulate inside the 
cavity and instead either dissipate along the lossy sidewalls or convert into MSBVWs that quickly 
decay, since MSBVWs cannot exist at these frequencies. Although the geometry has not yet been 
fully optimized, the observed backward transmission approaching the noise floor indicates that 
this structure already provides near-optimal suppression of undesired reflections and spurious 
responses.

width of the yokes to 2 mm, which is the vertical dimension of the rectangular yoke base in Fig.

re-enter the LNA and induce ripple or oscillations, degrading sensitivity and linearity. Therefore, 
the ability of the nonreciprocal filter to absorb and attenuate reflected signals in the Rx path ensures 
stable impedance conditions, suppresses undesired feedback, and preserves signal fidelity across 
the entire operating bandwidth.

Answer to the reviewer:  
We thank the reviewer for the insightful and technically precise question. 
Regarding the frequency shift due to transverse quantization, we agree that for waves with normal 
(positive) dispersion, introducing a transverse quantization component kx = nπ/w increases the 
total wavevector magnitude k = √kx2 + ky2 , and the frequency would normally increase, as 
observed in acoustic, optical, or electromagnetic waveguides. However, spin-wave dispersion in 
magnetostatic systems is neither isotropic nor uniformly normal. For MSSW, and in particular for 
backward-volume (BV) type modes, parts of the dispersion are anomalous: the frequency 
decreases with increasing k for certain propagation directions or polarization configurations. When 
a transverse mode introduces an additional kx component parallel to the magnetization (as in the 
stripe waveguide geometry), this reduces the effective dipolar energy contribution, thereby shifting 
the mode frequency downward. This behavior contrasts with systems exhibiting purely normal 
dispersion. 
Concerning the role of MSSW dispersion and anisotropy, we agree that the pure magnetostatic 
surface spin wave (MSSW, or Damon–Eshbach mode) shows normal, forward dispersion when 
propagating strictly perpendicular to the magnetization in an extended film. However, in a laterally 
confined structure, propagation is no longer purely transverse to the magnetization—the quantized 
component 𝑘𝑥 is now partly parallel to the direction of magnetization, M0. Because spin-wave 
dispersion is strongly anisotropic, the presence of this kx ∥ M0 component mixes in a backward-
volume-like character. As a result, while the fundamental MSSW mode remains largely surface-
localized, higher-order transverse modes (with larger 𝑘𝑥) exhibit progressively lower frequencies, 
consistent with the trends shown in Fig. 2 (a) or reference papers [R 2-3-1 and R2-3-2]. 
Finally, regarding the hybridization interpretation, the reviewer’s suggestion is indeed physically 
meaningful and consistent with prior discussions in the magnons literature [R 2-3-1 and R2-3-2]. 
When the propagation direction becomes oblique to the magnetization, or when lateral 
confinement introduces a finite kx ∥ M0 , the dynamic magnetization precession acquires both 
surface and volume characteristics. The resulting dipolar coupling hybridizes the MSSW and 
MSBVW modes, forming mixed MSSW–MSBVW eigenmodes. This hybridization provides an 
intuitive explanation for the observed frequency downshift of confined MSSW modes relative to 
the ideal plane-wave MSSW manifold.

Answer to the reviewer:  
We appreciate the reviewer’s question regarding the use and processing of the oxide hard masks. 
The ALD-grown oxide hard mask was annealed primarily to enhance its adhesion to the YIG 
surface. ALD was specifically chosen because it provides higher quality, which is essential for 
achieving high selectivity and maintaining mask integrity during the prolonged wet etching 
process required for the 18 µm-thick YIG films.

to an ALD oxide, making it unsuitable for deep YIG etching, but adequate for defining the shallow 
aluminum transducers.
