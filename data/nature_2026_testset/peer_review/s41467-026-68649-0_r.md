Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a novel and mechanistically detailed study demonstrating that intracellular Stenotrophomonas
(SP) within tumor cells impairs anti-tumor immunity by degrading STING protein, thereby suppressing the type I interferon
signaling pathway. The authors employ a broad range of techniques, including microscopy, flow cytometry, transcriptomics,
and functional in vivo models, to build a coherent narrative linking bacterial localization to immunotherapy resistance. 
The study is timely and of potential translational relevance, especially given the increasing recognition of bacteria-tumor
interactions in modulating the tumor microenvironment. The results are largely compelling and well supported by the data.
However, several aspects of the methodology, mechanistic interpretation, and data presentation could benefit from
clarification or further experimentation. For example, the contamination control methods are not fully described, some
mechanistic conclusions (such as the role of MHC-II or chemotherapy resistance) appear premature, and certain controls
(e.g., use of STING-deficient macrophages or validation of human IFNa2b in mice) are lacking or under-discussed. 
Strengthening these aspects would improve the rigor and impact of the study. Specific comments are provided below to help
improve clarity and robustness. 
1.Simplified explanation of contamination control 
Although the authors mention normalization using "reads per million microbiomes" and "reads per million total reads" to
control false positives, they do not clearly explain how contaminant species were distinguished from true intracellular
bacteria, nor whether negative controls or tools like decontam were used. Please elaborate on how potential contamination
was excluded. Were technical replicates, reagent blanks, or computational filtering tools (e.g., decontam) used? 
2.Premature conclusion on intracellular localization 
The classification of SP as an “intracellular” bacterium seems to rely mainly on sequencing and FISH data in figure 1.
Consider softening the claim of “intracellular” at this stage or adding a statement that further cellular validation is presented
in the following sections. 
3.Survival analysis methods not described 
The authors mention conducting a survival analysis but do not clarify the method used (e.g., Kaplan-Meier, Cox regression),
nor whether confounding clinical factors were controlled in figure 1. Please specify the survival analysis method used and
clarify whether clinical covariates (e.g., age, sex, TNM stage) were adjusted for in the analysis.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Feng et al., describes the effect of Stenotrophomonas (SP) infection in promoting the progression of
gastrointestinal tumor (GIT). SP specifically infects tumor cells and decreases STING expression by inducing ER stress,

leading to the downregulation of the type I IFN pathway response in GIT. The decreased expression of IFNβ by tumor cells
subsequently attenuates the presence of MHC-II+ macrophages and impairs effector CD8 T cell activity within the GIT

The research concept presented in this manuscript is interesting, well-designed, and generally well-written. The authors
have clearly invested significant effort into developing and articulating their study. However, there remain some aspects that
would benefit from additional supporting evidence or further clarification. Addressing these points would enhance the overall
clarity and impact of the work. The following major and minor comments are intended to help improve the manuscript. 
Major comments: 
Question 1. It is recommended to show the difference in SP levels between normal adjacent mucosa and tumor tissues in
AEG and GC in Figure 1D. 
Question 2. Is there any association between SP levels and the clinicopathological characteristics of patients with AEG and
GC? Additionally, could SP levels be an independent prognostic factor in GIT? 
Question 3. How did the authors select the five patients for fluorescent IHC in Figure 2A? It is recommended to increase the
sample size to improve the robustness of the results. Additionally, could the authors please provide the detailed
methodology used to assess the percentages of each immune cell type by fluorescent IHC, including the size and number of
areas or spots analyzed? 
Question 4. Did the authors perform fluorescent IHC for M2-TAM markers, such as CD163 and CD206, in Figure 2? 
Question 5. In Figure S9J, the in vitro experiments show that SP infection suppresses the expression of ISGs in tumor cells,
while enhancing the expression of ISGs in immune cells, including DC, macrophages, and T cells. The authors should
briefly explain the rationale or underlying mechanisms for this opposite response in each cell type. 
Question 6. Did the authors examine the expression of cGAS in GIT lines treated with or without SP in Figure 5E? 
Question 7. It would be preferable to test whether ER stress inducers, such as thapsigargin and tunicamycin, decrease the
expression of STING in GIT lines as a positive control (Figure 6). Additionally, although CB-5083 is used, it remains unclear
whether SP-induced ER stress is involved in the downregulation of STING expression in GIT lines, as there is no direct
evidence. If possible, the authors should investigate whether ER stress attenuators, such as 4-PBA and TUDCA, or
knockdown/knockout of ATF-6 or IER-1-XPB1 pathways can rescue the SP-induced degradation of STING in GIT lines. 
Question 8. It is recommended that the authors include a discussion on the mechanism by which SP specifically infects
tumor cells but not other cell types within the GIT. 
Minor Comments: 
Question 1. There are no explanations for Supplementary Figure S9K in the main text or the figure legend. 
Question 2. Why did the authors use ρ (rho) to indicate “phosphorylated” in the text? 
Question 3. In Figure 8A, there are no labels. It seems that the upper panels show GIT lines treated only with SP, while the
bottom panels show the same cells treated with SP in combination with levofloxacin? If this interpretation is correct, please
clarify it in both the figure and the legend. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a comprehensive study on how intracellular Stenotrophomonas (SP) targets the STING–IFNβ–
macrophage axis to promote tumor progression and immunotherapy resistance in gastrointestinal tumors (GIT). The authors
aim to provide mechanistic insights through a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments, ultimately establishing SP as a
tumor-intrinsic immunomodulatory pathobiont. While the study is conceptually innovative, several key issues in the
presented findings still require additional explanation or experimental validation. 
1. The authors report that STING agonist treatment in tumor cells induces significant IFN-β gene expression, and that the
resulting conditioned medium affects macrophage MHC-II expression. However, direct evidence of intracellular IFN-β
protein production—such as intracellular cytokine staining, ELISA, or IFNβ-promoter reporter assays—is still lacking. It is
scientifically insufficient to designate tumor cells as the primary source of IFN-β based solely on mRNA expression data. 
2. The authors quantified the proportion of tumor cells harboring Stenotrophomonas DNA using FACS sorting and qPCR,
and conclude that SP is exclusively present in tumor cells, not in immune cells. However, the evidence supporting this claim
appears limited. A key question remains as to why Stenotrophomonas selectively infects tumor cells and not immune cells,
and this has not yet been addressed with sufficient experimental support.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

3. Although the manuscript shows that tumor-conditioned medium leads to reduced MHC-II
⁺
 macrophages and decreased
IFNγ production by CD8
⁺
 T cells, no direct functional experiments—such as MHC-II blockade, macrophage depletion and
reconstitution, or T cell activation assays—appear to have been conducted to validate these causal relationships. 
4. While the authors propose that STING degradation occurs via the ERAD and proteasomal pathways, and present results
showing STING restoration by proteasome inhibition, the distinction between this and lysosomal degradation remains
unclear. The specific molecular mechanism underlying STING degradation thus requires further clarification.
