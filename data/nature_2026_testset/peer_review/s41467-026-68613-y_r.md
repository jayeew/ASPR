Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors reported a concept of uniformly laser sintering nanoparticle thin film with curved structure in thickness. The
varying thickness of the inkjet-printed nanoparticle thin film to be sintered is used to adapt the Gaussian distribution of laser
intensity, thus making the heating and sintering of the nanoparticle thin film more uniform. The center of the deposited
nanoparticle thin film is thicker than that on the edges since the laser intensity at the center is higher than that on the sides.
The claimed advantage of this proposed method compared to the conventional approaches for reaching uniform laser
sintering includes reducing the complexity of the laser optics, as well as the cost of the system. Review comments are as
follows: 
1. Although the temperature uniformity of the curved nanoparticle thin film is improved during Gaussian laser scanning,
owing to the nature of heat transfer, the real-application feasibility of this approach of forming laser-sintered nano/micro
structures for circuity remains unclear. For instance, the curvature, shape, width, and thickness of the inkjet printed thin film,
which is optimized accordingly, are restricted by the laser intensity distribution at the same time. This limitation greatly
affects the flexibility of circuitry design, which is in contradiction to the common idea that manufacturing technology shall
support the design and function of the designated products. 
2. In addition, the trend of circuitry and electronic devices is 3D stacking and packaging. For this purpose, circuitry patterns
with uniform and controllable thickness are preferable to curved circuitry. Therefore, curved circuit patterns made by the
proposed method in this study may raise significant concerns in this regard. 
The current manuscript also contains some flaws in writing. For instance: 
3. It is unclear why several axial distances of the laser beam after the focusing lens were used in this study while measuring
the laser intensity distributions. Specifically, on page 4, line 50, the range of z is 125~155 mm. On the same page, line 71,
the range of z is 130 to 145 mm. 
4. Esy in Equation (2) is apparently different from that shown in Figure 2(e). 
5. In Figure 5(c), those symbols are not shown in the legend correspondingly. 
Overall, this concept, using curved thin film deposited by inkjet printing to adapt a Gaussian laser beam intensity in order to
reach a uniform temperature of the thin film to enable uniform sintering quality, is interesting. The mathematics and
approaches are quite straightforward. The main concern is the feasibility of applying this proposed manufacturing
technology to the real products of circuitry.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents an approach to addressing the critical issue of Gaussian beam-induced defects in laser sintering/melting
by tailoring circuit thickness distribution via inkjet printing. However, several aspects require clarification and strengthening
to fully support the claims and ensure robustness: 
1.Droplet Spreading Dynamics Omitted: The model and fabrication strategy assume ideal inkjet droplet deposition and
controlled thickness distribution. Crucially, the analysis does not account for the fundamental role of droplet surface tension
and substrate wettability (hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity) in determining droplet spread shape, final footprint, and
consequently, the actual printed height/thickness profile. This is a significant simplification, as these factors directly govern
the achievable resolution and thickness uniformity in inkjet printing. The authors should address how variations in these
parameters (e.g., across different substrate materials or ink formulations) might impact the fidelity of their thickness-matching
strategy and discuss potential mitigations or calibration requirements. 
2.Non-Rectangular Cross-Section Assumption: The mathematical optimization and performance analysis appear to assume
an ideal rectangular cross-section for the printed conductive traces. This assumption is frequently inconsistent with the
reality of inkjet-printed lines, which typically exhibit rounded, bulging, or coffee-ring effect profiles. The discrepancy between
the model's assumed geometry and the actual printed morphology could substantially affect the accuracy of the predicted
conductance, laser absorption/heat distribution, and ultimately, the sintering outcome and final properties. The manuscript
should acknowledge this limitation and discuss its potential implications or validate the model against realistic cross-
sectional profiles. 
3.High-Frequency Signal Integrity Concern: While the demonstrated DC/low-frequency conductance improvement is
impressive, the proposed "stair-step" thickness profile (inherent to the layer-by-layer inkjet approach) raises concerns for
high-frequency circuit applications. Such non-uniform, stepped cross-sections can introduce significant impedance
discontinuities, signal reflections, and increased transmission losses at microwave/RF frequencies. If the technology targets

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

high-performance conformal circuits broadly, the authors should explicitly discuss this limitation for high-frequency operation
or provide data/analysis demonstrating acceptable RF performance. 
4.Justification for Laser Sintering vs. Thermal: The manuscript positions the laser intensity modulation approach as a
solution to problems inherent in laser sintering. However, it does not adequately justify why laser sintering is essential for
this application compared to conventional thermal sintering processes. Thermal sintering (oven, belt furnace, localized
heating) often offers superior process efficiency, potentially higher final conductivity, and avoids the specific complexities of
laser beam shaping and Gaussian distribution issues altogether. The authors should strengthen the rationale for choosing
laser sintering specifically. A direct comparison of the performance (conductivity, transmittance, speed, energy efficiency)
and potential limitations (e.g., substrate thermal stability) of circuits fabricated using their laser-sintered, thickness-optimized
approach versus thermally sintered circuits (with or without thickness optimization) would be highly valuable.

Comment: 
5. The method appears optimized for small-scale lab demonstrations. What technical barriers exist 
for scaling to industrial production speeds (e.g., m/s printing rates)? 
Reply: Thank you for your comment. We agree that th e present work has been demonstrated only 
in a small-scale laboratory environment, with an inkjet printing speed of approximately 10 mm/s. 
Scaling up to industrial production speeds on the order of meters per second would indeed raise 
several technical barriers. 
1. Droplet stability: at high firing frequencies, the stability of droplet ejection and placement may 
deteriorate, compromising line continuity and registration accuracy. 
2. Fluid dynamics: rapid solvent evaporation during high-speed printing can alter the balance of 
Marangoni and capillary flows, thereby reducing the effectiveness of coffee-ring suppression. 
3. System reliability: at such high throughput, nozzle clogging, synchronization of multi-nozzle 
arrays, and substrate thermal management become increasingly critical. 
These challenges underscore important directions for future development, and we have begun 
exploratory efforts toward industrial-scale implementation to address the above issues, and we also 
have noted this limitation in the revised manuscript (page 19, lines 347-352). 
“Limitations and outlook. In terms of processing speed , our current demonstrations are limited
