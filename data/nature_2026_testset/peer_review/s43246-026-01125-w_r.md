Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript reports the fabrication of multifunctional Au–Ag alloy nanostructures with a distinctive “nanomushroom-on-
nanowire” morphology through an electron-beam-assisted physical synthesis route. The hybrid nanostructures are claimed
to combine strong PL, enhanced electrical conductivity, and efficient photothermal conversion, enabling simultaneous tumor
visualization and photothermal ablation. 
The concept of morphologically engineered metallic nanostructures for dual imaging and therapy is appealing, and the
authors’ approach could contribute to the ongoing development of multifunctional theranostic nanomaterials. However, the
manuscript requires substantial revisions to strengthen its novelty claims, provide quantitative experimental evidence, and
improve clarity and presentation. 
Major Comments 
1. The manuscript introduces an intriguing “nanomushroom-on-nanowire” architecture, but the novelty relative to existing
Au–Ag nanostructures (e.g., nanostars, nanocages, branched nanowires) is not sufficiently established. The authors should
clearly demonstrate how this morphology provides distinct advantages (e.g., structurally, optically, or functionally) over
previously reported plasmonic nanostructures. A comparative discussion or schematic summarizing these distinctions would
strengthen the conceptual foundation. 
2. The claims of enhanced photoluminescence and photothermal conversion efficiency require robust experimental
validation. Quantitative data such as emission spectra, quantum yield, absorption spectra, temperature elevation curves, and
calculation of photothermal conversion efficiency should be presented and compared with appropriate controls (e.g., Au–Ag
nanowires without mushroom caps). The relationship between structure, alloy composition, and optical performance should
be discussed in detail, ideally supported by compositional and structural characterization. 
3. The manuscript attributes PL enhancement to both molecular-like quantum confinement and LSPR. These mechanisms
should be more clearly distinguished and substantiated. The authors are encouraged to include computational or
electromagnetic simulations (FDTD or DFT) to visualize local field distributions and support the proposed enhancement
mechanism. 
4. The statement that the material “achieves precise tracking and efficient killing of tumor cells” must be supported by
sufficient experimental data. To verify the advantage of FA-Au-Ag NMW for tumor targeting, the confocal microscopy should
be used to image and quantitatively analyze the colocalization of the material and the nucleus, using FA modified Au–Ag
nanowires without mushroom caps as the control. To demonstrate the efficient killing of tumor cells, more quantitatively
studies should be provided. Cellular viability experiments by CCK-8 should be carried out for concentration-dependent of
FA-Au-Ag NMW, with or without laser irradiation, and with or without FA modification, etc. 
5. The methodology of all cellular experiments are missing, the author should add them in supporting information. 
6. The author claimed the HAADF-STEM images in Figure 1f, 2n, 2p, 3c, 3j, are actually not HAADF-STEM images. 
7. The author claimed green and yellow fluorescence in Figure 4g, but the images only show green fluorescence. 
8. The introduction presented general discussion of nanomaterials, optical properties, and biomedical applications without a
clear logical sequence. It should be restructured to progress from current limitations in PL metallic nanostructures to
rationale for the nanomushroom design, and then to the key findings and implications of this study. 
Minor Comments 
9. Clarify terminology: “complex metal nanostructures,” “complex metal nanoparticles,” and “nanomushrooms” appear to
describe different systems. It is recommended to unify them or define them early. 
10. Line 14: “facile physics method” should be revised to “facile physical approach.” 
11. Line 61: correct “high-capcaity” to “high-capacity.” 
12. Line 90: correct “According the element mapping” to “According to the element mapping.”

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This work presents the preparation of Au-Ag alloy nanomushrooms nanowire (Au-Ag NMW) for dual-functional applications
in tumor cell tracking and killing. The concept is innovative, and the proposed nanomaterial demonstrates potential in both
fluorescence tracking and photothermal therapy, which could have promising implications in the field of nanomedicine.
However, I have significant concerns regarding both the scientific rigor and the presentation of the work. I strongly
recommend a major revision before this manuscript could be considered for publication. Some questions are listed as
follows. 
1.The high-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of Au-Ag alloy nanomushrooms on nanowires in
Figure 2i-m are unclear and needed to be improved. 
2.How can the high content of oxygen (O) in Figure 2p be explained? 
3.Please explain why only some of the Au-Ag nanoparticles transform into Au-Ag nanomushrooms after electron beam
irradiation in Figure 3a. 
4.The authors should include information on the distribution of sulfur (S) of the FA-Au-Ag NMW in Figure 4b. 
5.The authors should improve the characterization of the photothermal performance towards Au-Ag NMW, including
photothermal heating curves of Au-Ag NMW at different concentrations under the irradiation of LED with different power
densities. Additionally, Figure 4j shows that the temperature of Au-Ag NMW solution only increase to 40.3°C under LED
irradiation, which does not seem to support claims of excellent photothermal effects. 
6.When discussing the targeting of tumor cells by FA-Au-Ag NMW, the authors should provide a wider field of view to
observe more tumor cells. 
7.In Figure 5a, the red fluorescence in the treated group is unclear, making it difficult to demonstrate the significant apoptosis
of tumor cells. 
8.In Figure 5f, the expression of heat shock proteins in the FA-Au-Ag NMW+Light group is significantly higher than in other
groups, which may be detrimental to the photothermal treatment of tumor cells. Please explain this observation. 
9.The author should evaluate the biocompatibility of FA-Au-Ag NMW. 
10.There are numerous writing errors throughout the manuscript, such as "Au-AAg alloy Nanom ushmroom" in Figure 1b,
"Au-Ag alloy cluster nanood" in Figure 2a, and "Au-Ag alloy Nanocluste" in Figure 3b. Please thoroughly review the
manuscript for such errors. 
11.There are many detailed errors in the figures. For instance, the color of the arrows in the TEM images does not match the
corresponding descriptions in the text, and the fluorescence color in the cells does not match the text description in Figure
4g. Please thoroughly review the manuscript for such errors. 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The study reports the synthesis of folic-acid-modified Au–Ag alloy nanomushroom nanowires capable of selectively
targeting folate receptor–positive tumor cells. These nanostructures exhibit strong photoluminescence, enhanced
conductivity, and notable photothermal conversion under light irradiation, enabling simultaneous fluorescence imaging and
cancer cell ablation. Overall, the work proposes a multifunctional platform combining targeted imaging and mild
photothermal therapy, though the mechanistic details and quantitative validations require further clarification. Please see my
comments below: 
1. The authors are encouraged to report detailed electron-beam parameters—such as accelerating voltage, current density,
and total irradiation dose—to verify that the observed lattice reconstruction is not simply a beam-induced artifact. 
2. It would be valuable to perform quantitative STEM-EDS or EELS line scans across the reconstructed lattice to confirm
Au–Ag alloy formation and provide precise atomic ratios at different irradiation stages. 
3. Clear indexing of SAED or FFT patterns showing the evolution of lattice constants would strengthen the claim of structural
transformation from separate Au and Ag lattices to a unified alloy phase. 
4. The study should test whether the alloy structure remains stable after stopping the electron beam to rule out transient
beam damage or reversible changes. 
5. Quantitative time-resolved EDX mapping is recommended to correlate the volume growth of nanomushrooms with the
depletion of Ag in the nanowire, validating the atom migration hypothesis. 
6. Since TEM transparency can arise from thickness variations, thickness mapping or electron tomography would help
confirm that the transparent region indeed corresponds to Ag loss. 
7. Control experiments using pure Au and pure Ag nanowires under identical irradiation conditions would clarify whether Ag
migration is the key driving factor behind the observed phenomenon. 
8. Statistical validation of Figure 2q should include the number of samples analyzed, standard deviations, and error margins
to confirm that the growth truly stabilizes after 10 seconds. 
9. More detail on AFM-based conductivity measurements—such as bias voltage, contact mode, and calibration method—
would make the conductivity comparison more reliable. 
10. It is advised to verify the “three orders of magnitude” conductivity increase using an independent technique, e.g., a four-
point probe or conductive AFM with controlled tip pressure. 
11. Discussion is needed on whether improved conductivity results from true alloying effects, enhanced contact area, or
possible beam-induced carbon deposition on the surface. 
12. For photothermal conversion, the inclusion of direct experimental temperature-rise curves and calculated efficiencies

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

under defined laser powers would substantiate the claim of enhanced photothermal performance. 
13. Providing absolute quantum yield values and PL lifetimes would help distinguish plasmon-enhanced fluorescence from
alloy defect–related emission. 
14. The emission wavelength discrepancy between the earlier 684 nm red emission and the current 560 nm peak should be
explained—possibly arising from differing growth stages or alloy compositions. 
15. Quantification of folic acid surface coverage and uniformity across several nanowires would support claims of dense
ligand attachment. 
16. The variation in fluorescence color (green and yellow) with electron beam irradiation time should be analyzed more
systematically — include excitation–emission spectra, lifetime measurements, and compositional correlation to explain
emission tunability. 
17. The photothermal heating data (40 °C) are below the typical apoptosis-inducing threshold (~45 °C). The authors should
discuss alternative non-thermal mechanisms of cell death, if any, or extend irradiation duration/power to achieve effective
PTT temperature. 
18. It is unclear how a temperature of only 40 °C resulted in significant cell death. The authors might explore reactive oxygen
species (ROS) formation, electron transfer, or plasmon-induced chemical effects as possible causes of apoptosis. 
19. The authors should specify the light source parameters used for irradiation (wavelength range, power density, duration,
irradiation area) to enable reproducibility of the photothermal experiments. 
20. The claim of “exceptional targeting functionality” should be tempered unless supported by quantitative receptor
competition data and surface coverage characterization of FA ligands. 
21. The photothermal simulation and experimental data should be plotted together to validate the model predictions;
discrepancies can reveal overlooked parameters such as heat dissipation or optical scattering.

biologically relevant and mild irradiation conditions. Due to heat dissipation into the

temperature increase is inherently limited and does not dire ctly reflect the local 
photothermal intensity at the nanoparticle–cell interface.  
Therefore, we employed nanoscale infrared spectroscopy coupled with atomic force 
microscopy (AFM-IR) to directly characterize the photothermal conversion behavior 
of the nanomushroom structures in Au-Ag NWMs and their adjacent regions (Fig. R3). 
By mapping the electric field density distribution, we were able to indirectly evaluate 
the conversion efficiency of incident light at the nanomushroom sites. Specifically, 
upon resonant coupling with electrons in the nanostructures, incident light is confined 
into high-density localized fields, with the majority of its energy converted into thermal 
energy. 
In this study, we systematically compared the localized field densities between  
regions decorated with nanomushrooms and those devoid of nanomushrooms on Au -
Ag NWMs. The results revealed that the localized field intensity at the nanomushroom 
sites was over 200-fold higher than that at the non-decorated regions (Fig. R3(b)). These 
experiments further confirmed that localized photothermal conversion is confined to 
the nanomushroom domains (with an area of approximately tens of square nanometers), 
which can be approximated as nanoscale point sources generating localized high -
temperature regions. In contrast, the photothermal conversion efficiency and 
temperature values measured for the entire Au-Ag NWM nanostructures represent only 
the macroscopic average of the overall thermal effect, rather than the localized thermal 
response at the nanomushroom sites.

and confinement sites. Under high -dose electron beam irradiation, Ag atoms in the 
nanowire are activated and become mobile. These activated Ag atoms tend to migrate 
toward regions with lower local Ag atomic density, such as intrinsic defects or stress -
concentrated sites within the Ag nanowire. 
At these defect sites, Ag atoms accumul ate and undergo localized volumetric 
expansion. Owing to the presence of a highly ductile and deformable Au layer on the 
nanowire surface, the accumulated Ag is confined within the Au coverage and expands 
outward, leading to the characteristic “nanomushroom” morphology. In contrast, 
regions without sufficient defects or suitable Au confinement do not support this 
localized mass transport and expansion.
