Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an approach to low-energy manganese photoredox catalysis using in-situ assembly of Mn(II/III)
salts, ligands, and reagents. The potential shift from high-energy (blue light) to low-energy (NIR, 850 nm) photocatalysis for
generating N-centered radicals and enabling alkene functionalizations (hydroazidation, hydroxyazidation) is significant. The
demonstration of two distinct transformations—anti-Markovnikov hydroazidation and aerobic hydroxyazidation under
different light conditions showcases the versatility and tunability of the system. However, several critical issues must be
addressed before the manuscript can be considered for publication. 
1. For the hydroxyazidation reaction under Condition B, it is important to clarify whether the observed reactivity could be
influenced by photothermal heating. The irradiation may lead to significant temperature increases in the reaction mixture, it is
essential to determine whether the transformation is truly driven by photochemical excitation rather than a thermal process.
The authors should provide information on how the reaction temperature was monitored and controlled during irradiation.
Was the temperature maintained constant throughout the experiment (e.g., via external cooling or a thermostated reactor)? If
not, could the observed conversion be partially or fully attributed to localized heating? For example, running the reaction
under identical conditions but in the dark, with heating applied to match the maximum temperature reached during the
photoreaction; Comparing the reaction efficiency under irradiation at different light intensities (and corresponding
temperatures) to assess the contribution of thermal effects. 
2. In the light on/off experiment shown in Figure 2P, the reaction appears to proceed more slowly during the initial irradiation
period (0–3 h) compared to the later stage (6–9 h). Could the authors provide a plausible explanation for this kinetic
behavior? 
3. In the catalyst screening section, the authors have evaluated several Mn(II) and Mn(III) salts. To provide a more
comprehensive understanding of the oxidation state dependence and the scope of manganese species active in this
transformation, it would be valuable to include data on other accessible oxidation states, particularly Mn(I) and Mn(IV)
complexes. 
4. The authors have not experimentally verified the proposed valence state changes of the manganese species during the
catalytic cycle, and have not provided supporting literature evidence for such transitions. Techniques such as XPS of
reaction samples or in-situ/operando spectroscopic methods should be employed to directly monitor and elucidate the
manganese valence states throughout the reaction. 
5. The references cited in the main text require careful verification; for example, there are errors in ref No.7 
and ref No.50 . 
6. The Supporting Information (SI) contains several inconsistencies and inaccuracies that must be addressed. For instance,
the products depicted in "Fig. S5" and "Fig. S7" are incorrect. Additionally, the NMR spectra 10, 12, 16, 28, 47, 54, 55, and
58 require further purification. There are also significant inaccuracies in the NMR data: for example, the ¹³C NMR spectra for
products 67 and 68 are incorrect, and the spectrum for product 78 is erroneous too. Furthermore, inconsistencies in data
formatting are observed, such as mismatched significant figures in the ¹H NMR data of spectra 31 and 32. It is strongly
recommended that the authors carefully verify all data and NMR spectra in the Supporting Information. Additionally,
providing 1–2 sets of single-crystal X-ray diffraction data would be preferable if feasible. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript submitted by Lan, Zhang, Li, Niu and co-workers, and entitled "Evolution of manganese low-energy

photoredox catalysis from high-energy visible light photocatalysis" describes the radical hydroazidation and
hydroxyazidation of alkenes using interesting manganese photocatalysis. The first part focuses on the development of
Mn(II)-based photoredox catalysis to achieve the hydroazidation of non-activated alkenes, which can be considered a more
straightforward manner to test the photochemical properties of coordination transition metal complexes in comparison with
the isolated photocatalysts. The second part is more intriguing, focusing on the development of the aerobic hydroxyazidation
of alkenes in a single step using Mn(acac)3 and visible light at wavelength of 850 nm. The scope of alkenes is broad. It is
exciting that the low-energy photocatalytic strategy indeed has unique advantages compared to the high-energy visible light
catalysis. Overall, this strategy is truly fascinating. Therefore, I recommend this manuscript to be published in Nature
Communications after the minor revision is made. 
Essential revisions: 
1. Can the two Mn-based photochemical systems operate using other azide reagents such as NaN3 and TBAN3? What
about the corresponding yields of hydroazidation and hydroxyazidation of alkenes? 
2. For the reaction conditions B, if CH3CN is replaced by C(CH3)3CN or other common solvents that do have unnegligible
coordinating ability, what is the influence on yields of hydroxyazidation? 
3. Besides the substrates shown in Figure 3 and 4, the authors should examine the reactivity of phenyl acrylate and vinyl
benzoate, which would give some useful insights for the reaction mechanism. 
4. In Figure 5b, it is shown that the good yield of the hydroxyazidation of an alkene bearing the anthraquinone moiety
increases with the utility of Mn-based low-energy photocatalysis. Whereas I do appreciate this result, it would be better to
compare the catalytic performance of 3d metal iron (Carreira JACS 2023 145, 22347−22353; Shi Nat. Commun 2022
13:7880) under the photochemical conditions. 
5. Regarding the mechanistic studies, this reviewer strongly suggest that the authors should use HRMS to detect the
possible intermediates during the initial stage of the two reactions. 
6. Stoichiometric H218O should be added to the reaction system of hydroxyazidation. I want to know whether the -18OH
could be detected. 
7. There are also multiple formatting errors in the references and supplementary information. They are not listed here. It is
hoped that they will be carefully revised. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Song and co-workers report a practical manganese-photocatalyzed radical hydroazidation and
hydroxyazidation of alkenes, and they establish a paradigm that modular in-situ assembly of readily available Mn catalysts,
TMSN
₃
 and/or bipyridyl ligands can rapidly unlock an effective Mn-based low-energy photoredox system (near-infrared, λ =
850 nm) from the corresponding high-energy visible-light precursor (blue, λ = 440 nm). In the introduction, the authors
highlight the advantages of preparing photoactive metal complexes in situ through combination of a first-row transition metal
and a ligand. The work is divided into two parts: the first part showcases the development of Mn(II)-based photoredox
catalysis that under 440 nm visible light irradiation and using water as the H-source, enables selective anti-Markovnikov
hydroazidation of unactivated alkenes; the second part shows how to quickly unlock an intriguing manganese-based low-
energy photoredox catalytic system for the direct aerobic hydroxyazidation of alkenes. Considering all the above, I think this
work does contain the expected conceptual novelty to justify its publication in Nature Communications. Several minor
revisions should be made before the formal acceptance. 
(1) Figure 1 shows that the in situ assembly strategy proposed by the authors involves forming light-absorbing complexes
through the in-situ assembly of metal salts, ligands, and solvents, which release radical species upon photoexcitation.
However, in the reactions achieved using low-energy light, the authors did not employ ligands. This distinction should be
clarified in the introduction. 
(2) In Figures 2A–2D, the authors measured UV-Vis data using DCE as the solvent, but the actual reactions were conducted
in a mixed solvent system of DCE and water. To ensure experimental rigor and comprehensiveness, I recommend that the
authors supplement the UV-Vis data using either water or a DCE/water mixture to verify or rule out the potential influence of
water on the system's light absorption. Additionally, the corresponding UV-Vis experiments for Mn(acac)
₃
 should also
include the data obtained with HFIP or CH
₃
CN/HFIP as the solvent. 
(3) Although both reactions demonstrate good substrate compatibility with alkenes, I still recommend that the authors present
some unsuccessful substrate examples in SI to provide readers with a more comprehensive understanding. 
(4) The following key references should be included for completeness: 10.1021/jacs.4c18366 and 10.1038/s41557-024-
01446-8. 
(5) I noticed several grammatical errors, which I won't enumerate here, but I recommend the authors conduct a thorough and
meticulous proofreading of the entire text.

Our Response: We sincerely  thank the reviewer for the suggestions. In the revised 
Supplementary Information, we have provided detailed discussions on the valence state 
changes of Mn during the catalytic cycle based on the results of UV −Vis, HRMS, and

XPS experiments (Supplementary Figures 40 and 42). 
1. For the Mn( II)-based blue light photocatalytic system, UV −Vis evidence 
(Supplementary Figure s 13 and 15 ) and radical clock experiment (Supplementary 
Figure 28) demonstrated that blue light irradiation photolyzed the  
Mn(acac)2/L1/TMSN3 mixture under a N2 atmosphere, generating azido radicals. This 
photolysis process is concomitant with a reduction in the manganese valence state from 
+2 to +1.  The HAT process of radical adduct  VIII, which is essential for the 
hydroazidation (Nat. Commun.  2025, 16, 7906 ), is likely mediated by the unstable 
[Mn(I)] intermediate in the presence of the co-solvent H2O (Supplementary Figure 40); 
the transient nature of this [Mn (I)] species accounts for the exclusive detection of 
[Mn(II)] by XPS  (Supplementary Figure 41 ). Therefore, we proposed that the 
manganese valence cycles between +2 and +1 during the hydroazidation reaction 
process.
