Reviewer #1

Review of manuscript #NCOMMS-25-10524 
Title: China’s offshore wind power potential revisited: a realistic appraisal suggests substantially lower estimates than
precedent studies 
The study presents a detailed, farm-level evaluation of offshore wind power potential in China, incorporating realistic turbine
layouts derived from remote sensing data, wake loss modeling, and future climate scenarios. The findings also show that
China’s offshore wind potential is significantly lower than previous estimates. The study is very relevant to the current
understanding of wind energy potential and its projected impact on the overall energy mix. The underprediction of potential
is an important realization when it comes to planning and policy activities, not just for the studied country, but also for other
countries with very aggressive approach to adoption of offshore wind. The manuscript is very understandable and well
written in terms of English language and style. Despite this, the overall presentation of the manuscript does not facilitate the
reader in understanding the discussion and context. 
Major reorganization of the manuscript sections is necessary due to the following observations: 
1. The Materials and Methods section is presented at the end of the paper. This is an awkward position since the discussion
refers to this section as early as line #98 of page 3. Since the section is an important part of the building up of the study
storyline, it has to be put near the start so that the reader is not forced to shuffle between the first few pages and the last
pages just to understand the topic being discussed. Presenting it earlier will avoid questions being raised due to incomplete
setting of the stage. 
2. The manuscript cross references multiple figures (S1, S2, etc.) that are part of the supplementary document. This is not
appropriate since the paper needs to be intact and should stand on its own. Supplemental information may help in extended
appreciation of the subject matter but may not be necessary to make the paper whole. If the authors' desire is to make the
cross references intimate parts of the arguments and analysis, these figures should be integral parts of the paper, not
supplemental information. 
3. Most of the figures are composite images of a lot of information. It is suggested that these figures be deconstructed into
more manageable illustrations of ideas as they are more understandable piecewise. Having multiple ideas illustrated and
combined into one figure also causes these illustrations to have very tiny components, including the text within. They have
become unreadable and difficult to understand even when maximised across the width of the paper. These should be
revised so that the figures are separate. 
4. The authors refer to "vertical windward spacing" multiple times. What does this mean relative to "windward spacing"? Do
they mean lateral/crosswind spacing? "Vertical spacing" does not make sense without proper reference to either cardinal
directions, or wind directions. A better term should be used instead of this. 
5. Is the "Discussion" section (line 308, page 8) the conclusion section? It reads like that. The conclusion section should be
properly labeled so that there is closure in the paper. 
The authors present a cost model considering water depth and distance to shore and conclude that LCOE is significantly

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents a comprehensive analysis of China’s offshore wind power potential, incorporating wake loss
effects, economic feasibility, and technical constraints. The study is relevant and provides valuable insights into wind energy
research. 
The authors compare three wake loss models and select wind turbines based on existing wind farms, enhancing the
practical relevance of their approach. They utilize MERRA-2 reanalysis data along with a bias-corrected CMIP6 dataset for
dynamic downscaling. While the inclusion of bias correction improves reliability, further clarification on its methodology and
effectiveness in reducing model biases would strengthen the study. Additionally, a more detailed discussion of how their
findings compare with previous literature, sensitivity analyses of wake loss models, and transparency in economic
assumptions would enhance credibility. While the methodology appears sound, additional details on wake modeling and
economic constraints would improve reproducibility. 
Overall, the study is significant, but revisions are needed to clarify validation approaches, and methodological transparency
before publication.

perspective on the novelty of your work, rather than focusing solely on differences in TWh/year, which are largely influenced

(Remarks to the Author) 
This paper makes a valuable and timely contribution to the literature on renewable energy resource assessments. By
integrating high-resolution remote sensing data, realistic turbine layouts, multiple wake modeling approaches, and future
climate scenarios, the study provides one of the most comprehensive and realistic farm-level evaluations of China’s offshore
wind potential to date. The finding that potential is considerably lower than earlier estimates—primarily due to reduced
usable area and more accurate wake loss modeling—represents an important correction that will help policymakers and
planners set more achievable energy targets. 
I particularly commend the authors for the careful attention they paid in addressing reviewer feedback. The inclusion of a
structured comparative table detailing methodological differences with prior studies significantly enhances transparency and
highlights the novelty of their approach. Similarly, the addition of real-world turbine layout examples from Chinese offshore
wind farms, along with open access shapefiles, greatly strengthens the credibility and reproducibility of the results. The
revisions to cost modeling, including the explicit treatment of soft CapEx elements, array cable costs, and financial
assumptions (WACC, FCR), bring the economic analysis in line with best practices in the field and ensure robustness.
Clarifying assumptions on distances, foundation costs, and substructure cost projections further adds to the methodological
rigor. 
The study’s results—showing lower overall potential but more realistic assessments of technical and economic feasibility,
particularly for floating wind in deep-water regions—will be of high value for both the research community and energy
policymakers. By carefully distinguishing between bottom-fixed and floating offshore wind, the authors provide actionable
insights into the trade-offs between technical potential, spatial constraints, and costs. 
Overall, this is a well-revised manuscript that makes a meaningful and original contribution to offshore wind energy research,
and I support its publication.

We sincerely appreciate the reviewer's careful evaluation and feedback on our work. We fully 
understand the reviewer's observation that "the impact of water depth and distance to shore on 
LCOE" and "higher capacity factors do not necessarily correspond to lower LCOE" are well -
established concepts. However, we would like to further clarify the substantive contributions 
of this study and explain why these seemingly "obvious" factors still present significant 
potential for misjudgment in practical assessment and policy related decision-making. 
1. Comprehensive empirical analysis of depth and distance effects on LCOE. While water 
depth and distance to shore are indeed recognized variables in LCOE models, our contribution 
lies in systematically quantifying their impacts through high-resolution geographic 
observational data covering the entire national territory, based on a detailed  farm-level 
modeling. We developed and validated a comprehensive LCOE cost model that distinguishes 
between bottom -fixed and floating wind turbines while incorporating multiple climate 
scenarios and wind speed data (see Fig. 4A-D and SI Fig. S7 -S9). These analyses reveal a 
critical nonlinear characteristic: increased offshore distance enhances wind speeds but 
simultaneously increases water depth, leading to rapidly escalating installation, operation and 
maintenance, and transmission costs. Different regions (such as Fujian versus Guangdong) 
show significant ly different LCOE values under similar capacity factors, precisely due to 
variations in geographic -economic parameters (detailed comparison in Fig. 4C and Fig. S6 
overlay analysis). Therefore, we argue that the "apparent existence" of these cost factors is not 
equal to "adequate quantification," thus  we address this gap by providing nationwide cost 
mapping based on real-world remote sensing and GIS data, offering important benchmark for 
regional potential assessment and cost quantification. 
2. Systematic validation of the non- monotonic relationship between capacity factor and 
LCOE. We fully agree with the reviewer that LCOE inherently considers capacity factor effects 
already. However, our work provides the first farm -level empirical evidence across thousands 
of wind farms throughout China, clearly demonstrating the geographic distribution patterns of 
this non-monotonic relationship. For instance, in the comparative analysis of Fig. S6 and Fig. 
4, we found that in Zhejiang and Fujian provinces, higher capacity factors indeed result in lower 
LCOE, while in Guangdong and Shandong provinces, despite high wind speeds and capacity 
factors, LCOE remains elevated due to greater water depths and distances from shore. Again, 
such "geographic counterexamples" have practical implications for real-world energy planning 
and investment decision making, indicating that capacity factor or wind speed alone cannot 
serve as preliminary screening criteria and must be analyzed in conjunction with LCOE spatial 
heterogeneity. 
In conclusion, while the theoretical relationships between these factors and LCOE might have 
been qualitatively established, our study further bridges the knowledge gap between conceptual
