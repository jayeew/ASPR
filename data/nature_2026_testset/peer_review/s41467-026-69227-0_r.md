Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript describes a link between the production of sclerostin by bone cells in response to adrenergic signaling and
beiging of inguinal fat tissues. Although the reported effects are somewhat small, the study is well documented and
convincing. 
Specific comments 
• The increase in beiging markers shown in figure 1b appears inconsistent between the 2 mutant samples. Additional
samples should be shown. 
• Stronger evidence of a direct effect of Sost on Wnt signaling in adipocytes should be shown. Likewise, showing that
downstream signaling of Adrb3 is affected by Sost/Wnt signaling would strengthen the data. 
• To allow a better comparison between the in vivo and in vitro studies, the same parameters should be assessed;
adrenergic expression should be quantified in the cultured cells and Sost-associated Wnt signaling should be studied in
iWAT samples. 
• The effect of CL316,243 on the ObDSost mice should be shown in the main figure, at least for weight and fat mass. This is
an important result.

Reviewer #1 
This study evaluates the response of Sclerostin-deficient mice to a series of challenges to the thermoregulatory system. It proposes that 
these mice respond to these challenges with increased adipocyte beiging compared to wildtype control mice. Considering that 
Sclerostin acts by modulating -catenin regulatory activity it then analyzes a postnatal adipocyte-specific constitutively active model for 
this factor and shows that it also blocks the effect of thermoneutrality on fat.  
The manuscript presents a very large amount of data using multiple models resulting in a comprehensive analysis. It, however, does 
not bring significant advances on the biology of adipocyte beiging or the role of Sclerostin and beta-catenin in this process, especially in 
the light of previous studies. The specific effects observed upon treatment/housing challenges are often small and it is not clear how 
much they can be primarily attributed to sclerostin. The observation that control mice significantly respond to CL316,243 treatment and 
cold exposure despite an increase in Sclerostin levels weakens the notion that this factor is a major player. To address this point more 
rigorously, the changes in treatment response of mutant compared to wt mice would need to be normalized as mutant mice already 
have a phenotype. Analyzing a gain-of-function or Sclerostin infusion model would also be important since such models have previously 
been shown to also increase adipocyte beiging. 
 
We thank reviewer #1 for the evaluation of our manuscript.   
 
While we appreciate the reviewer’s viewpoint, we disagree about the significance of our manuscript.  Our work clearly demonstrates a 
link between bone and adipose tissue that has the potential for therapeutic manipulation.  Moreover, our data suggest that sclerostin 
can be temporally controlled by an adipose to bone relay. 
 
The reviewer is correct that the Sost-/- mice start with a baseline phenotype, which is unavoidable in nearly all studies using transgenic 
mice.  We attempted to minimize the impact of the baseline phenotypes whenever possible.  For example, the thermoneutrality studies 
performed in Figure 3 were started before the adipose phenotype is present.  For the CL316,243 treatment studies were errored on the 
side of treating young mice as the adipose tissue phenotypes becomes more dramatic as the mice age.  Finally, the romosozumab 
studies in Figure 6 were performed in C57BL/6 mice wherein the phenotype should be similar after the high fat diet lead-in period.  
While some of the effect sizes could be considered small it is important to point out that the treatment periods in our study were general 
fairly short (i.e. 10days for CL316,243 treatment). 
 
Unfortunately, we were unable develop a suitable gain-of-function model in a reasonable amount of time.  Our prior work used an 
adeno-associated virus to direct sclerostin over-production but we were not able to quickly obtain sufficient quantities of the virus and 
regulatory approval to do these experiments for the present study.  However, we added new experiments to figure 1 and 2 where we 
treated stromal vascular cells that had been differentiated to adipocytes with recombinant sclerostin and CL316,243.  These in vitro 
studies indicated that sclerostin is able to reduce the effects of -adrenergic signaling.  Additionally, we added data in supplementary 
figure 3 that shows an osteoblast/osteocyte-specific Sost knockout exhibits the same phenotypic responses as the Sost-/- mice.  We 
hope the reviewer will agree that this solidifies our interpretation of the data.  
 
Additional points: 
 Fig. 2C,D do not show a significant effect, these data should not be emphasized and should be moved to supplemental data. Likewise 
for Fig. 3C. 
 
To address the comments from the other reviewers it was necessary to increase the sample size in much of figure 2.  In doing so, a 
significant effect of CL316,243 was observed on bone mass.   As a result, we updated figure 2C and 2D and kept them in the main 
figures.  Figure 3D was moved to the supplementary material since the bone volume only trended downward in the control mice (likely a 
result of the short exposure period). 
 
 It is unclear that CL316,243 treatment and cold exposure affect Sost expression/levels specifically or if the decreases observed 
reflects a more general effect on osteoclast biology. The same can be said for the thermoneutrality experiment. Other markers of these 
cells should be analyzed. 
 
This is an important point that we have attempted to further emphasize in the manuscript.   Data in figure 5 shows that Sost gene 
expression in bone and serum sclerostin levels can be acutely regulated by thermogenic and 3-adrenergic signaling.  In these studies, 
Sost expression was examined within 6 hours of stimulus exposure.  At this time point the number of osteoblastic cells is unlikely to 
have changes.  We now also show that 10 days of CL316,243 reduces markers of osteoblast proliferation and the RANKL/OPG ratio. 
 
 The beiging of adipocytes should be analyzed by a quantitative assessment of mitochondrial proteins in addition to the presented 
histological images. Again, given the presence of a phenotype in mutant mice, it is difficult to assess their response to challenges 
compared to the one of wt mice. 
 
We have added both a quantification of the % multilocular adipocytes to multiple figures as well as a quantification of mitochondrial 
proteins for the 10 day CL316,243 experiment in figure 2.  
 
 Given that the expression of adrenergic receptors on iWAT cells is not changed, the mechanism of Sclerostin effect on their 
adrenergic response to stimulation should be further analyzed. 
 
 
This is fair point and one that we hope to address with additional studies.  In this manuscript we elected to focus on the mechanism by 
which 3-adrenergic signals regulate sclerostin expression.  Further analysis of the mechanism of action in adipose tissue will require

Reviewer #2 
This is an interesting paper on the impact of sclerostin and sclerostin inhibition on adipocyte biology, including adipocyte tissue beiging. 
In previous studies, sclerostin, an osteocyte-derived inhibitor of Wnt signaling, was found to concurrently suppress bone formation and 
stimulating adipose tissue accumulation. Since sclerostin-deficient mice developed pockets of multilocular adipocytes in subcutaneous 
adipose, the authors extended their studies on the impact of sclerostin on thermogenic and 3-adrenergic stimuli-induced beiging of 
white adipose tissue. In the present study, they discovered a control loop, whereby 3-adrenergic agonists via the adipocytes induce 
bone expression in bone and elevate sclerostin levels. Sclerostin acted on the white adipose tissue and inhibited beiging. SOST-
deficient mice had a lower fat mass and more beige adipocytes after treatment with the 3-adrenergic agonist or cold exposure. 
Combined exposure to the 3-adrenergic agonist and a sclerostin-blocking antibody synergistically improved body composition and 
metabolic parameters in a mouse model, where obesity had been induced by a high-fat diet. 
Overall, this study reports novel and exciting findings. The design and the experiments were very well performed. The data are robust, 
and the conclusions are backed by experimental evidence. I have a few minor comments: 
 
We thank reviewer #2 for the positive evaluation of our manuscript and were encouraged that the reviewer felt the study reports novel 
and exciting findings.   
 
1. Introduction. This section reads somewhat narrow. Sclerostin and sclerostin inhibition have effects on more tissues than just bone 
and fat tissue. The potential role on neural cells and the cardiovascular system could be included here. 
 
 
Thank you for the suggestion.  We have added a sentence to paragraph 4 of the introduction and the first paragraph of the results 
section to highlight effects in the cardiovascular system and the new study in the brain.  We thought this was a good compromise to 
bring attention to other sights of action, without distracting from the focus of our study.   
 
2. The bone phenotyping is limited to micro-computed CT. Were histology or histomorphometry performed? 
 
Since the effects of sclerostin on bone was not a major focus of our manuscript, we did not perform histological or histomorphometric 
studies on the bone samples.   However, we further examined gene expression in the femur, using the same samples we used to 
examine Sost expression, and observed a reduction in cyclin D mRNA levels and altered RankL/OPG ratio after CL316,243 treatment.  
This data can be found in Supple. Figure 2A and 2B. 
 
3. Did the mice that were exposed to the human antibody romosozumab develop auto-antibodies or was that taken into account and 
countered by the fairly high dose and short interval of romo administration? 
 
The mice were indeed treated with the humanized romosozumab antibody.  While we did not directly examine the development of 
autoantibodies, we did not observe any signs of an immune response.  For instance, spleen size, which would be expected to increase 
if the mice were mounting a response to romosozumab, was identical in vehicle and romosozumab treated mice.   Similar results were 
obtained in separate studies where mice were treated with vehicle or romosozumab for 12 weeks.   The dosing of romosozumab 
(30mg/kg weekly) in this study was selected to be similar to that used in mouse studies using the preclinical antibody.  
 
4. Is there evidence that women with osteoporosis that are treated with romosozumab (120 mg/4 weeks) for 12 months experience 
benefits in terms of body composition or metabolic parameters? 
 
This is an excellent question and one that we have also been curious about.  To the best of our knowledge metabolic parameters or 
measures of body composition were not endpoints in the romosozumab clinical trials and were not reported on in the related 
publications.  We inquired about such data several years ago but our request was dismissed by the company.  We hope to design a 
study that will answer this question in the near future.  
 
 
Reviewer #3 
The authors present a very interesting model whereby sclerostin functions to inhibit adipocyte beiging as induced by beta3-adrenergic 
stimulation. Conversely, beta3-adrenergic stimulation and free fatty acid production by adipocytes increases sclerostin production, 
possibly setting up a fat to bone signaling loop. The study is well written, however, there are concerns with the transparency of the data

directly contradicted by what appears to be an excellent publication showing that sclerostin can promote (rather than inhibit) beiging by 
downregulating Wnt signaling (PMC5525330). This point needs to be clarified and discussed by the authors. With suitable revision this 
work would be of high interest to the readers of Nature Metabolism. Additional recommendations are provided below.

further improved the manuscript.

This manuscript describes a link between the production of sclerostin by bone cells in response to adrenergic 
signaling and beiging of inguinal fat tissues. Although the reported effects are somewhat small, the study is well

is viewed positively.  We acknowledge that in some instances the effect sizes can be somewhat small.  With this in 
mind, we think it is important to recognize that many of the treatment protocols were carried out over a short amount 
of time (i.e. 10 days of CL316,243, 14 days of cold exposure).  Longer treatment periods might have resulted in larger 
effects sizes for organ size, etc, but we tried to balance this with the histological phenotype which we think were 
actually quite profound.  Similarly, we think it is quite striking that thermoneutrality could reverse the metabolic 
phenotypes.
