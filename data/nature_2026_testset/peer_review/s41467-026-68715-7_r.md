Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript titled "Unraveling magnetic field mediated tuning mechanism for dynamic thermal management under
variable operating conditions" focuses on developing a magnetic field-based contactless tuning strategy to regulate the heat
transfer performance of phase change material (PCM)-based systems, aiming to address the inadequate adaptability of
conventional thermal management approaches under diverse operating conditions. The study introduces Fe3O4@carbon
nanotube (CNT) nanoparticles as thermally conductive reinforcements into n-Eicosane to form magnetic composite PCMs,
investigates the mesoscale aggregation behavior of nanoparticles under magnetic fields, and verifies the performance
through simulations and experiments. Overall, the research direction is of practical significance for dynamic thermal
management of high-performance electronic systems. I have the following concerns: 
1, The approach of enhancing the thermal conductivity of composite materials by combining magnetic particles with
thermally conductive fillers and utilizing magnetic fields to achieve the orientation of thermally conductive fillers is not
innovative. What are the innovations of the experimental method adopted in this study for composite preparation? The
authors should emphasize thin in the introduction. 
2, For the SEM characterization, only the qualitative description that "nanoparticles are uniformly distributed" is provided,
while quantitative analysis data (e.g., particle size distribution, aggregate size statistics) are lacking. It is necessary to
supplement statistical results (e.g., analysis via ImageJ or other image processing tools) to more rigorously validate the
dispersibility of nanoparticles . 
3, Has the thermal resistance among thermally conductive fillers, magnetic particles, and PCMs been considered? In the
simulation, the "thermal conductivity of CNTs in the dense-packed mat state" is employed to correct the overestimation
caused by the perfect interface assumption. However, the basis for selecting this thermal conductivity value (e.g., whether it
is derived from specific literature or experimental measurements) is not clarified. It is essential to specify the rationality of
parameter selection and conduct a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of this parameter on the simulation results . 
4, The study achieves thermal resistance tuning by adjusting the tilt angle of the magnetic field, but it fails to explain the
correlation mechanism between the aggregation structure of Fe3O4@CNT nanoparticles and the heat conduction path from
a microscopic perspective. For instance, how does the magnetic field direction specifically affect the orientation of CNTs,
thereby altering phonon transmission efficiency? It is recommended to supplement the evolution process of heat conduction
paths at the nanoscale through molecular dynamics simulations or phonon spectrum analysis. Additionally, the article
mentions a "1.8-fold thermal resistance tuning ratio"; it is necessary to clarify the correlation between this value and
magnetic field strength (e.g., a curve illustrating the tuning effect under different magnetic field strengths) to verify the
sensitivity of the proposed method to magnetic field parameters . 
5, The article only states that "the reliability of dynamic tuning is confirmed via cycling tests," but the number of cycles is
insufficient. For long-term service scenarios of electronic devices, the cyclic stability of the material is critical. It is
recommended to supplement characterization results, including DSC curves, thermal conductivity changes, and
nanoparticle aggregation structures, after at least 100 thermal cycles. Meanwhile, it is necessary to evaluate whether the
magnetic saturation properties of Fe3O4 degrade under long-term magnetic field exposure and its impact on tuning

performance . 
6, In the low-temperature standby state, the core mechanism for achieving thermal insulation with a 90° tilt angle is that the
"horizontally distributed heat transfer network suppresses vertical heat loss." However, the reason why this structure reduces

thermal insulation time reported in the article meet practical application requirements?

【Reply】: 
We appreciate the reviewer's keen observation, which allows us to clarify a subtle 
but important point.  
【Part 1】: Apparent contradiction in Fig. 5b. 
We are sorry for not describing clearly in the previous submission, but the 
temperature plotted in Fig. 5b is the temperature of heat source rather than the 
temperature distribution within the PCM composites. The captions of Fig. 5a and 5b 
have been revised to include the missing information: 
“a Infrared thermal images of the thermal buffer which are  recorded within one 
operating cycle. The results obtained when applying original composite PCM are 
compared with those achieved through the dynamic magnetic field regulation approach. 
b Transient temperature variation properties of electronic devices through continuous 
operating cycles. Analyzed by contrasting the original composite PCM with the 
dynamic magnetic field regulation approach.” 
It is correct that higher thermal conductivity might result in faster heat propagation 
in the PCM, ultimately leading to a slower temperature rise at the heat source. We thank 
the reviewer for this insightful observation. The apparent contradiction arises from a 
critical distinction between the local temperature at the heat source  and the heat 
dissipation capability of the thermal buffer. The faster initial temperature rise for the 
composite PCM with randomly dispersed nanoparticles is, in fact, a direct consequence 
of its inferior vertical heat diffusion, not superior performance.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

The key to understanding this lies in the thermal diffusivity (α = k/ρcp), which 
governs the rate at which heat propagates through a material. In the 0°-aligned 
configuration, the magnetically induced vertical networks create pathways of high 
effective thermal diffusivity, enabling heat to be rapidly drawn away from the heat 
source and distributed throughout the PCM volume. This efficient spatial distribution 
of thermal energy results in a slower temperature rise rate at the heat source. 
In our case, the ‘composite PCM’ curve in Fig. 5b represents the sample without 
an applied magnetic field, where nanoparticles are randomly dispersed. The lack of 
directed pathways leads to a lower effective thermal diffusivity in the vertical direction. 
The heat from the source cannot be efficiently dissipated, leading to severe heat 
localization and a steeper temperature gradient right at the bottom (as clearly seen in 
the thermal image of Fig. 5a). This localization causes a faster temperature rise at the 
measurement point, even though the overall heat dissipation is poor. Once the aligned 
structure is fully active, its superior vertical heat conduction dominates, leading to the 
eventual lower maximum temperature, as seen in Fig. 5b. We have clarified this 
dynamic initialization process in the revised manuscript on Page 19. 
Added text:  “It is revealed that the composite PCM with randomly dispersed 
nanoparticles exhibits a faster temperature rise than the sample with magnetic field 
regulation. This is attributed to the difference in the effective thermal diffusivity of the 
system along the vertical direction. The composite PCM possesses lower vertical 
thermal diffusivity, causing heat to accumulate locally at the bottom and resulting in a 
more rapid local temperature rise, which indicates inferior heat dissipation performance. 
In contrast, 0°-aligned sample rapidly distributes heat away from the source due to its 
high-diffusivity vertical pathways, thereby moderating the source's temperature 
increase.” 
This interpretation is robustly supported by the direct measurement of anisotropic 
thermal conductivity (response to Q1) and the local thermal resistance analysis in Fig. 
5d, which unequivocally show the enhanced heat spreading in the 0°-aligned state after 
the initial phase. 
【Part 2】: Physical reasonableness of the ~11 °C difference. 
Fig. 5b primarily depicts the temperature variation over time at the simulated heat 
source. The ~11°C temperature difference is physically reasonable, and we may verify 
the result with the following assumptions: 
 Fixed heat flux, q = 5000 W/m2. 
 Claimed thermal resistance switching ratio, ~1.8. 
 Assume R0 is the average thermal resistance in the aligned state (0°). The thermal 
resistance for original composite PCM is ~1.8×R0. 
 Assume that during a heating cycle, the temperature at the heat source location 
rises to T1 and T2 respectively for dynamic regulated PCM and original composite 
PCMs without magnetic field control. The additional temperature difference due to 
the higher resistance is (T2- T1) = q×∆R = 0.8×q×R0. 
During the dynamic testing, the average temperature difference between the heat 
source (rising from 20 to 75 ℃) and the environment (constant low temperature: 5 ℃)
