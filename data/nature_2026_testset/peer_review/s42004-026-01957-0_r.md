Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Nikesh et al. report the construction of aggregates composed of polyionic lapidated flavonoids (LFs) and the dynamic
interactions of the LFs with lipid membranes, which offer a unique opportunity to explore the aggregate materials as antiviral
applications. Simple coacervates like this example have recently attracted increasing attention, and this manuscript is likely
to have a significant impact across a broad range of relevant scientific communities. Overall, the manuscript is well written,
and the findings are compelling. Therefore, the work may be of interest to certain readers of this journal, Communications
Chemistry. However, the reviewer has several concerns that may preclude the immediate acceptance of the manuscript in its
current form. 
Major comments 
-Figure 3: Background intensity for the images of panels B,D,G (and Figure 4, panel C) looks rather high, which indicates the
non-negligible influence of the glass surface on the images, i.e., the properties of the self-assembled structures. This point
should be clarified. 
-Not only fluorescence images but also DIC or phase contrast images should be presented. 
-To claim that the droplets are fluid, fusion (coalescence) events between multiple droplets should be observed. 
-FRAP experiments: Images after photo-bleaching and their recovery should be presented. 
-The concentration of fluorescent dyes (e.g., Rhodamine B, Coumarin-7) should be clearly indicated. Since the
concentration of LFs was low (several tens µM) to obtain aggregates, the influence of the fluorescent dyes on the properties
of the aggregations should be carefully assessed. 
-The cytotoxicity of MFEA needs to be investigated. In this context, the inhibitory action of MFEA presented in the manuscript
is intriguing. However, from this reviewer's perspective, it remains unclear how a real therapeutic application could be
achieved without addressing the potential cytotoxicity arising from the direct membrane insertion of MFEA. 
Minor comments

-Typos: missing a single space before and after the “=” sign. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Dewangan et al. presents a novel and compelling study on the design of small-molecule self-coacervates
with tunable dynamic properties and demonstrates their potential as broad-spectrum antiviral agents. The work is innovative
and addresses a significant challenge in supramolecular chemistry. However, the manuscript is currently undermined by
significant conceptual gaps and unsubstantiated mechanistic claims. The nature of the membrane-bound domains is not
rigorously proven, the proposed fusion inhibition mechanism contains contradictions, and the antiviral mode of action is not
clearly resolved. A major revision is required to address these fundamental issues. 
1. The central claim of forming MCDs on the membrane is based on indirect evidence (AFM height profiles, dye co-
localization) that is open to alternative interpretations, such as simple 2D aggregation. There is also a critical disconnect

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

between the complex tubular/sheet-like structure of the bulk coacervates (seen in cryo-TEM) and the simplified model of
domains on the membrane, which is not addressed. 
2. The authors propose that the MFEA coacervates inhibit fusion by increasing membrane order. This contradicts their own
FRAP data showing that these same coacervates are highly dynamic and fluid-like. It is counterintuitive for a fluid domain to
induce rigidity in the adjacent membrane, and this paradox is not explained. 
3. The manuscript proposes two separate antiviral mechanisms: a non-specific, broad-spectrum membrane fusion inhibition
and a specific interaction with the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD. These two pathways are not integrated into a coherent model,
leaving the primary mode of action unclear and questioning the relative importance of each effect. 
4. The text incorrectly claims a "linear correlation" for fusion inhibition, which is contradicted by the data showing an
optimum at n=2. 
5. FRAP data show significant immobile fractions, which should be discussed. 
6. The physical basis for coacervation (H-bonding vs. electrostatic repulsion) in the membrane environment needs clearer
explanation. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a series of custom lipidated flavonoid molecules designed to self assemble in water, forming liquid-like
coacervates at remarkably low concentrations (10 µM). The main goal of the study is to evaluate whether these flavonoid
coacervates can protect cells from viral infection by SARS-CoV-2 and Influenza A. Overall, the manuscript is well written,
novel, and would be of interest to Communications Chemistry readers. The data generally support the authors’ conclusions,
with a few notable exceptions. 
1. The most significant weakness is the manuscript’s failure to address the non-monotonic relationship between methylene
linker length and the observed biological and physical properties. The data consistently shows that the compound with two
methylene units (MFEA, n = 2) exhibits the most pronounced effects, which then decrease for three units (MFHA, n = 3). The
authors’ discussion often implies a simple trend in which longer linkers increase fluidity, but they do not explain this clear
“optimal length” phenomenon. It would be valuable to probe this further, for example by performing FRAP measurements to
assess coacervate-membrane fluidity or ITC experiments to quantify membrane-coacervate interactions. 
2. I also found the description of the “porous network” of interconnected sheets and tubules seen in the cryo-TEM images
(Fig. 4g) difficult to reconcile with the general understanding of fluid like disordered coacervates. This apparent discrepancy
was not adequately explained in the manuscript and merits clarification. 
If the authors address these points, I believe this work would be suitable for publication in Communications Chemistry.

Reviewer #2  
The manuscript by Dewangan et al. presents a novel and compelling study on the design of 
small-molecule self-coacervates with tunable dynamic properties, demonstrating their 
potential as broad-spectrum antiviral agents. The work is innovative and addresses a 
significant challenge in supramolecular chemistry. However, the manuscript is currently 
undermined by significant conceptual gaps and unsubstantiated mechanistic claims. The 
nature of the membrane-bound domains is not rigorously proven, the proposed fusion 
inhibition mechanism contains contradictions, and the antiviral mode of action is not clearly 
resolved. A major revision is required to address these fundamental issues. 
We sincerely thank the reviewer for the comments, which helped us to improve the quality of 
the manuscript. 
1. The central claim of forming MCDs on the membrane is based on indirect evidence (AFM 
height profiles, dye co -localisation) that is open to alternative interpretations, such as 
simple 2D aggregation. There is also a critical disconnect between the complex 
tubular/sheetlike structure of the bulk coacervates (seen in cryo -TEM) and the simplified 
model of domains on the membrane, which is not addressed. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for bringing this important issue to our attention . We 
mentioned in our previous version of the manuscript on the formation of 2D domains (2D 
aggregation) in the abstract , results and discussion . However, as per the reviewers' 
suggestions, we have emphasised more in the revised version (page 8, yellow highlighted). 
‘Since the LF-based self-coacervates contain a hydrophobic lipid tail, their interaction with 
the membrane is likely to be unique and differs from the conventional coacervate -
membrane interaction. Coacervates generally wet the membrane and exhibit endocytosis. 
Given the lipidic nature of the LFs, they may exhibit additional partitioning properties to 
the bilayer, which complicates the membrane interaction equilibrium. Therefore, we 
propose that the LFs may wet the membrane and partition into the bilayer, resulting in the 
formation of a 2D domain. Additionally, the lipidic tail may also inhibit the e ndocytosis of 
coacervates, and thus a membrane -bound nano-coacervate state may be formed under 
appropriate conditions. It is possible that the membrane -bound nanocoacervate  state 
(MCD) is a metastable state, and further studies are required to understand its phase 
behaviour.’  
2. The authors propose that the MFEA coacervates inhibit fusion by increasing membrane 
order. This contradicts their own FRAP data, which show that these same coacervates are 
highly dynamic and fluid -like. It is counterintuitive for a fluid domain to induce rigidity in 
the adjacent membrane, and this paradox remains unexplained. 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful observations, which helped to improve 
the manuscript. We added the following sentences on pages 8 (see above) and 16. 
‘At first glance, it appears that the highly dynamic and fluid -like MFEA coacervate (FRAP 
data) and membrane ordering are contradictory. However, the lipidic nature of the LFs 
(hydrophobic acyl chain) results in membrane partitioning through their lateral interactions 
at the membrane surface and induces the formation of 2D domains. The lipidic tail appears

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

to inhibit endocytosis of coacervates, a phenomenon frequently observed in coacervate -
membrane interaction. We therefore suggest that the interaction bet ween lipidic 
coacervate and membrane is unique and differs from that of conventional protein -based, 
DNA-based, and polymeric coacervates. Once MFEA coacervate interacts with the 
membrane, the partitioning and subsequent remodelling processes lead to a dyna mic 
equilibrium among the membrane, coacervate wetting, 2D domains, and might result in 
the formation of a metastable membrane -bound nanocoacervate state. The coacervate -
membrane interaction has been reported to enhance lipid packing and membrane stability. 
(Refs: 45, 50, 51)’ 
3. The manuscript proposes two separate antiviral mechanisms: a non-specific, broad-
spectrum membrane fusion inhibition and a specific interaction with the SARS-CoV-2 
spike RBD. These two pathways are not integrated into a coherent model, leaving the 
primary mode of action unclear and casting doubt on the relative importance of each 
effect. 
Response: In the revised manuscript (page 15, yellow highlighted), we present a unified 
model that describes the dual mode of action of the LFs. 
We propose that the primary function of MFEA, the broad -spectrum mechanism, arises 
from its ability to inhibit membrane fusion by altering the membrane's physical properties. 
It exhibits potency against various viruses, including SARS -CoV-2 (IC 50 ~ 39.3 µM) a nd 
influenza (IC50 ~ 155.8 µM). In addition, the secondary interaction of MFEA with SARS-CoV-
2 spike RBD provides an additional layer of specificity, and the antiviral potency is higher 
towards SARS-CoV-2 than that of influenza. 
4. The text incorrectly claims a "linear correlation" for fusion inhibition, which is 
contradicted by the data showing an optimum at n=2. 
Response: We rephrased the sentence on page 12 (yellow highlighted) as ‘Next, the role 
of fusion inhibition of MFEA with two methylene units and MFHA with three methylene 
units was tested. MFEA reduced the membrane fusion to ~ 3%, and MFHA reduced the 
fusion to ~4%. We plotted the percentage of fusion inhibition against th e number of 
methylene linkers and found that the percentage of  inhibition nearly saturated at n = 2 
(MFEA) (Figure S25). These results indicate that the methylene linker length at the terminal 
end might be important in membrane fusion inhibition, and MFEA with two methylene 
units is a potent inhibitor.’ 
5. FRAP data show significant immobile fractions, which should be discussed. 
Response: FRAP experiments were performed again, and the pre-bleach, bleach and post-
bleach images were provided in the revised version (Figure 3M-P). 
6. The physical basis for coacervation (H-bonding vs. electrostatic repulsion) in the 
membrane environment needs clearer explanation. 
Response: We measured the zeta potential of the MFEA coacervate at various pHs. The 
decrease in zeta potential of MFEA from pH 7.4 to pH 9.5 (Figure S2 9) suggests that at pH 
7.4, not all the carboxylates were deprotonated. Therefore, COO -…HOOC - hydrogen 
bonding interactions are possible at a pH of 7.4. However, strong associative interactions

(H-bonds) often lead to aggregation, and a balance of electrostatic repulsion between the 
carboxylates is crucial for forming coacervates (included on page xx, highlighted in yellow). 
Upon membrane interaction, the balance of these interactions may be drifted as the 
portioning of the acyl chain to the membrane bilayer might lead to 2D domain formation. 
As mentioned earlier, coacervates have a tendency for endocytosis, and the gradual 
membrane interaction may lead to the formation of membrane-bound nano-coacervates. 
The formation of membrane-bound coacervates is well reported in the literature.
