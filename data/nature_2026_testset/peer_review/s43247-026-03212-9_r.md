Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Guan et al. conducted a series of deformation experiments on bridgmanite at 25 GPa, 1700-2100 K. They found that below
1800 K, the [010](100) slip dominates and produces high seismic anisotropy while the [100](010) slip system dominates at
temperature higher than 1800 K and produce much less anisotropy (nearly isotropic). Combined with previously published

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

deformation maps of bridgmanite, they concluded that the deformation of the upper part of the lower mantle may be
controlled by dislocation creep of bridgmanite. 
I think the experiments themselves are robust, but it needs to clarify several points before reaching their conclusion. 
1. Authors think that the temperature close to the slab is below 1800 K. At 25 GPa, normal geotherm temperature is about
2000 K (Katsura, JGR, 2022). Namely, authors think the temperature close to the slab is 200 K below the normal geotherm.
Can authors justify the reason? In my understanding, the slab interior is much colder than the ambient mantle but the
surrounding should be close to the ambient mantle because of the continuous heating from the surrounding mantle. This
may be especially true for the regions below the slab because the bottom part of the slab, contrast to the top part of the slab,
is always heated by the underlying mantle since oceanic lithosphere forms. If the regions close to the bottom part of the slab
do not have too low temperature, the seismic anisotropy should be low according to the authors experiments results, which
is inconsistent with the seismic observation. 
2. The key conclusion of this paper is that the high temperature fabric of bridgmanite shows nearly no seismic anisotropy.
However, this conclusion is based on the elastic wave velocities calculated from the bridgmanite LPO formed at 1850 K. The
LPO at this temperature was not developed very well, especially the slip direction. It shows a girdle on the slip plane. It
indicates that both [100](010) and [001](010) slip system are dominant. At higher temperature, the fabric may develop better
and therefore may show a larger seismic anisotropy. Could authors exclude this possibility before reaching their conclusion?
3. Tsujino et al., Nature, 2016 reported the [001](100) slip system are dominant at 1875 K and used it to explain the observed
the seismic anisotropy near slab very well. Although authors state that the difference between the current study and Tsujino
et al., 2016 is due to the temperature difference, the slip system observed by Tsujino et al., 2016 belongs to neither high
temperature slip system nor the low temperature slip system in this study. Could author explain this point?

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The textures of bridgmanite obtained from deformation experiments in a multi -anvil apparatus at 
25 GPa and between 1700 -2400 K have been studied using X -ray diffraction. The authors have 
performed an impressive number of deformation experiments using pure bridgmanite and Fe -
bearing bridgmanite as starting materials. The results surely deserve to be published, but, in my 
opinion, their contribution on our understanding of the deep interior is minimal and therefore not 
suitable for Nature Geoscience.  
The LPO and associated seismic anisotropy presented in the paper are very weak (the strongest 
anisotropy is far less than 1%) and therefore on the order of the uncertainties of the X -ray 
measurements and consequent analysis. The change in LPO occurring as a function of temperature 
is difficult to rationalise in terms of dislocation creep, since both the (100) and (010) slip systems 
should have similar probability as pointed out in several studies. Very likely both systems are active 
at all temperature and there should be a interval where both are present. The extreme smoothing of 
the data used in this study as presented in Figure 2 may prevent to observe the presence of the two 
slip systems (albeit in different proportions) at all temperatures. Even if the clear cu t between the 
low and high temperature is real (which needs to be proved more rigorously and some reasoning of 
why that should be possible should be given since structurally the two slip planes appear the same), 
the negligible anisotropy of these LPO (even  at low T) will make such textures very difficult to 
detect seismically and will behave as isotropic material. I found, therefore, that the conclusion 
remark aimed at explaining several features of P -wave azimuthal anisotropy are not rigorous and 
reflect an overstatement of the importance of these textures.  
We thank the reviewer for the thoughtful and constructive feedback. Below, we provide a detailed 
point-by-point response:  
 
1. About the magnitude of seismic anisotropy and its detectability 
We acknowledge the reviewer’s concern regarding the relatively weak seismic anisotropy (<1%) 
reported in our study. However, recent global and regional seismic studies have detected similarly 
weak yet coherent anisotropic signals in the uppermost lower mantle (e.g., Ferreira et al., 2019; Ma

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

et al., 2019). In the revised manuscript (lines 201-216), we emphasize that the shear-wave anisotropy 
derived from our experimentally deformed bridgmanite —particularly the calculated  (VSH/VSV)2 
values of ~1.000–1.012—is consistent with the range observed seismologically near subducting 
slabs (e.g., Kurile and Honshu arcs ; Figure 3c ). This suggests that even subtle LPO features 
developed during dislocation creep are seismically detectable and geophysical relevant. Moreover, 
the near-isotropic values ( VSH/VSV)2~ 1.000 obtained from high -temperature samples also closely 
match global average seismic observations in the uppermost lower mantle  (lines 221-223 in the 
revised manuscript ), further supporting the validity of our findings.  Our main conclusion —that 
dislocation creep is likely the dominant deformation mechanism in the uppermost lower mantle—
is supported not only by the LPO observations, but also by consistency with both local and global 
shear-wave anisotropy patterns. 
P-wave azimuthal anisotropy is not the main focus of this study. We agree that P-wave azimuthal 
anisotropy is weak ( less than 1% ), while the observed P-wave azimuthal anisotropy  in the lower 
mantle is also weak, with most regions less than 1% (Ma et al., 2019). Our discussion in the revised 
text is only limited to the fast velocity directions observed beneath region of the Japan Arc (lines 
198-201 in the revised manuscript ), without explaining the magnitude and talking about other 
features of P-wave azimuthal anisotropy in other regions. 
 
2. About the distinction of slip systems and LPO transition 
We agree with the reviewer that both (100) and (010) slip planes may be active over a range of 
conditions. Our intention was not to exclude simultaneous operation of mul tiple systems but to 
emphasize the dominance of one over the other at specific temperatures. Notably, the transition in 
LPO patterns observed between 17 00 K and 1 900 K —across both simple shear and uniaxial 
experiments—was repeatable and occurred in both Fe-free and Fe-bearing bridgmanite. As shown 
in Supplementary Figs. 7 -8, the dominant (100) and (010) slip systems  were observed at 1700 K 
and 1900-2100 K in both Fe-free and Fe-bearing bridgmanite, respectively. We supposed that there 
would be an interval where both slip systems are present , at the temperature around 1800 K . This 
consistency across compositions and experimental configurations supports a systematic 
temperature-dependence in slip-system activity. We now discuss this more explicitly in the revised 
manuscript (lines 92-118 in the revised manuscript).  
We added simple discussion about the possible mechanism of the change in LPO in the revised 
manuscript (lines 149-151 in the revised manuscript). “Although the precise mechanism remains 
unclear, similar transitions in dominant slip systems have been reported in other orthorhombic 
minerals, such as olivine40,41.” 
 
Furthermore, the Fe influence on such LPO is only briefly discussed in that the authors simply state 
that Fe -bearing bridgmanite presents the same LPO feature of the bridgmanite end -member. 
However, it is well known that Fe has the effect of diminishing the elastic anisotropy of bridgmanite, 
so that would give rise to even more negligible LPO. more discussion on the results of Fe -bearing 
bridgmanite should be given.  
We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comment regarding the role of Fe in influencing the LPO 
and elastic anisotropy of bridgmanite. We have expanded the discussion of Fe-bearing bridgmanite 
and provided a more detailed comparison with the Fe-free end-member (lines 92-118 in the revised 
manuscript). Specifically, we now describe the LPO characteristics observed in Fe -bearing

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

bridgmanite under both low - and high -temperature conditions, highlighting that they follow the 
same temperature-dependent transition from to as in MgSiO 3 bridgmanite. Supplementary Fig . 8 
has been added to show the pole figures and inverse pole figures of the Fe-bearing samples. 
 
Regarding the elastic anisotropy, we agree that Fe substitution alone is known to reduce the elastic 
anisotropy of bridgmanite (e.g., Ballaran et al., 2012 ). However, bridgmanite in the lower mantle 
typically incorporates both Fe and Al, forming a more realistic (Mg,Fe,Al)(Si,Fe,Al)O3 composition. 
Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations and high-pressure experimental studies have shown that 
the elastic anisotropy of (Fe,Al)-bearing bridgmanite is broadly comparable to or slightly greater 
than that of pure MgSiO3 bridgmanite (Criniti et al., 2021; Kurnosov et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2016). 
These findings suggest that the modest reduction in elastic anisotropy due to Fe is mitigated when 
Al is also incorporated.  Therefore, although Fe slightly decreases anisotropy, the overall 
contribution of Fe -bearing bridgmanite to se ismic anisotropy in the lower mantle remains 
geophysical meaningful—particularly when deformation-induced LPO is considered under realistic 
mantle compositions and conditions. We added the  clarification of  this point in the revised 
manuscript (lines 187-194). 
 
Some minor notes: please avoid the word: “elastic constants ” given that the elastic stiffness 
coefficients (as they should be named) change with pressure and temperature calling them constants 
is not correct.  
We corrected the word “elastic constants” to “elastic stiffness coefficients” in the revised manuscript 
(lines 154 and 156) and Supplementary Table 3. 
 
The space group used for the analysis of LPO should ALWAYS be reported to avoid 
misunderstanding since there are different setting of the unit -cell in space group 62 a non -expert 
reader may not know that the usual space group used for bridgmanite is Pbnm and not for example 
Pnma (as used in the physics community for orthorhombic perovskites). 
We added the “space group Pbnm” in the introduction part in the revised manuscript (line 58). 
 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
Summary 
 
The manuscript addresses seismic anisotropy in the lower mantle. Implications are made from 
measurements obtained from deformation experiments of bridgmanite at lower mantle conditions 
of high pressure and temperatures. 
 
Samples of iron-free (MgSiO3) and Fe-bridgmanite (Mg0.90Fe0.10SiO3 and Mg0.97Fe0.03SiO3) 
are first synthesized in a multi -anvil apparatus and then deformed in uniaxial and simple shear 
experiments. The uniaxial and simple shear deformation experiments are carried out at 25 GPa at 
two temperatures 1700 K and 1900 - 2100 K. Lattice preferred orientation (LPO) analysis is carried 
out on the samples recovered from the deformation experiments. 
 
It was observed that bridgmanite has dominant slip systems of [010](100) at 1700 K, which then

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

switches to [100](010) above 1800 K. These results demonstrate a temperature-dependent transition 
in the LPO in bridgmanite from 1700 K to 1900 - 2100 K. 
 
The effect of iron on LPO development in bridgmanite is observed to be negligible. 
 
They then calculated elastic seismic wave velociti es corresponding to these LPOs. Their results 
indicate that the LPO in bridgmanite can explain observed seismic anisotropy in the lower mantle. 
 
Three main conclusions are made from the temperature -dependent LPO observed in bridgmanite: 
(1) Strong P- and S- wave seismic anisotropy observed near cold subducting slabs, can be attributed 
to the low-temperature LPO (of [010](100)), (2) Strong P-wave azimuthal anisotropy observed near 
hot upwelling that can be attributed to the high -temperature LPO ([100](010)),  and, (3) Almost 
isotropic S-wave seismic behavior in regions away from subduction zones and upwellings  
can be attributed to high -temperature LPO under horizontal shearing, and that this doesn ’t require 
the absence of LPO or deformation by non-dislocation creep. 
 
Comments and suggestions 
 
The authors could show the FT -IR spectra measured to determine the water contents in the 
bridgmanite samples and give actual values of the water contents calculated, however small. 
We thank the reviewer for making this point. Following the reviewer’s suggestion, we showed the 
FT-IR spectra of both Fe-free and Fe-bearing bridgmanite samples and give the calculated values of 
the water contents (lines 79-82, lines 273-274 in the revised manuscript and Supplementary Fig. 2). 
The water contents in our starting bridgmanite samples were very low (less than 20 ppm), consistent 
with previous studies (Bolfan-Casanova et al., 2000, 2003, 2005; Liu et al., 2019; Ishii et al., 2022).  
 
While MgSiO3 - and (Mg1-xFex)SiO3 - bridgmanite have been considered in this study, no mention 
or discussion is made on the possible role of aluminum ((Mg, Fe)SiO3 -Al2O3 - bridgmanite). 
Aluminum is an important component in bridgmanite. This should have been included in the study 
for completeness. 
We added discussion about the possible influence of Al on LPO and anisotropy  of bridgmanite in 
the revised manuscript (lines 176-194), as shown below: 
“Influence of aluminum on the deformation and elastic anisotropy of bridgmanite 
In the Earth ’s lower mantle, aluminum (Al) can also be incorporated in  bridgmanite (as 
(Mg,Fe)SiO3-Al2O3) through charge-coupled (CC) or oxygen-vacancy (OV) mechanisms43,44. While 
its effect on LPO development of bridgmanite appears limited compared to primary variables like 
temperature45. Experimental study has shown that Al -bearing bridgmanite with CC substitution 
exhibits a similar temperature-induced LPO transition from (100) to (010) with increasing 
temperature, as observed in both Fe-free and Fe -bearing bridgmanite45. Al though Al -bearing 
bridgmanite with OV substitution shows different slip system at high temperature 45, the content of 
OV component in (Fe,Al)-bearing bridgmanite is thought to be limited due to the preferential 
formation of the FeAlO 3 component46,47. In add ition, t he dominant deformation mechanism, 
dislocation creep, remains largely unaffected by aluminum content45. Although Fe substitution alone 
was reported to reduce the elastic anisotropy of bridgmanite 48, both ab initio molecular dynamics

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

simulations49 and high -pressure experimental studies 50,51 indicate that the elastic anisotropy of 
(Fe,Al)-bearing bridgmanite is comparable to that of MgSiO3 bridgmanite. Therefore, the presence 
of Al is not expected to significantly modify the LPO transition behavior, elastic anisotropy, or the 
dominant role of dislocation creep in the deformation of bridgmanite  under the uppermost lower 
mantle conditions.” 
 
During the deformation experiments, are the strain rates measured once steady state is achieved 
after the change of each temperature? How is this steady state determined or confirmed? 
In this study, w e conducted the que nch deformation experiments. Each experimental run  was 
performed at a single, fixed temperature . No temperature change occurred during an individual 
deformation run. Therefore, the concept of achieving steady state after a temperature change does 
not apply in our case. 
 
To clarify the strain rate determination, we have added a detailed explanation in the revised 
manuscript (lines 305-306) and in the caption of Supplementary Fig. 3. Specifically, strain rate was 
calculated by dividing the strain by the duration of the plastic deformation stage. This plastic 
deformation interval was identified from the load -stroke curves of the differential rams 
(Supplementary Fig. 3), which show a transition from the initial elastic stage to plastic stage.  
 
In conclusion, the authors present important results of LPOs in bridgmanite, which could have 
significant implications on seismic anisotropy in the lower mant le. I would have preferred a more 
complete study that also discussed the role of aluminum in LPO development in bridgmanite, if any. 
The authors did deliberately focus on studying dry bridgmanite, but it would also have been 
important to mention if water or hydrogen would influence their observations and conclusions. 
We appreciate the reviewer’s thoughtful comment. We added discussion about the possible influence 
of Al on LPO and anisotropy of bridgmanite in the revised manuscript (lines 176-194). 
 
We didn’t discuss influence of the water or hydrogen on our observations and conclusions. Although 
water is one factor known to influence the LPO of mantle minerals (e.g., Karato, 2008 ; Jung et al., 
2002), bridgmanite at the top of the lower mantle is generally considered to be nearly dry, as that in 
present study. Several studies have shown that bridgmanite in this region cannot act as a major water 
reservoir (Bolfan-Casanova et al., 2000, 2003, 2005; Liu et al., 2019; Ishii et al., 2022). Although 
some reports su ggest that bridgmanite can incorporate up to 0.1 -0.2 wt.% H 2O (Fu et al., 2019 ; 
Litasov et al., 2003; Murakami et al., 2002), this is thought to be largely attributed to the presence 
of hydrous inclusions such as superhydrous phase B or hydrous melt, eithe r within grains or along 
grain boundaries (Bolfan-Casanova et al., 2000, 2003, 2005; Liu et al., 2019). Thus, we only showed 
the water content and compared it with previous studies in the revised manuscript (lines 79-82). 
 
My decision is to accept for publication in Nature Geoscience after the suggested revisions 
mentioned above are made. 
 
 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
Guan et al. reported new experimental results on temperature-induced bridgmanite fabric transition.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

They found that with increasing temperature, the dominant slip system changes from [100](010) to 
[001](100) (I will also use slip systems to represent the fabric hereafter). They also calculated the 
seismic anisotropy produced by these two fabrics and concluded that it could match th e seismic 
signals observed in the upper part of the lower mantle. Therefore, they concluded that dislocation 
creep dominates the bridgmanite deformation in the Earth’s lower mantle.  
 
I found the experiments themselves are well done, but the conclusion cannot be directly drawn from 
their experimental results.  
 
Specific comments: 
1. To determine the deformation mechanism in the lower mantle, one must construct a deformation 
mechanism map under the lower mantle conditions. To do that, one must do deformation  
experiments at different pressure, temperature, grain size, strain rate, such as Tsujino et al., 2022 
did. Simply showing that the seismic anisotropy by [100](010) is small cannot rule out the 
possibility that bridgmanite deformation is controlled by disl ocation creep, which is the main 
conclusion of this paper. 
We agree with the reviewer’s comment. But we would like to clarify that our main conclusion of 
deformation mechanism is not based on the magnitude of seismic anisotropy but through the direct 
microstructural evidence  and combining with deformation mechanism map  (lines 8 6-123 in the  
revised manuscript ). The deformation mechanism map s were constructed based on the study of 
Tsujino et al. (2022) and Yamazaki et al. (2000). Our experimental conditions were located in the 
dislocation creep region (which were plot based on the grain size and strain rate). Our experiments 
provide direct microstructural evidence (e.g., the development of LPO, dynamic recrystallization 
and grain size reduction) that supports dislocation cr eep as the dominant deformation mechanism
