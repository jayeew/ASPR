Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript entitled, “Localizing electrophysiologic cue-reactivity within the nucleus accumbens guides deep brain
stimulation for opioid use disorder” presents the interesting case of a single participant who was previously treated for
tardive dyskinesia (bilateral globus pallidus interna DBS, IPG placed in right chest) along with unilateral (left) lead
placement in the nucleus accumbens (left chest IPG placement) to treat opioid use disorder. With very high dose (14.5mA)
stimulation between contacts 4 and 5 (in the ventral anterior limb of the internal capsule) the patient had achieved sustained
remission from opioid use (almost 4 years). The high stimulation led to low battery life but a rechargeable was not deemed
feasible (clinical destabilization when charge was too low). Recurrent infections led to multiple surgeries and then wound
erosion/infection necessitated removal of the left IPG and explanation/externalization of the nucleus accumbens extension
wire/lead. This provided an opportunity to administer a personalized cue-craving while recording from the lead contacts. The
investigators report convincing results, demonstrating increased power in the 1-7hz range during cue vs. neutral which was
attenuated following short periods of bipolar stimulation at 1-2+ at 4-6mA, pulse width of 90 and frequency of 130. There are
few examples of direct recording from the nucleus accumbens in the realm of addiction – the results, which are consistent
with this group’s prior work in binge eating disorder, are quite interesting and if replicated would be a major advance toward
adaptive/closed loop stimulation approaches in this population However, several questions/concerns remain.

The authors presenting an intriguing case study with important implications for the biological underpinnings and treatment
potential of opioid use disorder. The report is well-written and includes important within-subject controls to give confidence

results may generalize across substance use disorders, that cannot be known from this data. Thus, the abstract should be
constrained to this disorder (OUD). Further, the title should include “case study”. Overall, I think the manuscript has high
potential impact, but I think could benefit from addressing the following points: 
- Line 133: “Positively correlated” implies a statistical comparison. Please comment on whether/how this was done, or if
simply descriptive finding. 
- Figure 1: Why is there a focus on 4.5-6 seconds? I see the pattern in c which I suspect led to the analysis in e and f, but
please comment on the reason for the delay, or why this was analyzed rather than 0-1 sec which would likely be significant
in the opposite direction. Moreover, why was permutation not used for this comparison? Any reason sham results were not
included in this figure?

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled “Localizing electrophysiologic cue-reactivity within the nucleus accumbens guides deep brain
stimulation for opioid use disorder” by Qui et al shows that personalized drug-related cues elicited a specific rise of low-
frequency micro-EEG power at 1-7 Hz in the most ventral region of the NAc that was strongly associated with a subjective
feeling of desire for drug use in a patient with in situ DBS electrode implanted unilaterally in the ALIC-NAc region. High-
frequency DBS of the same region delivered acutely with clinically relevant parameters in a preexposure mode normalized
the electrophysiological signal and suppressed the drug craving. Chronic stimulation at remarkably low intensity in a focally
selected subregion within the NAc yielded successful abstinence of drug use for over 6 months without noticeable adverse
effect while improving mood and reducing anxiety. 
The authors did a great work by taking advantage of well-established cognitive tasks, especially the cue-reactivity test that
has conventionally been using in functional imaging studies, together with electrophysiological recording from previously
implanted electrode in the NAc in a patient with SUD. Simultaneous measurement of intracranial EEG and drug craving in
response to precisely selected drug cues provided an opportunity to find a specific electrophysiological biomarker of craving
that was subsequently targeted by clinical DBS. 
For closing the loop on SUD, we already knew that: (1) the best characterized region to be targeted was the NAc; (2) there
was a need for a biomarker of craving; (3) it should be of electrophysiological nature because the stimulation is electrical; (4)
it should be in the form of local field potential; (5) ideally it should be recorded from the same region where the stimulation is
given; (6) it should be specific for drug reward and not occurring in response to natural rewards; and finally (7) we knew from
the work of Wu et al. PNAS 2018: pnas.1712214114 that it should be around delta frequency band. In my opinion, this case
study put nearly all together and even though it only dealt with programming, but it provides us with clues to resolve the
historical need for a reliable biomarker of craving that can be used for adaptive on-demand delivery of DBS for treatment of
intractable SUD. 
There are some concerns though that should be addressed by the authors to improve the manuscript: 
The authors had better consider revising the title. It is inferred from the title that electrophysiological response to drug cue
can guide DBS treatment of addiction. Although the concept is correct and is supported by the presented data (the evoked
signal was more prominent in the ventral NAc and stimulation was more effective in the same region), the
electrophysiological response was not directly used for DBS programming of the patients as it was the behavioral response
(subjective craving) that finally guided programming (Fig 2a). This issue should be taken into account to soften the claim
made in Lines 215-216. 
Line 117: Stimulation had originally been given in the vALIC. More caution should be taken when stating this because the
stimulation mode was monopolar (C+/4-(60%)5-(40%)) that is known to provide more widespread stimulation (than bipolar)
and contact 4 was located in the NAc core based on the image shown in Fig S1c. In addition, Fig 2f shows spread of the

stimulation in the NAc as well as the vALIC. 
Fig 1f and lines 155-159: Student’s t-test was used. It seems that a two-way repeated measures ANOVA have to be used.
Please mention the tests in figure legends. 
Lines 133-134 and 219-221: There was positive correlation between the elevated power at frequencies 1-7 and the
subjective drug craving. No statistical test supported the claim. Although craving and power were simultaneously plotted in
Fig 1f, there have to be a correlation test (Pearson, Spearman, or …) between these variables as well as fitting a regression
line. 
Fig 1b, e, f: There is no indication of the timing and specification of stimulation relative to both baseline and evoked
electrophysiological response. 
Lines 138-139: The 1-7 Hz frequency power elevation was not elicited by non-drug incentive cues (Fig S2a-b and Fig S3).
At least in Fig 2b (Monetary incentive delay (MID) test) it is obvious that there is an increase in the power at frequencies 3-21
Hz, which encompasses part of the response elicited by drug cue presentation (1-7 Hz). Therefore, there is a concern
regarding the specificity of the electrophysiological response (elevated low-frequency band power) to drug-cue. Therefore,
3-7 Hz power elevation occurred in response to both drug-cue and a surrogate natural reward. It also seems that
electrophysiological response happening at the time of cue presentation and fixation (first 4 s) in the MID test cannot be
conceptually comparable with that happening during drug cue presentation in cue-reactivity task because the former only
represents reward expectation while the latter represents recollection of personalized drug-related cues. This concern gets
even more serious when there is no indication in the manuscript that MID test was performed with a hypothetical or real
reward because previous studies have shown that these two types of MIDs may have different effects on the brain activation
profile (Xu et al. Scientific Reports 2018: 10.1038/s41598-018-21820-0). In this sense, including more realistic cues of
natural reward (food, sex, …) similar to those used by Regier et al, Addiction biology 2021: 10.1111/adb.13028 would better
capture the problem. 
How the stimulation parameters mentioned in Fig 2a were first chosen? Was there a stepwise increase in the stimulation
intensity to reach the therapeutic effect? 
Lines 207-208: Please emphasize that preclinical studies also highlighted stimulation in the shell subregion of the NAc for
SUD. 
Preclinical studies suggest that an extinguished drug seeking behavior can be reinstated by drug cue/context, drug priming,
or stress. Human SUD patients may also experience withdrawal-based and cue-induced craving (people, places,
paraphernalia, etc.). This study emphasized the presence of an electrophysiological biomarker of cue-induced carving in the
NAc shell and its modulation by focally targeted DBS. Previous studies (Mantsch et al. Neuropsychopharmacology 2016:
10.1038/npp.2015.142) also implicated NAc in the stress-induced reinstatement of drug seeking. This was not seen in the
present manuscript. Does the elevated low-frequency band power also signal stress-induced craving? Is that modulated by
DBS in the NAc shell? 
Line 52: please use “in a patient” instead of “in an adult”. 
Line 113: The expression “… an objective read-out of at-risk pathophysiology” is ambiguous and difficult to understand at
least in the place it appeared in the text before giving any data. Please consider using a clearer statement. 
Line 236: Please correct “Based our findings, …”. “On” is missing. 
Supp videos: Please provide subtitles for non-native English readers.

Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a case study of a personalized approach to deep brain stimulation (DBS) for severe opioid use
disorder (OUD). The authors report using an externalized nucleus accumbens (NAc) DBS lead (available due to a temporary
clinical necessity) to record intracranial EEG during a personalized drug cue-reactivity task. They identified a specific low-
frequency (1–7 Hz) oscillatory “biomarker” in the ventromedial NAc (presumed NAc shell region) that was robustly elevated
by opioid-related cues (and correlated with subjective craving levels) but not by neutral cues or non-drug control tasks.
Using this information, they reprogrammed the patient’s DBS to specifically target the NAc contact showing cue-reactivity, at
a much lower stimulation amplitude than previously used. Over six months of follow-up, the patient experienced sustained
suppression of drug cravings and continued abstinence from opioid use (meeting DSM-5 criteria for early remission), with no
adverse effects and improved efficiency of the DBS therapy. 
This should be the first reported instance of using intracranial electrophysiological cue-reactivity data to guide DBS

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

programming in addiction. The work leverages a unique clinical opportunity to gain insight into the neural underpinnings of
opioid craving at high spatiotemporal resolution to guide programming. Rather than trial-and-error methods, one could use a
patient’s own neural response to drug cues to “personalize” the DBS target and settings. This approach could increase
efficacy (as seen by the craving reduction here) and minimize side effects (by reducing unnecessary volume of stimulation).
Given the ongoing opioid overdose epidemic and the lack of effective treatments for severe, treatment-refractory OUD, this
report is timely and could be of high interest to the neurotherapy, addiction psychiatry, and broader neuroscience
communities. 
Major comments: 
1. While the findings are intriguing, the manuscript’s scope as a single-case study may raise questions about broad interest
and translatability for a journal like Nature Communications. The authors should more explicitly frame how this case
provides a “proof-of-principle” for a generalizable approach. For example, could this personalized biomarker-guided DBS
strategy be implemented in other patients or tested in a clinical trial? In particular, since individualizing cues to elicit
sufficient craving is a challenge in this field, how they designed their individualized cue tasks so would have been important
to include. The discussion would benefit from highlighting a path toward broader application – e.g., using sensing-enabled
DBS devices or temporary externalization in a larger cohort – to convince readers that this is not an isolated anecdote but
rather a demonstration of a scalable therapeutic concept. The authors do note that this is the first such use of
electrophysiological target engagement in SUD and propose the cue-induced low-frequency signal as a candidate
biomarker for future studies. 
2. Statistical Validation: The authors used a cluster-based permutation test to find the time-frequency region of significant
difference between drug and neutral cue trials, which is appropriate. However, when quantifying the effect, they report
statistical significance with simple t-tests (e.g., drug vs neutral power without stimulation, p=0.018; drug without vs with
stimulation, p=0.039; craving ratings difference p=4.32×10^–7). Given that these tests are performed on multiple trials from a
single subject (e.g., 7 drug versus 10 neutral trials, as noted in Fig.1f), the independence assumption is arguable –
essentially, the subject is the only experimental unit. It would be wise to clarify the statistical approach in the Methods. For
instance, are these unpaired two-sample t-tests treating each trial’s averaged power as an observation? If so, a non-
parametric test (e.g., permutation test on condition labels or Wilcoxon rank-sum) might be more robust for small N. In any
case, the authors should caution that p-values in an N=1 context reflect within-subject reliability of the effect rather than
population-level significance. The extremely low p-value for craving reduction does convey a very large effect size, which is
convincing, but a brief discussion of statistical limitations would enhance rigor. 
3. Correlation with Craving Ratings: The text notes that the low-frequency power increase was “positively correlated with the
patient’s ratings of drug cravings on a visual analog scale,” but no correlation coefficient or statistical significance for this
correlation is provided. To substantiate this important point, the authors should report the correlation analysis (e.g.,
Spearman or Pearson r between trial-by-trial 1–7 Hz power and craving rating for drug-cue trials). If this analysis was
performed, including the correlation value and p-value (even if just in the supplement) would bolster the claim that the neural
signal tracks subjective craving in real time. 
4. Specificity of the Signal: The authors did a good job including control tasks (the Monetary Incentive Delay and the “Urge-
For-Action” blink suppression task) to test whether the 1–7 Hz power increase was specific to drug cues. The manuscript
states that no such low-frequency elevation was elicited by non-drug incentive cues in the MID or the blink task (and Fig. S2,
S3 presumably show this). It would be helpful if the authors could summarize these negative findings in the Results text with
a bit more detail. For example, confirming that only the drug cues (and not generic reward anticipation or urge to blink)
produced the significant power increase underscores the pathophysiological specificity of the biomarker. This helps rule out
alternative explanations like general arousal, stress, or motion artifact causing the low-frequency activity. Furthermore, their
findings would have been strengthened if it included individualized cues that cause other types of craving or pleasureable
feelings. 
5. The stimulation testing involved an interdigitated approach, where “DBS pretreatment was delivered for ten minutes (1-
/2+, 6mA, 90µs, 130Hz) prior to administration of the task, and an additional 15s stimulation period was delivered before
each cue presentation,” but the washout period between these stimulation periods was not specified, which could have
carryover effects. Furthermore, “opioid craving levels were recorded using a VAS scale compared to baseline level,” which,
if I understand correctly, was comparing every craving level in response to stimulation to the level of craving before
stimulation began. If that is the case, there could be several confounders, such as carryover effects, patient fatigue, extinction
to the cues, etc. 
6. The manuscript includes a structural connectivity analysis comparing the original vs. new stimulation sites (Fig.2c–h). In
particular, they indicate that stimulation at the NAc shell was efficacious. Providing a comparison of NAc shell vs. the core
would enhance the discussion section. The new, focal stimulation (NAc shell) primarily engaged a circuit involving the
subcallosal gyrus (SCg), whereas the original broader stimulation of vALIC had a much larger volume of tissue activation,
presumably recruiting additional regions. This finding is notable as the subcallosal gyrus (Brodmann area 25 region) is more
commonly known in the context of mood disorders (a common target for depression DBS). Why might connectivity to the
subcallosal area be beneficial for opioid craving? Currently, the text simply notes the difference; but readers would benefit
from understanding how the connectivity results inform the mechanism of clinical improvement. If there are other notable
differences in connectivity (e.g., did the original stimulation engage motor or cognitive networks that the new one spares?)
mentioning those would add insight.

Reviewer #6 (Remarks to the Author): Comment 1 What was the rationale behind the cue-reactivity design? Was an a priori power calculation completed beforehand for the number of cues to present?  Response: We thank the reviewer for this important question. As this was an exploratory study of a single subject’s off-label clinical experience with deep brain stimulation for OUD, no formal a priori power calculation was applicable. For cue-reactivity research, task designs attempt a delicate balance between cue repetitions (to support signal-averaging statistics) and the risk of reduced response due to too many exposures, whether through unlearned (habituation) or learned (extinction) mechanisms, or both. To minimize the exposure-related reductions in responding, cue sessions can be less frequent or spaced widely apart. Given these general considerations for cue-reactivity tasks, the specific number of cues and length of cue presentation (videos) was adapted based on our fMRI-based cue-reactivity studies, and our awareness that exposure-related reduction in cue-triggered responses are common and sometimes occur quickly (Ekhtiari et al., 2021). Our existing cue-reactivity task is designed with blocks of six cues, and we decided to administer a single block during each session the task was performed to minimize exposure-related decreases in response. The repeat session design balanced the need for adequate within-condition averaging to improve signal-to-noise ratio with the need to minimize exposure-related reductions in cue responding, which can impact both subjective craving and the degree of cue responses. Our approach allowed for sufficient statistical testing using non-parametric, within-condition permutation methods while maintaining the ecological validity of the personalized cues. We have now revised the exiting text for the task description:   Manuscript (pg 14, Online Methods, lines 518-529): Each session included six drug-related cues and six neutral (video) cues, presented in a quasi-randomized block design. The task was designed based on our team’s clinical judgment and cue-reactivity paradigm expertise of one of our senior authors (A.R.C) to balance the need for cue repetitions (for signal averaging) with risk of reduction of cue-induced responses with too many exposures (habituation or extinction effects). We made edits in the Methods section, and copy these additions below for review convenience:  Manuscript (pg 15, Online Methods, lines 568-580): To identify significant time-frequency regions of interest without predefining time epochs and frequency bands, we applied a data-driven procedure across the full time-frequency spectrogram (1-115 Hz, log-scaled). For each time-frequency sample, we computed a test statistic, a two-tailed paired-sample t-test, to compare responses to drug vs neutral cues across trials. Samples exceeding an uncorrected threshold of p<0.05 with the same sign were grouped into connected sets, i.e., ‘clusters’, based on spectral and temporal adjacency68.
