The presented manuscript “Experimental realization of dice-lattice flat band at the Fermi level in layered electride YCl” by
Songyuan Geng et.al. is devoted to finding a possible experimental realization of a dice lattice with a flat band in van der
Waals (vdW) electride [YCl]. Specifically, 
a noticeable dispersionless electronic band was discovered and observed at the

The experimental method explored in this paper was the angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES). This is one
of the most powerful experimental techniques for directly probing the electronic band structure of various solids. It is often
used to find the key electronic and topological properties of a solid, such as band gaps, effective electron masses, and
quasiparticle lifetimes. 
The article is timely and very important for a broad readership in condensed matter physics. The flat band leads to
significantly new electronic, collective, and topological properties in various materials, and here we see a vast amount of
strong experimental evidence to support the existence of the desired band structure. It is specifically important that this paper
provides both experimental verifications and further guidance for the theoretical research in dice lattices, Lieb lattices, and
the so-called alpha=T3 model. 
Therefore, I can undoubtedly recommend this paper for publication in Nature Communications, provided that the authors
address the following, rather minor, comments and suggestions. 
A flat band in the band structure means strong correlations and a rather small momentum and kinetic energy of the electrons
that belong to this band. 
I would like to know more about the possible conditions of the stability of this flat band and when it
actually remains flat: 
under which external perturbations it would stay flat, and when it receives finite dispersions. 
In general, 
to what extent can you say that your experimental results provide evidence for the validity of the alpha-T3 model
with a Dirac cone and a symmetric flat band? Recently, there have been substantial arguments regarding the validity of the
model, how many materials it can describe, and to what extent. Theoretical work has been much ahead of specific
experimental verifications. My question is whether we can count this work as another step towards validation of the alpha-T3
model. 
The Hamiltonian is based on the three-band tight-binding model. This is predetermined by the fact that there are three types
of subletuses: A, B, and C. 
However, is it possible that you find more than three bands in a close vicinity to each other? This
could happen, for example, for Kekule-patterned dice lattices. 
Finally, 
I believe it will be crucial to provide a little more detail of your DFT calculations, of course, if the size of the
manuscript allows. 
You mentioned the package and the general method, but could you please explain more about the
theoretical concepts of your specific DFT calculations, 
what equations and recent advances in this field have been used?

They also note that 1T-TMD possess a similar lattice geometry in L51. Please comment on this why 1T-TMD does not already have dice lattcie flat bands.  Reply: We thank the reviewer for pointing out this important comparison between YCl and 1T-TMD. Clarifying the relationship between lattice geometry and the electronic structure in these two systems is indeed crucial.   The dice lattice consists of two sublattices, namely, A/B-sites on the corners of the hexagonal unit cell and the C sites at the center. While the atomic arrangement of 1T-TMDs host a similar lattice geometry with A/B sites (chalcogen atoms) and C sites (transition-metal atoms) that resemble the dice lattice, it is important to note, however, that the electronic Hamiltonian is far from the ideal dice lattice limit - the 1T-TMD is conventionally described by a minimal tight-binding model of a triangular lattice formed by the transition-metal atoms (Phys. Rev. B 8, 3719 (1973)). This is be-cause the low energy bands are dominated almost exclusively by the transition metal 𝑑-orbitals that are located exclusively on the C-sites only. The chalcogen 𝑝-states, which have their Wannier centers located at the A/B sites, have a much higher energy and thus lying far away from the Fermi level. Consequently, the electronic bands are effectively decoupled into a triangular lattice band formed by electrons bound to the central metal ions and a honeycomb lattice band formed by the chalcogen 𝑝-orbitals – this explains why dice flat bands, which requires relatively close on-site energies among A/B and C sites, cannot form in 1T TMDs.  To clarify this issue, we added the following description in the main text line 51-59:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

“For instance, 1T-phase transition metal dichalcogenides (1T-TMDs) adopt a dice-like lattice ge-ometry, however, their alternating anion/cation sublattices occupation lead to large on-site energy difference. The low energy bands in 1T-TMDs are dominated by transition metal d-orbital states on C-sites only, with chalcogen p-states located at much higher binding energies that are far away from the Fermi level. Thus, such systems assume the minimal tight-binding model of a triangular lattice rather than a dice lattice 31,32. A true dice lattice requires relatively close on-site energies among the two sublattices (A/B and C sites), which requires same-charge ions on all sublattice sites, but the inherent electrostatic repulsion renders such structures unstable and rare to find in nature. ”  2. As for the AB antibonding state, it is indeed an essential part of the dice flat band (Fig R3) (Phys. Rev. A 80, 063603 (2009)). In an ideal dice lattice, the nearest-neighbor hopping between A and B sites is broken. In this case, regardless of the strong coupling between the A/B site and the central C site, there always exists a parity-odd AB antibonding state that is fully decoupled from the parity-even site C state. And thus, the kinetic energy of this AB antibonding state is quenched, leading to a dispersionless band (flat band as shown in Fig. R3). The opposite signs of the flat-band state wavefunctions arising from the A/B site IAEs (Fig. R2) are consistent with this condition of dice flat-band formation.

The stability of the flat band in YCl is dictated by the specific symmetry and connectivity of the dice lattice. Specifically, the flat band arises from a parity-odd antibonding state formed between the A and B sublattices. In the limit where the direct hopping between A and B sites is suppressed, the kinetic energy of this AB antibonding state is quenched as it is strictly forbidden from coupling to the central C site state with even parity. Consequently, the flat band is robust against parity-preserving or weak-parity-breaking perturbations. The band only acquires finite dispersion primar-ily when the direct A-B hopping becomes non-negligible.
