This manuscript described a design strategy for the synthesis of nanomotors-loaded microneedles for enhanced C. acnes
biofilm penetration and facilitated acne treatment. This work is interesting and provides an available and meaningful method
for designing these complex devices. A significant proof of the concept was demonstrated through an in vivo experiment on
mice. However, some issues are still confused. Taken this together, this work is suggested to be published in Nature
Communication after a revision as follows. 
1. The concept of loading nano/micromotors inside bigger microdevices is not novel
(https://doi.org/10.1002/smll.202206330), also loading nanomotors inside microneedles is not novel as well
(https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.1c03177, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcis.2023.05.080,
https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.5c01877) 
What are the new findings in this work? 
2. How does the 25-minute incubation and irradiation protocol used for Z@P-M and Z#P-M NPs compare to the much
shorter 3-minute irradiation time reported by Maric et al. ( https://doi.org/10.1002/adhm.202203018) which still achieved
significant biofilm reduction (71%) while meeting biosafety criteria? Could a shorter exposure time be similarly effective in
your system? 
3. The description of the in vivo experiment in the main text is rather limited—could you please provide more detailed
information regarding the experimental design. 
4. The manuscript mentions that different microneedles were pressed onto the skin at the acne site and left for 10 minutes
with or without NIR irradiation. However, it is unclear how the pressure was applied. Did the authors control the pressure?
How do you know that the same pressure has been applied in each repetition? It is essential to check if the applied pressure
impacted the tissue, for example, causing irritation or damage. 
5. It is unclear what happened to the microneedles after application—did they fully dissolve, and if so, how long did this
take? The authors should clarify whether the dissolution process was monitored and if any residue remained on the skin. 
6. The authors did not specify the duration of NIR irradiation in the in vivo experiments. Could the authors clarify how long
the mice were irradiated and whether any assessment was made of potential skin damage or irritation resulting from that
time applied for irradiation? 
7. The microneedle patch used in this study was prepared by dissolving 150 mg of sodium hyaluronate in 1 mL of ultrapure
water containing Z@P-M (100 μg/mL ZnO
₂
). The mechanical properties, particularly stress-strain behavior, were evaluated
as shown in Figure 2C. To further understand the influence of formulation parameters, it would be beneficial to perform
additional experiments to investigate how variations in sodium hyaluronate content and nanomotor concentration affect the
mechanical performance of the microneedle patch. 
8. For the antibiofilm evaluation experiments of Z@P-M MNs in Figure 4f, can the authors describe the experimental steps in
more detail, e.g. how the incubation process was performed? 
9. For some steps in the 'Methods' section, it would be good if the authors could explain them in more detail, such as the
‘Detection of Zn2+ level’, ‘O2 release’ test, ‘Penetration of Z@PM in Biofilms’, etc.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
General comments 
Hu et al. present a NIR-driven nanomotor therapy for acne, delivered to the skin using microneedles. The integration of
ZnO
₂
@PDA-MnO
₂
 (Z@PM) nanomotors within dissolvable microneedles is an effective combination that addresses multiple
aspects in acne therapy, such as overcoming the stratum corneum barrier, penetrating biofilms, and mitigating hypoxia. The
system’s capacity for in situ oxygen generation and NIR-driven photothermal activation, aided by the asymmetrical PDA
coating for thermophoretic mobility, reflects a thoughtful design with multifaceted therapeutic potential. 
While such hybrid therapeutical approach is novel, many of its core components/principles, such as MnO
₂
-mediated
oxygenation, PDA-enabled photothermal therapy, and microneedle-based delivery, are known in the SoA
(10.1021/acsami.4c01790, 10.1126/sciadv.adf0854, https://doi.org/10.1111/dth.13189). Similar multifunctional
nanoplatforms have been reported for infection control and wound healing, including systems that combine oxygen
generation, photothermal effects, and nanoparticle propulsion. Nevertheless, the novelty of this work lies rather in the
innovative combination of existing technologies than in fundamentally new mechanism or conceptual advance. The
manuscript provides a compelling in vivo validation of the therapeutic efficacy of the Z@P–M MNs system using a murine
acne model, with histological, immunohistochemical, and inflammatory marker analyses. While this significantly strengthens
the manuscript, additional mechanistic insight into nanoparticle–biofilm interactions and comparison to clinical standards
would further enhance its impact. I therefore recommend acceptance after major revision. 
Specific comments 
• The list of keywords does not reflect properly the scope and interdisciplinarity of the manuscript. For instance, “enhanced
penetration” is meaningless if not specified in what or where. The authors should consider including more specific and
representative terms to align with the content presented. Keywords such as “ZnO
₂
 nanomotors,” “photothermal therapy,”
“biofilm penetration,” and “acne hypoxia” could be considered, without limiting to. 
• The manuscript refers to the active component as ZnO
₂
 but does not provide clear evidence confirming its chemical identity
or stability. Given that ZnO
₂
 is metastable and readily decomposes to ZnO under aqueous or acidic conditions, it is

necessary to confirm that the particles used are indeed ZnO
₂
 and not ZnO. XRD or spectroscopic data (e.g., Raman) may
distinguish between ZnO
₂
 and ZnO, along with a brief discussion of ZnO
₂
 stability during synthesis and application. 
• The H
₂
O
₂
 release was assessed using a dimethylphenol orange assay under different pH conditions, which is appropriate
for evaluating ZnO
₂
 responsiveness. However, the data presentation could be improved by including time-resolved release
profiles with statistical replicates. Additionally, potential interference from PDA or MnO
₂
 with the assay should be addressed.
Supporting the pH-dependent release behaviour with quantitative details would strengthen the mechanistic claims. 
• The manuscript lacks direct evidence of catalytic oxygen generation by MnO
₂
. This should be addressed using oxygen-
sensitive fluorescent probes or dissolved oxygen measurements under acidic and neutral pH conditions. 
• A study showing how ZnO
₂
 behaves under different pHs (neutral vs. acidic) would clarify the system’s responsiveness and
therapeutic selectivity. 
• In vitro cytotoxicity is limited to NIH3T3 fibroblasts. Tests should be extended to keratinocytes, macrophages, or immune-
relevant cell lines to ensure broader biocompatibility. 
• The manuscript does not assess selectivity against C. acnes. Comparative antibacterial studies with non-target skin flora
(e.g., S. epidermidis) are necessary to evaluate off-target effects. Skin microbiome preservation is a very important issue. 
• No data are provided on the mode of bacterial killing (e.g., membrane disruption, DNA damage). Techniques such as
bacterial TEM imaging or TUNEL assays should be considered. 
• For clinical relevance, the authors should compare the treatment performance to benchmark acne medications such as
benzoyl peroxide or topical and oral antibiotics. 
• H&E and IHC staining are well-presented, and quantifying inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, HIF-1α, CD31)
supports the therapeutic outcomes. However, adding quantitative scoring of bacterial burden or follicular disruption could
further substantiate treatment effects. 
• The authors should provide absorption spectra (UV-Vis-NIR) of Z@P–M to correlate its optical properties with its
photothermal response. 
• Photothermal stability over extended irradiation durations (beyond five cycles) or under physiological conditions would
provide further insight into its robustness. 
• It would also be helpful to include material characterisation (e.g., TEM, DLS, or zeta potential) before and after irradiation to
confirm the structural stability of Z@P–M post-heating. 
• It should be clarified whether the heating is due solely to PDA coating, MnO
₂
, or a synergistic effect, possibly via
comparative experiments using Z and Z@P alone under identical conditions. 
• Lines 200-213, while the data show increased H
₂
O
₂
 release at pH 5.5, the source of H
₂
O
₂
 should be defined, i.e., whether it
arises from ZnO
₂
 decomposition, interaction with buffer components, or a catalytic process involving MnO
₂
. This distinction
is critical for the mechanistic understanding. 
• The O
₂
 release measurement appears to be presented as a single end-point value. Including time-resolved oxygen release
profiles would provide insights into the dynamics of oxygenation and fuel availability for the catalytic cycle. 
• The authors report values for H
₂
O
₂
 and O
₂
 release, but their concentrations should be contextualised relative to the levels
typically required to disrupt biofilms or relieve hypoxia in infected tissues.

2. The disadvantage of photothermal therapy is that it is difficult to penetrate the skin,

therapy penetrate the skin to destroy the bacterial biofilm in the dermis? 
Answer: Thanks for your valuable suggestion. The advantage of microneedles (MNs) 
is the painless drug delivery. It allows active substances loaded in MNs to penetrate the 
stratum corneum and epidermis and be delivered to the dermis (DOI: 10.1038/s41563-
024-02115-4; DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.adf0854). In our manuscript, Z@P-M MNs were 
pressed onto the skin and then irradiated with NIR light. The loaded Z@P-M were 
released from MNs into the dermis directly. Under the NIR laser, asymmetrically grown 
PDA absorbs NIR laser to generate a thermal gradient to induce self -thermophoresis, 
which endows the nanomotor with enhanced diffusion to penetrate the dense bacterial 
biofilm. Therefore, the generated photothermal therapy is directly functioned in the 
dermis due to the help of MNs. 
 
3. Although the article prepared the infrared responsive Z@P -M, it did not show the 
unique advantages of the prepared self-driving motor in treating the biofilm formed by 
subcutaneous Propionibacterium acnes. What are the unique advantages of Z@P-M in

Answer: Thanks for your concerns. Self-propelled nanomotors can convert external 
energies such as chemical reaction, light, magnetic field, or ultrasound into mechanical 
motion, demonstrating enormous application potential in biomedical field. The motility 
of the nanomotors and their ability to generate mechanical force s make them ideal 
candidates for constructing mechanical -antibiofilm systems (DOI: 
10.1002/adma.202408221). In our manuscript,  Janus Z@P-M nanomotors generate a 
thermal gradient to induce self -thermophoresis, which provide mechanical force to 
penetrate biological  barriers, synergizing with PTT effect endowed by NIR to 
efficiently clear bacterial biofilms  (Fig. 4 ). In addition, the microenvironment of 
bacterial biofilms is generally acidic (pH=4.5 -6.5) and hypoxic ( DOI: 
10.1002/adfm.202415559; DOI: 10.1002/agt2.587). Therefore, acid -responsive ZnO 2 
could supply a large amount of H 2O2 via acid ic decomposition within the biofilm 
microenvironment. The produced H2O2 can be further catalyzed by MnO 2 to generate 
O2, thereby alleviating hypoxia of biofilm and reducing inflammation.  
 
4. ZnO2 can hydrolyze to produce H2O2 in a weakly acidic environment. How do you 
eliminate the influence of H2O2 on the antibacterial rate?

H2O2 detection kit. At pH 5.5, the H 2O2 release levels of Z and Z@P within 12 h

12 h.  This big difference in H 2O2 concentration indicated that the Z@P -M’s MnO 2 
decomposed the generated H2O2, leading to a decrease in H 2O2 levels in Z@P -M. 
Furthermore, antibacterial assay and in vivo experiment results showed that Z@P and 
Z@P-M exhibited a certain degree of antibacterial activity ( Fig. 4g-l), mainly 
attributing to the synergistic effect of Zn2+, H2O2 and MnO2 nanosheets with extremely 
high aspect ratio edges  (DOI: 10.1021/acs.nanolett.0c01371). As reported, H2O2 with 
concentration of 10-5 – 10-4 M indeed inhibited bacterial and biofilms activity to some 
extent in vitro and in vivo, but H 2O2 concentration as high as 166 mM  - 1 M were

seconds. What was the skin temperature of the mouse at the irradiation point? During 
this period of time, whether the microneedles were present or completely dissolved

Z@P-M MNs patches in the skin, a penetration experiment was performed by porcine 
skin with similar structure to human skin ( DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-46328-2). The 
Z@P-M MNs patches were pressed on the porcine skin for 5 min, followed by NIR 
irradiation (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. After the treatment, the patches were carefully removed. 
As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the MNs on the patches completely dissolved 
during the 10 -min treatment, indicating that Z@P -M MNs exhibited favorable skin 
solubility. To further investigate the dynamic dissolution and penetration process of 
Z@P-M MNs in the skin, Z@P-M was labeled with RhB in the MNs patch, and z-stack 
fluorescence images of the skin were captured by confocal laser scanning microscopy 
(CLSM) under different treatment. After inserting the Z@P-M MNs patch into the skin 
for 5 min, strong fluorescence was observed within t he 0 - 300 μm depth range. The 
patch was then exposed to NIR irradiation for 3  - 5 min. At 3 min of irradiation, the 
fluorescence intensity in the 0  - 200 μ m depth range decreased sharply, while the

fluorescence intensity increased in the 400  - 500 μm depth range. After 5 min NIR
