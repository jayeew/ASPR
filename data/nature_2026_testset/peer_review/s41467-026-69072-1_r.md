Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study determines a TWIK2 cryoEM structure and uses a commercial automated patch clamp setup to assess the
function of TWIK2 channel mutants and pharmacology. The structure of TWIK2 had not been previously determined and this
study provides a first look at the TWIK2 channel architecture. To accompany the structure, the authors perform
electrophysiological recordings of TWIK2, a channel that has been notoriously difficult (though not impossible) to record
from. Overall, the methodology in the structural and functional work appears to be sound and the writing is clear. There are
novel observations and interesting results in the manuscript but an informative connection between knowledge learned from
the TWIK2 structure and the functional studies performed is largely lacking. The Y111H designed to mimic the TWIK1 pH
sensing histidine and the S4 site threonine residues are all positions that did not require a cryoEM structure to be interesting
candidates for mutation. While the M135A mutation is a candidate site based on the TWIK2 structure, it is unclear why the
authors chose this position as it does not occlude the pore or play any clearly observable role in the TWIK2 structure. The
experiments examining the effects of ML365 and NPBA are interesting but somewhat superficial, as the mutants show some
effects on channel pharmacology but do not yield any substantive insight into where the drugs might bind or how they might
act. 
Major Points 
1) In the section entitled “Ion conduction pore of TWIK2”, the authors suggest that the carbonyl oxygens of selectivity filter
residues V107 and Y109 face away from the central permeation axis in TWIK-2 and argue for the relevance of this SF
orientation to TWIK2 function. Given the ~3.7 angstrom resolution of the overall structure, I cannot ascertain how the authors
come to this conclusion. 
Using the map and model provided by the authors, I can easily model the selectivity filter within the
map in a conventional orientation and find that I improve the number of residues in favored orientations when the SF is
modeled more conventionally. 
If there is a biophysical argument to be made that the positioning of the valine and tyrosine sidechains in the TWIK2 map
somehow promote an unconventional SF backbone configuration, this should be provided. Simply based on the map
provided by the authors, I find this section to be unsupported by experimental results. 
If the authors do feel their selectivity
figure configuration is accurate, a supplemental figure showing how the experimental map is better fit by the outward rather
than inward facing carbonyls at V107 and Y109 would be extremely helpful to make the authors case on this point. 
2) In Figure 2B, the pore radius profile shows a constriction of the pore at around 5A from the cytoplasmic face of the
channel. This cytoplasmic constriction does not seem to be discussed in the manuscript. 
Is this constriction brought about by
the lipids modeled in the structure? 
If so, is this at all relevant to the function of TWIK2? 
If the authors believe that these lipid
densities are important to shaping the TWIK2 pore, then this pore radius plot is appropriate. If the relevance of these lipids is
unclear and could simply be an artifact of the protein purification process, it would be better to omit the lipid densities when
running HOLE (or include a second HOLE profile in the absence of the lipids) to show the profile of just the TWIK2 protein. 
It would also be appropriate to discuss the lipid densities present in the map in the results section of the manuscript, rather
than the discussion. This would be a good spot to do so. 
3) For the experiments where G/GMAX–V curves are calculated, it does not appear that the recordings reach a steady state
at 200 ms. 
The current is still rising for TWIK1 wt and this is even more pronounced in the mutants that slow activation
(Figure 3B). 
Is it possible that the differences in V50 are artifactual, since the maximal steady state current is reached at
200ms in some of the mutants but not in all of them.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript of Ma et al, provides new insights into the modulation of the K2P channel TWIK-2 using structural and
electrophysiological data. The study provides the first cryo-EM structure of the full-length TWIK-2 at 3.7Å resolution, which is
accompanied by mutagenesis studies and the use of a whole-cell patch clamp electrophysiology workflow. The authors
show that TWIK-2 is pH-insensitive but slightly voltage-gated, and suggest potential different binding modes or mechanisms
of inhibition in TWIK-2 for two of the four tested K2P modulators (ML365 and NPBA). 
The work is well executed and well written. However, overall the study fall a bit short. The new insights provided do not
represent a big step forward in improving our understanding of how TWIK-2 (and K2P channels in general) function and are
regulated. The substance and depth of the study could be/have been strengthened by more structure-guided mutagenesis
studies, a more robust comparison of their new automated electrophysiology setup, a better literature comparison with
(mutagenesis studies of) TWIK-1 and other K2P channels, or even more structural data or structural analysis. While I
understand that addressing them all might be out of the scope of this study, the manuscript requires a bit more of depth.

Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have undertaken a significant reworking of their manuscript, and the revised version is an improvement over the
original. 
Despite another group publishing a similar TWIK2 structure while this paper has been in revision, this work remains
impactful as it is an early effort to define the structure and function of TWIK2. I have only one major criticism of the revised
manuscript, again having to do with modeling of the selectivity filter. 
I appreciate the significant effort undertaken by the authors to model all possible conformations of the filter and to calculate
refinement statistics. However, these values only reflect aspects of the geometry of the various models and do not at all
reflect the correlation between map and model. Using small changes in refinement statistics as the sole justification for a
non-conventional orientation of the potassium selectivity filter is unconvincing. Several points suggest that the flipped
orientation of the carbonyls is at best unsupported by the experimental data (and likely, erroneous). 
1) At this point, multiple significantly higher resolution TWIK2 cryo-EM structures in similar detergent solubilized conditions
have been reported. None of them show an equivalent model of the filter. 
All show a conventional model of the filter. 
2) In the TWIK1 structure that the authors reference as an example of a non-conventional model, the flipped carbonyl at the
S1 site are only seen in a pH condition that functionally leads to inactivation, involves a loss of ion occupancy in the
structure at the site where the carbonyl flips, and also is a result of a large rearrangement in the loops near the S1 site. 
In this
example, it is the biological context of the sum of the data that supports the modeling of this flipped carbonyl. 
3) In the KcsA and Kv examples the authors cite, these models are based on multiple higher resolution crystal structures,
often accompanied by anomalous diffraction signal with thalium ions to definitively show the locations of ion occupancy. In
these examples, the structural results are significantly more compelling and are often supported by accompanying MD/single
channel data/EPR. 
These studies have both structural and biological support to underpin their model. 
A large majority of the field lacks expertise in structural biology and once a model is published, very few go back to the
underlying EM density data. Published models simply become dogmatic truth to many and have the capacity to be
destructive if incorrect. 
I am simply not convinced that there is sufficient 1) biological context or 2) strong structural evidence
to support the model being proposed by the authors. While carbonyl flipping is relevant to filter inactivation in many
potassium channels, for this specific case with TWIK2, the authors do not present any biological rationale to justify why the
experimental condition would lead to an inactivated filter. In fact, other than pointing out that the filter sequence in TWIK2 is
slightly different than other K2Ps, there really isn’t any argument made about why these flipped carbonyls are present or
relevant. I again would ask the authors to either provide a biologically compelling argument to support their decision to
model an inactivated filter, or to model the filter in a more conventional orientation. 
One additional minor point: GNTI- cells do not prevent glycosylation. Rather, the lack of N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase
means that these cells don’t produce complex glycosylation patterns. 
They do however often add (GlcNAc)2Man5 sugars,
such that the expression system is not a reason for a lack of TWIK2 glycosylation.

We thank the reviewer for a thorough review of our structure. In our TWIK-2 structure, the SF has 
been modeled with carbonyl oxygens of three amino acids – V107, Y109, and L217 – facing away 
from the ion-conductance pore. While we agree that at ~ 3.7 Å resolution it could be challenging 
to resolve the orientation of carbonyl oxygens in the dynamic loops of the SF region 
unambiguously, we would like to highlight that the local resolution for this region is much better 
than 3.5 Å ( Supplementary Fig.  S3F in the manuscript ). As p er the reviewer’s critique, we 
attempted to model these three carbonyls in various combinations of them facing away or towards 
the SF. However, just based on visually comparing the fits of these various models to density, it 
is not straight forward to identity one particular combination of the carbonyl oxygens that fits better 
to the map (see figures & table below). Hence, based on a comparison of the model statistics 
such as bond RMSDs, Ramachandran outliers, Rama -Z score, & C aBLAM outliers for the SF 
region, and the overall model quality statistics for the whole protein, we believe that the model as 
it has been presented in the manuscript is the best possible fit (Response Table 1 in this rebuttal). 
In K2P channels, the two conserved threonines  at the bottom of the selectivity filter (SF) are the 
only amino acids within the SF that coordinate K + ions through their side chains. All other amino 
acids at the SF coordinate K + ions through carbonyl oxygens. It has been shown by multiple 
groups previously that reorientation of the carbonyl oxygens away from the SF is a means to

7. Could the authors comment/expand on the voltage-dependence of TWIK-2 in the, and whether

In a  2016 study S chewe et al.  provided significant insights into the voltage -dependent gating 
mechanisms of K2P channel, challenging the traditional view that voltage -sensing channel 
requires a canonical voltage -sensing domain. Instead, they proposed the SF to have  a “check 
valve” gating behavior. They also note that this type of voltage -dependency has been observed 
in most K2Ps except TWIK113. Apart from the residues at the SF, voltage-dependent behavior in 
K2Ps, especially the TREK family, is also regulated by other factors such as mechanical force, 
ligands, and lipids 14–18. These reports suggest that the same K2P channel can switch between 
voltage-dependent and voltage -independent current responses depending on their conditions, 
perhaps a regulatory mechanism of certain K2P channels. Because we see that the central cavity 
is occupied by lipid -like densities in our cryo -EM map, and because TWIK -2 has an ability to 
translocate between endosomes and plasma membrane and has been proposed to interact with 
other proteins via its C -terminus, we believe it is possible that voltage -dependency in TWIK -2 
could be a context dependent phenomenon.
