(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Ware and colleagues investigated the role of microglia in subgranular zone neurogenesis in adult
microglia-specific Tgfb1- and Tgfbr1-deficient mice. The authors convincingly demonstrated that Tgfb1- and Tgfbr1-deficient
mice develop dyshomeostatic microglia (as previously shown by the same group) resulting in transiently increased
neurogenesis in the hippocampal subgranular zone which was accompanied by altered anxiety-like behaviour. Using
elegant transgenic models and BrdU injections, the authors revealed that enhanced survival and not proliferation of DCX+
cells is essential for the observed increased adult neurogenis in the presence of Alk5-deficient microglia. Depletion of

neurogenesis. Moreover, using scRNAseq, the authors identified reduced PTEN signaling in new born neuroblasts and
reversed the transiently heightened neurogenesis by rapamicycin-induced mTOR signaling inhibition. Overall, the study is
well designed, well structured, and the results are presented with high quality figures. Together, the study provides valuable
data which further broadens the knowledge of microglia-regulated hippocampal adult neurogenesis and highlights the
essential role of microglial TGF-beta signaling.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Summary 
This potentially interesting study investigates the role of TGF-β signaling in microglia and its impact on adult hippocampal
neurogenesis and behavior. The authors use inducible knockout 
models targeting various TGF-β pathway components (e.g.,
Alk5, Tgfbr2, Tgfb1) specifically in microglia. They report that disruption of TGF-β signaling leads to microglial
dyshomeostasis, increased proliferation and survival of adult-born neurons in the subgranular zone, and altered anxiety-like
and cognitive behavior. scRNA-seq and pharmacological interventions suggest this effect is mediated via downregulation of
PTEN and activation of the mTOR pathway in neuroblasts. The findings suggest that activated microglia can stimulate adult
neurogenesis and modulate affective and cognitive behavior, but they do not demonstrate this directly. Additional
experiments are needed to establish a causal link. Furthermore, more thorough analyses of the histological data, the animal
models, and sex of mice are needed. 
Major Comments: 
1. Although both Cx3cr1CreER and P2ry12CreER drivers are used, the reliance on Cx3cr1CreER raises concerns about off-
target recombination in BAMs and peripheral myeloid cells. Additional validation using BAM-specific markers or region-
specific analysis (e.g., meninges, choroid plexus) would strengthen the microglia-specific conclusions. 
2. The degree of Alk5 deletion and recombination efficiency at the protein level is not shown. Demonstrating this with IHC or
Western blot for ALK5 in sorted microglia would reinforce the mechanistic link to TGF-β signaling loss. 
3. The study concludes that TNF-α and IGF-1 are not responsible for the observed increase in neurogenesis. However,
given known redundancy and interplay in cytokine signaling, additional discussion or testing of combinatorial cytokine
blockade would be important. 
4. Rapamycin only partially rescues the adult neurogenesis phenotype. This suggests that alternative or parallel pathways
might be involved. Additional discussion is needed to address the possibility of other downstream signals in activated
microglia. 
5. While the manuscript demonstrates increased adult neurogenesis following TGF-β signaling disruption in microglia, the
causal molecular mechanism remains underdeveloped. The study eliminates IGF-1 and TNF-α as single factors and
implicates the PTEN/mTOR, it does not explore whether the changes in PTEN/mTOR in neuroblasts are a direct result of
microglial-derived cues (e.g., cytokines). 
6. The authors report reduced avoidance behavior in the EPM and diminished cognitive performance on the Barnes maze in
microglial TGF-β iKO mice but cannot attribute this directly to increased adult neurogenesis. Without this important
connection, it is impossible to interpret the microglia effects as working through changes in adult born neurons. 
This
possibility could be assessed by blocking adult neurogenesis in the MG-Alk5-iKO during the behavioral testing window. 
7. The cell count data are presented per section or per image field. This type of analysis can lead to inaccurate comparisons
if the brain regions of different treatment groups differ in size/volume. 
The data should be presented as overall counts in a
given brain region or as cell densities (number of cells per volume). 
8. The n size varies greatly across experiments. What is the justification for this? In cases where just 3 mice are analyzed, it
is questionable whether reliable results will be obtained. Also, some of the data are presented with points representing
individual cells. This is misleading and the data should be shown as points for individual mice. 
9. 
The distribution of males and females is not even across the studies. Did the authors investigate whether trends toward
sex differences exist? 
If not, it is difficult to compare across measures when some of them have with mixed sex mice and
others have only one sex (e.g., Figure 4H). 
10. Avoidance behavior on the EPM has been linked to ventral hippocampus, not dorsal hippocampus. The images are all
from dorsal hippocampus and it is not clear whether the entire hippocampus was analyzed or just the dorsal part. Ventral
hippocampus should be included. 
11. Terms like "microglial activation," "dyshomeostasis," and "reactivity" are used interchangeably. Consider defining and
using them more consistently for clarity. It is not clear at all what microglial dyshomeostasis is. Unless there is something
different from microglial reactivity, it should be eliminated from the text. If there is something different, it should be defined as
such. 
12. The authors note that increased adult neurogenesis is transient (DCX+ peaks at 3 weeks). Discussion on whether this
reflects compensatory regulatory feedback, or microglia reversion to a homeostatic state might be helpful. Whether changes
in neurogenesis correlate with sustained changes in microglial profile (e.g., do activated microglia persist at 6–12 weeks?).

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigates the consequences of microglia-specific TGF-β signaling inhibition on hippocampal adult
neurogenesis and behavior. Using multiple inducible knockout models (targeting TGF-β1, ALK5, and TβRII), the authors
demonstrate that loss of microglial homeostatic signaling leads to a transient increase in SGZ neurogenesis and a lasting
increase in mature neurons. Single-cell RNA sequencing and pharmacological inhibition experiments support a mechanistic
link via downregulation of PTEN and upregulation of the mTOR pathway in neuroblasts. Behavioral testing indicates
reduced anxiety-like behavior but no alteration in forgetting. 
This work is conceptually innovative, methodologically rigorous, and highly relevant for the fields of neurogenesis, microglia-
neuron crosstalk, and neuroinflammation. However, clarification and strengthening of mechanistic interpretation, figure
presentation, and behavioral conclusions would improve the manuscript. 
Major Comments 
1. The finding that activated microglia can stimulate, rather than inhibit, adult neurogenesis in the absence of injury
contradicts longstanding assumptions and presents a paradigm shift. 
2. The integration of transcriptomic and behavioral data makes this study of broad relevance to neuroscience and
regenerative biology. 
3. The authors argue that the observed transient increase in DCX+ neuroblasts results in a long-term gain in mature
neurons. While this is supported by BrdU/NeuN data, the term "transient" may be misleading. Please clarify this distinction
and emphasize that the transient increase refers to proliferation/differentiation, not integration. 
4. The PTEN-mTOR link is well-supported; however, the authors should discuss whether other converging pathways from
their DEG data (e.g., synaptic organization, apoptotic signaling) might also contribute. 
5. Behavioral phenotypes (reduced anxiety-like behavior and spatial learning deficits) are presented as consequences of
increased neurogenesis. However, causality remains correlative. While the authors acknowledge limitations, the discussion
would benefit from more elaboration on future strategies to test this directly (e.g., combining NSC-targeted inhibition with
microglial KO background). 
6. The fear-conditioning “forgetting” paradigm might not be ideally timed. Consider clarifying whether pre-treatment with TAM
would be more suitable to test the influence of neurogenesis on memory clearance. 
7. The Barnes Maze was used to assess spatial learning, but reversal learning—a component of cognitive flexibility strongly
linked to adult hippocampal neurogenesis—was not tested. Given prior work by Kempermann and others demonstrating that
reversal learning in tasks like the Morris Water Maze is sensitive to neurogenesis status, the authors should briefly discuss
this limitation or provide justification for the behavioral test battery selected. 
8. The scRNA-seq data are rich, but dense. Please consider a summary table or schematic of key pathway-level changes
(PTEN/mTOR, IGF1, TNF-α) for clarity. 
9. Improve visual clarity of UMAPs and volcano plots (Figure 7), including font sizes, axis labels, and color keys. 
10. Some experiments (e.g., PLX5622 repopulation) use n=3–4 animals. These data should be interpreted cautiously, and
limitations of statistical power noted. 
11. Sex as a variable and tamoxifen effects: The inclusion of both sexes and annotation of data points by sex across figures
is commendable. However, no sex-specific analysis is reported. If the study was not powered or intended to detect sex
differences, please state this explicitly in the Methods or Discussion sections. 
Additionally, tamoxifen is a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM), and estrogen signaling differs between males
and females—particularly in the hippocampus where it influences both neurogenesis and microglial function. Although
tamoxifen is used here solely for Cre activation, the potential for sex-dependent off-target effects should be acknowledged,
especially when interpreting behavioral and neurogenic outcomes. 
Minor Comments 
12. Figure 7K–N: Highlight DCX+ cells migrating out of SGZ more clearly with arrows or labels. 
13. Clarify whether the scRNA-seq was done on the whole hippocampus or DG-specific regions. 
14. Refer consistently to “dyshomeostatic microglia” or “TGF-β-deficient microglia” rather than broadly as “activated
microglia.”

impact on adult hippocampal neurogenesis and behavior. The authors use inducible knockout

experiments are needed to establish a causal link. Furthermore, more thorough analyses of the 
histological data, the animal models, and sex of mice are needed.

Cx3cr1CreER raises concerns about off-target recombination in BAMs and peripheral myeloid 
cells. Additional validation using BAM-specific markers or region-specific analysis (e.g., 
meninges, choroid plexus) would strengthen the microglia-specific conclusions. 
- We have now added data to show that peripheral myeloid cells are not affected in the 
Cx3cr1CreER Alk5 iKO mice (New Supplemental Fig 1), consistent with our previous 
report in the Cx3cr1CreER Tgfb1 iKO mice (PMID: 38906887). Additionally, we 
quantified the pSMAD signal in microglia, perivascular macrophages, and choroid plexus 
macrophages in both the Cx3cr1CreER and P2ry12CreER/CreER Alk5 iKO mice (New 
Supplemental Fig 7). Interestingly, both perivascular macrophages and choroid plexus 
macrophages show reduced pSMAD3 signaling in the Cx3cr1CreER mice. In the P2ry12 
CreER Alk5 iKO mice, microglia show partial pSMAD3 loss compared to Cx3cr1CreER 
Alk5 iKO microglia, consistent with the lower recombination efficiency of this mouse 
line. Perivascular macrophages show similar pSMAD3 levels, and choroid plexus 
macrophages show reduced pSMAD3 levels, consistent with the previous report of the 
lack of reporter recombination in perivascular macrophages but presence of 
recombination in choroid plexus macrophages (PMID 32573436 and 38217856). Based 
on this data, we have now revised the results and discussion to reflect that the phenotype 
of increased DCX+ cells might be due to changes in parenchymal microglia and/or 
choroid plexus macrophages.  
2. The degree of Alk5 deletion and recombination efficiency at the protein level is not shown. 
Demonstrating this with IHC or Western blot for ALK5 in sorted microglia would reinforce the 
mechanistic link to TGF-β signaling loss.

-     We were not able to validate any good antibody for ALK5 via Western blot or 
immunostaining (using WT and constitutive Alk5 KO tissue), therefore, we used the 
downstream signaling effector of the Alk5 receptor, phosphorylation of SMAD3 
(pSMAD3) as a readout for ALK5-mediated TGF-β signaling. We have now provided 
data on pSMAD3 staining in the Alk5 and Tgfb1iKO lines at 3, 6 and 12 weeks post TAM 
(New Supplemental Fig 3 and 6).

neurogenesis. However, given known redundancy and interplay in cytokine signaling, additional 
discussion or testing of combinatorial cytokine blockade would be important. 
-    We have revised our conclusion to be that increased TNF-α or IGF1 in the iKO microglia 
is not necessary for the observed increase in neurogenesis. The results on Igf1 KO are 
consistent with a recent publication showing that in humans, microglia-derived IGF1 is 
critical for interneuron neurogenesis, which is not recapitulated in mice (PMID 
40770097). There might be species differences at play here. Nevertheless, we have now 
added more discussion on combinatorial effects of growth factors as well as redundancy 
and interplay in cytokine signaling (pages 17, 24-25).

alternative or parallel pathways might be involved. Additional discussion is needed to address 
the possibility of other downstream signals in activated microglia. 
-     Rapamycin in vivo treatment completely rescues the adult neurogenesis phenotype in the 
measurements of DCX+ cells and dendrite arborization (no difference was observed 
between the WT and KO in the rapamycin-treated group). Rapamycin shows an effect of 
its own on the migration of these cells in the DG. Our new in vitro experiments of the 3D 
co-culture of WT NSC with WT or Alk5 KO microglia show a similar phenotype in 
differentiated immature neurons, which is also reversed by Rapamycin treatment (New 
Fig. 9).

Reviewer #4 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript investigates the consequences of microglia-specific TGF-β signaling inhibition 
on hippocampal adult neurogenesis and behavior. Using multiple inducible knockout models 
(targeting TGF-β1, ALK5, and TβRII), the authors demonstrate that loss of microglial 
homeostatic signaling leads to a transient increase in SGZ neurogenesis and a lasting increase in 
mature neurons. Single-cell RNA sequencing and pharmacological inhibition experiments 
support a mechanistic link via downregulation of PTEN and upregulation of the mTOR pathway 
in neuroblasts. Behavioral testing indicates reduced anxiety-like behavior but no alteration in 
forgetting. 
This work is conceptually innovative, methodologically rigorous, and highly relevant for the 
fields of neurogenesis, microglia-neuron crosstalk, and neuroinflammation. However, 
clarification and strengthening of mechanistic interpretation, figure presentation, and behavioral 
conclusions would improve the manuscript.
