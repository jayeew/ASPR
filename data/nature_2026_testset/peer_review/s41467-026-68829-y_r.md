Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The article puts forward a very interesting proposal: transforming shale gas formations themselves into net carbon sinks by
applying techniques such as CO
₂
 hydraulic fracturing, the huff-n-puff method (enhanced recovery with CO
₂
 injection), and
storage in depleted wells. The approach is innovative and is framed within the idea of achieving geological negative
emissions—something that could have a transformative impact on the carbon balance of the unconventional gas industry.
One of the manuscript’s most striking claims is that the potential for net emissions reduction could reach 1.981 billion tons of
CO
₂
 (1.981 Gt CO
₂
) between 2024 and 2060, which would represent a spectacular climate benefit if validated. However, this
ambition contrasts sharply with the scientific execution of the article, which presents significant shortcomings both in its
conceptual framing and in its development. The main issues identified are outlined below in an orderly and reasoned
manner. 
1) The introduction lacks solid and specific references to support its key claims. The concept of geological negative
emissions is mentioned as emerging, but no prior scientific literature from countries with mature shale gas industries—such
as the US or Canada—is properly cited. As a result, the reader lacks the necessary context to situate this work within the
current state of knowledge. 
2) Throughout the article, highly ambitious figures for the potential CO
₂
 sequestration are presented, yet the traceability of
those calculations is not clearly or transparently explained. For example, the authors state that a total of 1.98 Gt CO
₂
 could
be stored by 2060 using a combination of technologies, but the assumptions behind this estimate—such as injection
coefficients or average recovery factors—are not broken down or justified. This undermines the credibility of the conclusions.
3) The article fails to address emissions associated with methane, both in terms of fugitive leaks and full lifecycle impacts
(e.g., indirect emissions from supply chain activities). This is a critical omission, as recent studies have consistently shown
that methane leakage is often the key obstacle to achieving carbon neutrality—or net negativity—in shale gas systems.
Here, methane is barely mentioned, and certainly not quantified. 
4) There is no distinct conclusion section or a clear and concise summary of the key findings. The final ideas are scattered
across various parts of the discussion, making it difficult for the reader to extract the article’s main contributions, limitations,
or policy implications. 
5) The structure of the manuscript is confusing. There is no clear delineation between the methodology, results, and
discussion sections. Technical concepts, emissions calculations, and interpretative commentary are blended without smooth
transitions. This lack of structure makes it harder to follow the line of reasoning and diminishes the overall impact of the
work. 
6) The mathematical methodology is underdeveloped. While a few formulas are included, there are no worked examples or
validation cases to demonstrate the robustness of the models used. For instance, the net capture cost (NCC), net present
value (NPV), and internal rate of return (IRR) are all mentioned, but not a single complete numerical example is provided
using real or assumed data. 
7) The figures are mostly low-resolution maps focused exclusively on China. Although it is logical for the study to focus on

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

that country, it would be beneficial to include at least some international comparison or broader context, given the global
relevance of the topic. Moreover, the maps do not provide sufficient technical detail—such as geological characteristics,
pressure, depth, or estimated storage capacity. 
8) Internationally comparable economic units are not used. All costs are expressed in yuan per cubic meter, with no
conversions to dollars or other widely used benchmarks. This limits the article’s accessibility to international audiences and
hinders comparison with other studies. 
9) More comprehensive visual tools are missing. There are no summary tables that compare the different technologies (CO
₂
fracturing, huff-n-puff, injection into depleted wells), nor graphs illustrating CO
₂
 capture over time or economic profitability
curves. This weakens the communicative value of the analysis. 
10) Although the manuscript mentions the use of Monte Carlo simulations to assess uncertainty, it does not explain the
underlying assumptions, input distributions, or the interpretation of the results. No probabilistic outputs, sensitivity analyses,
or uncertainty ranges are shown. This part of the analysis remains underdeveloped and unconvincing. 
11) From an editorial perspective, the manuscript requires a thorough review. There are spelling errors, confusing phrases,
and misused terms (e.g., “puff-n-huff” instead of “huff-n-puff”). Furthermore, there is some repetition of ideas that could be
avoided with more careful editing. 
12) The article does not critically analyze the risks, limits, or barriers of the proposal. For example, the possibility of CO
₂
leaks, infrastructure costs, social acceptance, or regulatory implications are not discussed. The presentation is overly
optimistic and lacks a balanced reflection on potential flaws or uncertainties. 
13) The structure of the article urgently needs improvement. There is no hierarchical numbering of sections and subsections
that allows the reader to easily orient themselves. The discussion mixes several thematic lines without an easily identifiable
order. It is strongly recommended that authors include an outline or "content map" at the beginning of the document,
specifying the main sections of the manuscript (e.g., methodology → results → economic discussion → climate
implications), and that consistent numbering be used throughout the text (e.g., 1, 1.1, 1.2, 2, 2.1, etc.). This formatting
improvement would greatly facilitate understanding of the argument and critical reading of the document. 
In summary, the article starts with a very promising idea and addresses a crucial problem with remarkable ambition.
However, it needs a thorough revision to fulfill its promise. It lacks methodological rigor, clarity of presentation, risk analysis,
and solid references that situate the work within the existing international framework. If these aspects are corrected, the
manuscript could make a valuable contribution to the debate on negative emissions in the shale gas industry. In its current
state, however, the most reasonable recommendation is a major revision.

captured from air with DAC or BECCS. In addition, the system boundary has to be cradle-to-grave. Unless using the system
expansion LCA approach. 
6. When linking the TEA and LCA, it is worthwhile to look at the economic and environmental trade-offs. However, authors

[Response]: We sincerely appreciate your insightful comment regarding the carbon sink 
assessment methodology. Your observation about the role of adsorption and 
competitive adsorption mechanisms in CO₂ storage is highly pertinent and provides an 
important opportunity to clarify our methodological approach. 
 
We fully agree that, from a physicochemical mechanism perspective, injected CO 2 
interacts with organic matter in shale through adsorption during all three processes: 
CO2 fracturing, CO2 huff-and-puff, and CO 2 sequestration. However, our assessment 
methodology deliberately distinguishes between these processes based on their 
operational characteristics and long-term sequestration outcomes. 
 
For CO2 fracturing and CO 2 huff-and-puff, the well remains in production after CO₂ 
injection rather than being permanently sealed. During subsequent production, 
declining reservoir pressure causes partial desorption of previously adsorbed CO 2, 
which is then co-produced along with natural gas. The net CO 2 sequestered over the 
well’s lifecycle represents a dynamic balance among adsorption, desorption, and 
production processes. Theoretical calculations based solely on initial adsorption 
capacity would significantly overestimate actual long-term sequestration. Consequently, 
we adopt an empirical approach grounded in field data and observed flowback rates to 
estimate the net carbon sink more accurately. Th is methodology is consistent with 
established practices in the literature (Ji et al., 2023; Wilkins et al., 2016).

In contrast, CO2 sequestration in depleted wells involves permanent well closure after 
injection, with no subsequent production.  Under these  conditions, all storage 
mechanisms, including structural trapping, adsorption, and mineralization, can achieve 
their full theoretical potential and provide permanent sequestration. Therefore, we 
employ mechanism-based modeling to explicitly quantify each contribution (Equations 
3-7), including adsorption capacity calculated through competitive adsorption 
selectivity (Equation 5 and 6). 
 
We appreciate the Reviewer’s comment, which helps clarify this important aspect of 
our assessment framework. We believe this methodology provides a more robust and 
realistic evaluation of carbon sink potential across the shale gas value chain.

We fully agree with your assessment that the source of CO₂ is critical in determining
