Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents a protein engineering approach aimed at the deracemization of axially chiral allenes through the
development of designed photoenzymes. By integrating the photosensitive non-canonical amino acid BpA into a CTB10
protein scaffold, the authors successfully created tunable catalytic sites that facilitate enantioselective triplet energy transfer
under aerobic conditions. The authors further enhanced catalytic performance and stereocontrol through iterative structure-
guided mutagenesis, which introduced hydrogen-bonding interactions, reoriented substrate-adjacent residues to optimize
donor-acceptor distances, and fine-tuned the BpA microenvironment. Crystallographic analysis revealed selective binding of
the (R)-enantiomer within the chiral pocket and illuminated the mechanism of energy transfer leading to double-bond
isomerization via a biradical intermediate. This system demonstrated versatility, accommodating a variety of allenic
carboxylic acids, esters, and amides and achieving enantiomeric excess values of up to 98% for specific substrates. This
work illustrates the potential of integrating rational design, genetic code expansion, and photochemistry to generate novel
enzymatic functions for deracemization synthesis, providing a programmable and mild pathway to obtain chiral allenes. 
This is an important advance in energy transfer photoenzyme catalysis. I support the publication of this study after the
authors address the following concerns: 
1. Benzophenone was employed as the photosensitizer for the activation of allenes through energy transfer at 365 nm. It is
recommended that the UV-Vis spectra of model substrates be examined to determine if there is any background excitation of
substrates that could potentially compromise enantioselectivity. 
2. The mechanistic depiction in Figure 5d illustrates how enantiomeric enrichment was achieved through the
enantioselective discrimination between (R)- and (S)-substrates. However, the authors state that following energy
absorption, the (R)-substrate undergoes double-bond cleavage to generate a 1,3-biradical intermediate, which subsequently
dissociates from the photoenzyme. This free intermediate then recombines in solution. Given the short lifetime of triplet
diradicals, this scenario may be unlikely. The authors should clarify this situation in relation to the time scale of allene
diradicals and diffusion kinetics. 
3. The effects of triplet quenchers remain inconclusive (see Table S24). There is a recent study that explores the impact of
various triplet quenchers on the enantioselectivity of photoenzyme catalysis (Nat. Catal. 2025, 10.1038/s41929-025-01433-
3). The authors are encouraged to reference this study to perform extra quenching experiments to support their mechanistic
proposal. 
4. The use of purified photoenzymes is noted. What would be the outcome if cell lysates containing the overexpressed
photoenzyme were utilized? 
5. It is impressive that the evolved photoenzymes achieved >90% enantiomeric excess within 30 minutes, indicating
excellent enantioinduction and high enzyme kinetics; however, 10 mol% of purified enzyme was used. I recommend
reducing the enzyme loading and calculating the turnover number of the enzyme. 
6. The field of photobiocatalysis is rapidly evolving. Recent reviews on this topic should be included to provide an updated
overview of this area: Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2025, e202419262; Chem. Soc. Rev., 2025, 54, 5157. Additionally, recent
notable studies on artificial energy transfer photoenzymes should be cited: Nat. Chem. 2025, 17, 1083; Nat. Catal. 2025, 8,
822; Nat. Catal. 2025, 10.1038/s41929-025-01433-3; J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2024, 146, 19030.

Q2. The mechanistic depiction in Figure 5d illustrates how enantiomeric enrichment was 
achieved through the enantioselective discrimination between (R) - and (S) -substrates. 
However, the authors state that following energy absorption, the (R) -substrate undergoes 
double-bond cleavage to generate a 1,3-biradical intermediate, which subsequently dissociates 
from the photoenzyme. This free intermediate then recombines in solution. Given the short 
lifetime of triplet diradicals, this scenario may be unlikely. The authors should clarify this 
situation in relation to the time scale of allene diradicals and diffusion kinetics. 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer's insightful comments concerning the mechanistic 
rationale for diradical dissociation.  
According to the literature on diradical reactions  (Angew. Chem. Int. Ed. 2020, 59,  
21640-21647; Chem. Eur.  J. 2002, 8,  2464-2475), the 1,2 -diradical intermediate  would be 
expected to yield exclusively the R-isomer when recombined within a  chiral photoenzyme 
environment. In contrast, recombination in an achiral environment can lead to the formation of 
racemic mixtures, thereby facilitating enrichment of the S-isomer, which is consistent with the 
experimental outcomes. Furthermore, previous studies have reported the disso ciation of this 
diradical species from a chiral environment. Bach et al. reported its decay to a racemic mixture 
(1:1 ratio of R- and S-isomers) (Nature 2018, 564, 240 –243). Subsequent density functional 
theory calculations by the same group further indicate that the diradical possesses a symmetric, 
achiral structure (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2021, 143, 11209–11217). Therefore, they suggest that the 
intermediate exhibits a sufficient lifetime to allow dissociation from the catalytic environment  
and that excess energy derived from energy transfer drives rapid dissociation of the complex.
