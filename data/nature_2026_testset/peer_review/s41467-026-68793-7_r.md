Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments on “Delivery of Peptide Coacervates to Form Stable Interaction Hubs in Cells” by Matt Good and colleagues 
In this concise study, Tu et al. established a peptide-derived gel-like condensates for cellular delivery and cytosolic
exposure. A large portion of large delivered condensates (> 2 micron) remain stable in cells up to at least 5 days.
Functionalized condensates are able to recruit a cellular target via nanobody binding. This is an interesting technological
development with plenty of applications. However, the authors need to substantiate their study with some further
experiments before I can suggest its acceptance for publication in Nature Communications. 
Major comments: 
1)It is pivotal demonstrate cytosolic exposed condensates can indeed trigger endogenous signaling. For instance the
authors can functionalize the condensates with LC3-interaction regions to see the recruitment LC3-labeled membrane for
autophagosome formation. They can also functionalize the condensates with apoptosis-inducing domains to see whether
the condensates are sufficient to induce apoptosis. Etc...... 
2)The authors mentioned that previous papers reported that these condensates are engulfed into cells via micropinocytosis.
It is still instrumental to observe the internalization of the condensates via long-term imaging. 
3)They showed that about 10% condensates can actually recruite target protein (Figure 5E). It is also important to determine
what percentage of cells lack of any condensates that can recruite target protein. This issue is related to the future
application of this technology. 
4)It is also important to know which type of condensates is more stable, the ones entrapped in endosome or those escaped
from endosome. 
Minor comments: 
Figure 1D, 1E, the time for FRAP is only 5 minutes. Longer is desirable. 
There are many typos and inconsistent nomenclatures. To name a few below: 1) in Figure 1B, the unit for diameter is um. It
should be μm. 2) “using either 
method for to induce LLPS”, “for” is not needed. 3) “In an effort to build stable hubs than can be delivered to cells”, “than” is
“that”. 4) “We conclude that cells our large coacervates”, “cells” is not needed. 5) “for macromolecules larger than 2-10 kDA”,
kDA? 6) many protein names are not consistent in text versus in figures, e.g. GFP-HBP is used in text while GFPHBP is
used in Figure 2; VHH4-mCherry is used in text while VHH4 and mCherry were just stacked in Figure 4. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors describe the use of peptide coacervates as internal artificial organelles capable of promoting the targeted
localization of a protein (GFP) in cells. The results suggest that peptide coacervates can be explored as a viable platform for
the design of synthetic organelles and cargo delivery systems to cells. The results are interesting and raise questions that I

believe are needed to improve the impact and clarity of the work. The paper is well written and easy to read. I hope my
comments and questions below will be helpful. 
The work is based on the use of HBpep and HBpep-SA, which are peptides that have been previously reported and also
used for cargo delivery to cells. A substantial part of the manuscript is dedicated to the characterization of the coacervates
obtained using these peptides, which reproduces some of the main conclusions demonstrated in previous publications.
However, there are a few points that I feel need attention, as follows: 
1. What is the rationale for using HBpep-SA vs. HBpep? The main experiments in the paper, showing the functionality of the
hubs in the cells, only use BHpep-SA and do not really explain the unique properties of this peptide compared to HBpep.
More information on this is recommended for clarity. 
2. One of the conclusions from the GFP partitioning experiments is that the peptide coacervates could be used for a variety
of applications in drug delivery and engineering. I agree with this statement. Is it possible to demonstrate a more general
effect where different relevant model molecules are encapsulated using the described procedure? This would enrich the
manuscript and support the generality statement. 
3. The tagging approach to increase the partitioning of GFP is really interesting. However, the paper does not provide
enough information about the rationale for the particular tag used and the properties and effects observed when the tag was
used. Why does the tag work? Can it be applied to other types of molecules or does it only work with GFP? 
4. The manuscript says that the particles were generally more stable when they were larger. What is the hypothesis for this
size-dependent stability? Is it a kinetic effect? What is the stability of the droplets outside the cells? Is it comparable to the
observed stability inside the cells? Is there something special in the cytosol that contributes to the observed stability? 
5. Figure 4B. The labels can be confusing. It could give the impression that VHH4 was only tested with mCHerry (not GFP). I
recommend revising the labels. 
6. Figure 4B shows data for HBpep-SA only. Why not both peptides (HBpep vs. HBpep-SA)? How does peptide structure
affect nanobody partitioning? 
7. Figure 4B. GFP is enriched at the interface. Why is that? Figure 2B shows that GFP partitions homogeneously in HBpep
droplets (pH shift). Does this suggest that the nanobodies are not homogeneously distributed in the droplets? It would be
recommended to determine the distribution of nanobodies. 
8. The manuscript states that it should be possible to achieve higher internal partitioning of molecules with lower molecular
weights or by allowing incubation with coacervates for longer periods of time. A more detailed study showing the generality
of the approach for encapsulation and delivery of various cargoes is recommended (and quite interesting if successfully
demonstrated). 
9. Does the TAT-HA2 peptide promote endosomal escape in this paper? The manuscript says so, but I could not find a
control experiment showing that the peptide works as expected. A control showing the effect of this peptide is recommended.
10. The authors mention membranization of the coacervate droplets. This is not clearly explained. What is the nature of the
membrane? How can it be demonstrated that a membrane actually surrounds the droplets? Does the surface charge of the
membranized coacervates change? How does this affect the uptake of the coacervates and the stability of the droplets in the
cells? 
11. Figure 5B shows GFP homogeneously distributed in the droplets, while Figure 4B shows a different result (surface
accumulation). A more detailed explanation seems to be necessary. What causes the differences in GFP distribution? 
12. The conclusion of the paper states that the nanobody coacervates can function as stable hubs for days. However, this
data is not provided. Given the importance of this conclusion to the paper, I recommend that the stability of the droplets in
cells be investigated and the data included in the manuscript or supporting information.

REVIEWER COMMENTS (Tu et al, NCOMMS-24-82260)  Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author):  In this concise study, Tu et al. established a peptide-derived gel-like condensates for cellular delivery and cytosolic exposure. A large portion of large delivered condensates (> 2 micron) remain stable in cells up to at least 5 days. Functionalized condensates are able to recruit a cellular target via nanobody binding. This is an interesting technological development with plenty of applications. However, the authors need to substantiate their study with some further experiments before I can suggest its acceptance for publication in Nature Communications. We thank the referee for their careful review of our study and for their positive comments that our platform is innovative technically.  Major comments: 1) It is pivotal demonstrate cytosolic exposed condensates can indeed trigger endogenous signaling. For instance the authors can functionalize the condensates with LC3-interaction regions to see the recruitment LC3-labeled membrane for autophagosome formation. They can also functionalize the condensates with apoptosis-inducing domains to see whether the condensates are sufficient to induce apoptosis. Etc. This is a thoughtful point. Following reviewer advice, we sought to generate reactive hubs – and settled on a strategy to specifically degrade intracellular targets by co-assembly and delivery of a bioRPOTAC in HBpepSA coacervates. This bioPROTAC, comprised of a VHHGFP4 nanobody with partial SPOP (an E3 ubiquitin ligase), when delivered to cells selectively degraded an endogenously expressed GFP target protein (new Fig. 6, see below). Delivery of SPOP enzyme alone did not efficiently degrade targets; instead, showing it must be linked to the VHHGFP4 nanobody (Fig. 6C). Note, bioPROTACs such as these have very limited ability to cross the plasma membrane on their own. By coupling it to peptide coacervate based cell permeation, we demonstrate the utility of these deliverable hubs to serve as hubs not only for binding cellular targets but also as reaction centers to control target turnover. 
 Figure 6A, C. bioPROTAC-loaded hubs function as degradation centers for target GFP in cells   2) The authors mentioned that previous papers reported that these condensates are engulfed into cells via m(a)cropinocytosis. It is still instrumental to observe the internalization of the condensates via long-term imaging. We note that the uptake of coacervates has been previously established as a non-canonical pathway integrating features of macropinocytosis and phagocytosis by the Miserez lab in a recent publication1 (Shebanova, Advanced Science, 2024, DOI: 10.1002/advs.). In that study, three stages of coacervates uptake were observed and characterized: membrane adhesion, cytoskeleton

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

reorganization and internalization. Additionally, transmission electron microscopy and live imaging were provided in that study to illustrate each process and showed membrane surrounding the engulfed particles after internalization. Furthermore, they tested a potent macropinocytosis inhibitor EIPA, which decreased coacervate uptake by nearly 70%. This study utilized the same HBpep coacervate platform in cell lines and we feel it is redundant re-establish the process of uptake. Instead, our study focuses on long-term stability of hubs, functionalization with nanobodies and ability to interface with native targets in the cell, areas previously not investigated.  3) They showed that about 10% condensates can actually recruit target protein (Figure 5E). It is also important to determine what percentage of cells lack of any condensates that can recruit target protein. This issue is related to the future application of this technology. We believe the reviewer may be asking for clarification on two points: what % of cells show any significant target recruitment to hubs and what is the background level of target recruitment when only hubs are delivered without the co-assembled nanobody. First, the percentage of cells that show significant to recruitment to internal hubs is now quantified in Fig. 5F and Supp Fig 5E. This shows up to 60-80% of cells are positive for hub recruitment when the nanobody is present in hubs, whereas only 0-7% are in the control when no nanobody is present (control: cells containing hubs loaded with mCherry alone).  To the second point, we note that there can be no target recruitment when hubs are absent from cells, because there is no ‘body’ or ‘locus’ for which we can measure enrichment. Notably GFP is well-dispersed within the cytosol of this stable cell line in the absence of hubs (Supp Fig. 5C). However, we also note that there is very little target recruitment to hubs that are delivered without co-assembled VHHGFP4 nanobody (Fig 5A-F, and Supp Fig 5D). This demonstrates that the background non-specific binding of targets to hubs is very low or absent. Notably in a new stable monoclonal cell line expressing GFP, we observed 0% of control hubs with significant target recruitment and 0% of cells containing any significant target recruitment to cells (Supp Fig 5D,E). This demonstrates the specificity of the nanobody hub platform and why it has broad utility.  Additionally, in new experiments we found that 5 days after delivery, up to 40% of all hubs showed target recruitment when chloroquine was added to promote endosomal escape, which exceeds typical rate of endosomal escape rates of LNPs (Wang, Materials Horizons, 2025, DOI: 10.1039/D4MH01781A)(Beach, ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces, 2021, DOI: 10.1021/acsami.1c18359) (Supp Fig 5K). This further demonstrates the potential of these hubs to efficiently interact with native protein targets. These data are shown below.

4)It is also important to know which type of condensates is more stable, the ones entrapped in endosome or those escaped from endosome. The reviewer asks an interesting question. To date, it has not been reported whether hubs are stable for days within endosomes or when free and cytosolic. To address this question, we used target recruitment to hubs as a proxy for hub release and followed the fraction of hubs that did or did not recruit target from day 1 up through day 5 (Supp Fig S5K). Using HBpep-SA coacevates co-assembled with nanobody VHHGFP4, we found similar but increased percentages of hubs that showed target GFP recruitment at Day 5 (19%) compared to Day 1 (12%). This suggest that hubs have similar stability when entrapped in endosome or perhaps are slightly more stable when free in the cytosol. However, this is only a proxy metric for release, and we believe it is beyond the scope of the current study to track both entrapped and free populations and to pulse-chase them to measure turnover rates. Because it is possible that the kinetics of endosomal release and degradation are not matched, with the current data we cannot definitively determine whether a similar proportion of hubs are recruiting because of similar degradation rates or because of fast release and intermediate degradation which may result in a similar steady state to what we observe. Notably, the number of large hubs is not decreasing dramatically over 5 days (Fig 3E), so degradation rates appear slow in general, for either population.

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author):  The authors describe the use of peptide coacervates as internal artificial organelles capable of promoting the targeted localization of a protein (GFP) in cells. The results suggest that peptide coacervates can be explored as a viable platform for the design of synthetic organelles and cargo delivery systems to cells. The results are interesting and raise questions that I believe are needed to improve the impact and clarity of the work. The paper is well written and easy to read. I hope my comments and questions below will be helpful.  We thank the referee for their favorable assessment of our work along with their thoughtful suggestions for improvement.  The work is based on the use of HBpep and HBpep-SA, which are peptides that have been previously reported and also used for cargo delivery to cells. A substantial part of the manuscript is dedicated to the characterization of the coacervates obtained using these peptides, which reproduces some of the main conclusions demonstrated in previous publications. However, there are a few points that I feel need attention, as follows:  1. What is the rationale for using HBpep-SA vs. HBpep? The main experiments in the paper, showing the functionality of the hubs in the cells, only use HBpep-SA and do not really explain the unique properties of this peptide compared to HBpep. More information on this is recommended for clarity.  To address this comment, we have included more information on the utility of these variants in the background/introduction. First, nearly identical peptides have been assembled into and tested as coacervates previously and show differing levels of stability (Sun et al, Nature Chem 2022). HBpep is less insensitive to reducing environments whereas HBpepSA partially disassembles in the reducing condition of the cell cytosol, which we find helps with internal target recruitment (see Fig S5F,L). We have added text to the introduction to better clarify the rationale and we highlight their differences throughout the results section.  2. One of the conclusions from the GFP partitioning experiments is that the peptide coacervates could be used for a variety of applications in drug delivery and engineering. I agree with this statement. Is it possible to demonstrate a more general effect where different relevant model molecules are encapsulated using the described procedure? This would enrich the manuscript and support the generality statement.  We note that the delivery of various types of cargos using HBpep coacervates has been demonstrated previously in multiple previous publications. This includes (1) RNA2.3 (Sun, Nature Chemistry, 2022) (Sun, ACS Nano, 2023) (2) many different proteins including fluorophores2 (Sun, Nature Chemistry, 2022), enzymes4 (Cerrato, Molecular Pharmaceutics, 2025), CAS93 (Sun, ACS Nano, 2023) for editing, etc. In this study, we further expanded the deliverable protein types to nanobodies and bioPROTACs. Nonetheless, the focus of our study was to develop stable interactive hubs – rather the controlled release particles – which is why we honed in on testing nanobodies and bioPROTACs and their ability to interact with native proteins as opposed to delivery per se (experiments in Figures 5, 6).   3. The tagging approach to increase the partitioning of GFP is really interesting. However, the paper does not provide enough information about the rationale for the particular tag used and the properties and effects observed when the tag was used. Why does the tag work? Can it be applied to other types of molecules or does it only work with GFP?  We thank the reviewer for raising this question, which we clarify below. The use of disordered sequences to promote partitioning to a condensate has been demonstrated in a number of different systems and contexts. Our lab has previously demonstrated attachment of the disordered RGG IDP sequence to various target proteins to promote partitioning to LAF-1 RGG condensates in vitro5 (Schuster et al, Nature Comm 2018) and in vivo6 (Garabedian et al, Nature Chem Biol 2021). Other

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

groups have used the low complexity domain of FUS-LC to promote partitioning with opto-droplets7 (Zhao and Toettcher, Nature Chem Biol 2019). Indeed, this is a generalizable, modular strategy to tag a soluble cytosolic protein and promote its uptake or co-partition with a condensate. In this study we used the HBpep tag attached to cargos to enhance partitioning of GFP as well as nanobody VHH4-mCherry (Fig. 2 and Supp Fig 4C). Thus, this strategy works for both protein targets in our study and well as many other protein targets (enzymes) used in previous studies. Note: RNA-binding domains have been added to IDPs previously to recruit target RNAs8 (Nakamura et al, Nature Materials 2018).  4. The manuscript says that the particles were generally more stable when they were larger. What is the hypothesis for this size-dependent stability? Is it a kinetic effect? What is the stability of the droplets outside the cells? Is it comparable to the observed stability inside the cells? Is there something special in the cytosol that contributes to the observed stability?  The referee highlights an interesting question. Disassembly of particles is thought to be a kinetic process although the mechanism has not been definitively established. A previous study showed that for HBpepSA particles, the reducing environment of cell cytosol is likely destabilizing the particle2 (Sun and Miserez, Nature Chem 2022). In vitro, for HBpepSA particles, 60% of the peptide was reduced in presence of GSH at 24 hrs. In just buffer in vitro, we observe stability of particles across multiple days in vitro. Thus, we do not believe the cytosol is stabilizing the particle. Rather, in case of HBpepSA particles, the reducing environment is likely destabilizing the particle as noted above. Additionally, in new data, we found that addition of reduced glutathione (GSH) or low pH (4.5) is capable of altering coacervate permeability in vitro to allow for enhanced internal partitioning of protein targeted by the VHH4 nanobody (Supp Fig S5L). This is consistent with a model in which endosomal acidification and escape into the reducing environment of the cytosol can partially disassemble particles, and increasing their permeability. Ultimately, we feel quantitative measurement of disassembly or degradation kinetics in cells is beyond the scope of the current study. However, we added a sentence to the discussion of the results for Fig 3 noting that larger particles may be stable for longer related to the kinetics of disassembly.   5. Figure 4B. The labels can be confusing. It could give the impression that VHH4 was only tested with mCherry (not GFP). I recommend revising the labels.  Thank you for this note. We have edited the labels accordingly to prevent misunderstanding. We changed the name of nanobody to its full name VHHGFP4, which is a nanobody that binds to GFP. To clarify, in Figure 4B, VHHGFP4 nanobody was covalently attached to mCherry as one fusion protein for convenient tracking by fluorescence microscopy.   6. Figure 4B shows data for HBpep-SA only. Why not both peptides (HBpep vs. HBpep-SA)? How does peptide structure affect nanobody partitioning?  We indeed provide data for both. The data on target recruitment to HBpep coacervates is present in Supp Fig S4B,C. The data on HBpep-SA is present in Fig 4B,C (and also new Fig 4D,E)  7. Figure 4B. GFP is enriched at the interface. Why is that? Figure 2B shows that GFP partitions homogeneously in HBpep droplets (pH shift). Does this suggest that the nanobodies are not homogeneously distributed in the droplets? It would be recommended to determine the distribution of nanobodies.  We would like to clarify the spatial differences in partitioning relate to the type of experiment and whether the ‘cargo’ is co-assembled with the peptide into a coacervate, or whether the ‘target’ is recruited after coacervate assembly has already occurred. When co-loading GFP or VHH4 nanobody with HBpep into coacervates the loading appears uniform (see Fig 2B and Supp Fig 4A). In Figure 4B, the GFP is added into solution after coacervate formation and is being targeted by the nanobody pre-assembled in the coacervate.
