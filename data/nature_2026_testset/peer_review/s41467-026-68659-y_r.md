Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript introduces a neuromorphic hardware-based approach to spatiotemporal optical flow by integrating temporal
motion cues with spatial gradients through two-dimensional synaptic transistor arrays. The authors report substantial
performance gains while achieving or surpassing human-level motion prediction, segmentation, and tracking speeds. While
the novelty of the authors’ approach is impressive, several important points need to be addressed before the manuscript can
be considered for publication. 
1. The paper introduces “spatiotemporal optical flow” as a novel concept and applies it within a neuromorphic framework,
which is indeed innovative. However, the Introduction section lacks a broader survey of artificial synapse-based signal
processing beyond visual stimuli. Since voltage signals can be derived from other input modalities, a contextual overview of
related signal-processing trends is need not to mislead the readers. 
2. It is unclear whether the innovation lies in the specific design and construction of the synaptic transistor device or primarily
in its application to optical flow. If the development of their 2D-based synaptic transistor itself is also the main focus, the
underlying material science and device-level rationale are only briefly mentioned. A deeper explanation of why this device is
particularly suitable (over other types of synaptic transistor) for spatiotemporal optical flow would strengthen the manuscript. 
3. Figure 2 implies that the pulse-number linearity and array-level uniformity are not particularly high, compared to recently-
reported synaptic devices. The paper does not clarify whether the authors applied design optimizations, fabrication
techniques, or algorithmic corrections to address these limitations. Commenting on whether improving these characteristics
could further boost performance or how the possible error can be calibrated would be valuable. 
4. In Figure 2i, the authors emphasize long retention of their device, yet the mechanisms responsible for this superior
retention are not deeply explored. A more detailed discussion on the material and structural factors driving this performance
would enhance the scientific depth of this manuscript. 
5. Other works (e.g., Nano Energy 89, 106439 (2021); Nat. Nanotechnol. 17, 27 (2022)) have demonstrated photo-memory
devices that directly convert light to stored electrical signals. It would strengthen the paper to explain the practical
advantages of the authors’ split architecture (sequentially using an imaging array and synaptic-transistor temporal cues) over
such designs, particularly regarding efficiency, energy use, or system complexity. 
6. There is a minor typo in Figure 3a: “voltgae” should be corrected to “voltage.” 
7. The demonstrations mainly cover conditions with static or slow-moving backgrounds and a single fast-moving object. It is
unclear how the system performs when the background itself is moving rapidly (e.g., when mounted on a moving vehicle) or
under out-of-distribution motion patterns like sudden directional changes or objects approaching/receding. Have fallback
strategies been designed for such cases, or can the authors quantify how much the reported ~400% speedup would degrade
under these conditions? Would motion compensation or background separation mechanisms be required? 
8. The paper should provide quantitative comparisons of how input resolution and synapse-array block size (e.g., 4×4) affect
key metrics such as IoU, processing speed, ROI accuracy, and miss rate. Providing tables or graphs showing how much
temporal-cue information is lost at different resolutions would clarify the method’s tradeoffs. 
9. The supplementary demo shows that the system struggles to predict and track small, fast-moving objects like a table
tennis ball (~10 px, ~20 px/frame). Reporting the detection failure rates for such conditions and analyzing how they impact
the overall pipeline performance would help delineate the method’s limitations. 
10. Although the paper mentions the synapse identification time (1–2 ms), it does not provide a detailed breakdown of the
total pipeline latency. A stacked chart or detailed summary showing contributions from ADC conversion, data transfer,
synapse-array readout, ROI mask generation, and GPU computation would improve transparency and help identify

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

bottlenecks. 
11. The reported performance improvements (e.g., 213.5%, 157.4%, 740.9%) lack accompanying standard deviations or p-
values, making it difficult to assess statistical significance or reproducibility. Providing detailed statistical metrics (mean ±
standard deviation, n ≥ 3) and significance testing would enhance the credibility of the results. 
12. While the GitHub repository is referenced, the ROI preprocessing and optical-flow integration code is not available.
Could the authors provide the full codebase to ensure reproducibility

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study proposes a novel spatiotemporal optical flow method that uses the dynamic switching behaviour of neuromorphic
devices to encode motion information directly into the device state, thereby achieving ultra-fast optical flow calculation. 
Compared with the current in-sensor computing scheme that mainly relies on the device response characteristics to encode
visual information, the encoding process of this method is adjustable. In addition, this method has no special requirements
for the front-end optical imaging technology, and can even be a commercial optical sensor device, which greatly enhances
the practicality of actual deployment. This encoding strategy can be extended to various dynamic visual tasks and effectively
overcome the delay bottleneck in visual processing, which is very helpful for a variety of vision-dependent intelligent robot
scenarios. If the author can well address my following questions about the mechanistic and technical aspects of materials
and engineering, I recommend accepting this manuscript. 
Here are my main concerns: 
- In the introduction, the authors emphasize that floating-gate transistors achieve high-frequency response, strong retention,
and excellent durability due to the characteristics of two-dimensional materials. Please describe the impact of atomic
thickness and enhanced electrostatic control on these performance indicators. Is there a specific device design or material
selection that can further optimize these characteristics? 
- In device testing, the authors reported that the Vgs amplitude required for weight modulation is lower than that of other
studies. What aspects of the transistor design enable this reduction? 
-In the section “Motion Cues Generated by Neuromorphic Devices”, the authors describe the process of converting visual
stimuli into motion cues, but do not specify the voltage pulse width applied to the neuromorphic device. Can you explain the
pulse width range used, any limitations (e.g., minimum or maximum duration), and the maximum processing frequency it
supports? 
-In this method, when modulating the neuromorphic device for subsequent optical flow calculations and task execution,
device differences may introduce errors. How does neuromorphic optical flow mitigate the impact of device-to-device
differences while maintaining or even improving processing accuracy? 
-In the discussion, the authors claim that the encoding method can be integrated with object detection networks such as
YOLO. To demonstrate its versatility, please provide experimental results to demonstrate such integration. What specific
requirements does the encoding method impose on downstream algorithms (scope of application)? 
Here are some minor issues: 
-Add reference numbers for other papers shown in Figure 2i 
-The core code flow has been demonstrated in the supplementary material. Please upload the code to the GitHub repository
to reproduce the results.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors propose a neuromorphic optical flow approach that encodes temporal information in a synaptic transistor array.
The so-obtained temporal information is subsequently used for downstream tasks, to e.g., segment moving objects. Down-
selecting the region 
of 
interest in this manner leads to substantial speed-ups in processing. 
I have a few main concerns with the article: 
1. Optical flow. 
The authors focus on “neuromorphic optical flow”. However, if I understand correctly, the authors actually do not determine
optical flow with the synaptic array, but values encoding the change in brightness over time (Equations 1-3). This is quite
confusing. Optical flow is the apparent motion of objects in the scene due to relative motion between the objects and the
observer. This apparent motion, captured by a 2D image sensor, has two dimensions (in the x- and y-direction). This is not
the output of the synaptic transistor array, though. Indeed, later optical flow methods are applied to the regions of interest
selected with the transistor array. 
On the one hand, this should be made clear much earlier in the article. On the other hand, this is a bit of 
an anti-climax, since
brightness changes can also be determined really efficiently in software. This is then limited by the camera’s frame rate, but
it would form a good comparison for the authors’ study to show the benefits of the custom transistor array. Hence, the authors
should in my eyes compare their method with a software-based one that determines the value of Eq 3 in software on images
and performs the same down-stream tasks. 
2. Task difficulty 
The method is applied to different tasks. In a first case, 800 x 800 pixels are downscaled to 4 x4 regions to which the array is
applied. Large moving objects can then be detected. It is not really clear how good this method works. A major concern for
me is that substantial ego-motion in well-lit, textured scenes, would trigger lots of brightness changes, leading to the array
lighting up everywhere. This is very common for ego-motion when it includes rotations. The pedestrian task shows dark
images in which the car is driving very slowly. The UAV operation images show a drone against a uniform background (in
the video also against the sky over a landscape). Real-world applicability seems far away given these tasks. 
3. Event-based cameras. 
During the reading of the article, I have been continuously wondering when event-based cameras (neuromorphic cameras)
would be mentioned. These are similar in spirit, and many algorithms have been devised (both neural and non-neural) to
extract optical flow from the events that come from such cameras. These cameras offer the opportunity to have low-latency,
low-power optical flow determination. 
The authors only mention event-based cameras in the “Future directions” part, mentioning that: “By incorporating event-
based cameras as the front-end sensor, the synapse array in the neuromorphic optical flow can be modulated by event
streams, potentially delivering similar acceleration benefits for event-based optical flow methods.” How would this work?
Events are ON- or OFF events, and the processing of brightness over time happens in the hardware before. It is non-evident
to me how the proposed transistor array would be combined with neuromorphic cameras. Instead, this approach could (or
should) be compared with them. 
Although I am sympathetic to the idea of down-selecting regions of interest based on motion cues, the concerns above
currently do not make me very enthusiastic about the article.

I first want to acknowledge the substantial effort by the authors to address my and the other reviewers’ comments. The
authors’ response addresses some of the raised issues. However, I still have the following concerns and questions. 
--- 
1. Optical flow 
The authors acknowledge that their neuromorphic device does not produce optical flow, but identifies regions of motion for
downstream processing. 
However, they continue to use the term for the method that they propose. I maintain that this is misleading. Readers will
easily miss or misread the added sentence in the introduction. 
The title still is “Neuromorphic spatiotemporal optical flow: Enabling ultrafast visual perception beyond human capabilities”.
Since there are works in the literature that do actually determine optical flow in a neuromorphic fashion, this is actually
already misleading, especially when the authors later state in the introduction: “Here, we propose a neuromorphic optical
flow method that leverages two-dimensional neuromorphic synaptic transistors to form temporal motion cues directly in
hardware, …” Moreover, in Figure 1, the “movement velocity calculation” block lies in the “neuromorphic” part of the diagram.
However, traditional algorithms like Farnebäck are used for this. Hence, for correctness, this block should be part of the
traditional algorithm side of the diagram. 
Instead, the authors actually propose neuromorphic hardware for 
change detection. In line 71-72, the authors capture this
very nicely: “because biological vision excels at processing large volumes of visual information efficiently through
dynamically focusing on regions where motion occurs16,17.” 
In their response to my previous review, the authors state that they base themselves on the “biological retina-LGN
interaction”. This sounds like a much better justification to me. Although I am no expert on this area, reading a bit about the
LGN reveals that they perform the function of a (spatio-)temporal attention mechanism, with the emphasis on temporal. 
Hence, I propose that the authors focus more on this aspect, reflecting the change in the title and introduction. It should be
clear that optical flow is a downstream processing task that will be applied only to regions with motion. 
2. Background motion 
I appreciate that the authors have performed and included many additional experiments.

important to know which part of 
the speed-up is due to contribution a and which due to contribution b. The authors did
already measure this (and mentioned it in their reply), but it should be clearly visible in these result graphs. 
Moreover, in terms of accuracy of the methods, it should be really clear how this is determined. Is the conventional dense
flow evaluated in the entire image? And is the neuromorphic method only evaluated in the motion regions? If so, this would
not be a fair comparison, as optical flow will be easier to determine in areas in which motion is evident and texture is
