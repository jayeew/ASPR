Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript's primary objective is to elucidate the role of Krt8+ ADI transitional AT2 cells in pulmonary fibrosis. The
authors make use of a novel RNA-sensing-dependent protein translation technology (CellREADR) which uses
programmable RNA sensors (sesRNAs) to detect specific mRNAs (e.g., Sprr1a) and trigger effector protein expression (e.g.
EGFP or DTR). The authors validate the specificity of the CellREADR system using established transgenic mouse lines in
vivo. Using published scRNAseq data of bleomycin injury mouse models and human IPF they identify Sprr1a as a highly
specific marker for Krt8+ ADI in bleo and basaloid cells in IPF. The selective ablation of Sprr1a+ cells via their AAV-Sprr1a-
SeSR-DTR and diphtheria toxin (DT) administration approach significantly reduced fibrosis, as evidenced by decreased
collagen deposition, lower hydroxyproline content, and reduced expression of fibrotic markers (e.g., collagen I, fibronectin, α-
smooth muscle actin). The study also characterizes Sprr1a+ cells as exhibiting a senescence-like phenotype (high SA-β-gal,
p21, p53 expression, but low p16) and undergoing natural apoptosis in fibrosis resolution. Based on the depletion
experiments, the authors conclude that Krt8+ ADI cells are drivers of pulmonary fibrosis and propose that targeting these
cells could offer new therapeutic avenues, highlighting the potential of the RNA-sensing technology for studying specific cell
populations in disease contexts. Overall, this study adds valuable new functional data to the established hypothesis that
Krt8+ ADI and similar transitional cells in IPF/ILD are pro-fibrogenic and that depleting such cells may have therapeutic
value. The methods and interpretations are solid. 
Nevertheless, several potential problems were identified that could affect the robustness and impact of the study:

(1) Model Limitations: The reliance on the bleomycin-induced fibrosis model is a significant limitation. If available to them,
the authors could use human precision cut lung slices to assess the functional impact of SPRR1A+ cell depletion. 
(2) The observation of natural apoptosis peaking around days 17-19 post-injury is intriguing and suggests a potential self-
limiting mechanism, which could be harnessed therapeutically, though this aspect is underexplored. Additional data on this
aspect would greatly advance the novelty of this study. Which transitional cells go into apoptosis? Are AT2 cells after injury
producing a superfluous number of transitional cells and once AT1 is regenerated the rest needs to go through apoptosis? If
that is the case it should matter when transitional cells are depleted by Sprr1-SesR-DTA. The authors should address the
normal fate of transitional cells in terms of apoptosis and how depleting them can be beneficial or harmful depending on
local context. 
(3) Specificity of Targeting: While CellREADR is innovative, ensuring its specificity is critical, especially given the use of
DTR for cell depletion, which could have off-target effects. The manuscript should include more detailed data on the
specificity of sesRNA targeting, such as flow cytometry or scRNA-seq to confirm that only Sprrla+ cells are affected, and
discuss potential unintended consequences. 
(4) Long-Term Effects of Depletion: The study demonstrates short-term benefits of depleting Sprr1a+ cells but does not
address potential long-term consequences, such as impaired lung regeneration or altered epithelial homeostasis. Given the
role of AT2 and Krt8+ ADI cells as progenitors for alveolar repair, the authors should explore whether targeting these cells
could disrupt long-term lung function, particularly in aging or repeatedly injured lungs. 
(5) Implementation and evaluation of the CellREADR system (Fig 1). The authors should better describe the design. How
did they choose the location of sequences? Does it always work or is it important to have knowledge about secondary
structures? What is the impact of sequence length? What about stability of the RNA molecules? Can the authors discuss
better how a therapeutic approach might need modified bases? 
(6) Fig 1b/c: cell culture experiments (left panel) need to be quantified. Single color images of the respective channels on the
right panel should be shown. Since ADAR expression is essential for the system the authors should show cell type specific
ADAR expression in different scRNAseq datasets and maybe also protein if feasible. 
(7) Fig3. hi res images 
need to be shown split by channel and in Fig.3c a co-staining with Sprr1a antibody would be good. 
(8) Supplementary Material - the sequence for SFTPC-SesR-EGFP is wrong (same as for Sprr1-SesR-EGFP) 
This is a well-conducted study that makes significant contributions to the field of lung repair and regeneration, particularly in
understanding the role of transitional AT2 cells in pulmonary fibrosis. To enhance its impact, the authors should address the
limitations discussed, particularly regarding human relevance, mechanistic insights, and long-term effects. I recommend the
manuscript be considered for publication with revisions to incorporate these suggestions.

Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. To address it, we analyzed scRNA -

Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. Our study was designed to examine 
the short-term impact of depleting Sprr1a+ (Krt8+ ADI) transitional AT2 cells during their peak 
accumulation in the bleomycin model of lung fibrosis. Our experimental desi gn was based on prior 
work defining the kinetics of these cells in the single -dose bleomycin model ( Strunz et al, Nat

mouse models is currently lacking, which lim its the rationale design of depletion timing and 
precludes a thorough assessment of long- term consequences on regeneration and epithelial

maintaining a balanced transitional state for proper repair and regeneration after in jury. While our 
current study focused on depletion, we envision that Sprr1a sesRNA -guided targeting could also be 
adapted for regenerative purposes. For example, coupling this approach with delivery of 
transcription factors or modulators could allow selective reprogramming of transitional AT2 cells

models of aging or repeated injury will be essential to determine whether Sprr1a+  cell depletion 
has unintended consequences on regenerative capacity and epithelial homeostasis. We have revised 
the Discussion to highlight these important caveats and potential future applications of the 
approach (revised text page 20, lines 437-451). 
 
(5) Implementation and evaluation of the CellREADR system (Fig 1). The authors should better 
describe the design. How did they choose the location of sequences? Does it always  work or is it 
important to have knowledge about secondary structures? What is the im pact of sequence length? 
What about stability of  the RNA molecules? Can the authors discuss better  how a therapeutic

Response: We appreciate this comment. CellREADR  (and the related RADAR platform) is a 
modular, programmable RNA sensor that uses endogenous ADAR enzymes to convert a UAG stop 
codon into UGG in response to a target RNA, thereby gating translation of a downstream effector. 
This design has been systematically characterized for trigger location, sequence length, mismatch 
tolerance, and editing efficiency in the original reports by Qian  et al. (Nature, 2022, Ref #22) and 
Jiang et al. (Nat. Biotechnol., 2022, PMID: 36198772). For detailed principles of sequence choice,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

secondary structure, and RNA stability, we refer readers to these publications. For our work, we 
primarily used the web -based CellREADR design portal (https://cellreadr.neuro.duke.edu) to 
generate candidate sesRNAs. In some cases, we designed sesRNAs manually following the general 
rules described in the above studies (e.g., prioritizing 3′ UTRs, avoiding reverse -complement stop 
codons, considering split-sensor designs). In practice, not all sesRNAs generated by the online tool 
produced efficient output in our hands. We therefore recommend a two- stage validation pipeline: 
Stage 1: Screen multiple candidate sesRNAs in vitro  (e.g., in HEK293 cells) to identify designs 
that reliably induce specific mRNA -guided reporter expression. Stage 2: Validate the optimized 
sesRNAs in vivo  using multimodal approaches , including RNA FISH/RNA scope, 
immunofluorescence staining, and lineage -labeling transgenic mice. As noted in the original 
studies, stability and delivery are critical for translational applications. Therapeutic versions may 
require modified nucleotides or circular RNA scaffolds to enhance stability and reduce 
immunogenicity. Incorporation of such features, together with careful validation of specificity, will 
be essential for clinical translation. We have revised the manuscript to specify the use of the 
CellREADR portal and manual design rules, report our own experience regarding variable success 
rates, and recommend a practical two -stage validation strategy. We also highlight potential 
modifications for therapeutic applications (revised text page 19, lines 416-431).
