Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this article, the authors demonstrate a fast, electrically controlled, vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL)-based
holographic system. By changing the current of arrays of broad-area VCSELs, the chip, which is integrated with orbital
angular momentum (OAM)-multiplexing holograms, can produce holograms at high speeds, given the fast switching of
VCSELs. The approach is novel, and the demonstration and implementation are impressive. I recommend the publication of
the article after addressing the following points: 
- Comparing this approach to spatial light modulators (SLM) in terms of speed is not a fair comparison. This is mainly
because SLMs can be used to generate arbitrary waveforms, not just a fixed set of holograms. 
- How can the number of possible holograms from a single VCSEL be increased? Would a larger aperture help? 
- Is it necessary to rely on VCSELs with LP modes? Or is this the simplest technique to work with? Some recent work on
VCSELs with chaotic cavities has shown a that these chaotic-cavity designs can significantly increase the number of modes
with complicated spatial profiles dynamically changing at each current. Would that be possible to use? 
- How long does it take for the spatial profile of the VCSEL to stabilize. From my experience, changing the current of broad-
area VCSELs changes the spatial profile, but it takes some time (observable by the human eye) to stabilize. Is that the case
here? It would be insightful to include a video, if possible. 
- The article is concise, but it would still be great if some of the details in the supplementary information are moved to the
main article. 
- Why was some of the analysis done on a smaller VCSEL? Is this due to simulation limitations on the size? 
- The authors state that "resulting in a cylindrical waveguide structure with an effective refractive index difference of about
0.01 between the core and the cladding." How was this value estimated? Is it based on the literature? 
- More proofreading can improve the presentation quality of the paper.

Comment 1.2:

Response 1.2:
We sincerely appreciate these professional comments regarding the enhancement of the 
multiplexing capacity in a single holographic VCSEL device. Fundamentally, the multiple 
degrees of freedom of light (e.g., transverse modes and polarization) can be exploited as 
information channels for holographic multiplexing. Thus, the number of multiplexed 
holograms can be effectively increased when the illuminating structured light field generated 
by a single VCSEL has more optical degrees of freedom.
As you correctly indicated, enlarging the oxide aperture inherently enables VCSELs to emit 
more transverse modes, offering a viable strategy for enhancing holographic multiplexing 
capacity. To validate this, beyond the VCSELs with oxide aperture diameters of 8 μm, 10 μm, 
12 μm, 14 μm, and 30 μm demonstarted in the main text, we conducted additional experiments 
using VCSELs with oxide aperture diameters of 25 μm, 35 μm, and 40 μm, respectively. Based 
on the measurement method for OAM modes weighting coefficient spectra shown in Figure 3, 
we obtained 3, 4, and 6 independent current-addressed modes from these selected VCSELs, 
respectively, as shown in Figure R1a. Correspondingly, using these three VCSELs as 
illumination sources, we designed multiplexed holograms capable of reconstructing 3 (“N”, 
“I”, “R”), 4 (“L”, “E”, “N”, “S”), and 6 (“O”, “P”, “T”, “I”, “C”, “S”) distinct holographic 
images, respectively (Figures R1b–d). All reconstructed images exhibit average PSNR values 
exceeding 12.78 dB. Therefore, it can be expected that enlarging the oxide aperture can 
significantly expand the holographic capacity from a single VCSEL.
Beyond aperture scaling, as mentioned in the Discussion section, polarization can also serve as 
an additional physical dimension to increase the number of multiplexed holograms. Previous 
studies have demonstrated that the information channel number of meta-hologram can be

the transverse modes with distinct polarization states in VCSELs for holographic encoding

Comment 1.3:
Is it necessary to rely on VCSELs with LP modes? Or is this the simplest technique to work 
with? Some recent work on VCSELs with chaotic cavities has shown a that these chaotic-cavity 
designs can significantly increase the number of modes with complicated spatial profiles

Response 1.3:
We sincerely appreciate your insightful perspective on encoding modes selection strategies in 
modes multiplexing holography. In our work, the selection strategies of encoding modes are 
primarily governed by two standards. First, holographic multiplexing necessitates modes with 
superior orthogonality to effectively suppress channel crosstalk during information encoding 
[1]. Consequently, Laguerre–Gaussian (LG) and Hermite–Gaussian (HG) modes, have 
successfully been extensively adopted as information carrier in holography multiplexing. 
Second, from the perspective of the laser source, the lasing mode must be stable; mode 
instability would directly degrade the reliability of holographic reconstruction. Therefore, 
while LP modes are not the only feasible choice, they offer a practical and effective 
information-encoding channel that simultaneously ensures mode orthogonality and stable 
emission characteristics.
Your suggestion of using chaotic-cavity VCSELs to enhance holographic multiplexing capacity 
is highly insightful. The irregular cavity geometry of such devices can indeed support a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

substantially larger number of transverse modes [2-4], thereby enriching the potential channels 
available for multiplexing hologram design. However, unlike the orthogonal LP modes with 
determined expressions, implementation other transvers modes in the design of the 
multiplexing hologram requires accurate acquisition of the complex field distributions under 
different injection currents. Notably, the inherent non-ideal orthogonality among modes 
inevitably introduces channel crosstalk, which can degrade the reconstructed image quality. 
Besides, when operating in the chaotic regime, both the output intensity and the transverse 
modal profile fluctuate dynamically and non-periodically due to strong inter-mode competition 
[2]. This intrinsic instability poses significant challenges to maintaining reliable device 
operation in practical applications. In summary, although chaotic-cavity VCSELs offer great 
potential as illuminating source for mode-multiplexing holography, they still face notable 
challenges in terms of holographic performance and stability.

Comment 1.6:
Why was some of the analysis done on a smaller VCSEL? Is this due to simulation limitations 
on the size?
Response 1.6:
We appreciate your professional question regarding the VCSEL device size in simulation. The 
use of smaller oxide apertures for specific analyses was primarily driven by the need to balance 
simulation complexity and accuracy, which indeed relate to inherent limitations in the modeling 
process.
First, simulating the spatial current distribution and mode competition dynamics in VCSELs 
becomes more complex as the oxide aperturediameter increases, as detailed in Supplementary 
Note 2 ("Mode Competition Analysis in Multimode VCSELs"). Specifically, the computational 
load for mode competition escalates rapidly with the number of supported transverse modes. 
Since the VCSELs with larger oxide apertures support more transverse modes, both 
computation and solution convergence become significantly more demanding.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 13 -->

Second, simulation accuracy degrades for the VCSELs with larger oxide apertures due to 
increasingly non-uniform current injection. In practice, factors such as electrode geometry, 
etching profile, and oxidation conditions during fabrication strongly influence the spatial 
current distribution. These effects are challenging to model comprehensively, leading to larger 
discrepancies between simulations and experimental measurements.
Since VCSELs with small or large oxide apertures exhibit equivalence in elucidating mode 
dynamics, we selected VCSELs with small oxide apertures sizes for simulations to enhance 
computational efficiency and accuracy.

Comment 1.7:

Response 1.7:
We appreciate your professional question regarding the method for constructing the waveguide 
structure. The value of approximately 0.01 between the core and cladding in oxide-confined 
VCSELs is based on the reported literature [1, 2]. To be specific, this value is typically 
established using the waveguide approximation method. In this established modeling approach, 
the unoxidized central current aperture region is regarded as the waveguide core, while the 
oxide confinement layer acts as the cladding. As such, the transverse refractive index profile is 
modeled as a weakly guiding cylindrical waveguide. By computing the refractive indices across 
all epitaxial layers in the model, an average cladding-core index difference of approximately 
0.01 can thus be obtained [1, 2].
