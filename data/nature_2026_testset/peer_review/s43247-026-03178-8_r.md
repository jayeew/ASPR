Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Review of Communications Earth & Environment manuscript COMMSENV-25-3535-T “New normal? Investigating the
unprecedented global heat of September 2023 under present and future warming” by Seeber et al. 
The global warming spike 2023/2024 surprised the scientific community, and it was not hard to predict that the topic has
thereafter prompted a large number of publications explaining its causes. Many of the published studies focus on the event
at the longer, multi-month time scale or attempt to attribute the drivers of the entire 1–2 year anomaly. However, relatively few
studies have focused only on the most extreme month of the spike, namely September 2023. 
This paper takes that opportunity and provides a comprehensive investigation of September 2023 temperature anomaly and
the year-to-year jump. The authors calculate the likelihood of the event, and then apply a novel regression method to identify
the drivers of the temperature jump, as well as the discrepancies with the CMIP6 models. The key finding is that
downwelling SW radiation was distinctly outside the CMIP6 ensemble range, which therefore suggests a contribution from
external forcings to the downwelling SW radiation that is not present in the CMIP6 models. Accounting for that discrepancy,
the likelihood of September 2023 jump increases substantially, making it possible with internal variability. 
Overall, I was impressed about the quality of the manuscript. The scientific question and the motivation was convincingly
grounded in the literature review in the Introduction. The Results section followed a clear “red line” of reasoning, i.e. it began
with larger scale and basic diagnostics before narrowing down towards September 2023 and then to the analysis of the
temperature jump of September 2023. In all its simplicity, the linear regression model is a neat idea and gives some
numbers to the possible external forcing contribution. Furthermore, the CESM2 nudged simulation supported the results,
although the simulation plays a relatively minor role in the present study. 
The language was clear - I was not able to find any grammatical issues or ambiguous wordings. The figures were of high
quality. Overall I am sure the paper falls within the scope of Communications Earth & Environment and should be published.
I thank the authors for the opportunity to read and review a manuscript that is very close to publishable as it is. I only have
some relatively minor comments which I hope can improve the manuscript. 
L125-129. Here you state that Sep 2023 stands out as the most statistically extreme interval for each individual month and
all possible multi-month windows. While I do not disagree with it, it is quite hard to see / interpret from Fig. S1. Is the x-axis of
Fig. S1 the ending month of a given period in the y-axis? For example, it looks like the 5-month periods ending in Sep or Oct
would be depicted with darker colors indicating lower probability than the Sep 2023 alone in Fig. S1b. If that’s the case, is
the statement at L127-128 correct? 
L143. “reaches 60%”. But the y-axis in Fig. 1d reaches only 0.5 (50 %)? For me it looks like about 30 % in 2034. 
L152. Here you refer to Fig. S2b. I don’t understand the logic behind the dots and the annotations in Fig. S2a-b. For me, the
GISTEMP and HadCRUT values for Sep 2023 seem to be lower than the corresponding extreme(s) from CMIP6 distribution,
but still they are annotated as 100th percentile and referred to in the text as “absent” in CMIP6 simulations. You state the
same also at L360-361. 
L215. “Years are relative to when each model reaches the 2023 GWL”. Does this mean that you have shifted some model
data in time so that all models have the same GWL in 2023? 
L397. The last key result of the study is that the CMIP6 models project an increasing occurrence of these high-intensity
temperature jumps in the future under the SSP5-8.5 scenario. As these jumps are driven by internal climate variability, do
you have any idea why there might be more of them in the future? More frequent occurrences of triple-La Nina to El Nino
events? The physical explanation for the increasing trend of such jumps remained somewhat unclear. 
L559. The largest SW discrepancy was over extratropical ocean, which points to the IMO2020 as the culprit as discussed
here. However, the event was less anomalous over the ocean as you stated earlier at L175 and is shown in Fig. 2. So do
you have any idea / speculation why the event was the most anomalous over land? Intuitively, this does not seem to fit so
well to the suggested IMO2020 regulations. 
Section 4.1.1. You compare in-situ observational datasets with climate models. Do you think that the fact that GISTEMP and
HadCRUT use SST over the ocean while models use temperature at 2 meters (“tas”) can affect (markedly) your results? Are
the datasets fully comparable with each other? 
Section 4.1.2. It would be nice to know how many models and realisations are used. 
Section 4.3.1. Did you calculate what is the skill of your linear model? Fig. 3a-b shows the observations-predicted
comparison for Sep 2023 but I was wondering if the text would benefit from stating explicitly for example the R2 values
between observed and predicted temperature anomaly over the 1980-2022. 
Fig. S2: Consider changing the y-label for S2b to “Jump (K)” or similar. Also, what is the time span used for the distributions?
The caption says 20 years but the parentheses say only 2013-2022. If the aim was to have the same GWL in models and
observations, isn’t the past 20 years biased too much towards lower GWL values?

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This study focuses on the unprecedented global extreme heat event in September 2023, aiming to address three core
questions: 1) Whether the extreme heat (especially the ~0.6 °C year-on-year temperature jump from September 2022 to
2023) was driven solely by internal climate variability (e.g., El Niño-Southern Oscillation transitions) or required additional
external forcings beyond greenhouse gases; 2) Why the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) climate
models fail to reproduce this event, and whether they reliably capture long-term warming and interannual variability; 3) How
the likelihood of similar extreme heat events changes under future warming scenarios. 
To answer these, the research integrates multiple methodologies: probabilistic attribution; process-based analysis via
multiple linear regression to decompose the contributions of key drivers; and validation with observation-constrained
CESM2 simulations. 
The main conclusions are as follows: 
1.The September 2023 temperature jump was extremely unlikely under current warming levels if relying solely on internal
variability. 
2.The event resulted from a synergistic interplay of internal variability and external contributions: Key drivers included the
onset of El Niño, rising atmospheric water vapor (which amplified warming via feedback), declining soil moisture, and
anomalous downwelling shortwave radiation. 
3.Future likelihood of similar events depends on emission scenarios: Under the SSP5-8.5 high-emission scenario, the
probability of extreme absolute temperature anomalies (like September 2023) rises to 60% by 2034 (suggesting biannual
occurrences), but the ~0.6 °C temperature jump remains highly unlikely (<1%) in the next few decades. 
Given that numerous studies have already addressed the warming in 2023 and 2024 (e.g., Gyuleva et al. 2025), some of the
conclusions in this research have been mentioned in previous studies and thus need to be de-emphasized, while others
conflict with recently published works and therefore require focused investigation. In particular, the extremeness of the
September 2023 temperature jump has already been reported by Rantanen, M. & Laaksonen, A (2024). The authors
concluded that the temperature jump in September 2023 was effectively unpredictable (L120-121). It conflicts with the results
of Blanchard-Wrigglesworth et al.(2025) and Xie et al. (2025). 
In this study, a multiple linear regression model is used to estimate the contributions of the four divers (Section 2.2). Are
these four factors mutually orthogonal? If not, this assessment is not rigorous. 
In addition, the CESM2 simulation with observed SSTs and observed winds is performed. The resulting temperature jump
reaches 0.56
℃
. The difference between the simulated and observed jump is only 0.02
℃
. This is most likely due to the
model error (Fig S5). Additionally, the model does not account for sea ice (L662:Land ice evolution is not considered to
reduce computational expenses)—yet part of the contribution to the extreme temperatures in September comes from the
extreme warming in Antarctica and the reduction of sea ice (Jena et al. 2024). Therefore, this experiment seems to indicate
that internal variability alone can explain the September temperature jump, and the content that this part of the experiment
intends to prove is rather confusing. 
I recommend a major revision.

This study focuses on the unprecedented global extreme heat event in September 2023, aiming 
to address three core questions: 1) Whether the extreme heat (especially the ~0.6 °C year-on-
year temperature jump from September 2022 to 2023) was driven solely by internal climate 
variability (e.g., El Niño-Southern Oscillation transitions) or required additional external forcings

warming and interannual variability; 3) How the likelihood of similar extreme heat events

To answer these, the research integrates multiple methodologies: probabilistic attribution; 
process-based analysis via multiple linear regression to decompose the contributions of key 
drivers; and validation with observation-constrained CESM2 simulations.

warming via feedback), declining soil moisture, and anomalous downwelling shortwave 
radiation.

emission scenario, the probability of extreme absolute temperature anomalies (like September
