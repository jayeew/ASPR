Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In their article “Spatially-optimized forestation enhances carbon sink by reducing edge effects in China”, Dong and
colleagues quantified the negative effects of forest edges on carbon stocks in both natural and planted forests in China,
based on more than 9000 field measurements. They also designed an optimized forestation strategy to reduce forest edges,
and used a random forest model to predict the potential gains in carbon stocks from such a strategy. Their results suggest
that this optimized forestation strategy can lead to higher carbon sequestration compared to random forestation, under
various future climate scenarios. While their findings highlight the potential underestimation of future carbon sequestration
due to overlooked edge effects, I have significant concerns regarding their methodology, particularly the method edge effects
were separated in the measurement data and the approach to predicting carbon stocks. These issues prevent me from
recommending the acceptance of this paper at its current stage. 
First, regarding the decline in forest biomass carbon stocks near forest edges, I am concerned about the role of forest age in
this relationship. As is well known, forests closer to edges are more likely to be younger, often planted more recently. While
the authors attempt to control for age by categorizing forests into young-, mid-, and old-aged groups, I am sure that even
within these categories, forests near the edges are generally younger than those further inland. The parabolic curve of young
forests in the paper also confirm this concern. The authors explain the low carbon stocks at large edge distances (4,700 m)
as being due to the presence of very young trees in the interior. This definitely suggests that their edge distance-carbon
stocks curve is very easily affected by forest age, rather than purely by edge effects. It is important to disentangle edge
effects from forest age (also species composition). The authors' direct attribution of lower carbon stocks near edges to
microclimate and resource availability seems inappropriate. 
Second, for their random forest model used for predicting future carbon stocks, I noticed that forest age has the highest
importance score, far outweighing other variables. This makes me skeptical of their conclusion that optimized forestation
leads to higher carbon stocks. Their reported difference in carbon stocks between optimized and random forestation
strategies could simply be due to older forests being planted in regions with more favorable environmental conditions, as the
authors themselves note: “the optimized forestation strategy covers much less area in the central region due to reduced
water availability.” 
In this case, the difference in carbon stocks between optimized and random forestation may not reflect the
impacts of reducing forest edges. In addition, in Figure S1, the edge distance is a low-ranking factor, so its changes due to
forestation may have minor impact on carbon stocks in their random forest model. Moreover, the authors should check
whether the relationship between edge distance and carbon stocks in their random forest model align with that in their field
measurements. 
Third, in their prediction of carbon stocks, the flowchart in Figure S2 suggests that existing forest disturbance maps were
included in the analysis. However, I did not find any details of this in the paper. I think that accounting for natural
disturbances is very important when predicting future carbon sequestration, as disturbances can significantly reduce carbon
stocks, particularly in older forests, which are more vulnerable to events like droughts, heatwaves, fires, storms, and pest
outbreaks. Otherwise, their model could overestimate future carbon sequestration due to forestation. Additionally, I wonder
whether the authors considered tree competition after forestation, which can also influence carbon stocks.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an interesting and timely study examining the effects of forest edge disturbances on carbon sinks
in China.. The authors employ extensive plot-based observations in combination with a range of remote sensing datasets to
quantify forest carbon dynamics. The results are potentially significant. However, some methodological descriptions lack
clarity and sufficient detail, which may impede readers’ ability to fully evaluate the robustness and reliability of the findings. 
There are some several specific comments and suggestions that may help improve the clarity and rigor of the manuscript. 
1.Forest edges are the key focus of this study, and their accurate characterization is crucial to the validity of the results. The
authors state that “The forest plots were mapped on a land cover image to derive the proximity-to-edge values.” However,
the forest plot data from the 9th National Forest Inventory (NFI) were collected between 2014 and 2018, while the land cover
images used for matching (CNLUCC) have discontinuous temporal coverage (e.g., 2013, 2015, 2018, 2020, 2023). As noted
in the Introduction, China has ambitious afforestation plans and continues to expand its forest area substantially every year,
which means that forest edges are likely to undergo considerable changes annually. The authors should clarify how this
temporal mismatch was addressed in their analysis. I also suggest that they evaluate, perhaps in the Supplementary
Materials, whether using remote sensing data from different years might affect forest edge delineation, thereby providing
stronger support for the robustness of their results. 
In addition, the frequency distribution of distances from forest edges of in situ plots the could also be included in the
Supplementary Materials to provide readers with a more detailed understanding of the data. 
2.The potential forestable lands reported in this study differ from those in previous research (Yao et al., 2024. Carbon
sequestration potential of tree planting in China. Nature Communications. 
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-52785-6 ). In
this manuscript, the potential forestable lands appear to be more concentrated in semi-arid to semi-humid regions (“large
contiguous areas of potential 
forestation lands were distributed in the central and northeast regions” in Line 172-
173)
，
whereas earlier studies suggested that potential forestable lands are primarily located in southern China where water
and heat conditions are more favorable (“The remaining areas available for future afforestation in China are principally
located in the Hengduan Mountains, the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River Plain, and the Qinba Mountains”.
Yao et al., 2024). Authors should provide a thorough and candid discussion of the discrepancies between their results and
previous findings and the underlying causes. This is particularly critical because the delineation of potential afforestation
areas is fundamental to evaluating future forest carbon sinks and directly determines the reliability of the study’s
conclusions. 
3.The description in Methods, Section 3.2 Constraints on potential land for forestation is insufficient and somewhat unclear.
While Results and Discussion 2.2 states that a random forest (RF) model was employed to delineate the spatial distribution
of potential forest, the Methods section provides no details on how the RF model was implemented. Besides, some
methodological details require further improvement. For example, the use of the Hansen Global Forest Change dataset is
mentioned (Table S3), but the specific role it played in the analysis is not clearly explained. 
4.Methods, Section 3.4 Design of simulation experiments requires a more detailed description, as the current explanation of
the simulation experiments is somewhat confusing. As mentioned in the Introduction, “China launched an ambitious
forestation program, committing to creating an additional forest area of 49.5 million hectares (Mha) from 2021 to 2050.”,
however, a substantial portion of this planned expansion lies outside the potential forestable lands identified by the authors,
for example, the additional forest area associated with the Three-North Shelter Forest Program. This discrepancy should be
clarified. Besides, afforestation in China is guided by government policies and strongly influenced by policy frameworks,
which are typically defined at the provincial rather than regional level. Therefore, Table S4 may be of limited value and may
not accurately represent the actual afforestation plans. To enhance the credibility and feasibility of the simulation
experiments proposed in this study, it would be helpful if the authors could compare the afforestation plans for different
provinces used in their simulations with relevant official government documents, such as the primary references cited in the
afforestation scheme discussed in this article (State Forestry Administration of the People’s Republic of China, National
Forest Management Plan (2016–2050), 2016). In summary, the afforestation simulation experiments should fully discuss
their feasibility as well as the advantages and disadvantages compared with existing policies. 
In addition, the description of some simulation experiments needs to be moved from the Results section to the Methods
section.
