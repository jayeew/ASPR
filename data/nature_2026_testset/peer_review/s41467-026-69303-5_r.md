Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors propose two deep learning-based models to evaluate the conformations of small molecules generated in situ a
protein pocket and use this method to benchmark five in situ molecule generators. 
- The first stage "HEAD" classifies individual atoms in a conformation as anomalous or passing. For this an 'atomic energy'
is calculated by a deep learning model which is then compared to a ground truth distribution obtained on an per element
level on a reference data set. 
- The second stage "TED" classifies torsion bonds as strained or relaxed. For this, a deep learning model predicts the
potential energy surface (energy as a function of the torsion) for each bond, which is then used to classify torsion bonds. 
The benchmark considers five deep learning-based in situ molecular generation methods which are assessed using HEAD
and TED. 
Major results 
- The HEAD method is shown to have a somewhat higher F1 score than PoseBusters in the task of identifying strained
energy conformations, but only for conformations that differ relative to their energy minimized counterparts by 200 to 600
kcal/mol. Above and below PoseBusters has a comparable performance. 
- The TED method has a much higher F1 score than PoseBusters in identifying torsion strain. PoseBusters does not
explicitly check dihedral angles. 
Major comments 
- The task for the benchmark is in situ molecular generation but the proposed method does not take the surrounding protein
pocket into account when measuring plausibility. This is addressed in the discussion but makes the method and benchmark
appear half-finished. 
- Recent benchmarks for evaluating in situ molecular generation are not mentioned and should be discussed and compared
against: Ciepliński et. al. 2023, Jocys et. al. 2024, Harris et al 2023. In particular, citations are needed to provide support to
the claim that semi-empirical and MM methods have low accuracy for this task (l.57). 
- The head-to-head comparison with PoseBusters is incomplete. 
- As HEAD uses Ani-2x, it is limited to evaluating molecules limited to 7 atom types H, C, N, O, F, S, Cl, as mentioned on
l.162. PoseBusters does not seem limited in this way. 
- PoseBusters, unlike the proposed method, evaluates clashes with the protein. 
- Conformational validity, drug-likeness, and synthetic accessibility are separate concerns. For example, the molecules
shown in Extended figure 1 might not be good drug candidates, but their conformations might still be valid. 
- l.143: Please mention which force field was used for the refinement of the ligands in the LigBoundConf paper. Could you
please specifically cite the part of the LigBoundConf paper that shows how the energy refined version of the PDB is
obtained? 
Minor comments 
- l.52 unclear: "..., whose limited coverage may cause misleading when evaluating..." 
- l.65 The way PoseBusters is cited, it seems as if that paper only performed an evaluation of molecular conformations
before molecular dynamics relaxation. However, the paper did evaluations before and after. 
- l.143: missing indefinite article: "...and refined with force field in the presence of..." 
- l.157: missing definite article.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper presents two novel evaluation methods for 3D small-molecule conformations generated by Deep Learning
techniques. Developing such tools is crucial given the growing diffusion of AI in computational biology. 
While AI-based models are highly effective at generating multiple candidate conformations, they often produce unrealistic
molecular structures. To address this issue, the authors propose a two-modules approach. 
In the first step, they introduce the High Energy Atom Detector (HEAD), which identifies implausible atoms using an AI-
derived force field. HEAD is compared against the recent and popular PoseBusters method, demonstrating superior
efficiency in distinguishing valid from invalid models. Additionally, although less pronounced, HEAD achieves better recall
for valid conformations within a positive dataset. Furthermore, HEAD operates at a higher computational speed than
PoseBusters. 
The second step involves detecting rotatable bonds with high torsional energies using a deep learning network called the
Torsional Energy Descriptor model (TED-Model). When benchmarked against GFN2-xTB, TED exhibits a stronger
correlation between model prediction and DFT values of torsion fragments. Compared to PoseBusters, TED achieves better
performance in distinguishing rational and irrational cases, albeit with some accuracy limitations. 
Finally, HEAD and TED are applied to assess five AI-driven 3D molecular generation models (Lingo3DMolv2, Pocket2Mol,
PocketFlow, TargetDiff, and PMDM) across 102 targets, and their passing rates are compared to those obtained using
PoseBusters. The results indicate that both HEAD and PoseBusters show a higher passing rate for Lingo3DMolv2, while the
remaining models yield similarly low passing rates. Conversely, TED exhibits a higher passing rate for PocketFlow. 
While the proposed approach has certain limitations —such as the reduced discrimination power of HEAD for decreasing
force field anomalies and the generally modest accuracy of TED— the authors provide a thoughtful discussion on potential
improvements and future developments. 
Overall, this work advances the state of the art in an important area and highlights key challenges that remain to be
addressed by the community. The results are clearly presented and thoroughly discussed. However, some points require
clarification before publication. 
Major comments: 
1. 
One of the 3D molecule generation models used in the manuscript as a benchmark for testing the HEAD-TED module is
Lingo3DMolv2, which appears to be an updated version of Lingo3DMol [1]. Since no publicly available resources describe
Lingo3DMolv2, I am curious about what modifications or improvements have been made compared to the original version. 
[1] Feng, W., Wang, L., Lin, Z. et al. Generation of 3D molecules in pockets via a language model. Nat Mach Intell 6, 62–73
(2024). https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-023-00775-6. 
2. The authors assess the accuracy of the HEAD module using three databases, one of which —the Cambridge Structural
Dataset— consists of ligands in their unbound (apo) state. 
Given the inherent energy differences between bound and unbound conformations, the rationale for using apo
conformations as a benchmark to identify incorrect holo poses should be further clarified. 
3. Why weren’t the models in the GM-5K dataset filtered based on Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness (QED) and
Synthetic Accessibility Score (SAS)? 
4. To evaluate the performance of the TED-Model, its predictions on the LigBoundConf dataset were compared to those of
PoseBusters. However, since the conformations in this dataset were optimized using a force field, geometric anomalies -on
which PoseBusters relies for detection- were minimized. While this point was discussed in the manuscript, it could
nonetheless reduce the informativeness of the comparison. 
5. It would be interesting to include a more in-depth discussion regarding the stratification of the HEAD-TED results based
on the characteristics of the targets in the DUD-E dataset. This analysis could provide insights into how the inclusion of the
protein pocket in the evaluation of AI-generated molecules might influence the results.

6. The manuscript would benefit from an additional analysis of the HEAD-TED model as a unified protocol for screening
deep-learning-generated 3D molecules. While the authors provide clear evaluations of each module individually, the
discussion of the HEAD-TED system as a whole is presented primarily as a tool to evaluate molecular generative models,
rather than as a ligand selection method. Although the former is an important application that highlights the limitations of
current methods, it does not directly demonstrate the functionality of the HEAD-TED system as a “two-stage approach... for
the evaluation of AI-generated 3D molecular conformations”, as it is described in the manuscript. 
To strengthen the manuscript, a concluding analysis could be included, where the HEAD and TED modules are applied in
succession to label correct and incorrect models, showcasing the integrated use of both modules in the context of ligand
screening. 
7. While the authors provide a clear explanation of the underlying principles of the HEAD module in the Methods Section
and in the Extended data, the statement “Notably, the HEAD approach can, in principle, be applied to other MLFFs” should
be better justified in the main text. 
8. The authors compare the speed of HEAD and PoseBusters on LigBoundConf, CSD and GM-5K benchmark datasets in
Extended Data Table 1, mentioning in the main text that the former is faster than the latter. 
However, given the fundamental importance of time efficiency in methods developed in this field, I would recommend
highlighting this aspect more prominently in the main text. A more detailed comparison should be provided, especially
concerning the TED module, in order to fully emphasize its performance in terms of computational efficiency. 
Minor comments: 
1. Some figures in the manuscript are poorly curated from a graphical standpoint. There are several issues, including
overlapping borders and objects, excessively small font sizes, and misaligned labels.

PoseBusters citation (l.65): The reviewer noted that the original text implied PoseBusters only evaluates conformations
before MM refinement. The authors clarified that PoseBusters evaluates both before and after refinement and removed the
misleading citation. 
Grammar issues (l.143 and l.157): The reviewer highlighted missing indefinite and definite articles. The authors corrected
the sentences and included the force field name where appropriate. 
Unclear explanation of positive recall rate (l.197): The reviewer found the description of incorrectly classifying valid
conformations unclear. The authors rephrased the sentence and restructured the paragraph to clearly explain the concept of
positive recall rate for bound and apo conformations. 
Rationale for designing molecules with low torsion strain (l.378): The reviewer requested an explanation or citation. The
authors added a reference to a Bioorg. Med. Chem. Lett. study, illustrating with a real drug design example how minimizing
torsional strain improves ligand-target binding. 
Overall, the manuscript is now much stronger and more comprehensive and the points have been addressed by reviewer 2

“Methods 
4.1.7 Evaluation of Ligand-Protein Interaction Validity  
HEAD can also evaluate the validity of ligand-protein binding interactions. For a given ligand-
protein complex, the protein pocket is defined as all residues with atoms within a 20 Å radius of the 
ligand. This radius is chosen to enable evaluation of multiple ligands binding to the same protein 
pocket efficiently. Specifically, defining a large pocket region (20 Å) allows a single pocket 
definition to be reused across different ligands, thereby reducing the computational overhead 
associated with repeated pocket extracti on and energy calculation for pocket atoms. In contrast, 
using a smaller radius (e.g., 4 Å) would require redefining the pocket for each ligand individually, 
increasing processing time. The HEAD model then independently predicts atomic energies for the 
ligand and pocket in both their isolated state and its bound state.  Ligand -protein interactions are 
deemed invalid under two conditions: (1) local instability, where the number of invalid atoms (as 
defined in Section 4.1.6) increases in either the ligand or the pocket upon binding, or (2) global 
energy increase, where the total energy of the bound complex ( Ecomplex

ligand conformations, an area where our study emphasizes. Therefore, we have added a 
comparison between DrugPose and our method to the Discussion section of the revised

evaluating AI molecular generative models. We agree with DrugPose authors that a robust AI

be distinguished based solely on their ligand physicochemical properties, without requiring 
consideration of their binding interactions with the pr otein. This characteristic can indeed 
contaminate AI models designed for active compound identification and trained using DUD-
E's compound data. 
However, we wish to clarify that these biases do not affect our current research. Our work 
focuses on developing a technology for 3D conformation evaluation, which is subsequently 
used to assess the quality of molecular conformations generated by various A I models. 
Crucially, our model was neither trained on DUD -E compound data nor is it designed for

active compound identification.  Furthermore, DUD -E’s active and decoy compound

Our strategy specifically leverages the primary strength of the DUD -E dataset: its 
extensive and diverse coverage of protein targets, encompassing a wide range of important 
therapeutic classes such as kinases, proteases, nuclear receptors, GPCRs, ion channels, 
cytochrome P450s, and other enzymes. By utilizing these diverse targets, we ensure a broad 
evaluation landscape for the performance of AI models in generating molecular 
conformations. Specifically, we utilized all the 102 protein targets (PDB IDs) documented in

compounds or decoys documented in DUD-E was input in the models under evaluation.

strengths of the DUD-E dataset (diverse targets) while avoiding its documented weaknesses 
(biases in active/decoy compounds), ensuring that our study remains unaffected by the 
concerns raised.
