Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In the manuscript entitled “The focal adhesion kinases regulate leptin action and the weight reducing effect of HDAC6
inhibition”, Hadley and colleagues follow up on their recent published work demonstrating that pharmacological HDAC6
inhibition by TubA leads to peripheral production of a yet unidentified factor that increases hypothalamic leptin sensitivity
and reduces body weight in Diet induced obese mice. In the present study they find that the increase in hypothalamic leptin
sensitivity in this model requires the focal adhesion kinases FAK and Pyk2 and that these kinases sensitize leptin signaling
by interacting and phosphorylating Stat3, the main downstream regulator of leptin action. 
Specifically: 
- Using comparative in silico analysis of hypothalamic transcriptome profiles obtained experimentally to those of the
Connectivity Map database, the authors identify FAK and Pyk2 as candidates for mediating the leptin sensitizing effect of
Tub-A treatment. 
- Using pharmacological inhibition of focal kinases and genetic deletion of Pyk2 the authors demonstrate that the central
action of these focal adhesion kinases is required for the anti-obesity effect of TubA. 
- In cell culture assays, the authors confirm that leptin can activate Stat3 in a Jak2 independent manner and that Stat3
activation (ie phosphorylation) can be mediated by FAK or Pyk2. 
- Finally, in further in vivo studies the authors determine that FAK or Pyk2 are expressed in LepR expressing neurons, that
Pyk2 expression is increased by fasting in an ARC Lepr neuron population, that pharmacological inhibition of focal kinases
or genetic ablation of Pyk2 attenuates pharmacological leptin induced activation of stat3 and its short-term anorectic effects
in a refeeding paradigm. 
In general, the study is well constructed and logically presented and within the confine of my expertise I find no major issues
with both the in vivo and in vitro experimental approaches and data. 
Some clarifications and modifications in the presentation would benefit the manuscript: 
1) The initial model should be better described in the abstract and introduction. As currently written the reader is led to
believe that inhibitors of the cytosolic deacetylase HDAC6 increase central leptin sensitivity directly, which is not what the
authors have demonstrated in their previous publication nor what they are actually proposing here. 
The authors should make
it much clearer that the manuscript is about the hypothalamic effects of an unknown peripheral factor induced by HDAC6
inhibition by TubA and NOT by HDAC6 inhibition by TubA directly on the hypothalamus. Maybe adding a schematic
representation of the model as a first panel in figure 1 or as a supplemental figure could be useful. 
The discussion should also be clearer in that respect and address this upfront rather than in a convoluted way in the last
paragraph. As written, the discussion seems to imply that the study was motivated by the search of a non-canonical JAK2-
independent but STAT3-dependent leptin signaling mechanisms when instead it was initially motivated by understanding
the leptin sensitizing effect of a pharmacologically induced peripheral factor, leading to the identification of a JAK2-
independent but STAT3-dependent leptin signaling mechanisms of potential physiological relevance. 
2) There is no method section for the in silico analysis. The first paragraph of the result describing this analysis should be
more concise and most of the methodological description should be moved to the method sections were some of its aspects
should be expanded and more detailed.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors examined potential roles of Fak and Pyk2 in central leptin actions, independent of JAK2 pathway. Both in vitro
and in vivo evidence was provided to try to support the possibility that Fak/Pyk2 is a novel downstream signal of leptin,
contributes to stat3 phosphorylation in leptin-responsive neurons, and mediates leptin-induced anorexia. While the
hypothesis or model, if proven true, is quite significant and novel, the evidence as presented in the current manuscript falls
short and is not as convincing. A number of issues were noted as detailed below. 
1. A major concern is the study did not use a cell type-specific (e.g. LepR+ neurons) Fak or Pyk2 deletion model to really
examine the functional involvement of these factors in central leptin actions. Data obtained from the global Pyk2 KO mice
could not exclude the possibility that Pyk2 in peripheral tissues are somehow involved. 
2. Fig. 2a-b is missing important control groups: vehicle+vehicle, and vehicle+PF. Without such groups, data as presented
are very hard to interpret. 
3. For all body weight data, absolute body weight values should be presented, instead of the % change. The different
%change could be due to different baseline body weight when the treatment started. 
4. Pyk2 KO mice showed similar body weight as WT mice after a 24-week HFD feeding, while a previous study (ref#28)
reported a dramatic weight increase in Pyk2 KO mice after a 8-week HFD feeding compared to WT controls. The authors
should show the body weight curves for the entire 24-week HFD study. The lack of body weight phenotype in HFD-fed mice
suggests a minor role of Pyk2 in leptin signaling, which actually does not support the hypothesis. 
5. The protein interactions observed in vitro should be also examined in vivo using mouse hypothalamic tissues. In addition,
is this interaction regulated by leptin, fasting, HFD feeding, etc? 
6. Fig. 4c, f, and i are not convincing at all to support the statement that the majority of LepR+ cells at least express either
Fak or Pyk2. 
7. In Fig. 5c, PF pretreatment did not blunt leptin-induced stat3 phosphorylation. It is probably due to the short (30 min) leptin
action. In Fig. 5h, Pyk2 KO mice only blunted leptin-induced stat3 phosphorylation at 120 min time point. The same time
points should have been checked in Fig. 5c. This also brings up another possibility that is worth examining: whether JAK2
and FAK/Pyk2 pathways mediate leptin signaling at different time points. Given the focus of this manuscript, such a question
should be addressed to further enhance the significance of the study. 
8. The study seems to be exclusively done in male animals without examining similar phenotypes in female mice. At the
very least, this limitation should be acknowledged. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Hadley et al. used a combination of mouse genetics and in vitro cell culture systems to examine whether focal adhesion
kinases (FAK and Pyk2) mediate leptin action in body weight regulation. This study appears to be a continuation of the
authors' previous study that revealed improved leptin action as a mechanism underlying the body weight reducing effect of
HDAC6 inhibition. Physiologically, the current studies presented data supporting that body weight reducing effect of HDAC6
inhibition and food intake reducing effect were both blunted by pharmacological inhibition of FAK and Pyk2, and by genetic
knock out of Pyk2. Additional extensive cell culture assays showed that FAK and Pyk2 interacted with STAT3 and were
required for leptin action on activating pSTAT3. Although the study on potential mediators of leptin signaling is important and
of the effect of HDAC6 inhibition is significant, there appear to be some major concerns on the consistency issues regarding
the proposal mechanism and the observed results. 
1) If FAK and Pyk2 represent real important mediators of leptin induced pSTAT3 action, then Pyk2 KO or pharmacological
inhibition of FAK and Pyk2 should produce increased feeding and body weight. 
Loss of function of leptin signaling is known
to produce robust effects on hyperphagia and obesity as previously shown in hypothalamic STAT3 KO, leptin receptor point
mutation causing specific defective STAT3 signaling etc. However, the data presented in this study with Pyk2 KO and
inhibition of FAK and Pyk2 both failed to produce any effect on feeding or body weight, which argue against the key
hypothesis the authors proposed that FAK and Pyk2 mediate leptin action on pSTAT3 signaling. 
2) The colocalization data on FAK and PyK2 with leptin receptors are also not convincing. To bolster the authors' claim, it is
necessary to produce additional in vivo data on hypothalamic or leptin receptor specific KO of FAK and PyK2 (baseline body
weight as well as the effect of leptin action) that can be used to support the claim. 
3) It is not clear what is the functional relationship between FAK and Pyk2. The role of FAK is purely estimated from relative
difference in the degree of body weight reduction of HDAC6 inhibition between Pyk2 and TubA pharmacology, which may
not be reliable at all given the potential differences between mouse genetics and pharmacology. Pharmacological effects of
PF between control and Pyk2 KO mice related to feeding and body weight may be able to tease out the effect of PF
inhibition of FAK. 
4) Supplementing the data presented in Fig 5, alternative data on pSTAT3 immunostaining of the hypothalamic section will
reveal details of changes in pSTAT3 responses, which will add more confidence to the observed findings. 
5) It will be more helpful if the authors could provide more information on the background and function of FAK and Pyk2 as
well as speculation on how these kinases can be involved in leptin signaling.

The revised manuscript has 6 main figures and 10 supplementary figures and 2 supplementary tables. We also included an illustration as requested by the reviewers, summarizing the main theme of the current study and considering the previous findings.
