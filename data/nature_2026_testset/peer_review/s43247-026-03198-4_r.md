REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The paper addresses an interesting topic: the interaction between electricity system decarbonization and urban health
resilience under heatwave stress. The methodological framework integrates meteorology, epidemiology, and power system
modelling. The findings have clear implications for both energy policy and urban public health.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

The paper is worth publication in Comm Earth & Environment, and there are some aspects that deserve further
methodological clarification/strengthening. Please find some points that in my opinion, the authors can address: 
1) Causality in Epidemiological Models: The paper uses polynomial regressions to estimate the link between heat exposure
and mortality. However, there could be a problem of reverse causality and confounding (e.g., other environmental or
socioeconomic conditions correlated with both mortality and electricity load). It would be appropriate to discuss the
possibility of using instruments (IVs) or quasi-experiments to strengthen causal identification. For example, exogenous
meteorological variations (wind, cloud cover) or short-term shocks to the grid could serve as plausible instruments. 
2) Health Data and Granularity: The use of aggregated and calibrated data from the international literature is
understandable, but it does not capture intra-urban heterogeneity (neighbourhood, income, energy poverty, age) well. This
risks underestimating or overestimating the effects in particularly vulnerable subpopulations. I would suggest at least making
this limitation explicit, or indicating possible future extensions with micro health data 
3) It would be useful to include a comparative table with key numerical scenarios (e.g., excess mortality, load shedding,
costs) for the different cases (R Low, R Med, R High). 
4) The literature on urban health equity and energy poverty should be referenced to strengthen the social argument. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The paper presents a modelling study assessing the impact of changes in the power system from future (2030-2050)
decarbonisation measures on heat-related mortality / health risk for the megacity cluster of Guangdong/Hong Kong/Macao. A
high-resolution case study focusing on the optimisation of decarbonization strategies to mitigate health risks is conducted in
a limited domain (Zhuhai network). The authors focus on establishing a link between the frequency of power outages and
associated heat-related mortality and economic loss under future heat-wave scenarios (demand surges from cooling),
progressing power system decarbonisation (less flexible power mix) and grid congestion. Health-aware network expansion
scenarios highlight the need for energy storage systems (e.g., hydrogen). 
The paper addresses a relevant question, and I believe will be of interest to the scientific community. The study is well
designed and novel, results are concisely presented and visualised. The paper is written clearly, but with a certain degree of
repetitiveness. The supplementary information provide details on most aspects of the modelling, but some details,
particularly assumptions made in the conceptualisation of the model/study, need further clarification in the main paper.
Please find my detailed comments below. 
1. Repetitive writing 
Throughout the paper, there are several instances of (lengthy) repetitions of earlier statements, including reiterations of
general background information already discussed in the introduction (e.g., p. 5, ll 5-13; but also elsewhere throughout other
sections). It would be good to revise the paper to make it more concise. 
2. Generation of heat-wave scenarios (Sect. 4.2 and associated supplementary material) 
The authors have derived future heat-wave scenarios out to 2050 using an empirical approach informed by ERA5 reanalysis
data from 1973-2023 rather than using climate model projections. It would be good to bring these results into context with
expectations from (established) projection scenarios (e.g., CMIP5/6 SSP/RCP pathways) to both establish confidence in the
methods used (how realistic are the trends derived?) but also to characterise the paper's projections in terms of scenario
likelihoods. 
3. Health risk modelling (Sect. 4.3 and associated supplementary material) 
3a. Can you clarify whether any (if yes - which) socio-demographic/economic factors are accounted for in this modelling?
E.g., does your modelled mortality consider risk factors (e.g., age, pre-existing medical conditions) of the population living in
regions affected by a specific node? On p. 3 l. 7 you write that your modelling integrates "(...) meteorological, energy, and
public health data, (...)", but it is unclear how public health data have been used. It would also be good to clarify whether
spatial differences of such risk factors are accounted for. 
3b. In addition to the socio-demographic background of individuals, other factors impact health risk from heat exposure such
as the existence and availability of mitigation options or measures (e.g., availability of greenspaces, public cooling options)
or the nature of building composition (built volume, density, materials). It would be good to clarify whether any of these
aspects are accounted for in the modelling or not. 
3c. In your modelling, do you assume that all people living in your domain have access to air conditioning (at home/work)
and will use it if a certain a temperature threshold is exceeded? If not, then a comment is needed on proportions of excess
deaths from heat exposure due to failed air conditioning (power outages) versus from no air conditioning being available in
the first place. 
4. Planning model (Zhuhai case study; Sect. 4.4, 2.3) 
Can you clarify whether and how the strategies explored are constrained by actually available infrastructure and realistic
potential of network expansion /change? Are any of the measures modelled informed by current governmental planning and
assessments? Or is this an 'in an ideal world this would happen' kind of scenario?

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The article is well written and structured. In my opinion, only minor corrections are needed. The topic covered is extremely
topical and impactful. It is right to investigate what the repercussions on human health might be of actions, although
necessary, to reduce energy consumption and environmental impact. 
1. What are the major claims of the paper? 
The paper argues that deep decarbonization of urban power systems, while essential for climate goals, can unintentionally
exacerbate urban health risks during extreme heatwaves. Using the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao megacity cluster as a
case study, the authors claim that: 
• Decarbonization reduces system flexibility, leading to more frequent and severe blackouts during heatwaves. 
• These outages disproportionately increase excess mortality, especially among vulnerable populations. 
• A health-aware decarbonization framework that integrates power systems, meteorology, and health data can reduce
excess deaths by 47–57% and lower annual system costs by 6–12%. 
• Hydrogen storage and optimized PV deployment are highlighted as particularly effective strategies for reconciling climate
and health objectives. 
2. Are they novel and will they be of interest to others in the community and the wider field? 
Yes, the novelty lies in explicitly linking urban health risks (heatwave-induced mortality) with power system decarbonization
planning. While previous research has studied resilience-oriented grids or epidemiological impacts of heat, this paper
introduces an integrated framework that quantifies health outcomes within grid expansion planning. This interdisciplinary
coupling is likely to be of interest to researchers in energy systems, public health, climate adaptation, and urban planning. 
3. If the conclusions are not original, provide relevant references. 
The conclusions have to be added. 
4. Is the work convincing, and if not, what further evidence would be required to strengthen the conclusions? 
The work is methodologically solid, combining detailed multi-voltage grid simulations, data-driven mortality models, and
economic analysis. However, there are some areas where additional evidence would strengthen the claims: 
• Validation of the health model: While parameters are calibrated on historical mortality data, more discussion on uncertainty
ranges and sensitivity of health outcomes would be valuable. 
• Generalizability: The study focuses on the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao cluster. Demonstrating the approach in another
megacity (e.g., India, US, Europe) would show robustness and broader applicability (It's just a suggestion, not an obligation) 
• Social and behavioral aspects: The framework assumes air-conditioning as the main determinant of exposure.
Incorporating adaptation measures (cooling centers, urban design, behavioral change) (for the next works) 
5. On a more subjective note, do you feel that the paper will influence thinking in the field? 
Yes. The paper provides a timely and compelling perspective by reframing decarbonization not only as an engineering or
climate challenge but also as a public health imperative. This reframing could influence both academic research (e.g.,
integrating epidemiology into energy models) and policy (e.g., including health indicators in decarbonization roadmaps). Its
emphasis on health-aware planning is likely to inspire further interdisciplinary approaches in sustainable urban energy
transitions. 
Figure 2 could probably be made more readable. 
I suggest adding a paragraph with the conclusions.

1.2 Comment 1
Causality in Epidemiological Models: The paper uses polynomial regressions to estimate the link
between heat exposure and mortality. However, there could be a problem of reverse causality and
confounding (e.g., other environmental or socioeconomic conditions correlated with both mortality
and electricity load). It would be appropriate to discuss the possibility of using instruments (IVs)
or quasi-experiments to strengthen causal identification. For example, exogenous meteorological

Response
We highly appreciate the reviewer for this valuable suggestion. We fully agree that using in-
strumental variables (IVs) or quasi-experimental designs is a more rigorous approach to establish
the causal chain from outage→heat exposure→excess mortality. This entails assembling micro-
level health–meteorology–power datasets and adopting a clearer causal identification framework.
The principal challenge is data acquisition and integration: constructing a geo-temporal panel
that links city/tract–day (or hourly) mortality with fine-grained candidate instruments (wind and
cloud-cover anomalies), time-stamped geo-referenced outage records, and proxies for cooling capac-
ity and vulnerability (electricity load/AC usage, building characteristics, socioeconomic features),
all precisely matched in space and time. With these data in hand, a causal framework using instru-
mental variables and complementary regression models can be implemented to trace and quantify
the outage→heat exposure→excess mortality pathway.
However, due to data limitations, it is difficult for us to conduct this analysis at the current
2

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

stage. We acknowledge this limitation in our manuscript. In our subsequent work, we will collect
and construct micro-level health datasets to more clearly identify the causal relationship between
power outages and health risks and to characterize its spatiotemporal patterns. We are grateful
for the reviewer’s guidance and suggestions.
At the current stage, due to data limitations, we adopt a macro-level health-risk modeling
framework, supplemented by sensitivity analysis, to estimate excess mortality associated with
heat exposure during power outages. We regard this approach as reasonably reliable for three
reasons:
1. Health-risk model is theoretically grounded in epidemiological evidence. Existing studies
show that, over time, heat-related health risks are influenced by both short-term and cu-
mulative heat effects [1-3]. Therefore, we estimated the potential health risks of vulnerable
populations using both short-term heat exposure during cooling outages and cumulative
heat exposure over a week. This provides the theoretical basis for our use of a polynomial
specification to estimate health risks.
2. Macro-scale health data capture the effects of socioeconomic conditions. Socioeconomic
conditions can affect heat-related health risks. These influences are incorporated into the
cross-regional association macro health data (association between high temperatures and
heath risk); regions with weaker socioeconomic conditions may face higher health risk at the
same temperature.
3. Supplementary sensitivity analyses yield more reliable interval estimates. To capture inter-
regional social and environmental influences on health risks, we compiled temperature–health-
risk data from multiple cities—including Curitiba and S˜ ao Paulo (Brazil), Lanzhou (China),
Turin (Italy), A Coru˜ na and Bilbao (Spain), London (UK), and Paris (France)—to construct
risk intervals across temperatures. We then applied these intervals in sensitivity analyses to
estimate excess mortality under aggressive and conservative assumptions. By integrating
cross-regional macro health data with sensitivity analysis, our estimates more reliably reflect
the influence of socioeconomic and environmental differences across locations. The detailed
sensitivity analysis is provided below.

different risk levels. Finally, using the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao megacity cluster as a case
