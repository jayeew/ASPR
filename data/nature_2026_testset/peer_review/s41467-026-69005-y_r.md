Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Pan et al. show that Escherichia coli promotes colorectal cancer (CRC) liver metastasis by inducing
neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) via a RIPK2-dependent mechanism. These NETs, in turn, enhance calcium influx in
CRC cells through upregulation of TRPC1, leading to the formation of a STAT3/S100A8/9 trimer that stabilizes enhancer-
promoter loops (EPLs), particularly promoting Tns1 transcription and thus facilitating metastasis. This study provides
important insights into how intestinal microbiota modulate tumor immunity and metastasis through molecular crosstalk
between neutrophils and CRC cells. 
Overall, this is a scientifically compelling and technically well-executed study with important findings. The mechanistic data
related to NET formation and downstream signaling within cancer cells are particularly strong. However, several aspects
require clarification and additional experimental support to improve the coherence of the narrative, reinforce the mechanistic
conclusions, and enhance the translational relevance. 
Key points include: 
Major comments: 
1) The manuscript focuses on liver metastasis, but it remains unclear how the various targeting strategies (e.g., NETs,
RIPK2, TRPC1, S100A8/9, STAT3) impact the primary tumor. Do these interventions affect tumor growth, local invasion? It
would be valuable to include in vivo analyses—such as histological assessment of invasion or tumor burden in the colon—
to better understand the broader impact of these treatments. 
2) The study does not investigate whether E. coli is present within the metastatic lesions themselves. While the authors
show an enrichment of E. coli in the primary tumors of patients with liver metastases, it remains unclear whether E. coli
persists in the metastatic sites. This is an important point because it would clarify whether E. coli acts solely at the primary
site to initiate metastasis or whether it continues to influence tumor behavior after dissemination. The authors should either
evaluate E. coli presence in metastases or discuss its potential relevance to their proposed mechanism. 
3) The experimental design and interpretation of Figures S1b–d are difficult to follow. The legend refers to E. coli
transplantation, but this appears to have been performed only after broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment. Consequently, a
condition with E. coli alone is missing, making it hard to isolate the effect of E. coli itself. Additionally, the main text does not
clearly state whether immune cell depletion was performed in this experiment (but so does the legend), although this would
be crucial for interpreting the mechanism. The sentence “E. coli has been witnessed to promote liver metastasis through
CRC cells themselves (Fig. S1b–d)” suggests an immune-independent effect, yet the authors later shift focus to neutrophils
as key mediators. This inconsistency needs to be addressed. Moreover, if CD45+ cell depletion was used, it is unclear
whether neutrophils would also be depleted, which complicates interpretation. Overall, both the rationale and the
conclusions drawn from these experiments require clarification in the text. In the relevant figure panels, the authors report
"tumor volume," but since the study specifically investigates liver metastases, this should be referred to as "metastasis
volume" to avoid confusion. The terminology needs to be consistent with the biological context of the experiments.

4) The authors primarily use mouse models involving E. coli inoculation. However, in patient samples, they report that E. coli
enrichment is observed in the primary tumors of metastatic CRC patients, not necessarily through external inoculation. This
raises concerns about the model's relevance: if the tumor cells are already highly metastatic (e.g., MC38 model), it is unclear
why additional E. coli inoculation is needed. To better mirror the clinical situation, the authors could consider using a non-
metastatic or low-metastatic CRC model to assess whether E. coli can truly promote metastatic progression rather than
merely acting in an already aggressive setting. 
5) Neutrophils depletion must be properly validated (Fig. 1i). Using the same Ly6G antibody for both depletion and
confirmation of neutrophil removal is not methodologically sound and it seems like what was done here. The authors should
employ an independent marker—such as myeloperoxidase or neutrophil elastase staining—for validation. Additionally, the
exact reference and clone of each depleting antibody should be clearly stated in the Materials and Methods section. 
Minor comments 
1) In Figure S1a, the legend states that E. coli rRNA levels were measured in primary lesions of CRC patients with liver
metastases using fluorescence in situ hybridization. However, the data shown appear to be from mouse samples, not patient
tissues. This should be clarified, and if the figure indeed shows only mouse data, the legend must be corrected accordingly.
If patient data were intended, they should be shown explicitly. 
2) In Figures S2a–c, the data suggest possible crosstalk between macrophages and neutrophils, but this is not addressed in
the text. It might be important to clarify the effects of neutrophil depletion on macrophage populations, and vice versa
(discussion only is fine if the authors do not have already the data generated). 
3) In Figures S2d–f, the authors examine the effects of DNase I and PAD4 inhibitor on NET formation. However, the impact
of these treatments on NET levels is not directly validated. Experimental confirmation—such as quantification of Cit-
H3+/MPO+ structures or other established NET markers—is necessary to support the interpretation. 
4) In Figure 1g, the NET staining is unclear. There appears to be substantial Cit-H3 signal that does not colocalize with
MPO, raising questions about the identity of these structures. The authors should clarify what these Cit-H3+/MPO− signals
represent and whether they are truly indicative of NETs. Additional staining or explanation is needed to support the
interpretation. 
5) In Figures 1i–l, the authors report that E. coli effects on cadherins and vimentin are weakened by neutrophil depletion.
However, the figure panels do not clearly indicate which condition corresponds to which treatment, making interpretation
difficult. In addition, the neutrophil depletion group is only compared to untreated controls. To properly assess the specificity
of the effect, the Ly6G antibody treatment should be compared to an appropriate isotype (IgG) control. 
6) In Figure S3a, it would have been informative to include a condition where the inhibitors were tested alone, without E. coli
stimulation, to assess their baseline effects. Additionally, it remains unclear whether the same effects on NET formation
would be observed in non-tumor colon tissue. Including such controls would strengthen the conclusions. 
7) In Figure S3b, the authors state that neither Ripk2-cKI nor Ripk2-cKO altered immune cell counts following E. coli
infection. However, it is unclear from the figure whether these manipulations were systemic or restricted to CRC tissues.
Although this is clarified in the Materials and Methods, it would improve readability to briefly state in the main text whether
the knock-in/knockout was tissue-specific or systemic. 
8) In Figure 1n, the authors assess EMT marker expression in Ripk2-cKO and cKI conditions, but only correlate NET levels
with EMT changes in the knock-in model (Fig. S3C). To fully support their conclusions, NET levels should also be evaluated
in the Ripk2-cKO condition and correlated with EMT marker expression. 
9) The sentence “While Ripk2-cKI enhanced E. coli’s influence on EMT protein expression (Fig. 1n), indicating RIPK2 in
neutrophils is crucial for E. coli-driven CRC liver metastasis” is grammatically incorrect. The use of "while" implies a
contrast, but none is presented. Consider revising the sentence for clarity 
10) In Figure 2, the proposed RIPK2/ATF3/RelB signaling cascade is central to the mechanism, but it is not directly shown to
be dependent on E. coli. A simple in vitro experiment—such as stimulating neutrophils with E. coli and assessing activation
of this pathway—would strengthen the claim and help confirm that this signaling axis is specifically triggered by E. coli
exposure. 
11) The manuscript presents an elaborate signaling cascade (RIPK2 → ATF3/RelB → NCF4 → MLKL) leading to NET
formation. However, PAD4 inhibition is also shown to reduce NETs, yet the link between PAD4 activity and this signaling
pathway is not addressed. The authors should clarify how PAD4 fits into this model—does it act downstream, in parallel, or
independently of the RIPK2-driven pathway? This connection is important to fully understand the mechanism of NET
regulation in this context. 
12) The labeling within the figures themselves (not just the legends) is often unclear or misleading. For instance, in Figure
4d, it appears from the figure text that cells were treated directly with PMA, when in fact the treatment involved NETs
generated by PMA-stimulated neutrophils. This type of mislabeling occurs in several figures and can easily lead to
misinterpretation. The authors should revise the figure annotations to accurately reflect the experimental setup, without

requiring readers to cross-reference the main text for clarification. 
13) Given that RIPK2 can signal through both NF-κB and MAPK pathways, it would be informative to test whether
simultaneous inhibition of these pathways alongside ATF3/RelB/NCF4 modulation has additive or synergistic effects on
NET formation and metastasis. This could help clarify the relative contribution of each branch of the pathway and strengthen
the mechanistic understanding. 
14) Based on the current data, it remains unclear whether NET-induced pSTAT3 activation occurs in a TRPC1-dependent
manner. 
15) While the manuscript focuses on how neutrophil-derived NETs influence CRC cells via the
TRPC1/S100A8/9/STAT3/TNS1 axis, it is not addressed whether targeting these pathways in CRC cells has any feedback
effect on neutrophils or NET formation. Assessing whether interventions on TRPC1, S100A8/9, STAT3, or TNS1 in tumor
cells alter neutrophil behavior or NET production could reveal important bidirectional interactions within the tumor
microenvironment. 
16) In key experiments, it would be more informative to restrict pathway modulation to colorectal cancer (CRC) cells rather
than the whole organism, to better isolate tumor-intrinsic effects. While neutrophil-specific targeting would certainly
strengthen the mechanistic claims, it is not essential. However, tumor cell-specific interventions (e.g., via CRC cell-specific
knockdown or overexpression) should be considered to support the proposed cell-autonomous roles of
TRPC1/S100A8/9/STAT3/TNS1. 
17) The manuscript shows that NETs upregulate TRPC1 expression in CRC cells, but the mechanism underlying this effect
is not explored. Do the authors have any hypothesis or supporting data on how NETs induce TRPC1 transcription or
stabilization? Even a brief discussion of potential mechanisms—whether through direct signaling, DNA sensing, or
inflammatory mediators—would strengthen the interpretation of this finding. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The submitted work from Pan, et al, titled “Escherichia coli promotes colorectal cancer liver metastasis by maintaining
STAT3 enhancer-promoter loops through releasing RIPK2-related neutrophil extracellular traps”, shows promising
observations in combination with functional validations. However, I do have some concerns stated below. Note that many of
my concerns are widespread over multiple figures, however herein I have highlighted some of the figures, but I urge the
authors to evaluate all figures related to my comments. 
Statistical Methodology Transparency 
The manuscript lacks sufficient detail regarding the choice of statistical tests. The authors must specify which normality
test(s) (e.g., Shapiro-Wilk, Kolmogorov-Smirnov) were used to determine whether parametric or non-parametric analyses
were appropriate. Several data distributions appear visually normal, while others do not—this inconsistency raises concerns
about whether the correct statistical approach was consistently applied. 
Western Blot (WB) Controls and Integrity 
Across multiple figures, including Figures 1m, 2i, 2m, 3k, 3O, 4g–i, 4n–p, 4t–u, and Supplementary Figures S2e, S3a, S5c–
e, there are recurring concerns about WB presentation: 
Can the authors confirm that loading controls (e.g., GAPDH, Histone H3, Actin) were run on the same membranes as the
respective target proteins in all presented figures? 
In Figure 1m, the TLR4 replicate appears mismatched with its GAPDH control (replicate 3 vs. replicate 1), and similar
inconsistencies are noted for TLR2, NOD1, and NOD2. Please clarify the sample orders and confirm correct pairings. 
Figure 2i shows a GAPDH blot with ladders on both sides, whereas associated blots (e.g., ATF3, RelB) only show one
ladder—please verify that they were run on the same membrane. 
In Figure 3O, the close molecular weights of GAPDH and NCF4 (39/36 kDa) necessitate confirmation that these were
resolved properly and run on the same membrane. 
Figures 4g–i and 4t–u show only a single GAPDH control used for bands of very similar size (e.g., p-STAT3/p-STAT1 and
STAT3/STAT1). If these were separate runs, corresponding independent GAPDH controls should be provided. 
For Figure 4N, the actin blot must be confirmed as originating from the same membrane as the target protein. The provided
uncropped blots suggest overlapping lanes in the histone blot, which requires clarification. 
More broadly, there are issues throughout the uncropped WB source data suggesting that not all loading controls are
presented or properly aligned with target proteins. The authors should perform a comprehensive review of all WB
experiments, ensure correct control usage, and provide updated figures if necessary. 
Sample Size Clarification and Data Consistency 
On lines 67–68, the authors state that 12 CRC patients with liver metastasis were analyzed, referencing Table S1. However,
the table lists 19 patients with and 19 without metastasis. What criteria led to excluding some samples from analysis? This
needs to be clarified. 
In Figure S2m, it is unclear how the total of 13 patients was distributed across experimental groups. Please specify sample
allocation. 
In Supplementary Figure S2e, the treatment dosage is not reported. The authors must clarify both the dose used and the

rationale for dosage selection, including whether prior studies or titrations informed this. 
Replication and Quantification Issues 
Several figures, such as Figures 3e, 3g, and 6c, rely on only one or two replicates. To validate findings, at least triplicate
experiments are expected. Authors are strongly encouraged to repeat these experiments to ensure statistical and biological
reliability. 
In Figure 1n, it appears that the second E-Cadherin image may be zoomed out. All images should be presented on the same
scale, and scale bars must be clearly indicated. 
In Figure 3d, the lack of a scale bar makes interpretation difficult—please include it. 
For Figure 1n (and potentially others), the authors should verify whether images presented are from representative wells,
and clearly indicate which lanes correspond to the uncropped supplementary images. 
WB Quantification Presentation 
In some cases, notably with p-MLKL and MLKL, quantification is shown in a single bar graph. It may be more appropriate to
present these in separate graphs, especially when they represent biologically distinct outcomes. 
Microbiota Composition and Comparative Studies 
The authors state in line 69 that “Principal coordinate analysis showed distinct microbiota compositions between the groups
(Fig. 1a).” However, visual inspection of Figure 1a suggests considerable overlap between metastatic and non-metastatic
samples. The authors should justify their claim of separation and provide quantitative metrics (e.g., PERMANOVA p-value,
R²) to support this. 
A recent publication from June 2024, titled “Tumor-resident microbiota contributes to colorectal cancer liver metastasis by
lactylation and immune modulation”, presents findings related to microbiota and CRC metastasis that partially overlap with
the current study, particularly with regard to Figure 1b–c. The authors should acknowledge and compare their findings to this
recent work. 
Moreover, that paper reported no differences in E- or N-cadherin expression between E. coli-treated and control cells,
contrasting with the present study. The authors should discuss how their findings align or diverge from this prior evidence. 
Relevance of SD vs SEM in Supplementary Data 
In Supplementary Figure 1, the authors use standard deviation (SD) instead of standard error of the mean (SEM). Please
explain why SD was preferred and why it is more appropriate in the context of this data. 
Assessment of Metastasis: Tumor Burden vs. Incidence 
The use of tumor volume in the liver as a proxy for metastasis requires more nuanced interpretation. Supplementary Figure
S2C shows metastasis occurring with or without E. coli presence, though tumor burden increases with E. coli, especially
under immune cell depletion. This suggests E. coli may promote accelerated metastatic infiltration rather than outright
metastasis incidence. The authors should refine their interpretation accordingly.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study uncovers a new mechanism by which E. coli promotes CRC liver metastasis through neutrophil extracellular traps
(NETs) and enhancer-promoter loops (EPLs). This provides new insights into the role of intestinal flora in tumor metastasis
and immune responses. Technically, the authors employed a combination of techniques, including 2bRAD-M analysis,
single-cell RNA sequencing, and HiChIP assays, to investigate the complex interactions between E. coli, neutrophils, and
CRC cells. This multidisciplinary approach adds depth and credibility to the study. In the end, this study identifies RIPK2 in
neutrophils as a key mediator of E. coli-induced CRC liver metastasis. Targeting this pathway could potentially lead to new
therapeutic strategies for CRC patients. A few technical comments should be addressed for publication. 
1. Although the study uses patient samples, the findings need to be further validated in larger clinical cohorts. The current
sample size may not be sufficient to draw definitive conclusions about the clinical significance of the results. 
2. While the study focuses on the role of neutrophils in E. coli-induced CRC liver metastasis, the exact mechanisms by
which neutrophils interact with E. coli and promote metastasis are still not fully understood. Further studies are needed to
clarify the complex interplay between neutrophils, E. coli, and CRC cells. 
3. The study does not fully address potential confounding factors that could influence the results. For example, the impact of
other components of the intestinal flora, the patient's diet, or lifestyle factors on CRC liver metastasis was not investigated.
Can authors provide more evidence on this aspect using standard statistical methods such as PERMANOVA? 
4. The study was conducted using mouse models and cell lines. It is unclear whether the findings can be generalized to
humans, as there may be differences in the immune response and tumor microenvironment between mice and humans. If no
existing studies, authors may insert some limitation statements in the Discussion. 
5. Regarding 2bRAD-M applications in analyzing microbial composition in tumor tissue, can the authors briefly clarify the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

advantages of this technique over others (such as 16S amplicon sequencing) in this study? Is it possible to identify E. coli
strains in the clinical samples? 
6. Line 913. It is quite confusing to see "OTUs" here, as 2bRAD-M directly provides the taxonomic table to users. Bacterial
taxa at the species level were identified based on the species-unique 2b markers. OTUs should be revised as taxa. 
7. Line 918. Student’s t-test is NOT statistically valid to detect differentially abundant bacteria in the metagenomics data, as
the microbiome data is compositional and is NOT normally distributed. A few compositionally aware methods (ANCOM-BC,
ALDEx2, etc.) have been available in the field. Otherwise, you have to perform the Wilcoxon rank-sum test using CLR-
transformed data. 
Also, LDA or Metastats is an outdated statistical tool for this purpose, which may significantly increase the false-positive rate
of microbial marker identification. Please be careful and update the marker discovery results according to compositionally
aware statistical methods. 
8. Did the authors associate the tumor microbiome diversity with clinical phenotypes? Did the authors identify any bacteria
that co-occur with E. coli in the tumor tissues? Is it possible that a bacterial group, including E. coli rather than E. coli alone,
promotes CRC liver metastasis through neutrophil extracellular traps? Can authors track the tumor-derived E. coli in the
blood using 2bRAD-M? 
9. The extensive use of technical terms. While this is appropriate for a specialized readership, it could pose challenges to
those less familiar with the field. Some terms could benefit from more explicit definitions or explanations, especially when
they are first introduced. For example, when terms like "2bRAD-M", "SCENIC", and "HiChIP" are mentioned, a more
detailed description of what these techniques entail could help non-experts better understand the experimental methods and
results. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled “Escherichia coli promotes colorectal cancer liver metastasis by maintaining STAT3 enhancer3
promoter loops through releasing RIPK2-related neutrophil extracellular traps” by Pan et al. revealed that E. coli promoted
CRC liver metastasis via NETs. Mechanistically, the study demonstrates that HNRNPK, upregulated by E. coli-activated
RIPK2, enhances Atf3/Relb transcription in neutrophils, driving MLKL-mediated NET release. NETs then enhance CRC
liver metastasis by upregulating TRPC1 to form a STAT3/S100A8/9 trimer, stabilizing EPLs and the Tns1 framework. This
work offers new molecular insights into how E. coli contributes to NET-mediated CRC liver metastasis. While the manuscript
is interesting, it needs more evidence on the molecular regulation mechanisms, particularly between the upstream
molecules of MLKL and MLKL. Therefore, it should be revised before publication in this journal. The following major issues
need to be addressed: 
1. The existing conclusions regarding the regulatory role between NOD1/2, RIPK2, HNRNPK, ATF3/RELB with MLKL in
neutrophils lack supporting evidence. For instance, perform siRNA knockdown or overexpression experiments to detect the
changes in MLKL expression 
2. The regulation of E. coli and downstream HNRNPK-ATF3/RELB-NCF4 pathway in neutrophils should be supported by
evidence to establish causality. The authors should figure out if this happens in vivo in their models of bacterial
transplantation. 
3. The regulation of NOD1/2, RIPK2, HNRNPK and downstream NCF4 in neutrophils lacks evidence. 
4. The regulation of NET, TRPC1 and downstream TNS1 in CRC cells should be supported by evidence to establish
causality. 
Minor issues: 
1. Figure 1A and 1B, In the legends and the Results section, the number of samples per group should be specified. “n=12”
could be misinterpreted as a total of 12 samples. Additionally, the sample number in the figure is inconsistent with that in
Table S1. 
2. In Figure 2P, “citoh3” should be corrected to “citH3”. Furthermore, when citH3, a NET marker, is first introduced in the text,
it should be appropriately described. 
3. The statement at line 194 does not have a corresponding figure. 
4. There are many types of calcium channel proteins. How did you rule out other candidates and select TRPC1? 
5. In Figure 5A, there is no quantification of these images, so it is unclear how often is this phenomenon observed. Is all
STAT3 staining positive for S100A8/9 and viceversa? 
6. The authors should show p-values instead of asterisks for statistics. 
7. The replication strategy for each experiment should be defined. 
Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this work, Pan et al. studied on the role of E. coli in the progress of CRC. While some conclusions are of interest, there are
essential issues need to be considered before further processing. 
1. Although the article mentioned that the sample size was estimated based on preliminary experiments, the specific
calculation method and basis were not clear. When studying human diseases, the size of the sample directly affects the
statistical power of the results and the reliability of the conclusions. A small sample size may increase the chance of the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

results and fail to accurately reflect the real situation. 
2. Lack of long-term follow-up data. The study mainly focused on the short-term effects of E. coli on CRC liver metastasis, but
did not provide follow-up data on the long-term prognosis and survival rate of patients. This is insufficient for evaluating the
long-term role of E. coli in CRC metastasis and its potential value as a therapeutic target. 
3. There are significant individual differences between CRC patients, including genetic background, lifestyle, and dietary
habits. These factors may affect the composition and function of the intestinal flora, and thus affect the role of E. coli in CRC
metastasis. However, the article does not mention whether these individual differences have been fully considered and
controlled. 
4. This study mainly focused on E. coli, while the intestinal flora is a complex microbial community containing a variety of
bacteria, fungi, viruses, etc. Studying E. coli alone may not fully reflect the overall role of intestinal flora in CRC liver
metastasis, and ignores the potential impact of other microorganisms. 
5. Although cell experiments and animal model experiments were conducted in this article, there may be differences
between the results of cell experiments and in vivo experiments. Cell experiments cannot fully simulate the complex
physiological and pathological environment in the body, and animal models and humans also differ in physiological
structure and immune response. Therefore, the applicability of cell experimental results in humans needs to be further
verified. 
4. Despite a variety of advanced technical means, such as single-cell RNA sequencing and HiChIP, are used, these
technologies also have their limitations. For example, single-cell RNA sequencing may not be able to detect low-abundance
transcripts, and HiChIP technology may have false positive or false negative results when detecting chromatin loops. These
technical limitations may affect the accuracy and reliability of the research results. 
Data analysis 
5. Authors mentioned the use of statistical methods such as independent sample t-test and Wilcoxon rank sum test, but does
not elaborate on whether these methods are applicable to all data types and research questions. For example, when
comparing two groups of data, if the data do not meet the normal distribution or variance homogeneity, it may be
inappropriate to use the independent sample t test. In addition, for the comparison of multiple groups of data, it is not
mentioned whether multiple comparison correction was performed, which may lead to an increase in false positive results. 
6. When performing bioinformatics analysis on sequencing data, although some commonly used tools and databases were
used, the optimization of the analysis process and the rationality of parameter settings were not detailed. For example, when
performing gene expression difference analysis, it was not mentioned whether batch effects, data normalization and other
factors were considered. For the results of pathway enrichment analysis, no detailed biological explanation and verification
were provided, and the specific mechanism of action of these pathways in CRC liver metastasis could not be determined. In
addition, the details of 2bRAD-M profiling should be provided, e.g. database, sequence mapping tool and parameters. How
was the OTU (#915, a typo of OUT) table generated, and how a OTU was defined? 
7. Since the study found an association between E. coli and CRC liver metastasis and proposed a possible molecular
mechanism, this association does not necessarily mean a causal relationship. There may be other undiscovered factors that
affect the occurrence of E. coli and CRC liver metastasis at the same time, leading to the observed correlation. Therefore,
further experiments are needed to verify whether E. coli directly causes CRC liver metastasis and its specific role in the
metastasis process. 
8. This study revealed the potential role of E. coli in CRC liver metastasis, its feasibility in clinical diagnosis and treatment
has not been fully explored. For example, whether the detection method based on E. coli has sufficient sensitivity and
specificity, and whether the treatment strategy against E. coli is safe and effective. These issues are the key to converting
research results into clinical applications and need further research to solve. 
Reviewer #6 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a wide-ranging study in which the authors explore the role of E. coli in promoting metastasis of colorectal cancer
cells, via NET formation. The authors show a role for RIPK2 in neutrophils in response to infection, resulting in activation of
STAT3 in CRCs, in a S100A8/9 and Trpc1-dependent manner. They then show that the STAT3/S100A8/9 complex is
associated with enhancer-promoter loops and upregulates the expression of a number of genes including Tns1, which
promotes metastasis. 
My expertise is in chromatin biology, so I have mainly focused on these aspects of the manuscript. Overall, as a non-
specialist, I found the paper quite difficult to read, since there was very little explanation of the proteins or pathways involved
and insufficient description of the work being presented. A lot of the information required to understand the experiments was
only included in the methods section, with minimal description of the experimental set-up included in the results or figure
legends. This made it very difficult to critically assess the conclusions drawn from these experiments. 
I have two main concerns about the data in the paper (detailed below). Firstly, based on the data presented, there seems to
be very little reproducibility between replicates in the ATF3 and RelB CUT&Tag experiments. Secondly, there appears to be

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

an intrinsic flaw in the Hi-ChIP experiment, where STAT3 IP is used to look for looping events in the presence of a mutation
that likely disrupts STAT3 binding, potentially biasing the results. 
Major points 
1. DNA pull-down (Figure 2j,l,m, Figure 3e,g,m, 6c) is an unusual technique to demonstrate protein binding to DNA. Binding
of proteins to DNA would be more convincing (and much more quantitative) demonstrated by ChIP (either ChIP-qPCR or
ChIP-seq) or CUT&Tag. 
2. I can see that Atf3 and RelB expression is dependent on RIPK2, but are the authors arguing that this activation is induced
by E. coli? A western blot (or equivalent) showing whether Atf3 and RelB protein levels are increased in neutrophils
following E. coli infection would be helpful. 
3. ATF3/RelB CUT&Tag: what is the overlap between the two replicates for each experiment? The number of peaks is
relatively similar – but what proportion of them are the same in each replicate? Based on the venn diagram in Fig 3c there
seems to be very little overlap in the genes bound in the two replicates, which does not give me confidence that they are
reliably detecting the same binding – and therefore it’s difficult to draw conclusions from these data. Looking at the tracks in
Fig 3d the quality of the data looks relatively poor, which suggests that the peak calling may not have been entirely
successful. For me, I would like more reassurance of the reproducibility of these data to have any confidence in it. 
As a minor point, lines 181-2 say that the peaks are mainly at the TSS, but the pie charts in Fig S4e show that nearly half of
peaks are actually distal intergenic (and only about one quarter at the promoter). 
4. Immunoprecipitation (Figure 3k, l, Figure 4, 5, 6): According to the methods section, the IP was conducted in Tricine SDS
sample buffer. What is the composition of this buffer? What is the salt concentration? Without knowing the stringency of the
wash conditions it’s impossible to assess whether the interactions indicated by IP-WB are physiological or merely low-
affinity interactions that are the result of gentle IP conditions. 
5. Figure 5: The authors propose that S100A8/9 act as cofactors for STAT3 in CRC cells. They show co-
immunoprecipitation of soluble proteins, but no evidence that the proteins co-bind to DNA. The most straightforward way to
do this would be ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag for S100A8/9 and STAT3 and to compare the peak overlap between the two. 
6. A) Figure 5 Hi-ChIP: the authors perform Hi-ChIP for STAT3 in wild-type and STAT3/S100A8/9-24A cells to demonstrate
the loss of enhancer-promoter loops in the mutant, showing a large decrease in interactions. However, this experiment is
inherently biased. The authors don’t show much data on whether STAT3 chromatin binding is disrupted in the mutant
genome-wide (although Fig 6h,i indicate a reduction at Tns1), but this seems likely. If STAT3 binding is lost in the mutant, it
is obvious that STAT3-associated looping will decrease, because there is less STAT3 to pull-down in the Hi-ChIP. This
doesn’t mean that the loops are no longer present, merely that the technique can’t detect them in the mutant cells. If the
authors want to show that these enhancer-promoter loops are dependent on STAT3, they need to either show that STAT3
binding is not affected by the STAT3/S100A8/9-24A mutation (eg by reference-normalised CUT&Run), or they need to use a
