Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, the authors evaluated the natural experiment resulting from the cessation of sugar rationing in the UK in
Sep 1953 to address the impact of early-life sugar restriction (from gestation until age 2 years) on lifelong risk of heart failure
(HF). Amongst 61,179 UK Biobank participants born between October 1951 and March 1956, there were 22,449 participants
who had not experienced sugar rationing, while 38,730 had. Landmark analysis showed no significant difference in HF
incidence before age 45; however, after age 45, those exposed to sugar rationing had a lower incidence of HF. After
adjusting for confounders, participants exposed to sugar rationing had a lower risk of HF compared to those who were not
(Cox regression HR = 0.87, 95% CI, 0.78-0.97; FPSM, HR = 0.86, 95% CI, 0.77-0.96), and a delay in the onset of HF (linear
regression, β=2.57, 95% CI, 1.98-3.17). The protective effect increased with the duration of exposure to sugar rationing and
there was a positive synergistic interaction between genetic susceptibility and sugar rationing. The authors conclude as
follows: “Early-life sugar restriction may be associated with a lower lifelong risk of heart failure or delayed onset. However,
further experimental studies are needed to confirm these findings and explore the underlying biological mechanisms.”

Review of manuscript: Sugar Rationing During the First 1000 Days of Life and Lifelong Risk of Heart Failure 
Remarks to the authors: The research presented in this manuscript aims to answer a highly relevant question in the
nutritional epidemiology field, with potential to inform policy and clinical guidance. However, there are some reporting and
methodological points that I recommend be further clarified: 
1. Please ensure that the following is indeed mentioned in the cited manuscript “estimates from the Global Burden of
Disease Study, approximately 55.5 million people worldwide were affected by HF in 2021”. If this estimate was taken form
an interactive visualization (i.e., http://ihmeuw.org/73oa) please ensure proper citation as indicated here:
https://www.healthdata.org/data-tools-practices/interactive-visuals/gbd-compare 
2. The study cites Gracner et al. study as main source, which states upon termination of the rationing, sugar consumption
nearly doubled while consumption of other foods remained unchanged. However, it is not clear how diet was measured then
to determine the validity of this claim. Clear explanation of diet measurement is needed. 
3. Moreover, while it has been established that an ultra-processed foods based diet leads to increased caloric intake (Hall,
2019; PMID: 31105044), it is also known that the effect of individual foods/nutrients (e.g., sugar) on disease needs to be
assessed independent of total caloric intake. Otherwise, the observed effect could be due not to the nutrient intake but to the
metabolic effect of increased/decreased body weight / adiposity (Willett, 1997; PMID: 3521261). Please discuss how the
lack of caloric-adjustment might be limiting the results. 
4. Please clarify why age 45 was selected as a cut-off to assess HF incidence. 
5. The authors have a compelling discussion. However, further discussion about other factors could also be associated with
reduced incidence of HF and that were not possible to control for is recommended, including: 
a. Possibility of reduced total caloric intake and cardiometabolic impact 
b. The impact due to the increase or decrease of other foods/nutrients (e.g., foods generally consumed with sugary foods that
were no longer consumed, replacement of sugars with more healthful foods) 
c. Adjustment for socioeconomic variables. While this is briefly mentioned in the discussion, further comment about how
adjusting for this could impact the results would be valuable. 
6. The following phrase seems unnecessary or requires further explanation “Further rigorous randomized controlled trials
were essential.” 
7. In the conclusion, the authors mention “further experimental studies are needed to confirm these findings and explore the
underlying biological mechanisms.” However, this is an unrealistic and unethical approach. Longitudinal studies are widely
accepted for nutritional epidemiological research for several reasons, including that many of the diseases require years of
exposure before appearing (which would be harder to assess in RCT) and that it’d be unethical to have a group with a likely
unhealthy diet, especially for extended periods of time. I suggest reframing the conclusion.

In the newly submitted manuscript, we have provided additional clarification in the Covariates 
section: “This study primarily utilised a birth cohort design, focusing on the management of early-
life risk factors. The exposure period to sugar rationing was confined to the initial 1000 days of 
life, encompassing both infancy and the prenatal stage. Currently, conventional risk factors for HF, 
such as smoking, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), hypertension and diabetes history, are 
either unquantifiable or lack clinical significance and comparability. Consequently, drawing from 
prior research8, the primary analysis considered the following time-invariant covariates: 
birthplace, sex, race, parental cardiovascular disease (CVD), parental hypertension, parental DM, 
calendar month of birth, and polygenic risk score (PRS) for HF.” 
 
In addition, based on the data, the statement that “rationed individuals had a higher likelihood for 
CVD and diabetes family histories” is not entirely accurate. As shown in Table 1 of this study, 
among individuals exposed to sugar rationing, the proportions of parents diagnosed with diabetes 
(Rationed: 18.29%, Non-rationed: 19.39%) and hypertension (Rationed: 45.48%, Non-rationed:

57.10%, Non-rationed: 59.11%). Chi-square tests indicated statistically significant differences

V , and the effect sizes were small and likely of limited clinical significance (Cramér’s V < 0.1), 
suggesting that the “statistical significance” primarily reflects the large sample size. This also 
indirectly supports the appropriateness of the narrow exposure window to sugar rationing in this 
study, as the cohort characteristics were broadly similar.

Second, we fully agree that considering factors such as physical activity, body weight, BMI, 
hypertension, and socioeconomic status is reasonable. To address your concern, we conducted a 
sensitivity analysis. We discarded the initial birth cohort design, which commenced follow-up at 
birth, and opted to utilise the baseline assessment of participants in the UKB (2006–2010) as the 
follow-up initiation point. At this stage, most participants were in midlife, and a small subset had 
encountered outcome events (loss to follow-up or incident HF, n=187), which were excluded from 
the analysis.  
 
Four categories of potential confounders were considered: (1) time-invariant covariates 
(birthplace, sex, race, parental CVD, parental hypertension, parental DM, calendar month of birth, 
and PRS for HF); (2) current socioeconomic and health status, including BMI, DM, hypertension, 
and CVD; (3) lifestyle factors, including physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and 
dietary patterns; and (4) early-life growth and developmental factors. Socioeconomic status was 
assessed using the Townsend deprivation index at recruitment, which incorporated data on social 
class, employment, car availability, and housing. BMI was calculated by dividing weight in 
kilograms by the square of height in meters. DM history was characterised by self-reported 
physician-diagnosed DM, current use of antidiabetic medication or insulin, fasting plasma glucose 
levels of ≥7.0 mmol/L or glycated hemoglobin levels of ≥6.5% (48 mmol/mol). Hypertension 
history was characterised by self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension, current use of

antihypertensive medication or repeated measurements indicating an average systolic blood 
pressure of ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of ≥90 mmHg. CVD history was defined as 
self-reported physician-diagnosed heart attack, angina, or stroke. Physical activity was categorised 
into walking, moderate and vigorous activity according to total weekly metabolic equivalent task 
hours for all activities. Smoking and alcohol consumption were categorised as never, former or 
current. The evaluation of a healthy dietary pattern was conducted according to the consumption 
of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, processed meat, refined grains, fish, unprocessed red meat and 
sugar-sweetened food or SSBs. Scoring details are provided in Supplementary Table 130. Early-life 
growth and development factors encompassed self-assessed body size and height at age 10 relative 
to peers (thinner/shorter, about average, plumper/taller). To minimise the influence of the birth 
cohort effect, we performed survival regression analyses, using age as the temporal scale to 
investigate the assocation between early-life exposure to sugar rationing and the subsequent risk 
of HF in midlife and later life. We excluded participants who encountered outcomes during the 
initial two years of follow-up were subsequently excluded to reduce potential reverse causation. 
 
The results demonstrated that when the follow-up start point was altered and additional 
confounding factors related to heart failure were considered, the study findings remained 
consistent: even when individuals had reached middle age, early-life exposure to sugar rationing 
continued to be associated with a reduced risk of HF in later life (Supplementary Tables 5, 
Supplementary Figure 5)

“Abstract 
Background: Elevated sugar intake has been linked to an increased risk of heart failure (HF) in 
adults and poor cardiometabolic health in children and adolescents. However, the effect of sugar 
restriction during early life (from gestation to age 2) on the lifelong risk of HF remains 
unexplored. 
 
Method: After the cessation of sugar rationing in the United Kingdom (UK) in September 1953, 
national sugar consumption nearly doubled, whereas the intake of other foods and nutrient 
consumption remained unchanged. In this study, participants from the UK Biobank (UKB) were 
grouped according to birth timing, which determined their exposure to sugar rationing. A 
comparison of HF incidence between individuals who were exposed to sugar rationing and those 
who were not was performed using Kaplan–Meier and Landmark analyses. The association 
between the duration of sugar rationing and the risk of lifelong HF was investigated using a birth 
cohort design and Cox regression and flexible parametric survival models (FPSM) using age as 
the timescale. Moreover,  we also calculat ed population attributable fractions (PAFs) and 
assessed the interactions between genetic susceptibility and early-life sugar rationing. Sensitivity 
analyses were conducted using a parametric proportional hazards model based on the Gompertz 
distribution. Alternatively, the follow-up initiation was redefined to the UKB baseline recruitment, 
and additional adjustments for the conventional risk factors of HF were performed. 
 
Results: The study enrolled 61,193 participants born between October 1951 and March 1956. 
Females constitute 56.21% of the participants, and 22,456 participants had not experienced sugar 
rationing. Landmark analysis revealed no significant difference in HF incidence before the age of 
45. However, individuals over 45 years old who were exposed to sugar rationing exhibited a 
markedly lower incidence. After adjustments for confounders, participants exposed to sugar 
rationing exhibited a lower risk of HF than those who were not (Cox regression, hazards ratio 
[HR] = 0.87, 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.78–0.97; FPSM, HR = 0.86, 95% CI, 0.77–0.96) 
and a delay in the onset of HF (linear regression, β = 2.57, 95% CI, 1.97–3.16). PAF analysis 
suggested that early-life sugar rationing can prevent 4%–5% of HF events. The protective effect 
intensified with the length of exposure to sugar rationing. Additive interaction was observed 
between genetic susceptibility and sugar rationing, and sensitivity analyses confirmed the 
robustness of these findings, indicating that the protective effect of early-life sugar rationing on 
HF risk persisted to middle and older age. 
 
Conclusion: Early-life sugar rationing may be associated with a reduced lifelong risk of HF or 
delayed onset. However, additional studies that are feasible and ethically justifiable are needed to 
confirm these findings and explore the underlying biological mechanisms.”

Response: We thank you for your comment, which we consider to be a key point. We would like

First, based on the NFS data, the intake of nutrients including protein, fat, and cereals did not
