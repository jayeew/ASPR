Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a highly innovative and impactful study integrating a poison-resistant Ni single-atom catalyst (Ni
SAC) with engineered C. ljungdahlii for efficient CO2-to-isopropanol (IPA) conversion. The work addresses critical
bottlenecks (catalyst deactivation in bioelectrolytes, low CO supply for microbial upgrading) and demonstrates record-
breaking IPA production rates. The mechanistic investigation using in situ spectroscopy and DFT is rigorous. While the
study is fundamentally sound and technologically significant, several key aspects require clarification or expansion to fully
support the claims and ensure robustness. 
Specific Comments: 
1.Faradaic Efficiency (FE) Calculation for C2+Oxygenates: Calculating FE for IPA and ethanol (C2+ oxygenates) based on
the total current density (which generates CO/H2 via electrochemical CO2RR) is inappropriate. FE is an electrochemical
metric defined for products directly generated at the electrode surface from electrons consumed. IPA and ethanol are
microbial products derived from the consumption of the electrochemically generated CO/H2, not direct electrochemical
products. 
So it is suggested to Report FE only for the electrochemical products (CO, H2, possibly formate if detected). For microbial
products (IPA, EtOH, acetate), report electron recovery efficiency (ERE) or carbon yield based on the electrons/carbon
contained in the supplied CO/H2. Clearly state in the text and figure captions that the "FE" values for IPA/EtOH represent
electron allocation efficiency from the electrochemical donor gases to the specific microbial product, not direct
electrochemical FE. 
2.Assigning partial current densities to IPA and EtOH (e.g., "partial current density for IPA") with the fomula given is
fundamentally incorrect for the same reason as point 1. Current density is a property of the electrochemical reaction step
(CO2RR to CO/H2).

(Remarks to the Author) 
This is an interesting manuscript describing isopropanol production in an integrated CO2 electrolyzer – bioreactor system
using a genetically modified strain of Clostridium ljungdahlii. Advantages of using single-atom nickel catalyst (Ni SAC) for

the integrated system used in this study is different from a typical MES cell, which typically uses microbial biofilm at the
cathode for CO2 conversion and subsequent biotranformations.

Since the reviewer also mentioned the issue of decreasing current density with increasing ERE 
(“FE”) in Comment 3, we provide a comprehensive analysis here to address this point.  
First, we want to clarify that over the 4 -day operating period, both the overall current density 
and catalyst structure remained stable, as evidenced by Supplementary Fig. S21-S23. Therefore, 
we do not attribute the observed current behavior to catalyst degradation. In fact, when plotting 
Fig. 4b, we intentionally set the current density axis to a narrow range (7.5 -14 mA/cm2) to 
better visualize the fluctuations and error bars, which may visually exaggerate the appearance 
of a “decline”.  
In addition, current fluctuations can result from various factors, with the dominant one being 
the loss of the gas-liquid-solid three-phase interface caused by the gradual loss of gas-diffusion 
later (GDL)  hydrophobicity during long -term operation. This leads to a decrease in current 
density or CO FE. This issue remains a persistent challenge in the field  (Accounts of chemical 
research 51.4 (2018): 910 -918.; Energy & Fuels  37.20 (2023): 15283 -15308.). Compared to 
prior literature and our earlier studies, the current density of 10 -12 mA/cm2 in this work is 
relatively moderate, resulting in milder interfacial reactions. Moreover, the use of a pH buffer 
in the electrolyte helped  mitigate excessive local alkalization near the electrode under long -
term operation. As a result, electrode flooding was less severe, contributing to the relatively 
stable current and CO FE observed in our system (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. S21).  Over 
time, the increasingly larger error bars also indicate greater current fluctuations.  Nevertheless, 
the catalyst itself did not observe significant deactivation or degradation. We therefore attribute 
the slight decrease in average current density to the com monly observed loss of GDL

Although the authors provided a previous publication showing the CO₂ reduction performance of a bare electrode is not as promising as one decorated with a nickel catalyst, we cannot conclude if the bare electrode + microbe system would perform much worse than the proposed microbe–nickel catalyst system as there are potential synergies at the electrochemically active material–microbe interfaces for chemical synthesis in the previously reported works. Moreover, since cell viability is unknown, it is uncertain whether the performance stems from microbial metabolites or extracellular components rather than from living and active microbial cells. Experimental groups in the exact same setup with precise control of variables are critical for supporting the observed performance in the designed system. 2. The cell viability tests present the functionality of the microbes under testing conditions, whether in different growth conditions (Figure S1) nor electrochemical material-microbial conditions (Figure 4). The optical density (OD) tests themselves only represent the turbidity of the microbial culture (including living cells and all other large particles/aggregates), not the concentration of living cells nor their activity. Particularly, the OD at 600 nm shows large deviations when reaching a high value (often > 1 in the work, Figure S1 and Figure 4b), which introduces further errors in estimating the cell densities.
