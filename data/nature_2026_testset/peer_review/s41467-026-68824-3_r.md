Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a highly valuable and large-scale analysis of lunar regolith particles collected by the Chang’e-5
(CE5) and Chang’e-6 (CE6) missions, utilizing micro-CT scanning and machine learning-based classification. The study
provides rare and rich primary data that significantly contributes to our understanding of key lunar surface processes
including volcanism, meteoritic impacts, and space weathering. 
However, there are several critical points that require clarification and appropriate revision as follows: 
1. On the Saturation of Space Weathering and the Concept of “Dynamic Equilibrium”: 
The authors propose a central model in which particle morphology reaches a "dynamic equilibrium" through long-term space
weathering. However, this model appears to rest primarily on the observed statistical similarity in morphometric distributions
between CE5 and CE6 bulk soil particles. 
Yet, there are notable differences in the proportions of breccia, agglutinate, and fine particles between these two sites, which
suggests that the regolith as a whole has not reached equilibrium. The authors limit the model to the bulk soil size fraction
(20-200um), but considering the fact that larger particles supply the bulk soil through fragmentation, and bulk particles
themselves continue to break down into finer particles, all of these particle populations coexist and interact. Therefore, it is
questionable to assert that equilibrium applies exclusively to one size fraction without considering the whole regolith system.
Moreover, if such a size-specific model is to be proposed, a clear rationale for the selection and definition of the bulk soil
size range must be provided. 
Lastly, the authors suggest that morphological saturation has occurred by ~2 Ga, but this claim lacks direct comparison with
older Apollo regolith particles. Without examining whether Apollo particles also exhibit similar morphometrics, it is difficult to
confidently conclude that the saturation has been reached. Why don’t the authors interpret the observed morphological
similarity between CE5 and CE6 simply due to their similar ages? 
2. On regolith particle evolution processes: 
In paragraph [20], the authors note that impact gardening, thermal cycling, and solar wind irradiation contribute to
fragmentation and abrasion. However, the role of agglomeration processes is not discussed. For example, breccia particles
may be formed by the strong compression due to meteoroid impact, and agglutinate particles are formed by the gathering of
partly melted fragments. Also, fragmentation and abrasion can have opposing effects on particle morphology. Such
processes must be considered to accurately evaluate regolith evolution. The current interpretation risks oversimplifying the
particle evolution process. 
3. On the Comparison with Lunar Regolith Simulant (LRS): 
In paragraph [14], the authors observe that the LRS particles exhibit lower aspect ratios and higher sphericity, convexity, and
roundness compared to lunar basalt particles, and state that this resembles the morphology of asteroid surface particles.
However, since LRS particles are produced by mechanical crushing and have not undergone long-term abrasion, it is not
surprising that they resemble the asteroid surface particles which have very short surface residence times. 
4. On the Interpretation of Agglutinate Porosity Differences: 
The discussion in paragraphs [18] and [19] are largely speculative. Paragraph [18] explains the lower internal porosity

sections would benefit from a clearer presentation and more cautious language regarding the proposed mechanisms.

Response: We sincerely thank the reviewer for this insight and apologize that our original 
explanation of “dynamic equilibrium” was not sufficiently clear, which may have led to  a 
misunderstanding of our proposed mechanism. Additionally, in the initial version of the 
manuscript, we used both “saturation” and “dynamic equilibrium ” to describe the same 
mechanism, which may have introduced conceptual confusion. In the revised version, we 
consistently use the term “saturation” to articulate our mechanism. 
Indeed, as the reviewer notes, the CE5 and CE6 soils contain notably different proportions 
of impact-related components such as breccia and agglutinates (Reply Fig. 1a). This difference 
may reflect the slightly longer surface exposure age of the CE6 sampling site (~2.1 Ma; Zhang 
et al., 2025 b) compared with CE5 (~1.5 Ma; Zhang et al.,  2025b), which may have allowed 
CE6 soils to undergo more extended histories of space weathering and meteoritic bombardment, 
thereby producing a higher abundance of impact products (McFadden et al., 2024). CE6 soils 
also contain a larger fraction of fine particles (Reply Fig. 1b). These observations confirm that, 
in terms of impact products abundance and grain size, the regolith as a whole has not yet 
reached equilibrium.

However, this type of “imbalance” concerns the relative proportions of impact products  
and grain sizes, and is not directly linked to the saturation in morphological modification . In 
other words, the timescale to achieve morphological equilibrium is shorter than that required 
for impact products abundance or grain size equilibrium. 
For exam ple, McKay et al., (1983) modeled the temporal evolution of agglutinate 
abundance, showing that a balance is eventually reached because two opposing processes act 
simultaneously: micrometeorite impacts continuously produce new agglutinates, while 
external additions such as ejecta or burial remove or dilute existing ones, respectively. Over 
time, production slows as fewer fresh particles remain, while removal continues, until both 
processes balance at a constant agglutinate fraction. Similarly, Lindsay (1975 ) described 
equilibrium in grain size evolution as the long -term balance between destructive processes 
(comminution) and constructive processes (agglutination) driven by meteoroid impacts. 
It is important to note that grain size types of equilibrium are st rongly environment -
dependent. As King (1977) demonstrated, local factors such as proximity to boulders or recent 
craters significantly influence grain size characteristics. Thus, comparing CE5 and CE6 in 
terms of grain size cannot, by itself, determine whe ther equilibrium has been achieved at a 
given site. Agglutinate abundance, by contrast, can to some extent reflect regolith maturity, 
and equilibrium in this parameter is correspondingly more difficult to achieve (McKay et al., 
1983). 
From a morphological perspective, however, impact events exert simultaneous 
“agglutination” effects (forming irregular agglutinates and breccias), “fragmentation” effects 
(breaking down particles through high -energy impacts), and broad “ abrasion” effects 
(modifying particle morphologies by wearing down angular edges and other unstable surface 
features). Through repeated contact, displacement, and abrasion among particles, unstable 
structures are progressively worn away, and particles of a given type converge toward stable 
morphologies (for a detailed discussion of this mechanism, see our response to Reviewer #1, 
Comment 2). 
In our study, we classify particles as either “simple particles” (basalts, Hm, Mm and Lm) 
or “complex particles” (agglutinates and breccias), based on the presence or absence of internal 
aggregate structures. After extended gardening, both groups tend toward equilibrium 
morphologies: simple particles, despite mineralogical differences, converge toward similar 
shapes due to their internal simplicity ( Reply Fig.  1c), while complex particles achieve 
equilibrium at more irregular morphologies owing to their intricate internal structures ( Reply 
Fig. 1c). Importantly, the formation mechanisms driving this convergence are similar for both 
simple and complex particles; therefore, even though CE5 and CE6 regolith contain different 
proportions of particle types, their internal structures are fundamentally alike ( Reply Fig. 1d-
g), which in turn explains why stable morphologies are comparable across both sites despite 
differences in regolith composition. 
We have accordingly revised the manuscript to provide a clearer description of the 
mechanisms underlying saturation in morphological modification.
