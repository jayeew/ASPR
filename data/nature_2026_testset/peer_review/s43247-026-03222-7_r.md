REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript represents a comprehensive study that synthesizes numerous previous works on Arctic Ocean as well a
good example of fruitfull international cooperation. It utilizes an extensive database compiling multy-year measurements of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

gas emissions from the inner Laptev Sea region (MMH) and other areas, enabling the identification of diagnostic fields of
methane source endmembers. This data-driven approach enhances the reliability of modeling There is no doubt that using
large databases compiled from numerous publications is the only way to increase the reliability of modeling and subsequent
generalizations, even though this approach involves a high level of uncertainty, which is quite challenging to quantify. In this
regard, this paper is essential for understanding the current state of the Arctic methane cycle and for advancing our
knowledge further. The novelty of this work primarily lies in the integration and joint processing of such a vast amount of data
for the first time The second novelty of this work lies in the application of the triple isotope approach, extending beyond the
conventional dual-isotope methods originally developed by Michael Whiticar for elucidating methane genesis. This method
incorporates radiocarbon (14C) data to estimate the age of methane sources, while stable isotopes track fractionation
processes within the methane cycle. Keeling plots were used effectively to identify endmember isotope values by analyzing
the lower end of reversed mixing ratios, originally suggesting that mixing predominantly shapes the observed isotope
compositions. Crucially, defining the age of methane became key for identifying the main SPAM (Submarine Permafrost-
Associated Methane) endmember in the study. However, the radiocarbon scale inherently limits age detection to less than
approximately 50,000 years BP. For older methane, radiocarbon data tend to provide only a qualitative "yes/no" indication
rather than precise fingerprinting. This limitation does not reduce the value of the study but highlights a new challenge: better
separation of old methane sources, especially further splitting the FOTSEM (Fossil Thermogenic and Soil-Evolved Methane)
group into distinct constituents and quantifying their contributions. This issue is particularly relevant for the outer Laptev Sea
area, where thermogenic methane flux is believed to be dominant, suggesting potential for further refinement of source
apportionment in this region. 
The significance of permafrost (PF) as a global methane reservoir is undeniable, despite the wide variability in quantitative
estimates. PF acts both as a seal preventing upward gas migration and as a stabilizing environment for gas hydrates (GH),
which occur within or beneath it. Subsea permafrost (SPF) is rapidly degrading, irrespective of seasonal or climatic
variations. The formation of taliks indicates two critical processes: first, the release of methane from thawed PF; second, the
release of methane accumulated beneath the PF seal, including methane from GH. 
In our observations in the Kara Sea (Portnov et al., 2013; Serov et al., 2015; Semenov et al., 2019), we identified a similar
pattern characterized by increased gas migration and the formation of gas blowout structures beyond the proposed boundary
of subsea permafrost. The reviewed study represents the most detailed and reliable investigation of methane sources within
the water column across the subsea permafrost zone in the Arctic Ocean. Calculating atmospheric methane fluxes from
various sources using current data would be highly valuable, especially when compared to earlier estimates from the East
Siberian Shelf (Shakhova et al., 2013). Such comparisons are essential for refining our understanding of methane emissions
linked to subsea permafrost dynamics. 
he data processing methods are fully adequate and appear to be optimal for the study’s objectives. The use of isotope
geochemistry and statistical analysis relies on conventional, well-established techniques, ensuring robustness and
reliability. The manuscript is written clearly and accessibly, which is crucial for publication in a high-impact journal and for
engaging a broad audience. The figures are well-designed and easy to interpret. In particular, Figure 5, illustrating the
distribution of endmember fields along dual isotope scales, stands out as a valuable diagnostic tool for identifying methane
sources within the subsea permafrost zone. 
In the above text I tried to focus on topics covered in the article that seemed most important and interesting to me. Below I put
a few comments, take into account which - completely at the discretion of the authors. The very minor comments partly
coinciding with mentioned here can be found in the attached files of main manuscript (doc) and supplementary (pdf). 
1. The primary classification of methane in MMH into SPAM, FOPRIM, FOTSEM endmember pools was carried out based
on the number of parameters of molecular and isotope composition (14C; C1/C2+C3; d13CCH4, d13CO2; d2H etc.). The
procedure of selection was described in Supplementary 3. However, the certain sequence of sorting the samples would be
better to follow if drawn as flowchart or list (1,2,3), indicating the successive utilization the parameters listed above.
Considering the great importance of the samples selection for endmember appointment this will greatly facilitate visibility of
methodology 
2. In this work you examine water column only. Consequently, the gas mixture in the dissolved constituent of the total flow is
entering the water column after passing through a zone of anaerobic methane oxidation (AOM) in the upper sediments. This
process may be an additional factor of molecular and isotope fractionation of the initial hydrocarbon gas mixture and
methane respectively. Even though you only consider water and conclude the lack of the trends evident of aerobic methane
oxidation, I think it would be appropriate to mention AOM referring to local sulfate concentrations and probable potential of
AOM in methane cycle within the area of interest. 
3. This work is mostly devoted to SPF methane storage, consequently more ancient methane is less important here. At the
same time when you make a conclusion about not being able to separate thermogenic and secondary microbial in FOTSEM
I think it would be appropriate if you mention the possible ways to overcome this uncertainty for future works

I am listing a few examples here: 
Lines275-295 discussed the possible younger CH4 endmember to be from early diagenesis of shallow sediments, and

 Supplementary methods 
 Procedures for sampling seawater methane for concentration and isotopic 
analyses 
A Niskin Rosette, deployed from the research vessel, was used to sample seawater for onboard CH4 
concentration and onshore stable isotope and radiocarbon analysis. The 10 L Niskin bottles were 
individually filled at specific depths using an electrical triggering system. Silicon tubing was used for 
the subsampling from the Niskin bottles into either plastic 60 mL syringes for CH4 concentration 
measurements, 120-160 mL serum flasks for stable isotope analysis, or 30 L stainless steel kegs for 
radiocarbon analysis. After procedures of rinsing, filling, and overflowing to avoid a gas headspace, 
each serum flask or keg was dosed with respectively 0.5 mL or 200 mL 2000 g/L ZnCl2 solution to 
preserve the CH4 during storage. Serum flasks were sealed with 10 mm thick bromobutyl rubber 
stoppers and stored at approximately 4 ⁰C before the stable isotope composition was measured.   
 Methane concentration measurements 
Methane concentrations were measured onboard using plastic syringes filled with seawater and 
fitted with luer-lock 3-way stopcocks, and largely similar to earlier described methods1. A seawater 
volume of 35-50 mL was equilibrated with 10-15 mL of He gas at atmospheric pressure. The syringes 
were thereafter shaken at 500 rpm on a shaker table for 30 min, followed by 30 min resting at room 
temperature. The headspace gas was then transferred to a 10 mL syringe and consequently analyzed 
using gas-chromatography flame-ionization detection (GC-FID) by injecting 5 mL into a 1 mL sample 
loop connected to the GC column. The instrument was calibrated every 20 samples using 3-5 
different CH4 standards (ranging from 1.63 to 1067 ppm). In 2020, seawater volume, gas volume, CH4 
concentration in the gas phase, and Henry’s law adjusted to the sample's temperature were used to 
calculate the dissolved CH4 concentration. In 2016, 2018, and 2019, seawater volume, gas volume, 
CH4 concentration in the gas phase, and the seawater temperature and salinity were used to 
calculate the dissolved CH4 concentration using equilibrium solubility equations by Wiesenburg & 
Guinasso2. 
 Methane stable isotope analysis 
Serum flasks filled with seawater were onshore prepared for CH4 stable isotope analysis. For the 
samples collected in 2020, 30 mL He headspaces were created in the 160 mL flasks by using two 60 
mL plastic syringes fitted with 3-way stopcocks and hypodermic needles. After rinsing and filling one 
of the syringes with He (Grade 5.0), 30 mL seawater was replaced with He by pressing gas into the 
serum flask while simultaneously aspirating seawater into the other syringe. The flasks with He 
headspace were gently shaken on a shaker table for 1 h followed by 1 h of rest at room temperature. 
For the samples of 2016, 2018, and 2019, a headspace extraction (90-100 mL seawater, 20-30 mL 
He) was conducted before sample transport to Stockholm University. The resulting gas-filled ~23 mL 
glass vials with headspace gas and ~3 mL of saturated NaCl solution were stored upside down until 
analysis. 
The headspace gases from either the glass vials (2016, 2018, and 2019 samples) or serum flasks 
(2020 samples) were subsampled into a modified preconcentration device (ThermoFisher PreCon) 
and analyzed using gas-chromatography continuous-flow isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (GC-CF-
IRMS; ThermoFisher Delta V Plus IRMS with a ConFlo IV and GC Isolink II interface, combined with a 
Trace GC 1300) at SU. Subsamples of headspace gas were retrieved by adding a specific amount of 
He (Grade 5.0) to the headspace gas and taking the same amount slowly out of the vial/flask, i.e., by 
gas replacement. The amount of gas replacement was adjusted based on the known concentrations 
in the headspace gas and the optimal detection range for CH4 by the GC-CF-IRMS, i.e., within the

range where linearity of the isotopic signal versus sample size was confirmed. Some low-
concentration samples had to be retrieved from the vial/flask by displacement with NaCl solution to 
meet the instrument's limit of quantification. 
The modified PreCon largely resembled the setup by Rice et al.3 but with some modifications. Firstly, 
Liquid Nitrogen (LN2) was used for all three traps of the PreCon. Secondly, the third trap (cryo-
focusing stage of sample CH4) was followed by a 5 m length of PoraPLOT Q column (0.32 mm inner 
diameter; Agilent J&W GC Columns) cooled by dry ice (-78 °C) to separate CH4 from traces of N2 and 
O2. Finally, a fourth trap (30 cm length, inner diameter 0.32 mm PoraPLOT Q column) cooled by LN2 
was included as an additional cryo-focusing stage to counter peak broadening in the GC column, 
after which the methane pulse was released to the GC Isolink system. 
We used two stable isotope standards of 1.85 ppm CH4 in synthetic air with different isotope 
compositions (δ2H: -63 and -240 ‰, δ13C: -48.4 and -68.6 ‰; obtained from AirLiquide). We 
corrected our stable isotope measurements for mass (sample size) dependence of the measured 
δ13C and δ2H values, instrument drift over time, and isotope scale stretch/compression, in this order. 
As part of each measurement series (~8-16 samples), pure CH4 was injected six times at four 
different signal intensities by manipulating the GC injection split flow rate. This bypassed the PreCon 
and was used to check and correct for any mass dependence of the measured δ13C and δ2H values. 
One of the calibration standards was injected after every 6-8 samples and after finishing a 
measurement series to correct for instrument drift during a measurement series. To correct for the 
isotopic stretch, both isotope standards were each thrice measured at the start of the measurement 
series. Every 5th measurement day, a headspace sample was measured in 3-5 replicates to assess 
precision and ensure correct injection procedures. We observed a precision of 0.1-0.2‰ for δ13C and 
2‰ for δ2H. Replicate samples were analyzed for drift in δ13C during sample storage, and no 
significant systematic change was found over the course of three years (observed average shift was 
+0.1 ± 0.8‰, 1 σ, n=9).

Samples for radiocarbon analysis were sampled and prepared for radiocarbon analysis as described

 Description of endmember FOTSEM 
Fossil Thermogenic and Secondary Microbial (FOTSEM) gas is the endmember category that 
incorporates all forms of FOTSEM gas formed in the past below the region of subsea permafrost 
(SSPF) and currently present in natural gas reservoirs or hydrates below or potentially within SSPF. 
These fossil gases have no detectable 14C signal (14C age > 50000 y BP). 
Thermogenic gas is a hydrocarbon-rich gas formed by the thermal cracking (catagenesis) of oil or 
kerogen derived from organic matter6. Catagenesis occurs in deep sedimentary basins at a pressure 
of 300-1500 bars and a temperature of 50-200 ⁰C6,7. A sedimentary basin contains sedimentary rock 
in which the gas is formed7,8. After formation, this gas can stay in the source rock, move to a 
reservoir rock, accumulate below a cap of permafrost (PF)9, get incorporated in PF10,11, and when the 
physical conditions are met, turn into a hydrate. 
Secondary microbial gas is formed by the biodegradation of thermogenic petroleum, especially oil, in 
relatively cold (<70-90 ⁰C) reservoirs and has been shown to be present in Siberia12,13. As it is formed 
from thermogenic petroleum, it is expected to migrate similarly to thermogenic methane and mix 
with it. Furthermore, the stable isotope composition of thermogenic and secondary microbial gases 
largely overlaps14. Therefore, thermogenic and secondary microbial gases are grouped into one 
category of FOTSEM gas. 
We here use a global database of natural gas15,16 as a basis for constructing the endmember FOTSEM 
as an even more complete database14 was not available. Gas samples were first geographically 
restricted to Russia, Kazakhstan, Japan, China, Europe, Canada, and Alaska to include all Arctic 
regions and regions bordering Russia. Thereafter, gas samples were categorized as Fossil Primary 
Microbial (FOPRIM) or FOTSEM natural gas based on the presence of longer hydrocarbons (C2 and 
C3) and the δ13CCO2 value in the gas mixture.  
All samples with reported C1/C2, C1/(C2+C3), or C1/C2+ ratios were considered. When data showed 
a C1/(C2+C3) ratio smaller than 200, the sample was considered to contain dominantly FOTSEM 
methane and was categorized as such14. When the C1/(C2+C3) ratio was larger than 200, the sample 
was classified as FOPRIM or FOTSEM gas based on the measured δ13CCH4 value14. As primary 
microbial reservoir gases are predominantly formed by CO2 reduction and not fermentation14,17, only 
the CO2 reduction pathway was considered to classify FOPRIM gas for the natural gas reservoir 
samples. Furthermore, when no C1/(C2+C3) was reported, but a C1/C2+ was, this value was used as 
the best estimate of C1/(C2+C3). When also no C1/C2+ was reported either, the C1/C2 ratio was 
used as the best estimate of the C1/(C2+C3) ratio. 
After categorization based on C1/(C2+C3) ratios, all samples with reported δ13CCO2 and δ13CCH4 values 
were additionally considered. When the isotopic signatures uniquely identified FOTSEM or FOPRIM 
(as formed by CO2 reduction) gas when using the dual δ13CCO2 and δ13CCH4 plot14, the stable isotope 
composition of the sample CH4 was included in the endmember database. If no unique identification 
of either FOPRIM or FOTSEM gas was possible, the sample was excluded.

 Description of endmember FOPRIM 
Fossil Primary Microbial (FOPRIM) gas is the endmember category that incorporates all forms of 
FOPRIM gas formed in the past below (subsea) permafrost and nowadays present in preformed gas 
reservoirs or hydrates below or possibly within the current extent of subsea permafrost (SSPF). 
Specifically, FOPRIM gas is formed in the layer below the depth range of material accumulated 
during the most recent periods of permafrost existence from several million years BP onwards24. This 
means that FORPIM gas is fossil gas having no detectable 14C signal (14C age > 50000 y BP). 
Primary microbial gas is formed in sediments or sedimentary rocks by microbial conversion of 
organic matter (OM) at relatively low temperatures (up to 60-80 ⁰C)7,25,26, optimum range of 35-45 
°C27. It is typically formed up to depths of ~1 km while possibly buried deeper after formation25,27, 
but can also be formed deeper up to ~3 km26. It can be formed in various depositional settings, 
among which important ones are deltas, deep water clastic sediments, shelves, coal swamps, and 
coastal plains27. Several conditions are important for the generation and accumulation of FOPRIM 
gas27: anoxic environment, low-sulfate environment, abundant organic matter, enough pore space 
for microbes to function, a relatively rapid deposition rate of 200-2000 m/Ma, and timely formation 
of a seal or trap (e.g., rocks, organic-rich shales, carbonate cement, or hydrates). Most ancient 
primary microbial gas formations were formed during the Cretaceous or younger, while some 
formed during the Jurassic and Carboniferous27. Accumulations of primary microbial gas are 
predominantly formed by the CO2 reduction pathway14,17,27. Like Fossil Thermogenic and Secondary 
Microbial (FOTSEM) gases, FOPRIM gas can migrate away from the formation location and 
accumulate elsewhere26 in another accumulation positioned below permafrost (PF), get trapped 
below/within PF, or turn into a hydrate. Globally, about 20% of the world's natural gas reserves have 
a primary microbial origin, of which more than half (~11-13·1012 m3) is located in Siberia12,25,27. 
There are several differences between the two microbial endmembers (Subsea-Permafrost 
Associated Methane (SPAM) and FOPRIM. Firstly, there is a difference in timing. While SPAM is 
formed from OM located in the current upper region of SSPF (relatively young OM, up to several 
million years BP), FOPRIM is formed from OM of much older eras (up to several 100s million years 
BP). Therefore, there are likely differences in both OM and microbial communities. Secondly, while 
SPAM is formed at temperatures close to 0 ⁰C, FOPRIM gas generally forms at much higher 
temperatures (up to 80 ⁰C). This likely affects the microbial degradation process and the extent of 
fractionation between substrates and CH4, which is more apparent at colder temperatures. Thirdly, 
FOPRIM is formed by the CO2 reduction pathway until deep environments (1-3 km) using older and 
more degraded OM, i.e., the aging effect17. At the same time, SPAM is formed from fresher OM 
followed by trapping in PF layers and might include microbial CH4 formed by fermentation, as such 
shallow-formed CH4 might also get trapped in the frozen environments and not wholly lost to the 
atmosphere after formation. Fourthly, during burial, sediments get dewatered25,28. Both less 
available water and a larger fraction of the water already used will enrich the δ2H of the remaining 
water available for degradation of OM and thus enrich the δ2H of CH4 in FOPRIM gas compared to 
SPAM.

hydrocarbons (C2 and C3) and the δ13CCO2 value in the gas mixture.

 Description of endmember SPAM 
Subsea Permafrost-Associated Methane (SPAM) is the endmember category describing microbially 
produced CH4 from organic matter (OM) that was accumulated during the past several million years 
of permafrost existence, before freezing, during freezing, or after thawing. Therefore, this category 
comprises the release of preformed CH4 trapped by subsea permafrost (SSPF) and CH4 produced in 
situ upon thawing. Preformed CH4 in permafrost is primarily formed under nonfrozen conditions, 
followed by conservation by freezing or trapping between frozen layers41–44. Nonetheless, the 
formation of CH4 can occur in situ under sub-zero conditions42,43,45–49 and contribute to the pool of 
preformed CH4 in SSPF. At many locations, preformed CH4 in permafrost (PF) has been observed, 
either in PF cores43,49–57, or by gas blowouts from disturbed PF10,11,44,58. Furthermore, upon thawing, 
OM in SSPF is prone to additional degradation and formation of CH459. The endmember category 
SPAM thus includes both preformed CH4 in SSPF and actively produced CH4 from thawing SSPF. 
Release of SPAM would, in contrast to the other two endmember categories, per definition, be 
associated with SSPF degradation. 
The stable isotope composition of the endmember category SPAM is approached in two ways. In 
method A, existing stable isotope values of CH4 in PF are compiled. Method B uses observed stable 
isotope values of CH4 in gas bubbles released in lakes/ponds in Northern Hemisphere PF regions.  
Method A represents the stable isotope composition of preformed CH4 in PF, formed before or 
during freezing. A complication of this method, which uses CH4 in PF data directly, is that performed 
CH4 in PF may contain CH4 originating from deeper sources of fossil gas10,11,60–63. Therefore, this 
approach might include CH4 which is not purely microbially produced from PF OM. Nevertheless, as 
shown below, most preformed CH4 in PF is microbial and, more importantly, represents directly the 
type of SPAM that can be released when SSPF degrades. 
Method B approaches the stable isotope composition of CH4 released from thawing SSPF. It is 
important to note that this method, using stable isotope data of CH4 bubbles emitted from PF 
lakes/ponds, also has some drawbacks. Firstly, CH4 in gas bubbles released in such lakes/ponds may 
not wholly represent CH4 formed from SSPF OM. The latter is considered to be formed by old 
microbial communities using old OM from the Pleistocene epoch up to ~10 ky BP, which is the time 
of inundation of our study area64. In contrast, the gas bubbles in permafrost lakes/ponds may emit 
CH4 produced by younger freshwater communities and include recently accumulated and less 
degraded OM of a different origin. Nonetheless, there is evidence of significant degradation of 
Pleistocene OM in several PF lakes31,65–69. Consequently, the old OM may contribute substantially to 
the produced CH4 in permafrost lakes. Although archaeal communities might differ between subsea 
and terrestrial permafrost49, the origin of SSPF is for a large part terrestrial, affirmed by the stable 
isotope composition of water within SSPF70. This suggests parallels in OM and microbial communities 
between microbial CH4 production in SSPF and permafrost lakes/ponds. Secondly, interglacial 
periods during the Pleistocene epoch may have contributed layers of microbial communities and OM 
from marine environments to the SSPF. However, the periods of inundation during deglaciations are 
a minor part of the total period of SSPF formation71. Furthermore, back to ~100-110 ky BP, our study 
area of ~20-25 m sea depth was not inundated72. Given that the potential release of SPAM is 
expected to happen from the SSPF table downward and the SSPF table is expected to be younger 
than 100-110 ky BP59, the contribution of marine materials to SPAM is expected to be minor, 
resulting in a dominantly terrestrial character of SPAM.

Reviewer #1  
 
The manuscript represents a comprehensive study that synthesizes numerous previous works on 
Arctic Ocean as well a good example of fruitful international cooperation. It utilizes an extensive 
database compiling multi-year measurements of gas emissions from the inner Laptev Sea region 
(MMH) and other areas, enabling the identification of diagnostic fields of methane source 
endmembers. This data-driven approach enhances the reliability of modelling. There is no doubt that 
using large databases compiled from numerous publications is the only way to increase the reliability 
of modeling and subsequent generalizations, even though this approach involves a high level of 
uncertainty, which is quite challenging to quantify. In this regard, this paper is essential for 
understanding the current state of the Arctic methane cycle and for advancing our knowledge 
further. The novelty of this work primarily lies in the integration and joint processing of such a vast 
amount of data for the first time. 
The second novelty of this work lies in the application of the triple isotope approach, extending 
beyond the conventional dual-isotope methods originally developed by Michael Whiticar for 
elucidating methane genesis. This method incorporates radiocarbon (14C) data to estimate the age 
of methane sources, while stable isotopes track fractionation processes within the methane cycle. 
Keeling plots were used effectively to identify endmember isotope values by analyzing the lower end 
of reversed mixing ratios, originally suggesting that mixing predominantly shapes the observed 
isotope compositions. Crucially, defining the age of methane became key for identifying the main 
SPAM (Submarine Permafrost-Associated Methane) endmember in the study. However, the 
radiocarbon scale inherently limits age detection to less than approximately 50,000 years BP. For 
older methane, radiocarbon data tend to provide only a qualitative "yes/no" indication rather than 
precise fingerprinting. This limitation does not reduce the value of the study but highlights a new 
challenge: better separation of old methane sources, especially further splitting the FOTSEM (Fossil 
Thermogenic and Soil-Evolved Methane) group into distinct constituents and quantifying their 
contributions. This issue is particularly relevant for the outer Laptev Sea area, where thermogenic 
methane flux is believed to be dominant, suggesting potential for further refinement of source 
apportionment in this region. 
The significance of permafrost (PF) as a global methane reservoir is undeniable, despite the wide 
variability in quantitative estimates. PF acts both as a seal preventing upward gas migration and as a 
stabilizing environment for gas hydrates (GH), which occur within or beneath it. Subsea permafrost 
(SPF) is rapidly degrading, irrespective of seasonal or climatic variations. The formation of taliks 
indicates two critical processes: first, the release of methane from thawed PF; second, the release of 
methane accumulated beneath the PF seal, including methane from GH.  
In our observations in the Kara Sea (Portnov et al., 2013; Serov et al., 2015; Semenov et al., 2019), we

A common way to separate thermogenic from secondary microbial methane is by analyzing the 
δ13CCO2 of the fossil gas. When petroleum is biodegraded to CO2 and this CO2 is progressively 
converted to CH4, the remaining δ13CCO2 becomes more enriched in 13C.2,3 Furthermore, secondary 
microbial methane is characterized by high C1/(C2+C3) and the presence of biodegraded oil and 
gas.2,3 However, when dissolved methane is considered, the use of C1/(C2+C3) ratios and δ13CCO2 
values is not recommended. Firstly, the dissolved phase will predominantly contain CO2 from 
different origins, making the use of δ13CCO2 data impossible to use to constrain methane sources. 
Furthermore, the observed C1/(C2+C3) ratios in the dissolved phase will be affected by molecule-
specific chemical and physical interactions during transport to the water column and within the 
water column itself (e.g., solubility, oxidation). If one could retrieve gas and/or oil samples from 
underlying reserves directly, one could possibly use C1/(C2+C3) ratios, δ13CCO2 data, and petroleum 
biodegradation proxies to determine the presence of secondary microbial gas. We think that entirely 
different sample types would be necessary in future studies to separate between fossil thermogenic 
and secondary microbial gas.
