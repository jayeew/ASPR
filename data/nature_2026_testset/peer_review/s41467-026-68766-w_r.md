Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Zhu and colleagues investigate the role of Amsh glia in thermosensation in C. elegans, focusing on their
functional interactions with two neuronal pairs: the thermosensory AFD neurons and the nociceptive ASH neurons. Using
calcium imaging, behavioral assays, and fluorescence microscopy, the authors demonstrate that Amsh glia respond to both
heat and cold stimuli through two distinct molecular pathways: one involving the guanylyl cyclase receptor GCY-28 for heat,
and the other involving the glutamate receptor GLR-3 for cold. Activation of these pathways leads to GABA release from
Amsh glia, which in turn activates distinct downstream receptors in the AFD and ASH neurons. Importantly, these glia-to-
neuron signaling mechanisms significantly influence behavioral responses to temperature. The authors propose a “Hub-
and-Spoke” model, positioning glial cells as central regulators in thermosensory circuits. This study expands our
understanding of glial functions within neuronal networks and contributes new insights into the mechanisms of
thermosensation. Overall, the data are generally well controlled, but several key points need to be addressed. 
Major Points: 
1. Calcium Transient Dynamics: 
The manuscript shows that cooling triggers a single calcium transient, while heating induces multiple transients. Greater
clarity is needed here. 
a. How many calcium transients are typically induced by heat? 
b. Do different mutants show variations in the number of transients? 
c. Are these peaks random, or do they follow a defined temporal pattern? 
2. PDE Quadruple Mutant Effects: 
The PDE quadruple mutant shows only a modest effect on heat-induced calcium transients, with no statistically significant
difference in peak amplitude compared to wild-type. Given that only 7 cells were tested, can the authors increase the sample
size? Additionally, would it be possible to test pde-4, since some cGMP-gated channels can also be regulated by cAMP? 
3. Decay Time in osm-9 Mutants: 
The decay time of calcium transients in osm-9 mutants should be quantified and compared to wild-type to better understand
the kinetics of the response. 
4. Role of osm-9 in Heat Response: 
Is there any evidence that osm-9 contributes to heat-induced responses? 
5. Histamine Concentration Usage: 
The rationale behind using 2 mM histamine in some experiments and 10 mM in others should be clarified. 
6. Survival vs. Sensory Impairment: 
Figure 2F shows that gcy-28 is required in Amsh glia for thermosensation, and Figure 4F shows that gcy-28 mutants have
reduced survival. However, previous studies (e.g., PMID: 10617200) indicate that sensory-deficient mutants often live
longer. The authors should address this apparent discrepancy in the discussion. 
Minor Points: 
1. Please comment on the observation that expression of HisCl in Amsh glia has an effect on its own (Figure 4B). 
2. Figure 4J (right panel): The x-axis labels “24” and “26” are positioned above the axis line and should be corrected. 
3. Line 38: Change “acts a homeostatic gain control mechanism” to “acts as a homeostatic gain control mechanism.” 
4. Line 152: Change “guanylyl cyclase might be responsible” to “guanylyl cyclases might be responsible.” 
5. Line 160: Change “as previous study described” to “as a previous study described.” 
6. Figures 2C, F, and H show signs of photobleaching. It may be helpful to apply photobleaching correction to improve
signal clarity.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript investigates a novel and potentially transformative concept: that glial cells could function as dual-mode
thermosensors and modulators of temperature-related neural circuit activity in C. elegans. The authors propose that AMsh
glia are capable of autonomous thermosensation via co-expression of GCY-28 (a heat receptor) and GLR-3 (a cold
receptor), and that they release GABA to bidirectionally regulate warmth and cold-responsive neurons, namely AFD and
ASH, respectively. 
If fully supported by robust experimental evidence, the central message—that glia act as both sensors and modulators in
thermosensory circuits—would represent an exciting advance for the field. However, at present, many of the key mechanistic
claims, particularly those regarding cell-autonomous glial thermosensation and downstream circuit interactions, are only
partially supported by the data presented. Many of the conclusions rely on correlative or indirect evidence, and essential
cell-specific controls or pathway-dissection experiments are missing. 
The claims regarding cellular autonomy, sensor identity, and signaling pathways remain preliminary, and require substantial
additional experimentation to be convincingly established. Therefore, without substantial work to address the concerns
below, I cannot recommend publication at this stage. 
Major Points: 
1) The current experimental design does not conclusively demonstrate cell-autonomous temperature sensing by AMsh glia.
The observed delay in heat-evoked calcium responses raises the possibility that these are secondary to inputs from
neighboring thermosensory neurons. The recording in unc-13 and unc-31 mutants are not sufficient. Transmission of
thermosensory ‘signals’ could indeed be caused e.g. either directly via innexins (in particular hemichannels) transmitting
chemical messenger or indirectly via a modulation of the ionic composition of the extracellular medium connecting AMsh
glia and a variety of thermosensory neurons: AFD, ASH, but also others like AWC and ASER (which intriguingly express
GCY-28 and GLR-3, respectively, and are actually ideally placed to trigger response in nearby glial cells). Suggested
experiment: Examine AMsh responses in conditions lacking thermosensory neuron functon(e.g., broadly-acting che
mutation), or, better, in isolated conditions ex vivo. 
2) The significant expression of vap-1 in AWC, even if lower than in glial cells and even potentially undetectable with
fluorescent reporter, raises a concern that the observed rescue may be attributed to leaky expression in this neighboring
neuron (potentially others). The observed rescue could potentially indeed arise from off-target expression in adjacent
neurons. 
This impact all the experiments relying on the use of the vap-1 promoter for glial-specific expression. Suggested
solution: Employ a promoter with no detectable expression in neighboring sensory neurons to validate AMsh-specific
rescue. 
3) Missing data (in Fig. 2): Traces for gcy-8/18/23 triple mutants are not shown, making it difficult to interpret their role. 
4) The cell-specific expression of gcy-28 in AWB, intended to establish GCY-28’s role as a thermosensor, is nice but too
weak to make a strong case. A key surprising point is that the thermal upstep triggers a AWB calcium decline. This decline is
prevented when GCY-28 is expressed in AWB. This is not really a case where GCY-28 can “confer thermo-sensitivity”.
Instead, the data just show that GCY-28 overexpression block the cool evoked calcium decline in AWB. And this might be
interpreted very differently. 
Indeed, it could be that the constitutive elevation of cGMP production (via over-expression of a
protein that is not normally their and whose activity might be out of control), might cause cGMP saturation to a level where it
is very difficult to close CNG channels and then prevent the natural heat-evoked calcium reduction in AWB. Therefore, these
results can be explained even if GCY-28 is not a molecular thermosensor. Suggested experiment: Express GCY-28 in cell or
tissue that do not react to temperature, e.g., in cell line as used in previous studies (PMC6743979). 
5) No data supports that GLR-3 act as a cold sensor in AMsh, despite being a main claim of the paper. Suggested
experiments: 
Perform a glr-3 cell-specific rescue in AMsh to test for functional sufficiency. Perform cell-specific knock-down.
Verify cold-responsiveness of AMsh in ASER-ablated animals. 
6) Likewise, we have strictly no reason to believe that ITR-1 works cell-autonomously in glial cells, nor is the downstream
relationship between GLR-3 and ITR-1 adequately established. Suggested experiment: Conduct epistasis analyses using
glr-3; itr-1 double mutants to test for genetic interaction. 
Conduct AMsh-specific knock out of ITR-1 (e.g. using existing floxed
alleles of ITR-1 (PMC11087772) 
7) Missing data in Figure 4: The manuscript lacks a heat tolerance assay under conditions where AMsh activity is
suppressed, analogous to the cold tolerance assays shown. 
8) The calcium responses in AFD neurons with AMsh-specific gcy-28 rescue appear unusually elevated in Figure 5, raising
concerns about sensor calibration. Suggested control: Include an internal RFP control to normalize GCaMP and validate
calcium imaging signals. 
9) The reported ASH response defects in unc-25 and lgc-38 mutants (in Fig. 6) do not directly link to AMsh function.
Suggested experiments: To fully substantiate the proposed model, checking the ASH response of animals with cell specific
rescue of unc-25 and lgc-38 in AMsh and ASH respectively would be necessary. 
10) Similarly, to properly support the proposed model, the authors should also assess the AFD response in animals with
AFD-specific rescue of exp-1 to validate the proposed excitatory glia-to-neuron signaling. 
11) One general weakness of the behavioral analyses is that it is hard to relate the behavioral phenotypes (often extremely
mild, e.g, in thermal gradient) with the proposed inter-cellular mechanisms. A much deeper discussion is needed here,
before one can correctly judge of their relevance.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

12) The introduction focuses on the key cellular players under study. However, this seems too focused and cursory. It is
important for the large audience of this journal to have a broader view of what is known in C. elegans. In particular,
additional thermosensory neurons must be presented in more details, e.g. ASER, AWC, because ultimately, they might be
the ones triggering the apparent thermal responsiveness of AMsh glia. Likewise, a better introduction of the molecular
thermosensors is needed. 
Conclusion: 
This study proposes an intriguing and potentially field-shifting role for glia in thermosensory processing. However, the
current data fall short of definitively demonstrating most of the manuscript’s key mechanistic claims. Strengthening the
experimental evidence—particularly through rigorous cell-type-specific manipulations, genetic dissection, and assessment
of the new proposed thermosensor will be essential for the work to become convincing. I encourage the authors to pursue
these additional experiments, which will help clarify and validate the central hypothesis and significantly enhance the
manuscript’s contribution to the field. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
C. elegans AMsh glia associate with multiple sensory neurons, including the thermosensory AFD, and modulate multiple
sensory behaviors and sensory neuron properties. Building on this prior research, Zhu et al aim to define how glia do so.
They report that AMsh glia co-express GCY-28 (which acts as a heat receptor) and GLR-3 (which acts as a cold receptor),
and have calcium changes in response to noxious thermal changes. Through functional imaging and behavior studies,
authors propose that glial calcium activity drives ER calcium and GABA release, which bidirectionally impacts AFD and
ASH neurons in dictating animal thermal preferences. They conclude that this GABAergic hub-and-spoke architecture
allows 
homeostatic weighting of environmental temperature inputs. 
Overall, this study tackles an exciting and open question – glial roles in neural circuit processing. However, while the results
and model are intriguing, significant technical and logical concerns are noted. Thus, major revisions are recommended
before this manuscript can be supported for publication. 
Major comments: 
1. A key conclusion is that AMsh glial cGMP triggers calcium dynamics, partly based on blue-light triggered guanylyl cyclase
BlgC (Fig 2). This is tricky because multiple amphid neurons respond to blue light (PMID: 25640076). Additional controls are
needed to strengthen this conclusion. At minimum: 
a. key experiments should be replicated in lite-1; gur-3 mutants (see for e.g. PMID: 38194919, 27459302) 
b. traces with blue light stimulation without BlgC need to be documented. 
c. AFD should be chronically activated, and glial responses recorded. 
2. The authors use vap-1 promoter for AMsh glial studies. (PMID: 18974354) noted faint expression in AFD, a relevant cell-
type for this study’s conclusions. To address concerns for the central conclusions of this study, authors should 
a. detail which promoter fragment was used (in Methods) 
b. verify with another promoter (esp. BlgC & HisCl baseline, gcy-28 rescue). 
3. The authors report identifying GCY-28 from single cell sequencing (Line 153). Data for this key conclusion should be
either reported or cited. 
4. Many edits are needed for prior published literature to be cited, discussed or interpreted correctly. Some examples: 
a. Line 90 and Line 332. Authors state that AMsh glia were “hypothesized to serve primarily..”. However, multiple prior
studies (see PMID: 38565269 for recent review), including work cited here (Ray et al) from multiple labs including the
authors has already demonstrated that AMsh glia are not passive support, but active participants in different sensory
processing. This can be stated without detracting from the current study. 
b. Line 197-199. ..t is incorrect that the studies referenced 
reported “embryonic or larval stages” or “developmental deficits”.
This is incorrect because these studies imply post-developmental effect of glia on sensory neuron properties and animal
behavior. In fact, one of the few embryonic AMsh glial promoters (Pvap-1) PMID: 15935778) is used in THIS study (Line 200-
202), but not the others referenced! 
c. Line 332. Studies in (PMID: 22298710, 21350017) have shown AMsh temperature-dependent expression of ver-1 is
independent of AFD or sensory neuron activity i.e. AMsh glia are thermosensors. These should be referenced. 
d. AMsh glia engulf gcy- laden AFD membranes, and ciliated amphid neuron EVs (PMID: 33759761, 34533135); and AFD
engulfment was shown to impact thermosensory animal behavior (PMID: 33759761). Given that this study invokes AMsh
glial gcy function, this should be at least discussed as cGMP/gcy source. 
5. Figs. 1 & 2 do not adequately support the conclusion that glia independently detect temperature. To demonstrate this
conclusively, authors need to report on AMsh glial calcium dynamics in response to temperature in AFD and ASH ablated
animals. This is because it is also possible that glia have secondary bystander response to neuron activity, with AFD/ASH
as the sensors (see also PMID: 16837592). 
6. unc-13 does not affect AMsh glial calcium dynamics (Fig 1) but reduces AFD response (Fig 6). As stated, it seems the
authors imply that glial GABA release is via UNC-13 regulated neurotransmitters (Line 310-312). For this conclusion,
authors need to evaluate glial and pan-neuronal UNC-13 rescue. Also discuss altered AMsh glial response kinetics in these
mutants, which suggests neuronal input is relevant.

We deeply appreciate the constructive feedback provided by the reviewers. All 
raised points have been addressed in the revised manuscript, which integrates 
the requested experiments alongside updated text and figures. The added 
experiments conducted as suggested by the reviewers not only validate the 
interpretations presented in our original submission but also strengthen the 
study by providing substantial additional experimental data and deeper

Below is our point-by-point response to the reviewers' comments:
Reviewer #1 
1. Calcium Transient Dynamics: The manuscript shows that cooling triggers a 
single calcium transient, while heating induces multiple transients. Greater 
clarity is needed here. a. How many calcium transients are typically induced by 
heat? b. Do different mutants show variations in the number of transients? c. 
Are these peaks random, or do they follow a defined temporal pattern? 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s comment. While cooling triggers a 
single calcium transient, our results as shown in Fig. 1b, 1d and 2e-h 
demonstrate that heating also induces only a single calcium transient in most 
AMsh glial cells.
2. PDE Quadruple Mutant Effects: The PDE quadruple mutant shows only a 
modest effect on heat-induced calcium transients, with no statistically 
significant difference in peak amplitude compared to wild-type. Given that only 
7 cells were tested, can the authors increase the sample size? Additionally, 
would it be possible to test pde-4, since some cGMP-gated channels can also be 
regulated by cAMP? 
Response: As suggested by the reviewer, we expanded the sample size for 
heat-induced AMsh glial calcium transient analyses in PDE quadruple mutant 
worms (Fig. 2c-e). Our data demonstrate that the peak amplitude of these 
transients in the quadruple mutants was significantly higher than in wild-type, 
suggesting cGMP regulates heat-induced calcium transients in AMsh glia. We 
also tested pde-4;pde-6 double mutants (genes required for C. elegans cAMP 
hydrolysis) and found no increase in the peak amplitude of heat-induced 
calcium transients in AMsh glia (Fig. 2c-e). These results align with our model 
that cGMP signaling is involved in AMsh glial heat sensing.
3. Decay Time in osm-9 Mutants: The decay time of calcium transients in osm-9 
mutants should be quantified and compared to wild-type to better understand 
the kinetics of the response. 
4. Role of osm-9 in Heat Response: Is there any evidence that osm-9 contributes 
to heat-induced responses? 
Response: We have carefully considered potential explanations for the kinetic 
differences between osm-9 mutants and wild-type (WT). However, we noticed

that the data pertaining to osm-9 has relatively low relevance to other 
experiments in the study and thus stands as an isolated component. Therefore, 
we have decided to remove this section in the revised manuscript. This 
adjustment aims to better streamline the narrative and ensure the focus of the 
study remains concentrated on its core themes.
5. Histamine Concentration Usage: The rationale behind using 2 mM histamine 
in some experiments and 10 mM in others should be clarified. 
Response: As previously reported by Navin et al. (PNAS, 2014; PMID: 
24550306) and in our prior study (Duan et al., Neuron, 2020; PMID:32970991), 
10 mM histamine induces paralysis within two minutes in worms with pan-
neuronal expression of HisCl1. Therefore, for most experiments, where each 
worm is typically exposed for 5–15 minutes, we used 10 mM histamine to 
achieve optimal effects. However, in TTX experiments and cold/heat tolerances 
assays, worms require prolonged exposure to histamine. The total duration, 
from washing to distribution on TTX plates, is approximately 70 minutes for TTX 
experiments, and ranges from several hours to 72 hours for cold/heat 
tolerances assays. To minimize potential adverse effects of long-term histamine 
exposure, we employed 2 mM histamine in these specific assays. This detail 
has been clarified in the revised manuscript for accuracy (Line 577).
6. Survival vs. Sensory Impairment: Figure 2F shows that gcy-28 is required in 
Amsh glia for thermosensation, and Figure 4F shows that gcy-28 mutants have 
reduced survival. However, previous studies (e.g., PMID: 10617200) indicate that 
sensory-deficient mutants often live longer. The authors should address this 
apparent discrepancy in the discussion. 
Response: We appreciate this valuable suggestion. We have accordingly 
clarified and discussed this issue in the revised manuscript (line 301-307).
Minor Points: 
1. Please comment on the observation that expression of HisCl in Amsh glia has 
an effect on its own (Figure 4B). 
Response: Thanks for this insightful comment. We propose several potential 
explanations for this observation. First, it is not uncommon for the heterologous 
overexpression of proteins, particularly ion channels, to exert some degree of 
non-specific side effects in host cells. Second, prior studies have indicated that 
overexpressed HisCl1 may exhibit small basal (histamine-independent) leak 
chloride currents (Li et al, Cell Reports 2024, PMID: 38294902), which could 
account for the observed effect in the absence of exogenous histamine. In this 
study, we deliberately selected HisCl1 over HisCl2, as the former has a well-
documented lower basal activity and an overall safety profile in numerous
