Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zhang et al. used magnetic tweezers (MT) to probe multivalent-ion effects on DNA and RNA duplex structure, and employed
all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to examine how multivalent salts modulate their elasticity. MT measurements
provide single-molecule insights into salt-dependent structural changes, while MD simulations elucidate the underlying
mechanisms. They report a familiar non-monotonic response of DNA (softening then restiffening with charge inversion), but
an opposite, two-regime response for RNA: initial stiffening, 
followed by strong softening at higher salt, attributed to anion
accumulation and the local cation clamping that distorts the duplex geometry. These conclusions are not completely novel
as others have reported on the importance of counterions in reshaping the duplex geometry of DNA and RNA. (e.g.
Drozdetski & Tolokh, PRL, 2016, 117, 028101; Broekmans & King, PRL, 2016, 116, 258102; He & Chen, Sci Adv, 2021,
eabf6106; Zhang & Tian, Sci Adv, 2022, abn1384). Therefore, the manuscript’s novelty hinges on the depth and clarity of its
mechanistic description and quantitative analysis, and substantial revisions are required before it is suitable for publication. 
1. The data visualizations (e.g. Figure 3) are based on a single snapshot per simulated system. How the ‘representative’
frame was chosen needs to be clarified, via clustering selection, or simply final MD frame. 
2. Groove width should not be zero, yet the authors report zeros for indices 1 to 3 in Figure 4E. Please confirm the indexing
direction and groove width calculation. 
3.Instead of hand picked ion and duplex, authors ned to use ion distributions to support their claims. This is a major problem
of the MD simulations. Where are these ions around the duplex, how their distributions are impacted by salt, how DNA/RNA
differs from one another remain unclear. 
4. Lines 123 to 129 and 142 to 151, which discuss Figure 4, need substantial revision. Most subplot references are incorrect.
Please review and correct all panel labels, and make sure the text and caption match the figure. 
5. Regarding the artificial MD setup that keeps Cl- near the phosphates, the observed trends are clear, but this assumption
needs support or clarification. At high salt, anions are present with cations, so apparent chloride enrichment may simply
reflect cation binding preferences on RNA. Please report Cl- ion distributions from unrestrained MD to show that the artificial
restraint does not distort them; 3D density profile or spatial RDF would help. 
6. For the artificial “spring” introduced to mimic cation clamping, it appears the external force is applied to a specific pair of
phosphate atoms (Figure 4A). Please clarify how these atoms were selected. Was this choice based on the “local ion clamp”
site identified in Figure 3B? If so, how was that site defined, by any quantitative criterion? 
7. Following the point above, the “local ion clamp” site appears to vary with the cation species (Figure 3B: SPD3+ vs Ca2+).
If so, applying the same spring location across SPD3+, Ca2+, Mg2+, and Sr2+ (Figure 4B-C) may not be a fair comparison? 
8. The selection of ion models affect the dynamics of DNA and RNA. The authors are suggested to justify their selection of
GAFF for divalent ions.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zhang et al present a concise report on how increasing concentrations of multivalent cations impact RNA polymer
properties. Unlike the well-established behavior of DNA, in which charge neutralization softens DNA before charge
inversion re-stiffens the polymer, RNA exhibits a sharp drop in persistence length in high concentration multivalent cation
buffer. To explain this discrepancy, MD simulation is used, which predicts that instead of uniformly binding phosphate
groups along the backbone to neutralize charge, cations bind the major groove, either stiffening the RNA or locally bending
the RNA to increase or decrease, respectively, the effective persistence length. The presented experiments appear well
performed and display a striking difference between DNA and RNA results. The proposed mechanism appears plausible
based on the theoretical work presented. Overall, the publication of the manuscript could be recommended with the
concerns listed below addressed. 
The process through which the F-x curves obtained by MT were fit to the WLC to obtain the average values with uncertainty
(fig 2) is not well described. Was each individual curve fit by the WLC varying all three parameters simultaneously and then
these values were averaged for each condition along with standard error based on the absolute best fit? Or did each fit itself
have uncertainty based on the variability/noise in each f-x measurement, and these individual error ranges were propagated
over the averaging of these values? The exact method could impact whether the errors presented truly capture the reliability
of the fits, especially since the three fitting parameters are not independent, but highly correlated. That is, extension at low
force is increased by both increased contour length or persistence length while extension at high force is increased by both
increased contour length or reduced elastic modulus. 
The results section states experiments were performed with SPDCl3, CaCl2, MgCl2 and SrCl2, but only the calcium and
spermidine data are shown in the manuscript. It is unclear why the magnesium and strontium data are only in the
supplement and not in figure 2. 
Previous single molecule stretching experiments using trivalent cations (for example https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-
3495(00)76744-0) have observed condensation of DNA at concentrations similar to the spermidine concentration used in
this work. This presents as extensions at low force shorter than predicted by the WLC and abrupt elongation events
occurring as force is increased. Was this phenomenon ever observed in these experiments and did this impact the WLC
fitting? Were the nucleic acid constructs pre-stretched prior to the recorded F-x curves to remove any such condensation? 
Most of the MT experiment and MD simulation results agree within uncertainty (fig 2), but the experimental fits appear to
systematically measure smaller contour lengths than the simulations. For example, while the MD results return the widely
used and measured value of 34 angstroms for B-DNA, the MT measured Lc is consistently lower. Since the lengths of B-
form DNA and A-form RNA are well established, many previous single molecule experiments have consistently measured
these values, the included MD simulations predict no change, and no plausible mechanism has been suggested that would
alter this conserved feature, would it be more appropriate to fix this value in the WLC fits to reduce the degrees of freedom
and better define the variable persistence length and elasticity? Would doing so systematically alter these other parameters?

-Reply 
Thank you for your very nice comment. Regarding the selection of divalent ion models, our 
specific considerations are as follows: The SPD3+ force field parameters used in this study were 
developed based on the General AMBER Force Field (GAFF), which ensures intrinsic 
compatibility with the AMB ER nucleic acid force field (e.g., ff14SB) employed in our research, 
effectively avoiding parameter conflicts between different force fields. 
For divalent cations such as Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Sr²⁺, we directly adopted the validated models 
from the work of Li  et al57. These models, optimized within the GAFF framework, have been 
confirmed to accurately describe the coordination interactions (e.g., coordination number, 
binding energy) between divalent ions and nucleic acid phosphate groups or bases. Additionally, 
the Na⁺ and Cl ⁻ ions added to neutralize the system were taken from the classic ion models 
established by Joung and Cheatham58, whose compatibility with GAFF and AMBER nucleic acid 
force fields has been verified in numerous studies on ion-nucleic acid interactions. 
In summary, the ion models we selected (including the GAFF-based SPD3+ force field and 
derived divalent ion models) not only maintain parameter consistency with the overall simulation 
systems but also have clear literature support for their reliability in describing ion- nucleic acid 
interactions, ensuring the accuracy of the simulation results.

Abstract  20 
Multivalent cations are essential for DNA and RNA structures and their functions. Our single-molecule magnetic tweezers 21 
experiments reveal that increasing multivalent cation concentrations first softens and then stiffens DNA due to charge inversion. 22 
Oppositely, RNA first stiffens, then softens by over twofold. Our all-atom simulations reproduce these effects and reveal the physical 23 
mechanism: at low concentrations, multivalent cations clamp RNA major groove, stiffening RNA; at higher concentrations, anions 24 
disrupt this clamping effect, causing local cation-clamping which distorts and softens RNA. The mechanism also explains changes 25 
in stretching modulus, contour length, and twist-stretch coupling. Our findings reveal local cation-clamping sharply softens RNA and 26 
the significant roles of anions in modulating RNA structure. 27

Introduction 29 
The structural regulation and biophysical properties of DNA and RNA are critical for various biological processes such as replication, 30 
transcription, and translation1-5. As DNA and RNA are highly negatively charged, cations critically affect their biophysical properties 31 
including net charges, structures, and elasticities6. Monovalent cations such as Na+ and K+ neutralize the negative charges of DNA 32 
and RNA duplexes, thus increasing their stabilities and decreasing their bending stiffness7-12. Divalent cations such as Mg2+ and 33 
Ca2+ stabilize DNA duplexes by neutralizing their negative charges13,14. High-valent cations such as CoHex3+ and Spermidine3+ 34 
(SPD3+) soften DNA and induce DNA condensation by neutralizing the negative charges of DNA and attracting different DNA 35 
segments15-18. At high concentrations, excessive binding of high-valent cations can reverse the charges of DNA, which leads to 36 
DNA re-stiffening17,19,20 and the reentrant of DNA condensates16,21,22.  37 
Multivalent salts at high concentration bring more cations and anions into the vicinity of nucleic acid, resulting in more complex ion-38 
nucleic acid interactions and ion-ion correlations23-27. Recently, the specificity and strength of anion-nucleic acid interactions have 39 
been explored as well as the factors modulating them, such as anion type, nucleic acid sequence and structure, and other solution 40 
conditions24,26. There is still a lack of a deep understanding of how anions along with cations affect the structure and elasticity of 41 
RNA duplex. 42

Discussion  153 
Using experiments and simulations, we investigated how multivalent salts influence four elastic parameters of DNA and RNA 154 
duplexes across wide salt concentrations (Figure 5). Multivalent cations preferentially bind to the phosphate groups of DNA, initially 155 
neutralizing charges and decreasing PDNA before charge inversion. In contrast, multivalent cations bind preferentially to the major 156 
groove of RNA, forming cation clamps that dominate PRNA modulation (Supplementary Table 1). At low concentrations, cation-157 
clamps stiffen RNA. As cation concentrations further increase, anions invade RNA and disrupt these cation clamps, creating local 158 
clamping patterns that distort RNA structure and sharply decrease PRNA. These mechanisms also explain changes in Lc, K and 159 
dL/dN of DNA and RNA duplexes.  160

Local cation-clamping drastically distorts and softens RNA duplex

Abstract  
Multivalent cations are essential for DNA and RNA structures and their functions. Our single-molecule magnetic tweezers 
experiments reveal that increasing multivalent cation concentrations first softens and then stiffens DNA due to charge inversion. 
Oppositely, RNA first stiffens, then softens by over twofold. Our all-atom simulations reproduce these effects and reveal the physical 
mechanism: at low concentrations, multivalent cations clamp RNA major groove, stiffening RNA; at higher concentrations, anions 
disrupt this clamping effect, causing local cation-clamping which distorts and softens RNA. The mechanism also explains changes 
in stretching modulus, contour length, and twist-stretch coupling. Our findings reveal local cation-clamping sharply softens RNA and 
the significant roles of anions in modulating RNA structure. 
 
Introduction 
The structural regulation and biophysical properties of DNA and RNA are critical for various biological processes such as replication, 
transcription, and translation1-5. As DNA and RNA are highly negatively charged, cations critically affect their biophysical properties 
including net charges, structures, and elasticities6. Monovalent cations such as Na+ and K+ neutralize the negative charges of DNA 
and RNA duplexes, thus increasing their stabilities and decreasing their bending stiffness7-12. Divalent cations such as Mg2+ and 
Ca2+ stabilize DNA duplexes by neutralizing their negative charges13,14. High-valent cations such as CoHex3+ and Spermidine3+ 
(SPD3+) soften DNA and induce DNA condensation by neutralizing the negative charges of DNA and attracting different DNA 
segments15-18. At high concentrations, excessive binding of high-valent cations can reverse the charges of DNA, which leads to 
DNA re-stiffening17,19,20 and the reentrant of DNA condensates16,21,22.  
Multivalent salts at high concentration bring more cations and anions into the vicinity of nucleic acid, resulting in more complex ion-
nucleic acid interactions and ion-ion correlations23-27. Recently, the specificity and strength of anion-nucleic acid interactions have 
been explored as well as the factors modulating them, such as anion type, nucleic acid sequence and structure, and other solution 
conditions24,26. There is still a lack of a deep understanding of how anions along with cations affect the structure and elasticity of 
RNA duplex. 
 
Results 
Elasticities of RNA and DNA from experiments 
Using magnetic tweezers (MT) experiments, we measured the effects of multivalent salts, including SPDCl3, CaCl2, MgCl2, and 
SrCl2, on the elasticities of RNA and DNA duplexes through force-extension (F-x) curves (Figures 1C-1D). We fitted each F-x curve 
to an approximation formula 28 for the extensible worm-like-chain model following previous works10,29-34:  
x=NLc[1- (kBT/FP)1/2/2+F/K].                                                                          (1)

Also, we measured dL/dN at 8 pN to characterize twist-stretch coupling using torsion-constrained molecules following previous 
works35,39,40 (Figure 2D and Supplementary Figure 2). Here, dN is the change in helical turn and dL is the corresponding change in 
DNA or RNA extension. Additional details of the MT setup can be found in the Supplementary Note 1. The details of the preparation

As shown in Figure 2, the experimental PDNA initially decreases with increasing salt concentration until reaching a critical 
concentration, after which it increases, likely due to charge inversion. The critical concentration of SPDCl₃ agrees with the 
concentration where the net charge of DNA is fully neutralized16,17,41. The values of K, Lc, and dL/dN of DNA remain largely 
unchanged across different salt concentrations. (Figures 2B-2D).  
Opposite to PDNA, the experimental PRNA demonstrated an initial increase and then a sharp decrease with increasing salt 
concentration. When SPDCl3 increased from 1 µM to 1 mM and then to 0.5 M, PRNA increased from 64.4±2.2 nm to 72.6±1.6 nm 
and then sharply decreased to 32.2±0.8 nm. Similarly, when CaCl2 increased from 0.1 mM to 10 mM and then to 2 M, PRNA increased 
from 53.5±1.4 nm to 57.7±1.4 nm and then dramatically decreased to 30.3±1.3 nm. Simultaneously, Lc of RNA duplex decreased 
and then increased, and K increased and then decreased along with increasing the salt concentration. Additionally, dL/dN of RNA 
reverses to positive and then back to negative again as salt concentration increases. The increase in P and K, the decrease in Lc, 
and the first reversal of dL/dN for RNA at the low salt concentrations can be explained by the cation-clamping of the RNA major 
groove19,20,35,42. However, the new and counterintuitive results of P, K, Lc and dL/dN for RNA duplex at high salt concentrations are 
out of our anticipation and certainly require a new mechanism. 
Elasticities of RNA and DNA from simulations 
See Supplementary Figures 4-5, Supplementary Note 3 and our previous works38 for all-atom molecule dynamics (MD) 
simulations and the calculations of elastic parameters of RNA and DNA. As shown in Figure 2A, our simulations reproduced the 
changes in the experimental PRNA and PDNA. Correspondingly, as salt concentration increased, PDNA from simulations initially 
decreased and then increased, while PRNA showed the opposite trend: it increased first and then sharply decreased. Specifically, 
as SPDCl₃ increased from 1 µM to 1 mM and then to 500 mM, PRNA increased from 60.5±4.1 to 69.9±3.2 nm and then sharply 
decreased to 41.2±3.3 nm. Similarly, with CaCl₂ increasing from 100 µM to 100 mM and then to 2 M, PRNA increased from 55.5±3.8 
to 58.4±1.6 nm, then dramatically dropped to 36.6±1.4 nm. Also consistent with experiments, Lc of RNA duplex first decreased then 
increased, and K first increased and then decreased as salt concentration increased (Figure 2B-C). The simulations also captured 
the two reversals in dL/dN of RNA (Figure 2D). For MgCl2 and SrCl2, our experiments and simulations obtained the same trends of 
the salt dependence of DNA and RNA elasticities (Supplementary Figures 6-7).

Binding of multivalent cations to DNA and RNA 
We then analyzed the ion-binding to DNA and RNA (Figure 3 and Supplementary Figure 8). Figure 3A shows that PDNA closely 
correlates with the net charge of DNA together with binding ions. The PDNA decreases to the lowest values at critical concentrations 
(1 mM for SPDCl₃ and 100 mM for CaCl₂), where the charge of DNA is fully neutralized (Figure 3A). At higher salt concentrations 
of SPDCl₃ and CaCl₂, the net charge of DNA reversed, consistent with re-stiffening of DNA. For a comparison, the net charge of 
RNA did not obviously reverse at high SPDCl₃ or CaCl₂ concentrations (Figure 3A), which is consistent with that PRNA did not 
increase but sharply decreased (Figure 2A).  
To explore the counterintuitive dependence of RNA elasticity on multivalent salt concentrations, we analyzed the spatial 
standard deviation of major groove width along the axis of RNA, which is defined as σM (Figures 3 and Supplementary Figures 9-
10). The σM reflects irregularity of the major groove due to cation-clamping of RNA19,20,38,43. At low concentrations (≤1 mM SPDCl₃ 
or ≤100 mM CaCl₂), multivalent cations bind to the major groove of RNA (Figures 3B and C), creating cation-clamping that 
significantly reduces σM and decreases the bending angle (Figures 3D and E), thereby increasing PRNA. Also, both experiments and 
simulations confirmed the twist-stretch coupling reversal at 1 mM SPD³⁺ or 100 mM Ca²⁺ (Figure 2D), consistent with the cation-
clamping mechanism.  
Furthermore, we examined the distribution of cation clamps along the axis of RNA duplex at different salt concentrations 
(Figures 3B-3D). At 0.1 mM CaCl₂, the major groove of RNA kept wide and stable over MD time, which indicates very few cation 
clamps formed. At 1 µM and 1 mM SPDCl₃ or 100 mM CaCl₂, the major groove width became narrow and remained stable over 
MD time, which means uniform cation clamps formed, consistent with the increase in PRNA (Figure 2A and Supplementary Table 1). 
However, at high concentrations (0.5 M SPDCl₃ or 2 M CaCl₂), σM increased and the bending angle became larger (Figure 3E), 
which is consistent with the sharp decrease in PRNA (Figure 2A). The strong correlations between the distribution of cation clamps, 
σM, bending angle and bending stiffness of RNA indicate that PRNA is strongly related to the formation and destruction of cation 
clamps of the major groove (Supplementary Table 1).

phosphate atoms across the major groove of RNA in simulations (Figure 4A). We found increasing the spring constant k elevated

concentrations (Figure 4E). The simulation results showed a strong correlation between k and PRNA both for the systems with 
multivalent cation but without “spring” (Figure 2A) and for those from our artificial spring model (Figures 4C and 4E). The stronger 
the local cation clamp, the greater its effect on PRNA.

Anions at high concentrations invade RNA and lead to local cation-clamping 
Simulations showed that, at high concentrations, anions can invade RNA duplex (Figure 3B), destroying most cation clamps 
and resulting in an irregular local cation-clamping distribution (Figure 3D). This result is supported by twist-stretch coupling, which 
re-reverses at 0.5 M SPDCl₃ or 2 M CaCl₂ (Figure 2D), suggesting that most locations of RNA major groove return to unclamped. 
To test the origin of local cation-clamping, we designed a synthetic system with 10 or 20 restricted Cl⁻ ions around phosphate 
groups of the 20-bp RNA duplex to simulate anion invasion in simulations (Figure 4B), and found that the distribution of the RNA 
major groove became irregular, indicating a local cation-clamping effect (Figure 4D). At the locations with Cl⁻, the major groove 
became wide, indicating the loss of cation clamps, while major groove remained narrow at locations without Cl⁻. As the Cl⁻ number 
increased, PRNA decreased (Figure 4F), with the structure deformation closely similar to that at high multivalent salt concentrations 
(Figure 3B). The restriction of Cl⁻ around RNA destroys cation-clamps in this synthetic system supports our above analyses that 
anion invasion leads to local cation-clamps that sharply reduce PRNA. 
 
Discussion  
Using experiments and simulations, we investigated how multivalent salts influence four elastic parameters of DNA and RNA 
duplexes across wide salt concentrations (Figure 5). Multivalent cations preferentially bind to the phosphate groups of DNA, initially 
neutralizing charges and decreasing PDNA before charge inversion. In contrast, multivalent cations bind preferentially to the major 
groove of RNA, forming cation clamps that dominate PRNA modulation (Supplementary Table 1). At low concentrations, cation-
clamps stiffen RNA. As cation concentrations further increase, anions invade RNA and disrupt these cation clamps, creating local 
clamping patterns that distort RNA structure and sharply decrease PRNA. These mechanisms also explain changes in Lc, K and 
dL/dN of DNA and RNA duplexes.

coupling were shown. The arrows “↑” indicate an increase, “↓” a decrease, “↓↓” a sharp decrease, and “→” no change. 
Representative structures and surface electrostatic potentials of RNA and DNA duplexes were calculated at 0.1 mM, 100 mM, and

The functions and applications of RNA often depend on its structures and structural transformations, which are influenced by 
the stiffness44 and our results reveal that multivalent salts of high concentrations sharply reduce the stiffness of RNA duplex. We 
propose a new physical mechanism that anions brought by high-concentration salts destroy the uniform cation clamping for the 
major groove of RNA duplexes, creating local cation-clamping that sharply distort and soften the RNA duplex. While earlier studies 
generally emphasized cation effects7,8,18,20,24,35, our findings highlight the significant influence of anions on nucleic acid structures 
and elasticities. This work greatly enriches the understanding of structure and elasticity of RNA duplexes under extensive multivalent 
salt concentrations, which may be useful in rational control of RNA structures in material applications. Our findings extend beyond 
basic nucleic acid biophysics, potentially influencing fields such as RNA physics, drug development, and nanotechnology. 
 
Methods (mandatory, unless purely theoretical work) 
MT experiments  
Each nucleic acid construct (DNA or RNA) was labeled with digoxigenin on one end and biotin on the other end (Figure 1A). In 
the flow cell, the digoxigenin end was attached to an anti-digoxigenin-coated glass slide, while the biotin end was bound to a 
streptavidin-coated paramagnetic microbead (Dynabeads M-270). Experiments were performed in an ultra-clean room at 22°C. A 
10 mM Tris-HCl buffer was used to maintain a stable pH of 7.5. See more details of MT experiments in the Supporting Text 1. 
All-atom MD simulations 
We conducted all-atom MD simulations using the ssDNA sequence 5' -CGACTCTACGGCATCTGCGC-3' and the 
corresponding RNA sequence, where T was replaced with U, based on previous short DNA fragment experiments20,35,45,46. Initial 
structures were built using the nucleic acid builder (NAB) in AMBER47. The SPD3+ force field was developed using the general 
AMBER force field48 and cation models49 for Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Sr²⁺. Simulations were performed with Gromacs 4.650, using refined 
AMBER ff99bsc1+χOL347,51 force fields and the TIP3P water model52. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions were added to neutralize the systems, using 
the ion model from Joung and Cheatham53. Microscopic structural parameters were calculated with Curves+54 (Supplementary 
Table 1), and system neutralization at desired salt concentrations was confirmed (Supplementary Figure 5). Typical salt 
concentrations were chosen based on MT experimental results for both RNA and DNA. See more details of MD simulations in the 
Supplementary Note 3 and our previous works38 for the simulations and the calculations of elastic parameters of RNA and DNA.
