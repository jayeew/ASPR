Overall, this is an interesting, well-researched, methodologically rigorous and well-written paper. It has important policy and

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study raises a critical issue of urban shade inequity and offers valuable cross-city data, but the current version lacks
conceptual precision, methodological rigor, and analytical consistency. The cross-city Gini and SHAP comparisons, as
presently framed, may overstate generalizability. To strengthen the paper, the authors should clarify the conceptual framing
of shade as a public good, ensure statistical comparability across cases. 
While the manuscript makes an important contribution by highlighting shade inequality as an overlooked dimension of
climate adaptation, several conceptual and methodological weaknesses undermine the coherence and validity of the cross-
city comparative analysis. 
1. Conceptual framing of shade inequity — 
The introduction successfully situates shade within the context of urban heat mitigation and environmental justice. However,
it does not clearly articulate why shade inequity constitutes an urban design or public policy issue. The discussion currently
frames shade primarily as a climatic or biophysical phenomenon rather than as a question of governance, spatial justice, or
accessibility of urban amenities. This limits the theoretical contribution of the study. The authors should clarify how shade
can be understood as a common good or a spatial privilege—as briefly mentioned in the introduction (“whether urban shade
provisions are a common good or a spatial privilege”)—and explain how their empirical design directly engages with this
unresolved question. 
2. Case selection rationale — 
The nine study cities are described as “globally diverse,” but the rationale for their inclusion remains vague. Beyond climatic
and morphological diversity, the authors should elaborate on additional criteria guiding the selection of these particular cities
—such as data quality, governance typology, or representativeness within Global North–South contexts—to justify the
generalizability of the findings. 
3. Inconsistency in the comparability of Gini-based shade inequality metrics — 
The manuscript directly compares Gini coefficients across cities (e.g., “Stockholm exhibits the steepest deviations…
whereas Belém shows the lowest”), yet each city’s dataset differs substantially in sampling unit, indicator definition, and
socioeconomic data resolution. Without normalization or weighting, such comparisons may not be statistically meaningful.
The authors should clarify how the Gini values were computed (e.g., population-weighted or equal-area units), and consider
reporting confidence intervals or normalized indices to ensure cross-city comparability. 
4. Sample size imbalance and model heterogeneity — 
The XGBoost–SHAP models are trained on datasets with highly uneven sample sizes (ranging from <200 to >6,000 units),
but the results are interpreted as equivalent. This discrepancy likely introduces overfitting in small-sample cases and
underfitting in large ones. The reported R² values span an implausibly wide range (0.34–0.999; Figure 3 and Supplementary
Materials), suggesting model instability. The manuscript does not explain how these differences were handled, nor how
statements made “across all cities” are justified. Weighted aggregation, fixed grid sampling, or standardized model
configurations would improve comparability and mitigate bias. 
5. Unverified multicollinearity among explanatory variables — 
Several predictors—average canopy height, building height, and green coverage—are conceptually interrelated, yet no
evidence of multicollinearity testing (e.g., Variance Inflation Factor, Spearman correlation matrix, or SHAP correlation
analysis) is provided. Since SHAP decompositions assume independent predictors, such redundancy could distort feature
importance values. A correlation heatmap or variance partitioning analysis would help demonstrate robustness. 
6. Insufficient reporting of model validation and interpretability — 
The XGBoost–SHAP section states that the method was used “to elucidate these relationships,” but quantitative validation is
lacking. No model performance metrics (R², RMSE, MAE), cross-validation procedures, or hyperparameter settings are
provided. The wide heterogeneity in model fit across cities is not discussed. Consequently, the claim that “average canopy
height emerged as the most influential predictor” is not statistically substantiated, as mean(|SHAP|) rankings or significance

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

tests are not reported. 
7. Problematic cross-city interpretation of SHAP values — 
SHAP values are inherently model-specific and cannot be directly compared across independently trained models due to
differing baselines, target distributions, and scaling. The paper treats SHAP plots from separate city models as visually
comparable (Figure 3), but this lacks a sound statistical foundation. The authors should either present standardized SHAP
values (e.g., z-scores or relative importance ratios) or confine interpretations to within-city analyses rather than making
general claims “across all cities.” 
8. Omission of claimed nonlinear and interactive effects — 
The authors state that the model captures “nonlinear and interactive effects,” but provide no SHAP interaction plots or
bivariate dependence visualizations. Without these, the claim remains speculative. Including selected SHAP interaction
plots or partial dependence plots would substantiate this assertion.

Response to Comment 1.2 
Thank you very much for raising this important point regarding the mapping unit used in our analysis. 
We sincerely apologise for the confusion caused by the wording in the original manuscript. The 
sentence—“The average sunlit(i) was derived using zonal statistics from high-resolution raster datasets 
clipped to sidewalk or neighbourhood polygons” —was imprecise and may have suggested that we 
used different spatial units (sidewalks in some cities and neighbourhoods in others). This was not the 
case. In fact, all cities in our study followed a consistent two-step procedure: 
First, we generated high-resolution shade maps (tiled raster files covering the extent of each city) and 
masked them using sidewalk polygons to restrict analysis to pedestrian -accessible areas only. This 
ensured that our shade estimates reflect the conditi ons people actually experience while walking in 
urban environments. 
Second, we aggregated these sidewalk-level shade values up to the neighbourhood level by computing 
the mean shade across all sidewalk pixels within each neighbourhood polygon. Thus, the final 
analytical unit —the “average sunlit(i)” —represents the average s idewalk shade within each 
neighbourhood, not a mixture of sidewalk- and neighbourhood-level units across cities. 
These steps can confirm that non -public spaces (e.g., building footprints, private lots) were excluded 
from the analysis through the sidewalk masking step, so the shade averages reflect only publicly 
accessible pedestrian areas. 
We acknowledge that the original phrasing was ambiguous and did not clearly convey this consistent 
methodology. To address this, we have revised the relevant section (now on page s 15 and 16 in the 
revised manuscript) to read:

Response to Comment 1.4 
Thank you for providing this professional comment. Indeed, the hottest time of the year in mid to 
higher latitude regions is typically 1-2 months after the solstice day. The summer solstice was chosen 
as the primary reference day because it represents the maximum potential solar exposure for a given 
location, occurring when the sun is at its highest point in the sky. While the hottest day of the year may 
show variation due to atmospheric conditions (e.g., cloud cover, wind), the solstice provides a 
consistent and comparable baseline across cities for understanding shading distribution under ideal 
conditions. We clarified this in the Methods section: 
Shade map simulation (page 15) 
Half-hourly shade conditions were simulated for a representative summer solstice day (June 21 in 
the Northern Hemisphere, December 21 in the Southern Hemisphere) and additionally the equinox 
for Belem (March 21), where the solar angle is at its minimum. The summer solstice was chosen as 
the primary reference day because it represents the maximum potential solar exposure for a given 
location, providing a consistent and comparable baseline across cities for understanding shading 
distribution under ideal conditions. The simulated result is a stack of half-hourly binary shade rasters 
per city, which were then aggregated to calculate the shade metrics (shown in the next Section) per 
sidewalk polygon or pixel, representing the proportion of time that location is shaded during peak 
daytime hours. 
Furthermore, as stated in our previous Response to Comment 1.1, the study generated the combined 
shade, building shade, and additional tree shade, both on the summer solstice day and the hottest day 
of the year. Results show that when comparing the summer solstice with the single hottest day of the 
year, the neig hbourhood-level shading patterns change only minimally: all shade types show only 
modest, localised intensity or boundary adjustments rather than systematic reallocation of shade across 
neighbourhoods. These results indicate that the macroscopic pattern of shade inequality in the nine 
cities is robust to day -to-day solar angle differences in our aggregation framework and is primarily 
driven by the underlying spatial distributions of built form and ca nopy rather than by transient solar 
geometry.

The study raises a critical issue of urban shade inequity and offers valuable cross -city data, but the 
current version lacks conceptual precision, methodological rigor, and analytical consistency. The 
cross-city Gini and SHAP comparisons, as presently fram ed, may overstate generalizability. To 
strengthen the paper, the authors should clarify the conceptual framing of shade as a public good, 
ensure statistical comparability across cases. 
While the manuscript makes an important contribution by highlighting shade inequality as an 
overlooked dimension of climate adaptation, several conceptual and methodological weaknesses 
undermine the coherence and validity of the cross-city comparative analysis.

Discussion (page 12) 
Urban shade serves as a critical microclimatic amenity, mitigating thermal stress and enhancing 
outdoor habitability, particularly during hot seasons [40 -42]. Our findings directly engage the 
unresolved question raised in the beginning: whether urban shade provisions constitute a common

Comment 3.3 
3. Inconsistency in the comparability of Gini-based shade inequality metrics — 
The manuscript directly compares Gini coefficients across cities (e.g., “Stockholm exhibits the 
steepest deviations… whereas Belém shows the lowest”), yet each city’s dataset differs substantially 
in sampling unit, indicator definition, and socioeconomic data resolution. Without normalization or 
weighting, such comparisons may not be statistically meaningful. The authors should clarify how 
the Gini values were computed (e.g., population -weighted or equal -area units), and consider 
reporting confidence intervals or normalized indices to ensure cross-city comparability. 
Response to Comment 3.3 
Thank you for this insightful comment. We agree that appropriate clarification and robustness 
evaluation are essential when comparing inequality metrics across cities. 
First, in our previous analysis, the Gini coefficients were calculated using population-weighted Lorenz 
curves. Specifically, for each city, the cumulative proportion of population was calculated from 
community-level population data and compared against the cumulative share of green shade area. Thus, 
the Gini values are not based on equal -area units, but on population -weighted spatial units, ensuring 
that larger or more densely populated communities contribute proportionally to the inequality measure. 
Second, to address your concern about comparability and statistical robustness, we have further 
computed 95% confidence intervals for each Gini coefficient using a non -parametric bootstrap 
approach (500 resamples with replacement). This method does not ass ume normality and reflects 
uncertainty arising from spatial sampling variability. The resulting Gini values and their confidence 
intervals are now reported in the Supplementary Materials. 
 
We sincerely appreciate the reviewer’s constructive feedback, which has helped improve the clarity 
and robustness of the manuscript. We have revised the relevant section (on page 18):

Comment 3.4 
4. Sample size imbalance and model heterogeneity — 
The XGBoost–SHAP models are trained on datasets with highly uneven sample sizes (ranging from 
<200 to >6,000 units), but the results are interpreted as equivalent. This discrepancy likely 
introduces overfitting in small-sample cases and underfitting in lar ge ones. The reported R²  values

standardized model configurations would improve comparability and mitigate bias. 
Response to Comment 3.4 
Thank you for providing these suggestions on the XGBoost model. We understand your concerns 
regarding the uneven sample sizes across cities and the resulting variability in model performance. We 
have carefully addressed these concerns as follows: 
· Handling sample size imbalance: To mitigate the sample variations, we applied sample weights 
proportional to the city sample size during model training, ensuring that each city contributes 
appropriately to the model. 
· Standardised model configuration and hyperparameter tuning: To improve comparability across 
cities, we standardised the XGBoost model configuration and conducted hyperparameter tuning 
using GridSearchCV with 5 -fold cross -validation. This ensures that the model is optimised 
consistently and robustly across all cities. Feature values were also standardised to the same scale 
to further enhance cross-city comparability. 
· Extended model evaluation metrics: Beyond R² , MAE, and RMSE, we additionally report 
XGBoost feature importance metrics: Gain, Cover, and Weight, mapped to the original feature 
names and rounded to two decimal places. 
Thank you again for prompting this important methodological improvement, which significantly

Comment 3.7 
7. Problematic cross-city interpretation of SHAP values — 
SHAP values are inherently model -specific and cannot be directly compared across independently

Response to Comment 3.7 
Thank you for highlighting this issue related to cross -city SHAP comparison. Combined with your 
previous suggestions, we have addressed this concern by training a global XGBoost model and sample 
weighting using the combined dataset from all cities. By trai ning a global model on all city data 
combined, we ensure that the SHAP values are based on the same model with a consistent baseline. 
This makes the SHAP values inherently comparable across cities without needing additional 
standardisation. 
Applying z-score standardisation could distort the natural distribution of SHAP values, interpreting

SHAP values, potentially causing unreasonably large or small shifts in the feature contributions. This 
may lead to misleading interpretations of feature importance, as it would obscure the actual effect of 
each feature on the target variable. Therefore, we believe that standardisation (e.g., z -score 
normalisation) of SHAP values is unnecessary in this context.
