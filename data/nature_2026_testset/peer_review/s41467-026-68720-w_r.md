(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Guo et al. describe an efficient nickel-catalyzed asymmetric substitution reaction between propargylic
carbonates and sulfinate salts. The methodology is operationally simple and enables the divergent synthesis of a range of
biologically relevant propargylated, allenylated, and 1,3-dienylated sulfones in excellent yields with high regio- and
stereoselectivity. Notably, achieving such divergent selectivity represents a challenge in the field, and the authors have
conducted a thorough mechanistic investigation to elucidate the underlying pathway. 
In summary, this work represents a significant contribution to nickel-catalyzed propargylic substitutions, enabling the
construction of a series of valuable sulfone derivatives. Therefore, I recommend its publication in Nature Communications
after the following minor revisions are addressed: 
1. There appears to be an inconsistency between the results in Figure 3a–b and the conditions described in Table 4.
Specifically, Table 4 lists SMOPS at 1.0 equivalent and carbonate at 1.5 equivalents, yet Figure 3a suggests that under
excess carbonate conditions, only trace amounts of 5a are formed, while Figure 3b shows the opposite outcome. This
discrepancy should be resolved.

page S12 vs. pages S33–34).

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an exceptionally impressive and impactful piece of work in the field of asymmetric synthesis. The
authors have developed a versatile nickel-catalyzed system for the divergent and enantioselective sulfonylation of
propargylic carbonates. Strategic use of SMOPS as a sulfinate surrogate unlocks diverse chiral sulfonyl products—
propargyl, allenyl, and 1,3-dienyl sulfones—from simple precursors with high regio-, chemo-, and stereocontrol. The
substrate scope is extensive and well-explored, the mechanistic investigations are insightful, and the synthetic applications
elegantly demonstrate the method's power for the concise synthesis of complex, bioactive molecules like (+)-β-
sulfinylhydroxamic acid. This study represents a significant advance in organosulfur chemistry and asymmetric catalysis,
offering a powerful and flexible toolkit that will be of great interest to researchers in synthetic chemistry, medicinal chemistry,
and chemical biology. It is my strong opinion that this manuscript is well-suited for publication in Nature Communications
following minor revisions addressing the points below. 
1. 2-Biphenylcarboxylic acid is important for suppressing racemization in the propargylic substitution manifold; comparative
data with other carboxylic-acid additives should be provided to clarify whether the o-biphenyl motif is uniquely effective. 
2. Propargylic substrates in the present protocol rely on Boc as the leaving group. Would replacing the carbamate with Cbz
or methyl carbonate? A concise survey of these alternative electrophiles would clarify whether Boc is mechanistically
required or simply the most accessible choice. 
3. Have monodentate phosphines—simple triphenylphosphine or tricyclohexylphosphine—been tested? 
4. The author in citation 9 should be listed as Adam, G., and the phrase "Click to copy article link" in citation 56's title should
be removed. 
5. In the sentence “The fact that the excess amount of sulfates can accelerate…,” “sulfates” should be corrected to
“sulfinates.”

(7) The authors of this paper should indicate the reason why the isomerization of 3 into 4 proceeds 
without the loss of enantiomeric excess together with the exact role of sulfone moiety. This 
reviewer highly recommends the authors of this paper carry out DFT calculations on the 
proposed reaction pathway. Thus, this reviewer considers that a substantial revision is necessary

Response: Thank you for this insightful suggestion regarding the mechanism of the stereospecific 
isomerization from 3 to 4. We conducted detailed DFT calculations, which have provided a clear 
rationale for the complete retention of enantiomeric excess with Al₂O₃ as promoter. 
The calculations reveal that trace water adsorbed on the Al₂O₃ surface plays a critical role. Using 
a 2Al₂O₃·4H₂O cluster model, we found that the surface hydroxyl groups enable a concerted proton-
transfer process. This bifunctional action, where the surface acts simultaneously as a proton donor

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

and acceptor, allows the alkyne-to-allene isomerization to proceed through a single transition state 
(TS3) without forming a discrete anionic intermediate. This concerted pathway is the key to the 
observed stereospecificity. 
The strong electron -withdrawing sulfone group is essential for facilitating the initial proton 
abstraction due to the pronounced acidity of the α-proton. In the presence of strong organic bases 
like DBU or TMG—which lack the bifunctional capability—the reaction is forced into a stepwise 
mechanism. The base deprotonates the substrate to generate a sulfone -stabilized carbanion 
intermediate. This intermediate is configurationally unstable and leads to racemization upon 
subsequent protonation. 
In contrast, the hydrated Al₂O₃ surface possesses very weak basicity. Our calculations confirm that 
directly generating the same sulfone-stabilized carbanion on this surface is energetically prohibitive 
(ΔG = +41.2 kcal/mol). Therefore, under our catalytic conditions, the formation of the racemization-
prone anionic intermediate is effectively shut down. The reaction is channeled exclusively through 
the lower -energy, concerted mechanism enabled by the Al₂O₃/H₂O system, thus preserving 
enantiopurity. 
We have incorporated these computational insights into a revised mechanistic discussion in the 
manuscript, including the key energy profiles and structures in the Supplementary Information 
(Page S40). We are truly grateful for your suggestion, which has led to a much deeper and more 
rigorous understanding of the reaction pathway.
