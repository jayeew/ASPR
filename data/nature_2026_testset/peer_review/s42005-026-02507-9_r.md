Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Zhang et al. introduces the quantum mutual information, which the authors define as the Entanglement
Topological Invariant (ETI), to characterize second-order topological insulators in finite-size free-fermion systems. The
quantum mutual information capture the number of corner topological states and the transitions to/from the trivial topological
region. The paper applies this method to the well-known Bernevig-Hughes-Zhang (BHZ) model as an example. While the
paper is well-written and the results sound good, I cannot accept the manuscript in its current form due to concerns that the
authors need to address." 
1. The authors present the Entanglement Topological Invariant (ETI) as a new paradigm for studying the entanglement
properties of topological corner states. However, as far as I understand, the proposed invariant is essentially the quantum
mutual information applied to the system. What exactly is the novelty of this parameter? The authors should clarify this
aspect further to help readers better understand the real novelty of the proposed method. 
2. There are already several studies in the literature that apply a similar concept to study the topological phases of insulators
and superconductors (e.g., DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.55.1142, DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.107.115160). These studies show
that the quantum conditional mutual information (QCMI), by multi-partitioning the system, signals transitions from topological
to trivial phases, counts the number of edge states, scales correctly with system size, and can even capture the evolution of
topological bound states under current flux or generalized boundary conditions (e.g., DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.106.155407,
DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.107.064505). More importantly, the QCMI can also differentiate between topological order and
inversion symmetry breaking order (e.g., in the Ising-like model), with the latter connected to the former via a Jordan-Wigner
mapping. The authors should include a discussion of this existing topological order parameter, which is currently absent, to
strengthen the manuscript. In particular, they should elaborate on how the proposed ETI compares to the QCMI, especially in
terms of its advantages or differences. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript titled "Characterizing second-order topological insulators via entanglement topological invariant (ETI) in
two-dimensional systems" proposes an entanglement topological invariant to detect 2D topological corner states, and count
them. The authors focus on the Bernevig-Hughes-Zhang (BHZ) model (and 2 additional models in the supplementary. All
are classically simulated in polynomial time via the free fermion formulation) to verify their ETI and show numerically that it
indeed counts the number of non-trivial topological corner stats. The authors show the phase transition for a certain
parameter sweep for this model and discuss some finite-size effects. The authors emphasize the importance of this ETI as it
works for higher order topological modes and symmetries that other ETIs may not work for. 
The manuscript is well-written and explains thoroughly the numerical methods. The manuscript is well-presented and is
ordered in a way that is easy for the reader to follow. However, the manuscript may benefit from additional theoretical
analysis and discussion of limitations of the ETI (e.g. does it work if the system has 1D topological modes and not only

We would like to thank the first referee for the thorough review and constructive feedback.
We have carefully revised the manuscript and addressed all the concerns raised. Below, we
provide detailed responses to each comment and outline the corresponding changes made in
the revised manuscript.
Comment:
The manuscript by Zhang et al. introduces the quantum mutual information, which the
authors define as the Entanglement Topological Invariant (ETI), to characterize second-order
topological insulators in finite-size free-fermion systems. The quantum mutual information
capture the number of corner topological states and the transitions to/from the trivial topo-
logical region. The paper applies this method to the well-known Bernevig-Hughes-Zhang
(BHZ) model as an example. While the paper is well-written and the results sound good, I
cannot accept the manuscript in its current form due to concerns that the authors need to
address.”
Reply: We thank the referee for their thoughtful review and for acknowledging that our
manuscript is well-written and our results sound good. We have carefully revised the
manuscript to address the referee’s concerns, and we believe the revised version has been
significantly improved.
Comment:
1. The authors present the Entanglement Topological Invariant (ETI) as a new paradigm
for studying the entanglement properties of topological corner states. However, as far as I
understand, the proposed invariant is essentially the quantum mutual information applied to
the system. What exactly is the novelty of this parameter? The authors should clarify this
aspect further to help readers better understand the real novelty of the proposed method.
Reply: We sincerely thank the referee for this important question concerning the novelty
of the proposed Entanglement Topological Invariant (ETI). The ETI is indeed constructed
from the mutual information, but it is not equivalent to it. Unlike the conventional quan-
tum mutual information, which measures the total correlations between two subsystems,
the ETI is specifically designed to isolate the topological component of entanglement in
finite-size free-fermion systems under open boundaries. By selecting two spatially separated
boundary regions A and B and maximizing their distance, the ETI effectively filters out
short-range bulk correlations, leaving only the long-range quantum correlations arising from
localized corner states. As a result, the ETI behaves as a quantized real-space invariant
that directly reflects the presence of higher-order topological corner states, rather than a

general measure of mutual correlations. This distinction constitutes the essential novelty of
the ETI, which allows it to serve as a practical and physically transparent diagnostic for
higher-order topological phases.
In the revised manuscript, we have added some sentences to explicitly explain the con-
ceptual difference between the ETI and the conventional mutual information. (See page 2,
paragraph 3, lines 10-16 and 28-35).
Comment:
2. There are already several studies in the literature that apply a similar concept to
study the topological phases of insulators and superconductors (e.g., DOI: 10.1103/Phys-
RevB.55.1142, DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.107.115160). These studies show that the quantum
conditional mutual information (QCMI), by multi-partitioning the system, signals transi-
tions from topological to trivial phases, counts the number of edge states, scales correctly
with system size, and can even capture the evolution of topological bound states under
current flux or generalized boundary conditions (e.g., DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.106.155407,
DOI: 10.1103/PhysRevB.107.064505). More importantly, the QCMI can also differentiate
between topological order and inversion symmetry breaking order (e.g., in the Ising-like
model), with the latter connected to the former via a Jordan-Wigner mapping. The au-
thors should include a discussion of this existing topological order parameter, which is
currently absent, to strengthen the manuscript. In particular, they should elaborate on how
the proposed ETI compares to the QCMI, especially in terms of its advantages or differences.
Reply: We thank the referee for this insightful comment and for bringing these important
references on the Quantum Conditional Mutual Information (QCMI) to our attention. We
agree that the existing literature on QCMI as a topological order parameter is highly rele-
vant. We have addressed this point thoroughly in the revised manuscript.
As pointed out by the referee, the QCMI can signal transitions between topological and
trivial phases by partitioning the system into multiple regions. It can also count the number
of edge states and scale appropriately with system size. These properties are indeed consis-
tent with the design principles and results presented in our work. However, as highlighted
in the literature [Phys. Rev. B 107, 115160 (2023)], “A crucial step in order to extend our
results to include all topological quantum matter is to generalize the concept of edge-state
quantum conditional mutual information and edge-state squashed entanglement to generic
many-body systems in dimension D ≥ 2.” Thus, studies on QCMI have been mainly confined
to one-dimensional systems, with the hope of successfully extending it to two-dimensional
settings. In fact, this extension is one of the central topics of our present work.
A key limitation of QCMI lies in its computation of open-boundary bipartite entanglement
using pure ground states [Phys. Rev. Research 4, 033088 (2022)], which makes it highly

Comment:
The manuscript titled ”Characterizing second-order topological insulators via entangle-
ment topological invariant (ETI) in two-dimensional systems” proposes an entanglement
topological invariant to detect 2D topological corner states, and count them. The authors
focus on the Bernevig-Hughes-Zhang (BHZ) model (and 2 additional models in the supple-
mentary. All are classically simulated in polynomial time via the free fermion formulation)
to verify their ETI and show numerically that it indeed counts the number of non-trivial
topological corner stats. The authors show the phase transition for a certain parameter
sweep for this model and discuss some finite-size effects. The authors emphasize the impor-
tance of this ETI as it works for higher order topological modes and symmetries that other
ETIs may not work for.
Reply: We thank the second referee for his/her time and experience in reviewing our
manuscript. Below, we address the comments from the second referee one by one.
Comment:
The manuscript is well-written and explains thoroughly the numerical methods. The
manuscript is well-presented and is ordered in a way that is easy for the reader to follow.
However, the manuscript may benefit from additional theoretical analysis and discussion of
limitations of the ETI (e.g. does it work if the system has 1D topological modes and not
only corner modes? What about interactions? Do the authors expect it to work for those
cases? Is the method is expected to work also in 1D/3D?).
Reply: We thank the referee for raising the important question regarding the applicability
and limitations of the Entanglement Topological Invariant (ETI). The ETI is specifically
designed to detect zero-dimensional localized topological states, such as corner or end states,
by isolating the long-range correlation between spatially separated boundary regions.
When applying this method to a finite-size system with one-dimensional topological
modes, the ETI is non-zero but does not assume quantized integer values. This is because
extended one-dimensional boundary states are distributed along the edges of the system
(rather than being tightly localized), making it difficult for regions A and B to fully cover
all the lattice sites where their states are distributed. As a result, the topological charac-
teristics cannot be accurately captured. Therefore, this method is suitable for analyzing
strongly localized zero-dimensional bound states, such as higher-order topological corner
states. In such cases, the ETI not only detects the presence of these states but also quanti-
5

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

tatively corresponds to their number, allowing it to serve as a robust topological invariant
for characterizing higher-order topological phases.
Regarding interactions, the ETI remains meaningful in weak interacting systems, pro-
vided the protecting symmetries and bulk gap are preserved. Here, the many-body ground
state remains adiabatically connected to its non-interacting counterpart, and the topological
bound modes persist until a phase transition occurs, with their presence faithfully encoded
in the entanglement structure. In contrast, strong interactions may qualitatively modify
the entanglement properties: for example, they can gap out corner modes via spontaneous
symmetry breaking or fractionalize them into emergent excitations. In such regimes, the
correlation-matrix approach becomes inadequate, and a more general many-body treatment
using the entanglement spectrum or symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy is required.
In the revised manuscript, we have added a paragraph to discuss both the applicability
of the theory for interacting systems and its future prospects. (See page 5, column 1, para-
graph 2).
Building on the discussion above regarding the applicability of ETI to zero-dimensional
bound states, we emphasize that our method is general and can also be applied to both
one-dimensional and three-dimensional systems. The core principle of the ETI, which is
based on the entanglement between highly localized zero-dimensional bound states, is inde-
pendent of the system’s dimensionality.
