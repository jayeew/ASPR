(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors investigated the roles of different Bacteroides strains in depression and their underlying gut-brain
axis mechanisms. The results demonstrated that B. uniformis increased susceptibility to depression in mice, whereas B.
thetaiotaomicron and B. vulgatus alleviated depressive-like behaviors. These effects may be closely associated with the
modulation of Th1/Th17 cell differentiation and alterations in hippocampal inflammatory cytokine levels. 
I have some

statistical notation.

experimental requirements? 
3) Figure 1: In vitro experiments, inactivated bacteria were used to stimulate DC cells. However, inactivated bacteria may not

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Li et al investigates the roles of Bacteroides uniformis (B. uniformis), Bacteroides vulgatus (B. vulgatus), and
Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (B. thetaiotaomicron) in major depressive disorder. B. uniformis was found to increase
susceptibility to depression in mice, enhancing Th17 cell differentiation in vivo and in vitro, and upregulating the expression
of hippocampal cytokines, including IL-17A. Conversely, B. thetaiotaomicron and B. vulgatus attenuated depressive
behaviors in mice, significantly reducing the differentiation of Th1 and Th17 cells in vivo, and lowering the levels of
hippocampal cytokines. 
While the integration of mice studies, behavioral assays and flow cytometry analyses is commendable and reveals
interesting aspects of a potential gut-Th1/Th17 cells-brain axis, the study is limited by the absence of a clearly defined
underlying mechanism. 
Major comments: 
1) The study did not utilize germ-free mice; instead, conventional C57BL/6J mice were treated with an antibiotic cocktail
prior to gavage with the Bacteroides strains. Can the authors confidently exclude the presence of residual endogenous
bacteria that may have interacted with the introduced strains? Furthermore, was the administered Bacteroides strain
dominant within the gut microbial community at the end of the experiment? To address these concerns, the authors should
perform, at a minimum, amplicon sequencing to assess the composition and relative abundance of bacterial taxa under the
different experimental conditions, and dominance of the experimental Bacteroides strains introduced. 
Of note, mice experiments were conducted by using the type strains B. uniformis ATCC8492, B. thetaiotaomicron
ATCC29148, and B. vulgatus ATCC8482, that may not be fully adapted to persist in the mouse gut despite administered
every second day for 7 days. 
2) An lines 227–240, the authors describe methods for metagenomic sequencing of fecal samples from “participants.”
However, no corresponding results are presented in the manuscript, raising the question of whether some data were omitted.
This reviewer could not find any mention of outcomes related to this analysis. If these results were meant to be included, the
methods section should be expanded to provide essential details, including whether the sequencing was amplicon-based or
shotgun metagenomics, and a clear description of the bioinformatic pipeline used for processing and analyzing the raw
reads. 
3) As the in vitro experiments show that even heat-killed B. uniformis and B. thetaiotaomicron could modulate Th1 and Th17
cell differentiation, suggesting that this regulatory effect may be mediated by structural components, such as LPS,
lipoproteins, and capsular polysaccharides, rather than exclusively relying on microbial metabolites. To verify this
hypothesis, this reviewer strongly recommends generating deletion mutants to help pinpoint the underlying mechanism.
Alternatively, metabolomic profiling could be performed to assess differences in metabolite abundance between control and
experimental mice, further strengthening the hypothesis that the stimulatory or inhibitory effects do not depend on
metabolites.

Response: Thanks for your thoughtful suggestion. In our in vitro experiments, we used

approach has also been widely applied in previous studies 1,2, ensuring both safety and 
reproducibility of the experiments.

 We focused on the hippocampus

Response: Thank you for pointing this out. We acknowledge that the sample size of 13

Comment 8. The human immune system maintains homeostasis through two key 
regulatory axes: the Th1/Th2 balance and the Th17/Treg balance. However, the study 
primarily focused on Th1 and Th17 cells, with limited attention to Th2 and Treg 
subsets. Could the authors clarify it? 
Response: Thank you for this valuable comment. While both the Th1/Th2 and 
Th17/Treg axes are important for immune homeostasis, current evidence in depression 
has more consistently implicated Th1 and Th17 cells 8,9,13,14. In line with this, our 
cellular and animal experiments mainly showed significant alterations in Th1 - and 
Th17-related responses, whereas Th2 and Treg subsets exhibited mostly non-significant 
changes. Therefore, we focused on Th1 and Th17 cells in the pre sent study, while 
acknowledging that future work should further explore the potential roles of Th2 and 
Treg cells. 
Comment 9. The study demonstrated that the three Bacteroides strains modulated 
Th1/Th17 cell differentiation, but inconsistencies were observed between in vitro and 
in vivo experiments. Please discuss potential explanations for these discrepancies. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful comment. In our study, B. 
uniformis and B. thetaiotaomicron  exerted consistent effects on Th1/Th17 
differentiation both in vitro and in vivo, whereas B. vulgatus showed divergent results. 
Specifically, B. vulgatus did not significantly promote Th1/Th17 differentiation in vitro

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

but did so in vivo. As reported previously, B. vulgatus lipopolysaccharide functions as 
a weak TLR4 agonist 15, eliciting limited inflammatory signaling. We therefore 
speculate that its weak activation of dendritic cells may result in insufficient production 
of key cytokines (e.g., IL -12, IL -6, IL -23), thereby failing to induce Th1/Th17 
polarization in vitro. In c ontrast, the in vivo environment is more complex and may 
amplify its immunomodulatory potential through indirect mechanisms, such as 
alterations in host metabolites or other microenvironmental factors. These possibilities 
will be addressed in future studies. 
Comment 10. The study explored how the three Bacteroides strains regulated 
Th1/Th17 cell differentiation, but the exact molecular mechanisms remain unclear. 
Further experiments (e.g., metabolomics) are needed to elucidate these mechanisms. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. Consistent with the 
experimental results, clinical analyses revealed significant associations between B. 
uniformis and B. thetaiotaomicron and peripheral Th17 cell frequencies. To dissect the 
mechanisms underlying this immunomodulation, we performed high -throughput 
targeted quantitative metabolomic profiling of fecal samples in mice  (see revised 
Figure S9A-B and S10A-B). We foud that both live and heat-killed B. uniformis and B. 
thetaiotaomicron significantly altered fecal metabolite profiles. B. uniformis reduced 
butyric acid and cholesterol sulfate—metabolites that suppress Th1  and Th17 
differentiation—whereas B. thetaiotaomicron increased butyric acid, propionic acid, 
and biotin, which also inhibit Th1 and Th17 polarization 16-23. Moreover, previous 
studies have reported that B. vulgatus contributes to an increase in propionic acid levels 
in the brain24, suggesting its potential involvement in Th1 and Th17-related metabolic 
regulation. Collectively, these findings indicate that the distinct effects of the three 
Bacteroides species on Th1 and Th17 differentiation may involve both structural 
components and strain- specific modulation of host metabolism. Relevant results (see 
revised Results, Line 484–506) and discussion have been added to the revised 
manuscript (see revised Discussion, Line 647–666).
