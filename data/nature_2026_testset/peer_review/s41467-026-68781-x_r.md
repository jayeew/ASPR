Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The current manuscript presents a development of the IPOND technique aimed at capturing the proteome at UVC damage
repair sites (IPOND-Repair). This is an interesting new technique that expands the arsenal of methods to study DNA repair
in the context of chromatin, and could be further applied to explore the proteome at sites of diverse DNA lesions that require
DNA synthesis for their resolution. Using IPOND-R, the authors identify ~250 proteins enriched at UVC damage sites (one-
hour post-irradiation). These include several histone chaperones (DNAJC9, MCM2, CAF1, FACT…), nucleosome
remodelling enzymes and histone modifiers. The work then focuses on the function of DNAJC9 and MCM2 at UVC damage
sites. Chromatin is dynamically remodelled at UVC damage sites, including the transient displacement and recovery of ‘old’
histones, and the deposition of ‘new’ histones. A full picture of which histone chaperones are involved in this remodelling of
chromatin is missing, and this work represents an important advance. Here, the authors show that DNAJC9 is required for
incorporation of new histones H3 (H3.1 and H3.3 variants) at UVC damage sites (as well as their genome-wide
incorporation); and that DNAJC9 and MCM2 similarly function in the recovery of old histone H3.3 levels at UVC repair sites.
Together, the work implicates DNAJC9 and MCM2 in the maintenance of chromatin structure at UVC damage sites, without
affecting the actual repair of UV lesions. 
The main point that would greatly strengthen the manuscript is a deeper mechanistic understanding of how DNAJC9 and
MCM2 function at sites of DNA damage. First, the establishment of a functional rescue system will be key to unequivocally
assign the function of DNAJC9 and MCM2 during chromatin remodelling at UVC damage sites. Second, as also pointed out
by the authors, based on the current literature a number of mechanisms can be invoked to explain how DNAJC9 and MCM2
accumulate at sites of DNA damage to promote histone incorporation. These could be direct binding to histone H3-H4
(histones evicted from chromatin could become ‘visible’ to these chaperones?); interaction of MCM2 with other CMG
components; regulation of ‘damaged’ chromatin binding by the J domain of DNAJC9. Importantly, point mutants that disrupt
these interactions have all been described. The contribution of these mechanisms could be tested in a rescue system, and
would transform our understanding of how DNAJC9 and MCM2 function at DNA repair sites. 
Other points: 
Line 116: Figure S1E only shows XPB recruitment, not ‘UV damage dependent factors’, so the text should be amended
accordingly. 
Fig. 2D is not very informative, particularly the ‘reference number’ for the gene ontology terms. Can this panel be reshaped? 
How do the authors explain the difference in proteins retrieved by aniFOUND as compared to IPOND-R (Fig. 3A)? adding a
short explanation in the text would be helpful for readers to compare the two techniques. And how is the overlap between
IPOND-R and IPOND (untreated conditions)? 
In Figure 4, the pattern of H3.1 incorporation into chromatin is used to define whether cells are in S phase or not. However,
given that loss of DNAJC9 decreases new H3.1-SNAP incorporation and the percentage of S-phase cells (Fig. S5G-I), how
reliable is the observed H3.1 pattern? Can the authors test whether DNAJC9-depleted cells still consistently show the
expected ‘S-phase’ (early, mid, late) patterns for H3.1-SNAP, p150 and/or EdU? This would validate the use of H3.1-SNAP
patterns as a marker of S-phase in Fig. 4.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper by Plessier et al, describes a new proteomic method to isolate factors accumulating on newly synthesized DNA
after UV DNA damage, named IPOND-R. After validation of the method, the authors focus on analyzing the effect of
depletion of DNAJC9 on the dynamics of new and old histones at DNA damage sites. The authors find that DNAJC9
controls the dynamics of both histone populations, but does not affect DNA damage repair per se (i.e. recruitment of repair
proteins, or survival after DNA damage). The authors conclude by suggesting an interplay between MCM2 and DNAJC9 to
control chromatin dynamics at UV damage sites. 
The paper contains high quality analyses of the effect of DNAJC9 depletion, and also provides a well validated new
approach to study the proteome of DNA damage repair sites. In general, the findings are interesting and may attract attention
from several research communities involved in DNA repair and chromatin biology. However, there are few concerns that
need to be addressed before publication. 
The most critical issue is that it is not clear that the effects deescribed are direct and related to DNA damage repair, rather
than indirect effects from the cells going through S-phase without critical histone chaperones. DNAJC9, MCM2 as well as
CAF-1 and HIRA are critical histone chaperones and they will alter chromatin dynamics independently of DNA damage, as
previously shown. Because the authors do not see an effect of DNAJC9 on DNA repair or survival after damage, it could be
that the authors are simply measuring changes in chromatin dynamics that are not dependent on DNA damage, but related
to global chromatin alterations. This is due in part to the experimental setup of the assay: the use of siRNA and the
conditions used to inhibit DNA replication. Controls should be added to address this concern. 
1) The authors run many important controls to validate the IPOND-R approach to minimize the contribution of DNA
replication in the dataset. However, treatment of cells with HU and Ara-C to inhibit replicative synthesis also stresses these
cells significantly. I wonder if the authors could use synchronization of the cells in G1/S by Palbociclib (which is known to not
induce stress) to conclusively demonstrate the power of the method. Running IPOND-R and the follow up (key) experiments
in Palbociclib arrested cells will provide important confirmation that DNA replication 
and S-phase mechanisms are not at
play in the chromatin dynamics presented here. 
2) 
As the authors deplete their proteins with siRNA, the cells are cycling in the absence of the target proteins for at least one
full cell cycle. As the authors are targeting critical histone chaperones (DNAJC9, MCM2, CAF-1 and HIRA), it is expected
that chromatin is already affected at the time of DNA damage induction. The authors should find ways to circumvent this
experimental limitation (i.e. use rapid degron-based degradation or test their key findings in G1 arrested cells). 
3) In line 228-229 it is stated that the quantification of Figure 4B focuses on non S-phase cells. It is not clear to this reviewer
how one can reliably distinguish S and non S-phase cells from new H3.1 distribution (I can only imagine a difference
between early and late S-phase). I expect a difference could be in the total amount of new H3.1 (should be significantly
higher in S-phase cells), but not clear how the distribution can alone explain S vs non-S phase cells. At any rate, the authors
should include a quantification for S-phase cells to test if there is a difference. Could the authors use EdU to distinguish
various populations otherwise? Or synchronize with Palbociclib as suggested above? 
4) The involvement of MCM2 is also unusual, without evoking the passage of DNA replication forks. The authors do not
really mention this, but this needs to be addressed/discussed. How can they envision MCM2 working at these sites, or
together with DNAJC9, outside of DNA replication? Hypotheses need to be tested to clarify their data, which is very
preliminary in the current form. 
5) In Figure 1D, the authors should probe for DNA replication proteins to show that they are not pulled down (e.g. Cdc45,
GINS, or fork protection complex components). 
6) The authors should also show the overlap between IPOND-R and previously reported IPOND hits (in Figure 2), to show
how many of the fork proteins are present in the data. 
There is an instance of “data not shown” in the discussion (lines 339-340). These data should be shown, or the statement
needs to be removed. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study investigates chromatin dynamics during UV damage repair, focusing on identifying proteins that regulate histone
deposition at damaged sites. The authors modified the IPOND proteomic method, naming it IPOND-R, to analyze protein
dynamics specific to repair sites. Through this approach, they identified DNAJC9 and MCM2, which had not previously been
linked to UV-induced damage. DNAJC9 is suggested to facilitate the deposition of new histones and recovery of parental

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

histones, while MCM2 is proposed to collaborate in these processes to maintain chromatin integrity. This finding adds to the
understanding of the dynamic regulation of chromatin following DNA damage. However, several issues undermine the
study's conclusions. Many results lack appropriate controls, and the experimental descriptions are insufficiently detailed,
making the findings difficult to interpret and raising concerns about their reliability. Furthermore, the logical connections
between key results are unclear. These shortcomings lead to ambiguous conclusions, and some interpretations appear to
extend beyond what the data can support. 
Major: 
1. Based on the proposed model of chromatin repair (Figure 6F), it appears that DNA breaks are repaired first, followed by
histone deposition to restore chromatin structure. However, the authors did not assess when DNA repair is complete.
Notably, the persistence of γH2AX after 24 hours suggests that repair may not be fully resolved, and DNAJC9 is no longer
present at the damage sites at this time point. Furthermore, while DNAJC9 is transiently present at UVC damage sites, the
authors report no evidence of its involvement in DNA repair. These findings raise important questions about the proposed
model and the role of DNAJC9 at DNA damage sites. 
To clarify these points and strengthen the model, the authors should provide additional details, including: 
• Evidence indicating when DNA repair is complete. 
• Whether the loss of DNAJC9 affects transcription recovery near break sites following UVC damage. 
Addressing these points will help establish a clearer connection between DNA repair, histone deposition, and the function of
DNAJC9 in the context of DNA damage. 
2. The authors treated cells with 10 mM HU and 1 mM AraC to inhibit DNA replication, raising several concerns: 
• While AraC can inhibit DNA replication, it is also incorporated into repair sites, potentially causing repair defects. This
issue is not addressed in the manuscript, undermining the validity of the experimental approach. 
• The use of 1 mM AraC is an extremely high concentration for dividing cells, especially since concentrations as low as 40
µM can be toxic to some post-mitotic cells. The observation that cells accumulate in the G1 phase even 24 hours post-
treatment with DNA synthesis inhibitors suggests that the inhibitors themselves may have induced significant cellular stress. 
Given these concerns, we recommend replacing HU+AraC with other inhibitors, such as G1 arrest inhibitors (eg.
Pablociclib), which would allow the cells to arrest in G1 phase and prevent EdU incorporation during DNA replication. This
adjustment would enhance the reliability of the approach and reduce potential confounding effects. 
3. In Figure 5B, siXPG should prevent UV lesion removal, thereby halting DNA repair. However, the data still show a
recovery of old H3.3, which is confusing. If DNA repair is blocked, it would be expected to also impact histone deposition.
The authors should clarify how histone deposition occurs independently of DNA repair in this context and provide additional
insights into DNAJC9's function. 
Minor: 
1. To identify proteins that transiently bind to chromatin during repair, the authors compared IPOND-R results at 1-hour and
24-hour post UV time points. However, this approach does not rule out the possibility that cells with the most severe damage
at 1 hour post-UV may not survive until the 24-hour time point, thus create bias. To address these concerns, the authors
should perform EdU pull-down sequencing to confirm that the EdU-incorporated sites at 24 hours are comparable in
abundance and distribution to those at 1 hour post-UV treatment. 
2. The analysis in Figures 4 and 5 presents data at 20 minutes, 2 hours, and 8 hours post-UVC exposure without providing
any explanation for this shift, despite earlier data focusing on 1 hour post-UVC. This raises questions about whether
DNAJC9 remains present at UV damage sites at these time points in the control. To address these issues, the authors
should provide a clear rationale for the time-point selection. 
3. The IF results for new H3.3-SNAP in siDNAJC9#3 in Figures 4C and 4H display inconsistent deposition patterns
(completely absent in one instance and still present in another), which raises concerns about data reliability. The authors
should use figures displaying multiple cells instead of single cells to ensure greater consistency and reliability in the
presentation of the data. 
4. There is no description of how the survival assay was performed. It is unclear whether the UVC dose of 10 J/m² causes
85% cell death within 24 hours, 48 hours, or over the course of a week, which creates confusion and raises questions about
the experimental conditions. 
In light of these concerns, the paper's conclusions about DNAJC9's role in chromatin repair appear premature and not fully
supported by the data presented. A substantial revision addressing these issues would be necessary to strengthen the
study's findings and interpretations. 
Reviewer #5 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Plessier et al presents an innovative proteomic approach to capture chromatin in the process of UVC irradiation repair based
on the IPOND protocol (IPOND-R). This protocol relies on the complete inhibition of DNA replication such that EdU is only
incorporated during UV-damage coupled DNA repair processes. As can be seen in Figure 1B, the inhibition of DNA

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

replication with HU and Ara-C combined is highly effective. Furthermore, the post UVC repair EdU signal is similar in non S-
phase cells when not treated with these inhibitors (Figure 1C). These are important controls since small amounts of DNA
replication could significantly skew the interpretation of the resulting proteomic analyses from these experiments. Whilst the
proteomic datasets have merit, there should be a more detailed comparison and benchmarking of them compared to
classical iPOND experiments. For example, by comparing them to the datasets in Sirbu et al JBC VOL. 288, NO. 44, pp.
31458 –3146 (2013). This would help to highlight the differences to factors identified at the replication fork to those enriched
at sites of UV damage repair. 
Beyond this, I was not sufficiently convinced by the follow-up studies whereby DNAJC9 was selected as the key factor in
this process despite being close to the cut-off for statistical significance in the proteomic datasets. The most convincing
aspect of this was that depletion of dNAJC9 causes a loss of new SNAP tagged H3.3/H3.1. In the UV irradiated
experiments, the signal of DNAJC9 and MCM2 at irradiated sites was not convincing, no easily observable foci are formed.
Furthermore, since this could also be explained by bleed through from the GFP channel into the red channel, and I was not
able to find evidence that this has been correctly controlled for, this calls into doubt the validity of conclusions made based
on this approach – thus at this stage it is unclear of the significance of many findings. Furthermore, the statistical handling of
data is questionable. Whilst microscopy experiments have been performed with three biological replicates, paired t-tests
appear to use all data points from these experiments rather than the three means of the biological replicates. This is a very
confusing way to present the data and makes its difficult to assess how robust the conclusions made are. For example, are
the changes in Figure 5 really strong enough to conclude that DNAJC9 has a role in old histone recovery/histone recycling? 
Together, whilst I believe this is a promising story, it is not yet sufficiently developed for publication in Nature Comms.

Reviewer #1: 
The current manuscript presents a development of the IPOND technique aimed at 
capturing the proteome at UVC damage repair sites (IPOND-Repair). This is an 
interesting new technique that expands the arsenal of methods to study DNA repair in 
the context of chromatin, and could be further applied to explore the proteome at sites 
of diverse DNA lesions that require DNA synthesis for their resolution. Using IPOND-
R, the authors identify ~250 proteins enriched at UVC damage sites (one-hour post-
irradiation). These include several histone chaperones (DNAJC9, MCM2, CAF1, 
FACT…), nucleosome remodelling enzymes and histone modifiers. The work then 
focuses on the function of DNAJC9 and MCM2 at UVC damage sites. Chromatin is 
dynamically remodelled at UVC damage sites, including the transient displacement 
and recovery of ‘old’ histones, and the deposition of ‘new’ histones. A full picture of 
which histone chaperones are involved in this remodelling of chromatin is missing, and 
this work represents an important advance. Here, the authors show that DNAJC9 is 
required for incorporation of new histones H3 (H3.1 and H3.3 variants) at UVC damage 
sites (as well as their genome-wide incorporation); and that DNAJC9 and MCM2 
similarly function in the recovery of old histone H3.3 levels at UVC repair sites. 
Together, the work implicates DNAJC9 and MCM2 in the maintenance of chromatin 
structure at UVC damage sites, without affecting the actual repair of UV lesions. 
The main point that would greatly strengthen the manuscript is a deeper mechanistic 
understanding of how DNAJC9 and MCM2 function at sites of DNA damage. First, the 
establishment of a functional rescue system will be key to unequivocally assign the 
function of DNAJC9 and MCM2 during chromatin remodelling at UVC damage sites.  
We had established a functional rescue system for DNAJC9 (Supplementary Fig. 6d 
now with 3 experiments instead of 2) and we now provide a rescue experiment also for 
MCM2 showing that re-expression of wild-type MCM2 restores at least partially new 
H3.3 deposition at UV sites (Suppl Fig. 7f). 
Second, as also pointed out by the authors, based on the current literature a number 
of mechanisms can be invoked to explain how DNAJC9 and MCM2 accumulate at sites 
of DNA damage to promote histone incorporation. These could be direct binding to 
histone H3-H4 (histones evicted from chromatin could become ‘visible’ to these 
chaperones?); interaction of MCM2 with other CMG components; regulation of 
‘damaged’ chromatin binding by the J domain of DNAJC9. Importantly, point mutants 
that disrupt these interactions have all been described. The contribution of these 
mechanisms could be tested in a rescue system, and would transform our 
understanding of how DNAJC9 and MCM2 function at DNA repair sites.
