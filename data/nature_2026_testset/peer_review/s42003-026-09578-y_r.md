Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Mendez-Ruette et al. investigate the membrane-binding ability of alpha-SNAP M105I, a missense mutation
identified in naturally occurring mutant mice. 
Using molecular modeling and simulation, they reveal structural
rearrangements in the mutant protein, including concealment of the N-terminal hydrophobic loop and weakened lipid
interactions. These findings were further supported by in vitro membrane binding assays and in vivo subcellular distribution
analyses. Together, the work demonstrates that M105I mutation reduces alpha-SNAP’s membrane-binding capacity through
rearrangement of its N-terminal hydrophobic loop. 
The interaction between alpha-SNAP and membrane is vigorously explored through molecular simulations, with results and
methods clearly explained, and the protein-lipid interactions validated in both in vitro and in vivo contexts. Overall, the
manuscript achieves its stated objective. However, several concerns limit my ability to recommend acceptance in its current
form. 
Major Concerns: 
1. Scientific scope: 
This study convincingly shows that the M105I mutation reduces membrane-binding affinity. However, it would be valuable to
expand the investigation by experimentally testing additional critical residues identified in the modeling, such as F27, F28,
Q76, and L75, in both WT and M105I α-SNAP. Such experiments would not only validate the simulations but also provide
stronger mechanistic support. 
2. Figure 4c: 
A plasma membrane marker (e.g., SYP-mCherry) is required to confirm the presence of cell membranes. 
3. Figure 5 g-i: 
A plasma membrane marker is also needed to enable proper quantification of the protein distribution at the cell periphery. 
Minor points: 
1. Several abbreviations should be defined upon first use, with brief explanations provided. Examples include RMSD values
and the eABF method 
2. Clarify what “[SMA5]” represents in Figure 5i. 
3. In Figure 6f, the label “O.D.” requires clarification. Does it refer to the quantification of Figure 6e? The legend states that
Figure 6e was analyzed by silver staining—why was silver staining chosen over western blotting for detecting α-SNAP on
liposomes? Please justify this methodological choice. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Méndez-Ruette et al provided strong evidence that the M105I (hyh) mutation in α-SNAP impairs its function by
reducing its affinity for membrane lipids, specifically by concealing a critical N-terminal hydrophobic loop. These results

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

address the central question it set out to answer: why the mutant protein functions normally in lipid-free in vitro assays but is
defective in a cellular context. 
Major Points: 
1) What is the exact molecular mechanism by which the M105I mutation, located in a TPR domain, causes the
conformational change that buries the distant N-terminal hydrophobic loop? The paper shows that the mutation causes
global compaction and hiding of the F27/F28 loop but does not elucidate the allosteric pathway or precise structural
perturbations that link residue 105 to the N-terminus. 
2) Does the M105I mutation affect α-SNAP's interactions with non-SNARE protein partners? The mutation is located on the
convex face of the TPR domain, which is implicated in protein-protein interactions unrelated to SNAREs (e.g., with Gα12 or
AMPK). The gain-of-function in AMPK phosphatase activity mentioned in the discussion suggests it does, but these
interactions and their functional consequences were not explored in this study. 
3) Is the lipid-binding defect specific to certain lipid compositions or membrane properties? The study used total PM-derived
lipids. α-SNAP's binding might be influenced by specific lipids (e.g., phosphoinositides), cholesterol content, or membrane
curvature. The specificity of the interaction remains unexplored. 
4) Does the parallel membrane orientation adopted by the M105I mutant prevent it from adopting the correct conformation for
its role in the 20S complex? The paper suggests the perpendicular orientation of the WT protein is important for its
symmetric arrangement around the SNARE bundle. It remains a hypothesis whether the mutant's parallel orientation directly
impedes this specific function.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript addresses an important question regarding how the pathogenic M105I mutation in α-SNAP affects its
interaction with the lipids. Using in silico modeling and MD simulations, the authors suggest that exposure of two residues,
F27 and F28, in the hydrophobic loop is critical for stable interaction with the membrane surface. In the M105I mutant, these

Méndez-Ruette et al.: We appreciate the reviewer's critical observation about the importance of different residues in the α-SNAP-membrane interaction. Our work was designed to specifically investigate the molecular and functional consequences of the M105I mutation associated with the hyh phenotype. The aim of the study was not to perform a comprehensive mutational screen but to validate the consequences of M105I through multiple complementary approaches (in silico, in vitro, and in vivo). Extending the analysis to additional point mutations would represent a distinct experimental framework, requiring new constructs, expression, purification, and validation pipelines, which fall beyond the scope of this work.  On the other hand, F27, F28, L75, and Q76 residues were not identified as novel membrane-binding residues through our modeling. Rather, these residues served as structural reference points to track protein orientation and conformation during simulations. F27 and F28 were selected as critical reference points of the hydrophobic loop, based on the previous work of Winter et al. (2009), who described that mutations of these phenylalanine residues (α-SNAP F27S, F28S), impairs α-SNAP-membrane interactions. On the other hand, L75 and Q76 were monitored because our simulations revealed that when the M105I mutant does interact with membranes (albeit weakly), it adopts an aberrant membrane semi-parallel orientation that brings α3-helix (with these residues) into proximity with the bilayer instead of the canonical F27/F28-mediated perpendicular interaction. In that sense, the F27 and F28 in hydrophobic loop, and the L75 and Q76 residues in the last part of the α3-helix, were used as structural readouts to evaluate how the M105I mutation perturbs the orientation of α-SNAP relative to the membrane surface, rather than as independent experimental targets. Thus, while these residues provided valuable insight into the structural consequences of the mutation, they were not individually mutated or tested experimentally in this study.  We have explained better the use of these residues as reference points:  (see lines 130-133): Moreover, analysis of the radial distribution function, g(r), for the final 3 μs of the 20 μs refinement simulation revealed increased solvent exposure of residues F27 and F28 (reference points of the hydrophobic loop) in the WT structures compared to the M105I mutant (Supplementary information, Fig. S2c).  (see lines 164-176): Importantly, the WT protein exhibits marked conformational flexibility, transiently extending the N-terminal loop and exposing residues F27/F28 in the absence of membranes (Supplementary information, Fig. S3a-b, top). This dynamic rearrangement increases the solvent accessibility of these aromatic residues, positioning them in a conformation that is competent for potential membrane engagement. In contrast, the M105I mutant maintains the hydrophobic loop (F27/F28 residues) in a consistently buried state throughout the simulations, failing to adopt the extended loop conformation characteristic of the WT structure. Instead, the mutant preferentially exposes the helix α3 (L75/Q76 residues as reference points), which is a surface less favorable for membrane association due to its weaker hydrophobic character (Supplementary information, Fig. S3a-b, bottom). Consistent with these structural differences, the electrostatic potential surfaces of both proteins reveal predominantly neutral-to-positive patches in the regions that would interface with membranes, supporting the notion that

Méndez-Ruette et al.: We appreciate the reviewer's critical question regarding the allosteric mechanism linking the M105I mutation to the N-terminal conformational changes. We have now conducted additional analyses evaluating the effect of M105I mutation on TPR structures. The results of these analyses have been included in the Table S1 and in the manuscript:  (see new Table 3 and lines 147-163): To investigate the structural basis for the conformational changes observed in the M105I mutant, we analyzed the solvent accessibility of residue 105 throughout the refinement simulations. The M105I mutation significantly reduced the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) of position 105 compared to WT (WT: 0.62 ± 0.04 nm² vs. M105I: 0.32 ± 0.12 nm²; mean ± SD, measured during the last 3 μs of 20 μs refinement MD simulations and averaged across three replicas). This decrease indicates that the more hydrophobic isoleucine side chain becomes buried within the protein interior, positioning itself between helices α5 and α6.  To understand how this local perturbation propagates through the protein structure, we analyzed inter-helical angles throughout the TPR-like domain (twisted sheet; helices α1-α9) during the same time period (Supplementary information, Table S1). The burial of the isoleucine at position 105 causes a local expansion of the α4-α5 angle from 18.83° ± 4.66° in WT to 24.61° ± 3.22° in the mutant. This distortion propagates to adjacent N-terminal helical interfaces, increasing the α3-α4 angle from 22.18° ± 4.26° to 29.88° ± 3.91°. However, the α1-α2 junction shows compensatory compaction, with the angle decreasing from 33.00° ± 7.87° to 18.91° ± 7.69°. Remarkably, no significant changes were observed towards the C-terminal region of the twisted sheet (α5-α6 to α8-α9 angles). These coordinated structural changes -expansion in the middle region of the TPR-like domain and compaction at the N-terminal region- disrupt the geometric arrangement of the twisted sheet structure.  To evaluate how changes in TPR disposition could induce alterations affecting the orientation of the hydrophobic loop -and thereby the membrane-binding capacity of α-SNAP-, we analyzed the positioning of this loop at different stages of the simulation, as well as the electrostatic potential of the protein. This analysis provided insights into the polarity and apolarity features required for membrane association. These findings led to the inclusion of a new Fig. S3 from the refinement of models and the new Fig. S6 in the presence of membrane.   Membrane interaction appears predominantly hydrophobic in nature, as evidenced by the neutral/slightly positive electrostatic potential in the membrane-interacting regions of both WT and M105I systems (new Fig. S3). association due to its weaker hydrophobic character (Supplementary information, Fig. S3a-b, bottom).
