Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study presents a very exciting case of the dynamic evolution of sex determination and sex chromosome turnover. The
authors provide careful analyses and guide the reader through the layers of complexity in this system. They uncover a
classic scenario for the evolution of sex-determining genes: a newly duplicated gene involved in gonadal differentiation
becomes transposed, driving the emergence of new sex chromosomes. 
What is particularly exciting in this case is that the master sex-determining (MSD) gene appears to be evolutionarily young
and is still undergoing repeated duplication and translocation, facilitated by transposable elements. The authors also
present a cautionary tale about how reliance on a single reference genome can lead to misidentification of the sex-
determining region (SDR). Their use of a haplotype-resolved assembly from the heterogametic sex nicely demonstrates the
advantages of such an approach for studying sex chromosome evolution. I enjoyed reading the manuscript, and I believe
this sentiment will be shared by others working on sex determination and sex chromosome evolution. 
That said, I believe there are several major issues that need to be addressed before the manuscript is suitable for
publication. While the abstract is enticing, not all of its claims are substantiated in the main text. 
Demonstration of the sex-determining role of tesDwa and tesDwb: 
The functional study the authors performed was for the autosomal tesD gene in a sister species. While that experiment
clearly shows that the autosomal tesD, which lacks a vertebrate ortholog, is necessary for testis development in amphioxus,
it does not demonstrate that the W-linked duplicates (tesDwa, tesDwb) play a role in female sex determination. The authors
provide only their W-linkage as evidence. Key information needed to assess their role in sex determination. Such as
expression timing and tissue specificity, is missing. Since the authors show these duplicates are substantially diverged from
the autosomal copy, could they at least report whether these are expressed at embryonic stages before sex differentiation,
and in which tissues? Without this, I do not think the current data support the conclusion that these are bona fide sex-
determining genes. 
Transposon-mediated mobilization of MSD genes: 
This mechanism has been well documented in salmonids (Yano et al., 2012; 2013; Lubieniecki et al., 2015; Faber-
Hammond et al., 2015, among others). While this may reduce the novelty of the current findings, the authors should still cite
these studies and discuss the convergence between the systems. For example, in Chinook salmon, one sdy copy is non-
functional, and Bertho et al. (2022) explored why. 
Males carrying tesDwb: 
The authors report that some males carry tesDwb in a different genomic location. But how does genomic location alone
render tesDwb non-functional? The authors promote a model in which transposition of tesD duplicates drives sex
chromosome turnover—so why wouldn't the transposition of tesDwb to an autosome result in a new sex-determining
system? This contradiction needs to be clarified. 
Nature of tesDwa and tesDwb:

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

These are described as lncRNAs, but not all unspliced transcripts are lncRNAs. Additionally, the two mechanisms proposed
in lines 289–307 seem mutually exclusive. Could the authors clarify how these modes of action are reconciled? 
Balancing selection: 
The abstract mentions “balancing selection,” but I found no results supporting this claim. The discussion (lines 367–378)
raises interesting hypotheses, but such dynamics should be explicitly modeled or tested. 
Line 302–304: 
If misalignment is suspected, the authors should adjust their mapping parameters or at least report results with visual
evidence, instead of stating “data not shown.” 
Minor comments: 
Lines 379–381: The authors claim that sex chromosome heteromorphism is essential for dosage compensation or gene
repression. However, the results presented do not speak to this point, and I do not see how they challenge existing views on
the relationship between heteromorphism and dosage compensation. Please rephrase or remove this statement. 
Line 52: Possibly meant “polygenic” instead of “polymorphic”? 
Lines 95–97: The sentence is convoluted and could benefit from rewriting for clarity. 
Lines 265–266: The authors mention technical limitations in performing knockouts in amphioxus, yet earlier describe
successful CRISPR experiments in a closely related species. Please resolve this inconsistency in writing. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Li et al. presents a comprehensive investigation into the sex determination system of the amphioxus
Branchiostoma belcheri (Bb). The study is well-executed, combining state-of-the-art genomics, functional genetics, and
detailed bioinformatics to uncover a uniquely dynamic sex determination system in Bb. This work is novel, the data appear
to be of high quality, and the findings are of significant interest to the fields of evolutionary biology and genomics. The
manuscript is clearly written. However, to fully substantiate the conclusions, several major points should be addressed
before its acceptance. 
Major points: 
1. The manuscript suggests that lncRNAs from tesDwa/b repress the autosomal tesD, supported by correlations like female-
specific transcripts and chromatin accessibility differences. However, direct evidence for a causal mechanism is lacking. The
authors should acknowledge this limitation explicitly. To strengthen the claim, they could attempt experiments demonstrating
physical interaction. If this is not feasible, they should state that the data support a model of lncRNA-mediated repression
and note that the mechanism requires further work; 
2. The tesD knockout was carried out in B. floridae rather than B. belcheri. This raises a concern about species-specific
functional conservation. Given this, the authors should either (1) repeat the knockout in B. belcheri, or (2) temper conclusions
by clearly stating that functional conservation is inferred but not directly proven. Discussion of this limitation is essential; 
3. The authors propose that the tesDwb SDR is transposing, but there’s no direct evidence of transposition activity. The
authors could strengthen this point by providing data on TE expression or mobility, or they should rephrase the claim as a
hypothesis based on structural evidence; 
4. The discussion could explore why a stable system evolved, followed by a dynamic transposon-driven one. What are the
evolutionary implications of ongoing transposition? Address this balance between stability and innovation to improve the
manuscript’s conceptual depth. 
Minor points: 
1. Abstract: To avoid ambiguity, the abstract should explicitly state that the CRISPR/Cas9 experiment was performed in B.
floridae; 
2. Line 64-65: The sentence, "The tesDwb and its corresponding SDR on the autosomes is no longer transcribed and has
likely become non-functional," should be rephrased, perhaps as: "The transposed tesDwb copy and its associated SDR on
autosomes are not transcribed and have likely lost their function"; 
3. Line 357-358: The manuscript states that SDRs are "inserted into exons of the twai and vps9c genes, respectively". This
is inaccurate for vps9c, as Figure 2F and the main text show the insertion site is the 3' UTR. This should be corrected; 
4. Line 382: The proposal that balancing selection maintains the two SDRs is interesting but should be presented as a
plausible hypothesis unless formal population genetic tests can be performed; 
5. Figure 4C: The minor ATAC-seq peaks in type 'b' female ovaries are attributed to read misalignment from tesDwb. While
plausible, this slightly weakens the figure's visual impact. A supplementary figure showing that reads from a type 'b' female
map overwhelmingly to tesDwb and not the autosomal tesD would strengthen this point; 
6. The abbreviation for Saccoglossus kowalevskii (Sko) is used in a phylogenetic tree but is missing from the accompanying
list of abbreviations. This should be added.

pressures, population structure, and genomic context influence the equilibrium
