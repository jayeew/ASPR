(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present an intriguing study on the modulation of work function exceeding 1 eV in RuO
₂
/TiO
₂
 epitaxial
heterostructures, controlled by the thickness of the RuO
₂
 layer. The observed variation is attributed to interfacial polarization
induced by cation displacements, as revealed through electron ptychography. The manuscript is well-organized and the
experimental findings are of potential interest to researchers in oxide electronics and solid-state physics. 
However, I find that the current manuscript remains largely phenomenological. To meet the standards of Nature
Communications, I believe a deeper physical understanding is required. Specifically, I recommend the inclusion of
theoretical modeling or first-principles calculations to substantiate the proposed mechanisms. For instance, how does the
electronic density of states evolve in ultrathin RuO
₂
/TiO
₂
 heterostructures? Without such insight, the interpretation of the
experimental data remains speculative. Below, I outline several technical concerns that should be addressed: 
1. Oxygen Vacancy Formation at the Interface: 
The authors attribute the observed interfacial polarization to cation displacements. However, prior studies (e.g., Phys. Rev. B
86, 140102(R)) suggest that oxygen vacancies can induce similar effects in nominally non-polar oxides. Given the
significant lattice mismatch between RuO
₂
 and TiO
₂
, can the authors comment on the possibility of oxygen vacancy
formation at the interface? While post-annealing may suppress bulk vacancies, interfacial vacancies may persist and play a
critical role. 
2. Interpretation of Work Function Modulation (Fig. 2b): 
• Grounding Configuration: Fig. 2a indicates that the sample is grounded via the TiO
₂
 substrate, not the RuO
₂
 film.
Considering the higher work function of RuO
₂
 relative to the electron affinity of TiO
₂
, a Schottky barrier is expected at the
interface, especially for thicker RuO
₂
 films. This could impede electron transfer to ground. Yet, the KPFM data show high
signal-to-noise ratio. The authors should clarify the experimental setup and confirm that the measured signals originate from
RuO
₂
. Including data for a sample with RuO
₂
 thickness = 0 nm would be helpful. 
• Thickness Dependence: The abrupt increase in work function is attributed to the emergence of polarity in thin RuO
₂
 films.
However, the subsequent decrease in work function with increasing thickness—falling below the bulk value—is
counterintuitive. If bulk RuO
₂
 is nonpolar, one would expect the work function to asymptotically approach ~5.1 eV from
above. The authors should provide a physical explanation for this trend. 
3. Clarification of Figure 2e: 
The schematic for tRuO2>tc suggests the presence of an electric field throughout the film and polarization from the interface
to mid-film, implying surface charge accumulation. The authors should clarify the physical meaning of these illustrations and
distinguish between the cases more explicitly. 
4. Carrier Density Trends (Figure 4c): 
The observed minimum in 2D carrier density (n2D) as a function of RuO
₂
 thickness is not explained. Furthermore, the
increase in n2D from 4 nm to 17 nm suggests that the 3D carrier density (n3D=n2D/tRuO) also increases, without signs of
saturation. This implies partial carrier localization even at 17 nm thickness. The authors should discuss the possible origins
of this behavior and its correlation with the work function trend.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Referee report on Nature Communications manuscript NCOMMS-25-58504-T 
In this paper, the authors report on the realization of RuO2/TiO2 and TiO2/RuO2/TiO2 structures grown by oxide molecular
beam epitaxy, their characterization, and the study of some electronic properties. 
More precisely, advanced STEM analyses allow the polar displacements in TiO2 and at the TiO2/RuO2 interfaces to be
determined. The work function of RuO2 in RuO2/TiO2 structures is studied as a function of the RuO2 thickness using Kelvin
probe microscopy. Finally, transport properties are realized for both RuO2/TiO2 and TiO2/RuO2/TiO2 structures
(conductivity, mobility and Hall effect). 
The important finding is that the work function is found to depend on the thickness of the RuO2 layer in a non-monotonic
way. It increases a lot (>1eV) between 2 and 5nm before to decrease abruptly. The maximum work function is found to
correlate with the thickness at which the RuO2 layer relaxes. The STEM analyses show that a polarity is found in TiO2
layers and in the metallic RuO2 layer at the interfaces. This polar region does not extend over more than a few unit cells –
the electric field in this region is thought to modify the work function. 
This is a nice paper, interesting and well-written. The results are clearly presented and broadly interesting. I think the paper
can be published in Nature communications if the authors convincingly answer the comments and questions listed below. 
Comments and questions: 
-A TiO2 buffer layer is used on a TiO2 substrate. Is this necessary because of the unknown state of the substrate “polar”
surface? 
-STEM analyses. Can the authors provide the method used to measure the atom displacements in Figure 2c? Also, it seems
that the polarization at the bottom buffer TiO2 layer extends to the bottom edge of the reconstructed image. Does the
polarization also extends to the surface of the capping TiO2 layer? Furthermore, do the authors have information on what is
happening at the TiO2 buffer layer and TiO2 substrate interface? 
-The TiO2 layers are polar. I am not sure to understand the origin of this polarity. Is a single TiO2 layer on TiO2 found to be
polar or is this linked to the TiO2/RuO2 interface (although there is no polar discontinuity at the interface). 
-The authors talk about metallic RuO2 and metallic polar RuO2 regions (main text and conclusions). At the same time, the
transport analyses reveal the presence of dead layers (precisely the layer region that is polar). Should the phrasing be
modified? 
-The KPFM measurements reveal a linear increase of the work function and an anomalous enhancement between 2 and
5nm. What is the physical reason for the linear enhancement with RuO2 thickness? 
-In line with this question, the data demonstrate that the RuO2 films relax above 5-6nm. However, there is no marked change
in the work function linear increase with thickness (not taking into account the anomalous enhancement) – and no clear
change on the sheet conductance versus thickness – both seem unaffected by the strain relaxation. Is this understood? 
-One is also tempted to conclude from the data that the work function value can simply be tuned by thickness control of the
RuO2 layer. 
-A very important point that should be clarified. At 4-5nm thickness, there is an abrupt decrease of the work function. As I
understand, the authors propose that this abrupt decrease is linked to strain relaxation. But what is the reason for the
decrease? What is physically happening? Looking at Fig. 2e, for thick layers, the dipoles are still there – so what is affecting
the work function when strain is released? What is the explanation for the drop in the work function? 
-Following on this, do the authors have STEM and polar displacements profiles for “thick films” – something must be different
and this information ideally should allow the work function abrupt change to be understood. 
-The authors mention SHG measurements and conclude from a change in SHG intensity as a function of RuO2 thickness
that RuO2 becomes non-polar. I am not sure to understand this sinceTiO2 and the TiO2/RuO2 interface remain polar (Fig.
2e) – why would the SHG signal disappear? 
-In the conclusion the authors claimed that the intrinsic conductivity of the RuO2 layers in the heterostructure has
comparable value to bulk RuO2. Can the authors provide an explanation on why can the values of 15875 and 11700 be
comparable with 28400 (lines 218 and 225)? It seems like the values extracted are around half of the bulk value. 
-Finally, on Fig. 2c and 2d, the RuO2 film peaks are very difficult to spot – the Figure should be improved.

Dear Editor, 
In the manuscript entitled “Strain-Stabilized Interfacial Polarization Tunes Work Function Over 1 eV in RuO2/TiO2
Heterostructures”, the authors reported the interfacial polarization in metallic rutile RuO2 layers in epitaxial RuO2/TiO2
heterostructures. The interfacial polarization enables over 1 eV modulation of the RuO2 work function, as evident by the
KPFM. The polar metal state in RuO2 is an interesting topic. However, in my viewpoint, the main conclusions are not
supported by the provided experimental results. Moreover, some of my comments are close to the concerns raised by the
previous referees, yet the authors have not fully addressed them. Shortly, I can not recommend the current version of the
manuscript for publication in Nature communications. 
My main comments are: 
1. I question whether the RuO2 is polar. The authors claim that strain stabilizes a polar phase throughout the film (a
prerequisite for a "bulk polar phase"). However, quantitative STEM analysis in Figure 1d contradicts this. Significant polar
displacements (up to ~10 pm) are almost exclusively confined to the TiO2 layers. Inside the region marked as RuO2, the
displacements drop rapidly to near zero (0-2 pm). This observation invalidates the claim that the metallic RuO2 bulk sustains
a polar structural distortion. The phenomenon is strictly a localized interface dipole, not a bulk phase property. Also,
although there is measured SHG signal (which is weak), it is still not possible to determine whether the signal comes from
TiO2 or RuO2. 
2. The results of KPFM will be influenced strongly by surface absorption. 
3. the RSM results are poor. The lattice constants used for DFT calculation is 3.119

7. Misleading quantification of "1 eV" modulation (Substrate Effect vs. Tuning) 
The title and abstract claim to tune the work function of RuO2 by "Over 1 eV". However, Figure 2b shows that the work
function naturally increases from 4.25 eV (ultrathin limit, explicitly identified as close to the bulk TiO2 value) to 5.05 eV (bulk
RuO2 value). 
This baseline increase of 0.8 eV represents the natural transition from the substrate's electronic structure to the film's bulk
properties (band alignment), which would occur regardless of polarization. The actual modulation attributable to the specific
"strain-stabilized polarization" mechanism is effectively limited to the "overshoot" peak above the bulk value (approx. 5.40
eV peak - 5.05 eV bulk 0.35 eV). Attributing the entire transition range to polarization is scientifically misleading. The authors
must explicitly decouple the intrinsic thickness-dependent recovery from the polarization-induced shift. 
8. Invalid mechanism for work function dropping with strain relaxation). The authors attribute the sharp drop in work function
above 4 nm to strain relaxation (Fig. 2e). In heteroepitaxy, interfacial layers clamped to the substrate typically retain their
strained state even when the upper bulk relaxes. If the work function shift is driven by the buried interface dipole, the
relaxation of the top layers should not retroactively destroy the dipole already established at the bottom interface. The
authors' model implies a non-physical "action-at-a-distance" where surface relaxation erases the electronic signature of the
buried interface. 
9. Chemical intermixing and interface definition. Given the high growth temperatures, cationic intermixing is probable. If the
first unit cell layer labeled as "RuO2" contains a significant fraction of Ti cations (forming a RuxTi1-xO2 solid solution), the
minor residual polarization in this layer could be attributed to Ti cations rather than metallic Ru. I suggest the author to
provide element-specific spectroscopic mapping (e.g., EELS or EDX line scans) to prove the polarized layer is chemically
pure RuO2.
