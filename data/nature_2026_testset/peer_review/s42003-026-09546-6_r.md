Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Ren and co-authors investigated the autophagy receptor STBD1-mediated glycophagy in metabolic
regulation of the oyster Crassostrea gigas as well as the evolutionary origin of STBD1. Using relevant molecular and cellular
assays, they firstly demonstrated that STBD1-mediated glycophagy serves as a primary energy source in Crassostrea gigas,
and STBD1 preferentially bind to GABARAPL2 in Crassostrea gigas to drive glycophagy. Then, they traced the origins of
STBD1 through phylogenetic and comparative genomic analyses, and uncovered that oyster STBD1 and those in other
invertebrates contain an N-terminal rather than a C-terminal CBM20 domain, in contrast to that of vertebrate STBD1. Finally,
through structural modelling and functional analyses, they revealed that the N-terminal organization of the CBM20 domain in
STBD1 likely enhances glycogen binding, thereby facilitating the glycophagy process. Overall this manuscript is quite
interesting, and supports the conserved role of STBD1-glycophagy in different metazoan species. However, in the current
version of the manuscript some data and conclusions are not so convincing. In order to support the publication, additional
experiments and analyses are required. 
Major comments for the authors: 
1. The mRNA levels of Atg8 family-related genes, LC3A, LC3C, GABARAP and GABARAPL2, in the labial palp of oysters
after 14 d fasting followed by 48 h refeeding had been measured, as shown in Fig.2B. In order to support the authers’
conclusion, the relevant protein levels of LC3A, LC3C, GABARAP and GABARAPL2 in the labial palp of oysters should be
also provided. 
2. The authers should indicate which ligand glycogen is used for the docking analyses shown in Fig.3A-D of the manuscript.
Is it a branched maltotetraose? Based on the previous structural study of human STBD1 (PMID: 39236246), the CBM20
domain of STBD1 contains two different oligosaccharide-binding sites for recognizing the characteristic branched structural
unit at the α-1,6-glycosidic branch point of glycogen, however the current docking analyses in the manuscript are just based
on one oligosaccharide-binding site. Apparently, the current docking results are not very convincing. The authors should re-
conduct the docking analyses using the branched maltoundecaose together with two oligosaccharide-binding sites of
STBD1, or completely remove the current docking data. 
3. Based on the data shown in Fig.3, CgSTBD1 and CgSTBD1_mutant have different abilities for interacting with glycogen.
However, given that the CBM20 domain of STBD1 is responsible for interacting with glycogen and its structural folding
should be the same irrespective of whether it is in the N-terminal region or C-terminal region of STBD1, it is puzzling why the
CgSTBD1 and CgSTBD1_mutant have different abilities for interacting with glycogen? 
Minor comments for the authors: 
(1) The polysaccharide moiety of glycogen is mainly composed of α-1,4-linked linear glucose units branched with α-1,6-
glucosidic bonds, and is heterogenous. Line 161, the statement "the binding free energy of C. gigas STBD1 with glycogen
was -9.4 Kcal/mol" and Line 431, the statement “Molecular docking of glycogen with STBD1” are very misleading, and the
related “glycogen” statement should be changed. 
(2) Line 131, “each protein was co-expressed and their binding interactions assessed using co-immunoprecipitation” should
be changed to “each protein was co-expressed and their interactions were assessed using co-immunoprecipitation”.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents results from a study that aims to identify and characterize the glycophagic capabilities of the pacific
oyster Crassostrea gigas through the activity of the glycogen cargo receptor STBD1. Additionally, the manuscript aims to
describe the evolutionary origins of glycophagy in the Metazoa by phylogenetic and structural analysis of STBD1 and
specifically its carbohydrate binding module family 20 (CBM20) domain. 
The authors clearly demonstrated glycogen autophagy in C. gigas during starvation, using a combination of in- and ex-situ
methodologies such as immunohistochemistry, quantitative real-time PCR and co-IP. They show that starvation leads to (1)
upregulation of the autophagy marker LC3 and simultaneous diminishment of glycogen storage, (2) significant increase in
STBD1 mRNA expression and (3) STBD1 protein abundance decrease during fasting (due to lysosomal uptake and
digestion). Furthermore, they showed that STBD1 interacts with GABARAPL2, a known binding partner in chordate model
species. 
Additionally, using AlphaFold structural predictions and molecular docking simulation, the authors predicted a high glycogen
binding potential of the N-terminal C. gigas CBM20 domain of STBD1 which they subsequently confirmed experimentally by
heterologous protein expression and glycogen co-sedimentation assays. The authors hypothesized that the N-terminal
location of the CBM20 domain in STBD1 proteins is ancestral, with structural re-arrangement in the chordate root. 
The study is clearly structured, easy to follow and presents exciting theories about the evolution of glycophagy in
metazoans. The authors test their hypotheses through thorough experimentation and in silico analysis. Below, the authors
will find a few points of concern that are addressable without the need for further experiments before publication, which I
support. 
Major revision points 
1. The authors describe that the STBD1 models presented in Fig. 3A-D are AlphaFold3 models, “assessed for prediction
confidence based on pLDDT values”. However, the actual pLDDT values do not appear to be in the manuscript nor color-
coded in the structure. The models show that major parts of the proteins (except the CBM20 domain) are unfolded or
potentially disordered. Please (1) specifically describe in the figure caption that these are AlphaFold models and (2) present
pLDDT values in a sensible manner. 
2. In the manuscript, it is described that the structure of CBM20 domains is conserved across large evolutionary distances
with a RMSD of approximately 0.5 Å. Please show the superposition of selected CBM20 domain structural predictions from
non-bilaterian and bilaterian phyla. 
3. In Fig 3A-D and F it becomes clear that C. gigas STBD1 is much smaller than MmSTBD1 and DrSTBD1. If the vertebrate
proteins are longer in general (as indicated by the discontinuous line in 3A, B), this would require a separated discussion as
to the potential function. 
4. The key experiments demonstrating the enhanced glycogen binding affinity and "more rapid glycogen decomposition" by
oyster STBD1 (N-terminal CBM20) compared to vertebrate STBD1 (C-terminal CBM20) were performed in human 293T
cells. While valuable for showcasing biochemical differences in protein function, using a human cell line to draw broad
conclusions about the in vivo metabolic function across entire animal lineages (oysters vs. vertebrates) can be an
oversimplification. Please clarify this in the updated manuscript. 
5. A concern arises regarding the robustness of the proposed evolutionary scenario for the CBM20-containing protein
clades. While the authors conclude that glycophagy is deeply conserved in bilaterians, the evidence for the ancestral
presence and subsequent lineage-specific losses of these proteins warrants closer scrutiny. Specifically: 
a. The inference that LAF was present before the divergence of cnidarians and bilaterians is supported by the detection of a
single cnidarian protein (from Nematostella vectensis) in the LAF clade (Fig. 5A), as interpreted within a strict Dollo
parsimony framework. A demonstration of sequence and structural conservation between the N. vectensis and bilaterian
proteins would provide further support for this hypothesis. 
b. Furthermore, the assertion that all three CBM20-containing clades (GPCPD1, LAF, and STBD1) were present in the
metazoan ancestor is founded on the presence of poriferan sequences exclusively within the GPCPD1 clade. This
interpretation necessitates an assumption of widespread and prevalent lineage-specific losses of LAF and STBD1 in
numerous early diverging metazoan lineages. For instance, STBD1 is reported to be present "only in bilaterians" and

"STBD1

related genes in mollusks, nemerteans, annelids, and brachiopods”. The authors themselves acknowledge the challenging

Given these inherent difficulties, it might be more prudent to focus on the robust evidence for STBD1's distribution and

on phyletic/superphyletic-level relationships to simplify the presentation, while detailed trees showing individual proteins per
species can be moved to the supplementary materials for improved clarity.

length across animals and might provide a more reliable phylogenetic signal? 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The article presents research related to the following main claims: 
- that STBD1 (the glycogen cargo receptor) originated deep within bilaterians 
- that the N-terminal localization of STBD1 enhances glycogen binding 
- that glycophagy in deeply conserved in bilaterians 
The Authors use a variety of methods to support these claims, combining antibody-based approaches, recombinant protein
design and expression with bioinformatics. The origins and utilization of glycophagy is a very interesting topic and I think
that the Authors try to answer important questions which may be of general interest, as well as pushing the field forward. I
especially appreciate the usage of a non-canonical model organism, the oyster Classostrea gigas. The research is
reproducible, except some data related to the bioinformatics is not available. 
Firstly, the article might benefit from including a Figure or a Supplementary Figure which schematically shows the anatomy
of the oyster, for readers who might not be familiar with terms like "labial palp" and similar.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

The conclusions are indeed novel, but my impression is that the bioinformatics part is not as well executed as the
experimental part - is much more convincing and elegantly planned. I will start with experiments: 
- Figure 1: starving/refeeding with detection of LC3, glycogen and lipids, and qRT-PCR are well presented and statistically
sound. I could not find the numerical source data for Fig. 1B charts, presumably from ImageJ. I believe these should be
added to the supplement spreadsheets. 
- Figure 2: I am fully convinced of the Authors' interpretation of the experimental data and see no issues. 
- Figure 3: The experimental data presented and described in the text supports the claim that the N-terminal location of
CBM20 enhances the binding activity. 
A general comment about reporting numerical data originating from Western blots, for example in lines 133, 187 and 188. I
do not that believe reporting 2 significant digits when the confidence interval is higher than 0.1 conveys any information. 
I will now explain why the bioinformatics performed are less convincing. The Authors state these: 
Claim: GPCPD1 and laforin were present in the animal ancestor. Lines: 215-218. 
- GPCPD1-like proteins are present in many eukaryotic clades and may have been present in the eukaryotic ancestor. The
presence of "laforin", however, is not so clear cut. Looking at the data presented, we can only say that all animals have "a
protein" with a CBM20 domain which later gave rise to laforin and STBD1, but I do not think one can conclude it was laforin. 
- The phylogenetic analyses, according to the Methods section, were performed using full length protein sequences without
any trimming post-alignment. This is useful when comparing more closely related sequences, but less useful in case of less
closely related sequences like domain-shuffled proteins. 
- The Authors infer much of the evolutionary history of the LAF gene from the protein domain architecture. I am not convinced
this is the most informative part of the data. Instead, the domain itself should be considered as the unit carrying most
conserved evolutionary signal; see for example Richter et al. 2018, eLife: https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.34226. I would like to
see the analysis done only on the CBM20 domain sequences. This may bolster the Authors' claims or indeed lead them to a
different conclusion. Maybe using this approach, the CBM20 domain of C. gigas will turn out to be more similar to
vertebrate/chordate laforin? How would this affect the conclusions? 
- Can the Authors explore the (unlikely) possibility of the mollusk CBM20 domain protein originated from a lateral gene
transfer event? 
- When producing the phylogenetic analyses using CBM20 domain sequences, it might be useful for the Authors to include
examples of such domains from other eukaryotes: DPE2 (Disproportionating enzyme 2) is an enzyme prominently present in
plants and protists, and many fungi have GLAA which contains a CBM20 domain: https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2148-13-40 -
as mentioned in lines 68-69. 
- Including only 1 representative of Ctenophora and no placozoans in the analysis, despite multiple genomes having been
sequenced, is something to correct. Please include at least 1 placozoan and 2-3 ctenophores. The genomes of Cnidaria are
also plentiful, the Authors should consider sampling more of them as well. 
- Please make all the alignments and tree files available. 
Claim: STBD1 originated from LAF through gene duplication in the common ancestor of parahoxozoans. Lines: 235-237 
- I do not think this is supported by the analyses, but it is an important issue and I believe the Authors will be able to answer it
with improved methods. 
Claim: the ancestral animal CBM20 protein has the domain located N-terminally. Lines: 82-84 
- This is indeed likely, but should be confirmed by looking at more early branching metazoan species. 
The conclusion I would draw from the results, presented as they are, is this: apart from GPCPD1, another CBM20-containing
protein was present in the common ancestor of parahoxozoa. I will provisionally call it "CBM20-2" - not STBD1. All
parahoxozoans (Bilateria, Cnidaria, potentially Placozoa) inherited it. Within Bilateria, in Protostomia CBM20-2 retained a
laforin-like domain architecture but (especially in molluscs) evolved STBD1-like function. In Deuterostomia, CBM20-2
duplicated, probably during the second round of vertebrate whole genome duplication (this can be traced by the Authors
using literature, see Simakov et al. 2022: 10.1126/sciadv.abi5884), giving rise to STBD1 and laforin. These whole genome
duplications allowed genes with redundant functions to diverge, therefore the domain shuffling of CBM20 domain from the
N-terminus to the C-terminus was possible in one of the copies. Laforin seems to be the gene that retained more "ancestral"
characteristics. I do not agree with the scenario presented in Fig. 5B and I do not understand why the Authors argue that the
LAF/STBD1 duplication occurred in parahoxozoa, followed by punctuated losses. 
If the Authors find the scenario I proposed more likely, I do not think naming the oyster protein STBD1 is warranted, as this
protein would have evolved this function independently of vertebrate STBD1. We also do not know the function of the
ancestral CBM20-2, which might be overlapping. 
Another part of bioinformatics that I believe could be done in a more convincing way are modeling, docking and related
calculations. Briefly, the procedure included these steps: 
1. Modeling of proteins using AlphaFold and structural alignment with PDB 8X8K. 
2. Molecular docking of a glycogen tetrasaccharide to these structures using AutoDock Vina. 
3. Determining the optimal docking model based on the RMSD and reporting the binding energy. 
I have several issues/questions connected with this approach.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

- It is not clear whether the Authors used full protein sequences. Fig. 3A-D would imply that full sequences were used. Were
models made using all amino acids also used for docking, or were they pruned to the CBM20 domain only? Again, Fig. 3A-
D would suggest that it was full sequences. The unstructured loops can be cut out from the protein. 
- The Authors should include a Supplementary Figure showing the comparison of the ligand-binding region of CBM20 with
the known structure 8X8K. As the known structure also possesses free oligosaccharides, their binding poses should be
compared to the AlphaFold structures, as well as the residues around the sugar. This pose, if sensible, can be used as a
starting point for docking to the models, instead of a minimized glycogen conformation. 
- The Authors should provide the final models as Supplementary Data or upload them to a repository. 
- Was the sugar-optimized AutoDock Vina-Carb used? 
- How many docking poses were generated? 
- Docking alone is usually just the first step of in silico binding studies. This should be followed by molecular dynamics in
order to see whether the ligand stays in its position. Binding energies from docking studies are not too informative. The
protein side chain positions, as well as the actual ligand conformation, will influence it greatly. Therefore, MD simulations
should be performed and only then one can draw any conclusions from the energies obtained. 
- Usually the binding pose of the sugar molecule is quite stable among evolutionarily related CBMs. In Fig. 3ABD they seem
similar but in Fig. 3C one cannot tell this from the 2D view alone. Again, MD would help here. 
- Supplementary Figure S5, if indeed necessary to present, should be redrawn to show glycogen in the same orientation, if
possible. It will help with comparing the residues around it. 
In summary, I commend the authors for the design of this study and I am curious about their response to these comments.
Reconstructing early animal evolution is a difficult but timely and important task.

Lines 70-73: The sentence starting with "Work to date" should be rewritten. 
Line 86: The ending of the sentence suggests that bilaterian evolution has finished, please consider using different words,
for example "early bilaterian evolution". 
Line 373: Sentence truncation/typo around "freeze-drying". 
Line 423: Typo "onlin". 
Figure 4: The letter "M" for "Mollusca" is not used but exists in the Legend.
