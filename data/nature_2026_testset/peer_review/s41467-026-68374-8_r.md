Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Iliff et al. addresses an important question, whether and how sleep modulates brain clearance to the
plasma, which has been technically challenging to address. It uses a robust study design to manipulate sleep in humans
and test how sleep features are linked to plasma amyloid and tau, with extensive experimental measures and interesting
results, and the clearance field is in need of a study like this that uses sleep deprivation as a causal manipulation in humans.
The methods are not sufficiently described, and additional information and reporting is needed to fully interpret the results. 
Specific comments 
- it seems that in the kinetic modeling, the 'glymphatic model' is a model where rates change during sleep, and 'null model' is
where they don't, is that correct? It might be helpful to rename the models, as e.g. 'sleep-dependent model', if I understood
correctly, to clarify that the difference is not the route of clearance but rather the role of sleep, but this decision is up to the
authors 
- it is a bit confusing that then the same model names are used for the regression analysis, but this analysis does not involve
these kinetics 
- it seems like the kinetic modeling does not include altered amyloid/tau production during the night under both models,
which may be expected to occur, would this alter the results? 
- Several aspects of the Methods for the study design and analysis are insufficiently described. Since this information is
essential for interpreting the study, it should be included here. In particular, information for setting of the sleep (e.g., in lab?
how was wakefulness monitored during sleep deprivation?), EEG equipment and methods, how EEG bands are extracted
(whole night power? are noisy segments excluded from power calculations? how? what frequencies were included in each
band?), and HR calculation (one heart rate averaged throughout the sleep period? is it relative to baseline awake HR?). 
- There is also no method text to describe the investigational device and how R_p is calculated. A preprint is cited for the
validation study, but this is a novel method that is not known in the field and needs more information contained within the

it quantify resistance? Does the model use an average measure of R_p over the full night and do motion periods need to be
excluded? Is it affected by the sleep deprivation manipulation? Is it affected by head size/sex? 
- the single-index predictor Power_s is defined as a combination of EEG power bands on p. 10 ("three single-index
predictors: EEG power band predictors ...(Power_s)", but then on p. 11 it is stated that "R_p was the overwhleming
contributor to Power_s" - is the parenchymal resistance also included in the spectral power variable? 
- Table 3 seems like it could be a figure and show data distributions. Are the effects of sleep deprivation reported in table 3
significant? 
- This result statement is not clear: " When comparing the percent variance of morning plasma AD biomarker levels that was
explained by including overnight RP and EEG delta power in the glymphatic model and that was not explained by the null
model, the glymphatic model explained between 23.7% (np- tau217) to 48.4% (Ab40) of that variance". The glymphatic
model explained 23% of the variance that was explained by the glymphatic model and not the null model? Is this comparing
glymphatic model with and without those parameters? Please clarify this statistic. 
- It is difficult to interpret Fig. 3 and the conclusions in that paragraph. It seems like the authors are suggesting that equivalent
percentage changes in kinetic rate parameters vs. EEG composite index yield similar effects on plasma, but I'm not sure why
that would be the case or interpretable in this way, since they represent very different physiological processes and their
magnitudes do not need to follow similar linear scaling.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript addresses and important growing body of literature on the role of glymphatic clearance in sleep. The study
seeks to provide mechanistic insights into the role of the glymphatic system in the clearance of endogenous AD pathology,
i.e., amyloid and tau aggregates, during sleep in a cohort of older adults. This potentially addresses a critical knowledge gap
in the glymphatic literature and could significantly advance the field. A randomized crossover design was implemented at
multiple study sites to investigate sleep physiology (using electroencephalography and heart rate) in conjunction with
glymphatic activity (using brain parenchymal resistance to glymphatic flow measured by a novel investigational device).
Validated plasma AD biomarkers are obtained from two blood draws,12 hours apart, prior to and following the overnight
sleep and sleep deprivation sessions. Mathematical modeling of the pharmacokinetics of AD solute transport and exchange
from CSF to ISF is then used to develop two competing models – the null model, and the sleep-active glymphatic model.
While the predictions of the null model rely on a constant and brain state-invariant rate of CSF-ISF solute exchange, the
sleep-active glymphatic model accounts for increased rates of CSF-ISF exchange during sleep, guided by published
findings. Predictions of compartmental concentrations of AD proteins from each of the two models are then compared
against the predictions of analogous multivariate linear mixed models that assess the relationship between sleep features
(such as EEG power, hypnogram sleep stage, HR, and Rp) and the overnight change in plasma AD biomarker levels for the
analogue to the sleep-active glymphatic model, and between morning AD biomarker levels in plasma, and evening AD
biomarker levels along with covariates such as age, sex, APOE status, and study site. The authors conclude that their
findings are indicative of a sleep-active role for the glymphatic system (as estimated by reduced Rp) in the overnight
clearance of AD biomarkers from the CNS to the periphery and suggest a potential therapeutic role for glymphatic clearance
during sleep to combat AD onset and ameliorate AD pathophysiology. Despite the conceptual strengths and methodological
novelty of this manuscript, there remain some concerns that dampen our enthusiasm for the manuscript in its present form
that we feel must be addressed before we can endorse publication. 
These concerns are highlighted below. 
MAJOR 
1. Methods and Results: The methods section does not contain an adequate description of the EEG data processing
methodology. The approaches used for preprocessing (e.g., filtering, rejection of artifacts), and the methodology used for
spectral analysis should be added to aid evaluation of the merit of the manuscript and reproducibility. For example, it is not
clear from the manuscript whether spectral analysis resulting in estimates of delta and beta power was conducted separately
for NREM, REM, and Wake sleep stages, if spectral analysis was constricted to NREM sleep stages, or if it was conducted

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

agnostic to sleep stages. This is critically important, in part, because it would be inappropriate to analyze spectral data
agnostic to sleep/wake brain state because the functioning of the brain is fundamentally different in NREM, REM, and Wake
stages. While it would be reasonable to collapse across NREM stages, it is not reasonable to collapse across NREM and
REM sleep and expect estimates to accurately reflect the underlying biology. Not every instance of delta activity can be
ascribed to the onset or occurrence of slow wave sleep (N3), and spectral estimates across frequencies differ dramatically
across brain states. Individual differences in spectral estimates may be biased by how much a given individual spends in
any given sleep stage, e.g., you can have a subject with less N2 but robust spindle activity during N2 that is washed out by
increased wake and REM time being averaged into the estimates. There are also alternative explanations of power in
different sleep stages that change with brain state, including but not limited to changes in the aperiodic 1/f curve in power-
frequency space, artifacts from sweat or eye movement intrusions, or potentially even local expression of delta rhythms in
REM sleep. These details need to be presented in the methods, and if analyses were conducted agnostic to sleep stage,
they need to be recomputed focusing specifically on spectral estimates during NREM sleep or by each brain state (i.e.,
NREM, REM, Wake) separately. 
2. Methods: The exclusion criteria should be further clarified. While the manuscript specifies that exclusion criteria included
depression, neurological disease, and cognitive impairment, it remains unclear whether the study screened for and excluded
participants with sleep disorders. Sleep apnea may influence both glymphatic function and AD protein synthesis, as may
insomnia. REM sleep behavior disorder may be indicative of a synucleinopathy even before they are diagnosed. In addition,
it is not clear if the authors screened for caffeine and tobacco use, as these substances may also impact AD protein
dynamics and sleep expression. 
3. Methods: Blood draws were conducted at specific clock times 12 hours apart within a specific clock time window.
However, it is not clear this timing was also aligned to participants’ habitual sleep and wake times. The literature has shown
there are circadian influences on fluid AD biomarkers, and if measurements were taken at different circadian times, that
could confound study findings. If the participants were given a specific bed/wake time during the study, rather than their
habitual pattern, then were they given time to align to that new schedule and were there efforts made to make sure they were
compliant to this schedule? This should be clarified. If circadian timing or habitual sleep/wake patterns were not taken into
account, then attempts should be made to show that time between blood draws and sleep/wake times did not influence
study findings. 
4. Methods, Results, and Discussion: How were Abeta positivity status in the sample accounted for in model dynamics and
outcomes in relation to clearance dynamics and plasma concentrations of Abeta? It is possible that changes in plasma
concentrations of AD proteins could be explained by an ongoing “competition” between glymphatic clearance and plaque
absorption of Abeta peptides which may be nonlinear. Additionally, amyloid positivity status has been shown to impact
diurnal variation in Abeta protein levels as well. This would mean that evening-morning variations may become smaller or
larger based on circadian amplitude differences between individuals that is not fully accounted for in the null model and may
bias results to more complex models like the glymphatic model. While we agree that only 7 people being Abeta positive
limits power to make strong inferences, the inclusion of a mixed population with known changes in protein dynamics may
also impact the overall results and bias findings. If similar results can be observed in a supplemental sensitivity analysis
when the 7 Abeta positive participants are not included, then that could be reassuring. In the same vein, this study is not
powered to say there are not circadian effects in plasma Abeta proteins or changes in circadian effects with Abeta positivity
versus non-positivity due to the study design and lack of sufficient Abeta positives. Any statements about this should be
careful to not overinterpret the data. The statement below is one such statement that should be tempered, especially given
the extant literature on circadian changes in Abeta peptides. 
“Notably, despite seven participants being classified as ‘amyloid positive’, the ‘amyloid-positive’ covariate was non-
significant in two of the three glymphatic models and only marginally significant in the third, suggesting that at short
timescales, changes in CSF-ISF exchange are unaffected by baseline Ab42 and p-tau levels beyond what would be
expected of concentration-dependent first-order kinetics.” 
5. Methods and Results: Both the modeling methodology and its conclusions appear to overstate the strength of glymphatic
clearance as the sole explanatory mechanism that describes differences in morning versus evening plasma levels. In this
context, the sleep-active glymphatic model represents only one alternative hypothesis to the null model. There are other
conceivable competitors to these 2 theories that may offer explanations for changes in morning versus evening protein
levels but are not adequately addressed. For instance, changes in the rate of production of AD proteins during the sleep
period may impact the morning plasma levels of AD biomarkers independent of evening levels. Additionally, plasma levels
of AD biomarkers may receive contributions from non-nervous peripheral tissues. Aside from glymphatic clearance,
extracellular housekeeping mechanisms such as phagocytosis may also drive changes in blood biomarker levels
independent of glymphatic driven solute exchange. Circadian influences, which are hard to disentangle from sleep effects
given the study design, are also possible, though the sleep deprivation paradigm can be used to help with that interpretation
(though sleep deprivation can alter circadian function as well). Finally, plaque absorption rate may offer yet another
competing claim to explain reductions in morning plasma AD biomarker levels. 
It is not clear how alternative models such as
these are ruled out in strong favor of the glymphatic clearance model, especially when potentially informative parameters
such as rates of neuronal AD peptide synthesis, extracellular secretion, aggregation and plaque formation are excluded from
the kinetics model. While we agree it is beyond the scope of this article to test all possible models of AD protein change over
sleep, our concern is more about the lack of specificity of the current analytic approach. It is possible that another
mechanistic framework best predicts plasma protein level changes, but that, by chance, the interaction between plasma
evening levels and the rate constants k12 and k21 better captures this other process than the null model. This could result in

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

favoring the null model and mistakenly inferring that the only possible explanation is that it is a glymphatic process that is
involved. The finding of better model fit for the glymphatic model in the sleep deprivation condition when including delta,
beta power, and HR further supports this possibility. This should be addressed in the discussion or another model that is
more of an active control with a different framework, could help show that the effect isn’t about model complexity/nonlinearity
but about the specific predictions that went into it. Basically, if the interpretation is an active glymphatic process, the
manuscript would benefit from alternative interpretations being ruled out. 
6. Methods and Results: One of the main factors for evaluating model fit was the use of AIC. One concern about using AIC to
compare model fits between the glymphatic model and the null model, is that they are not equivalent in number of predictors
and complexity. In cases of smaller sample sizes, AIC can prefer overfit models. Did the authors use the corrected version of
AIC that corrects for this? This is important because when n (sample size) is relatively small for the number of model
parameters used, AIC_corrected should be used. Here, although the predictors are being fused into a single predictor using
linear combinations that optimize for max. likelihood, this is a data driven approach that does not actually reduce the model
complexity, it merely reparameterizes the model using weighted averaging. Therefore, the number of parameters to be used
for AIC calculations remains the same, and for n=34 and up to 6 parameters, n/k < 40 (which is the general rule of thumb
that, when satisfied, suggests that AIC_corrected should be used). If not implemented, this may artificially support the
glymphatic model over the null model, biasing interpretations. 
7. Methods and Results: There appear to be multiple different models predicting the same outcomes that are presented
throughout the results, but it is not clear if any multiple comparisons correction was applied. This should be clarified and
justified explicitly if not used. 
8. Results: A statement was made in the results, saying that “In this case, RP was the overwhelming contributor to PowerS”.
We are not sure what this statement means. Is this about which factor drove the relationship with biomarkers? If so, is it RP
to PowerS to biomarkers or PowerS to RP to biomarkers? Or is RP folded into the PowerS factor and the main driver of the
effect on biomarkers? This should be clarified, and, if necessary, the directionality of relationships is empirically testable
using a mediation modeling approach. It could be interesting to expand on it to know if the RP glymphatic effects are
mediate by or mediating spectral sleep effects on biomarkers or if they are truly independent factors. If independent, this says
that the sleep spectral estimates are not related to biomarkers because of glymphatic associations but that there is yet
another independent process not captured by at least RP. Or is the argument that there are other independent glymphatic
effects that are not captured by RP that spectral estimates capture? Regardless, this should be clarified. 
MINOR 
1. Abstract: The abstract describes significant associations between changes in Rp, heart rate, and EEG delta power, and
AD biomarker levels. However, it is not clear whether the reported p-values were obtained from the overall model, or from a
specific individual predictor within the model. Therefore, it is unclear to the reader whether the sleep physiology measures
explain the AD biomarker concentrations due to their association with glymphatic activity or whether there are sleep or
glymphatic independent factors in the model that drive this association. 
2. Introduction: There is a typographical error in paragraph 4 of the introduction – “Here we deigned a study...” – designed. ' 
3. Methods: The manuscript would benefit from a justification of the selection of HR instead of other related measures that
are typically more reliable and less noisy, like heart rate variability, especially since HRV has been shown be associated
with the activation of the locus coeruleus, which in turn has been linked to glymphatic activity. 
4. Methods and Results: It is unclear whether total sleep time (TST) and time in bed (TIB) are comparable across subjects,
and this may necessitate modeling the outcomes with percent time spent in different sleep stages instead of using total
minutes. 
4. Methods: Did the authors account for daytime naps in their study population? If so, were people who napped included,
excluded, were their data examined for floor or ceiling effects? If someone napped on the day of the study, perhaps the
movement in evening protein levels would be less dynamic, as they would have been impacted by sleep proximal to the
evening samples.
