Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Reviewer Comments: 
The manuscript presents a well-structured and technically sound study on the development of a high-performance, low-
carbon, and cost-effective 3D-printable concrete using cellulose nanofibers (CNF) and limestone filler (LF). The topic is
timely and highly relevant, particularly given the ongoing challenges in balancing printability, mechanical performance,
sustainability, and cost in 3D-printed construction materials. The authors effectively demonstrate significant improvements in

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

rheological and mechanical properties, alongside meaningful reductions in cement content, carbon footprint, and cost. 
However, there are a few points that require clarification and further discussion: 
Scale of Printing Demonstration: 
While the abstract claims "large-scale feasibility," the actual full-scale print described appears to be relatively small in the
context of construction-scale 3D printing. The authors are encouraged to clarify the dimensions and complexity of the printed
element and to moderate the claim of "large-scale feasibility" unless the print demonstrably reflects full structural scale. It
would be helpful to position this work within a more precise scale framework (e.g., lab-scale, component-scale, structural-
scale). 
Conclusion Section: 
The manuscript would benefit from a dedicated conclusion section. While the technical findings are well presented, a
concise and clearly demarcated conclusion summarizing the key contributions, implications, and future outlook would
strengthen the overall impact of the paper. 
General Comment: 
The manuscript is well written, and the experimental methodology appears rigorous. The integration of rheology, hydration
kinetics, and mechanical performance provides a compelling case for the proposed mix design. The sustainability and cost-
reduction aspects are also well substantiated and valuable. 
Overall, I recommend minor revisions to address the points above, particularly the clarification of the scale of the 3D printing
demonstration and the inclusion of a clear conclusions section. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study presents a novel approach to 3D printable cementitious materials using CNF and LF, but major revisions are
required before publication. Key concerns include: (1) poor manuscript organization (premature conclusions, unconventional
section order, missing supplementary materials); (2) insufficient mechanistic depth in linking rheology to printability
(unsubstantiated synergy claims, inadequate characterization of CNF-LF interactions); (3) flawed experimental design
(arbitrary additive dosages, unstandardized testing, confounding variables in 3DP validation); and (4) incomplete
economic/environmental analysis (missing costs, unvalidated carbon claims). While the research shows promise,
acceptance hinges on addressing these issues through rigorous methodology documentation, and clearer data
interpretation. The revisions needed are substantial and would impact the conclusions. 
1. The current structure of paper presents three major problems: (1) The introduction gives away all the main findings too
early, which makes the flow of the research story less clear. (2) The order of sections—starting with Results, then
Discussion, and ending with Materials and Methods—is unusual and makes it harder for readers to follow. (3) The
supplementary materials (S1, S2, etc.) are mentioned in the text but are missing together with fig and tables. 
2. The introduction should provide a comprehensive materials science foundation for both CNF and LF, detailing: (1) CNF
variants (TEMPO-oxidized/enzymatic/mechanical; their diameter & aspect ratio) and their surface chemistry (carboxylation,
dispersion in high-pH environments); (2) LF classifications (high-calcite vs. dolomitic, dry/wet-ground; d50) and packing
effects; (3) quantitative comparisons of cost (CNF and LF) and carbon footprint with proper resources; and (4) mechanistic
synergies in cement matrices according to current literature. 
3. The rheological analysis in this section (Lines 121-180) lacks sufficient mechanistic depth to properly connect hydration
kinetics with printability parameters. While key rheological properties are identified, the treatment of CNF-LF-cement
interactions is oversimplified: (1) The dismissal of LF-CNF colloidal interactions based solely on low shear stress
measurements fails to consider Ca²
⁺
-mediated bridging or depletion flocculation effects; (2) The calorimetry data shows LF
accelerates hydration but does not quantitatively resolve how competing mechanisms (nucleation vs. short-circuit diffusion)
govern early-age rheology; and (3) Critical time-dependent parameters like structuration rate (Athix) during the induction
period - which directly determines 3DP open time and layer adhesion - are not measured. To strengthen these claims, the
authors should employ in-situ rheo-Raman spectroscopy to correlate C-S-H formation with storage modulus evolution, apply
DLVO theory to model interparticle forces, and report thixotropic loop areas to assess interaction reversibility. Without these
advanced characterizations, the proposed rheology protocol remains descriptive rather than predictive for 3DP applications. 
4. Figure 2 exhibits inconsistent reporting of experimental variability, with select plots displaying error bars while others omit
them entirely. This raises critical methodological concerns: (1) Were rheological tests replicated to establish statistical
significance of the reported CNF/LF effects? (2) If replicates were performed, what determined the inclusion/exclusion of
error bars across different parameters (Lines 219-228).

6. The rheological interpretation contains several fundamental inconsistencies that undermine its conclusions. While the
hysteresis loops (Fig. 2b-c) purportedly demonstrate thixotropic behavior, the analysis fails to: (1) quantify the thixotropic

comparisons; (2) reconcile the paradoxical observation that CNF increases yield stress while decreasing storage modulus

enhancements originate from "soft colloidal networks" when no direct evidence (e.g., cryo-TEM of CNF-cement interfaces or
zeta potential measurements) is provided to exclude alternative mechanisms like CNF-induced pore fluid viscosity changes.
7. The 3DP validation (Fig. 2f) is methodologically flawed: the concurrent use of methylcellulose (0.8 wt%) and WRA
introduces confounding variables that prevent isolation of CNF/LF effects. The reported "synergy" (line 304) thus remains
unproven, as the experimental design cannot distinguish between additive interactions and formulation artifacts. For
rigorous claims, the authors should: (1) conduct SEM-EDS mapping of printed interfaces to verify CNF distribution; and (2)
re-test printability with a fixed admixture baseline or justify what they did since methylcellulose strongly change the 3D
printability and would change any printability conclusion that was made. 
8. While the 28-day compressive strength results (Fig. 4a) suggest CNF mitigates LF’s dilution effect, the claim of
"synergistic enhancement" (Lines 386–388) is unsupported by microstructural evidence. The TGA data (Fig. 4c) contradict
the mechanical findings: despite LF increasing chemically bound water (implying enhanced hydration), PLC-LF29 mixtures
show reduced strength, while CNF improves strength without consistently increasing bound water. This discrepancy
indicates the reported "bridging mechanism" (Lines 409–410) is speculative—no fracture surface SEM or nanoindentation
data confirm CNF’s pore-filling role. 
9. The flexural strength comparison (Fig. 4b) is misleading as printed beams (anisotropic) are compared to cast samples
(isotropic) without interlayer bond characterization. The normalized strength analysis (Fig. S8) also overlooks LF’s
nucleation effects, which may artificially inflate "cement efficiency" metrics. For robust conclusions, the authors must:
compare printed vs. cast samples separately; and 
correlate TGA with XRD to decouple hydration phases from filler effects. 
10. While CNF's dosage efficiency (0.04% w/w) for yield stress enhancement is notable, the study fails to account for critical
cost drivers: (1) methylcellulose additives (0.8 wt% per Line 293), which increase binder costs; (2) system-level processing
costs, including high-shear CNF dispersion energy and potential PCE superplasticizers to offset viscosity spikes; and (3)
carbon accounting gaps, ignoring CNF production emissions Furthermore, the claimed "seven-fold cost advantage" (Line
452) lacks normalization for printed volume or durability performance (carbonation, creep). For credible claims, the authors
must conduct a full cradle-to-gate LCA (ISO 14044) including all additives and processing energy, and report normalized
metrics (cost/MPa/m³). Without this, the purported economic and environmental benefits remain speculative, particularly for
industrial-scale applications where methylcellulose and CNF dispersion costs become prohibitive.

Specifically: (1) No justification is provided for the selected LF replacement levels (14% and 29%), such as particle packing
optimization (e.g., Andreasen curve modeling) or reactivity thresholds (e.g., CaCO

12. The study omits critical justification for the mixing methodology, failing to: (1) reference standardized protocols (e.g.,
ASTM C1738/EN 196-1) or paper for rheological testing; (2) demonstrate equivalence between lab-scale paste shearing
and large scale 3D printing conditions; (3) address scaling effects including aggregate-induced shear thickening, wall slip
phenomena, and thixotropic recovery kinetics; or (4) quantify mixing energy (J/g) relative to CNF dispersion percolation
thresholds and LF surface activation. For methodological rigor, the authors should characterize shear history effects on
thixotropic parameters, perform parallel mortar rheology tests, and correlate mixing shear rates with printing process
kinematics to validate scale invariance (lines 512-526). 
13. The methodology suffers from significant experimental omissions that compromise reproducibility and reliability. Section
S4 remains ambiguous regarding the rheological testing protocol, particularly the shearing schedule (including pre-shear
conditions, ramp rates, and recovery intervals) which critically influences thixotropic measurements. Furthermore, the study
fails to report: (1) the number of test repetitions (essential given the inherent variability in cementitious rheology), (2)

measurement system (container dimensions, gap height relative to maximum particle size (Dmax) to ensure wall slip
mitigation), and (4) the applied shear rate range and protocol (controlled stress vs. strain, linear vs. logarithmic steps). These
omissions violate standard rheological reporting practices (per ASTM C1749) and prevent meaningful comparison with

Rheological behavior. The cement –LF–CNF system is highly complex, involving both 
colloidal interactions and hydration-induced effects that govern its rheological performance. 
To elucidate the influence of LF and CNF on cement rheology, we adopted the framework of 
soft colloidal and rigid interaction mechanisms described by Roussel et al. (2012), Roussel et 
al. (2019) and Douba et al. (2022) 1–3. The soft colloidal interactions represent effects such as 
electrostatic forces among particles, while the rigid interactions are primarily associated with 
hydration reactions  such as C –S–H precipitation and growth . In this study, zeta potential 
measurements were performed to probe the soft colloidal interactions between cement, LF, 
and CNF. The combination of storag e modulus measurements, heat of hydration via 
isothermal calorimetry, and SEM images were used to ascertain the hydration-related origins 
of rigid interaction processes. Ultimately, Cement system represents a highly intricate system 
in which both soft and rigid interactions operate concurrently within a three -dimensional 
network. Although a complete mechanistic understanding of how new additives (such as CNF 
and LF) influence the rheological behavior of complex cementitious systems remains beyond 
current kn owledge, the combined experimental evidence highlights correlations that make 
alternative explanations unlikely 1–7. Within these bounds, the adopted framework, which is 
also used in the current literature 1–3, provides a valuable insight into the dominant mechanisms 
in the system.

Overall, the microstructural analyses demonstrate that the rheological behavior of the cement–
LF–CNF system arises from the interplay between accelerated hydration (rigid interactions) 
from LF and colloidal interactions between CNF and cement. Isothermal calorimetry in Fig. 
2(a)-(c) demonstrated that LF primarily modifies hydration kinetics, thereby contributing to 
rheological enhancement through improved packing density and accelerated C –S–H 
precipitation and growth. It is well established that accelerated hydration with rapid hydration 
product formation promotes rapid stiffening, resulting in increased static yield stress, storage 
modulus, and structuration rate 1–3, which aligns with the rheological observations in this study. 
XRD patterns (new Fig. 2(d)) of different mixtures show comparable phase assemblages. No 
distinct additional crystalline phases were detected, implying that the combination of CNF 
and LF is unlikely to introduce any new reactions.  
On the other hand, the enhancement in rheological performance such as static yield stress 
occurs despite CNF ’s insignificant effect on hydration (Fig. 2(c)). This suggests that the 
increase in rheological performance due to CNF arises primarily from physic al and colloidal 
structuring mechanisms, rather than from chemical stiffening associated with hydration 
product formation. In the revised manuscript, new zeta potential  measurements were 
performed to  support this interpretation  and probe the soft colloidal interactions between 
cement, LF, and CNF. Zeta potential measurements were performed following the methods 
reported in previous studies on cellulose nanomaterials in cement systems 8,9. The results (new 
Fig. 2(e)) indicate that CNF remains strongly anionic (−35 mV in DI water and −27 mV in 
simulated pore solution (SPS)), suggesting that self-adsorption is limited as it is considered 
“moderately stable” and is within the range of measurements of others  8–11. The reduction of 
zeta potential of CNF in SPS reflects electrostatic screening, whereby cations accumulate 
closer to CNF surfaces, with possible specific adsorption onto carboxyl and hydroxyl groups. 
Given the Ca²⁺-rich environment of cement pore solution and the near -neutral zeta potential 
of cement particles, these ions could mediate CNF adsorption onto cement surfaces , similar 
to other cellulose nano particles 8,10. Previous studies on CNF have also reported that the 
hydroxyl and carboxyl surface groups on CNF can effectively bind to the cement particles, 
inducing CNF -cement interactions  9,10,12,13. In addition, b ecause both CNF and LF are 
negatively charged in SPS, direct attraction is unfavorable. In SPS, cement is near neutral 
(−3.7 mV) while LF is weakly negat ive (−13 mV), so CNF appears more likely to interact 
with cement than with LF. Taken together, these results suggest that CNF fibers tend to adhere 
to cement particles, with CNF–cement electrostatic interactions dominating in the system and 
contributing to the observed rheological performances.  
Consistent with the interpretation of the rheological and calorimetric results, the zeta potential 
measurements confirm electrostatic interactions between CNF and cement particles. The 
increase in static yield stres s without a corresponding rise in heat of hydration or storage 
modulus, combined with the strongly anionic charge of CNF and the near -neutral charge of 
cement particles, collectively indicate that the dominant mechanism governing CNF ’s

Response: We thank the reviewer for this important point. We have added additional discussions 
in section S4 to clarify this point. The rheological testing protocol used in this study was developed 
based on previous studies investigating the influence of nanomaterials on the rheological behavior 
of 3D-printed concrete 10,14,28. Our protocol measured viscosity, static yield stress, storage modulus 
in series, to reduce the overall number of tests while simulating the 3D concrete printing process. 
In extrusion-based 3D concrete printing, the material typically experiences continuous shear while 
being pumped and extruded, followed by a stage after deposition during which each printed layer 
must maintain its shape and support the load of subsequent layers. Moreover, the specific shear 
history imposed on the material varies with the characteristics of the mixing, pumping, conveying, 
and extrusion systems used in the printing setup.  Therefore, it is essential that rheological 
measurements be conducted from a same well-defined, deflocculated reference state to ensure 
consistency across tests 10,14,29. To achieve this, a 10 -minute pre-shear was applied , allowing the 
material to reach a stable, reference deflocculated steady state. This pre-shear step also simulates 
the shearing experienced by the material during the pumping and extrusion stages. Following the 
pre-shear stage, the static yield stress was measured to evaluate the material ’s ability to retain its 
shape immediately after deposition. Subsequently, the stiffness of the fresh mixture was assessed 
to determine its resistance to elastic deformation and its capacity to maintain structural stability 
during the layer-by-layer printing process. Because the static yield stress measurement is part of 
the storage modulus shear history, the subsequent storage modulus reflects both intrinsic material 
stiffness and the influence of prior shear. During actual 3D printing operations, the mixtures 
undergo complex and varying shear histories throughout the pumping process, rather than being 
exposed to a single shear condition.  Therefore, this sequential testing approach enables consistent 
comparison between mixtures under a shear history analogous to that experienced during 3D 
concrete printing. Besides, all parameters remain directly comparable across mixtures because all 
tests were performed under consistent conditions, with composition being the only variable. Taking 
together, we believe that our approach is justified by the existing literature 10,14,29 and is consistent 
with the aim of this study, which is to assess how dif ferent additives influence the rheological 
behavior of cementitious materials.
