### General comments 
This manuscript tackles an important issue, namely the need to quantify the ability of a chemical system to “learn”, not
merely to be correlated with the environment, but to acquire specific properties that would make it better over time at “doing
something”. Here, “doing something” means “producing matter through autocatalysis, using given resources”. Yet I must say
I am still struggling to understand what “” means. I first expected that the paper would investigate how much productivity
increases across successive active–inactive cycles: 
would then mean “getting closer to the optimal strategy”. In fact, I think I
now understand that the paper (in Part
 
II) formalises the extent to which different patterns of environmental changes (with
different levels of predictability) translate into different optimal strategies and (in Part
 
III) how these different optimal
strategies could be implemented in a real system, if one could tune the kinetic rate constants. 
I enjoyed reading this paper in depth. In a sense, the findings may seem a bit trivial at the end, yet the path to understand
them is interesting in itself. The framework is one where a variety of replicators, differing in their replication speed and/or
their “adaptation” to different environments, are subject to active and inactive phases. During active phases, there is an
inflow of matter, translating into an output driven by autocatalysis. Environments (e.g. what matter gets in) can vary among
successive active phases. During inactive phases, the system gets back to equilibrium, more or less fast depending on the
kinetic rate constants. In brief, the analysis establishes that the optimal amounts of replicators at the beginning of an active
phase depend on the duration and probability of the different environments encountered. 
One would expect to see that positive correlation, or even anticorrelation, should modify the optimal degree of memory.
Changing the degree of memory would seem like a stronger form of . From what I understand, the model does not explore

II, where the degree of memory is not part of what defines an optimal strategy. In contrast, this idea is present in

The bias toward slow replicators is also something that I struggled to understand. I first had the impression that slow

One general question I have concerns the possibility of extending the findings: to what extent could these formulations be
applied to any kind of chemical reaction network? What would be required to make the approach more “bottom up,” starting
from an explicit reaction network where autocatalysis might involve multiple steps rather than simple self replicators, as
assumed here? I am asking this because self replication is much less common in chemical soups than autocatalytic cycles.
It is far from certain that the results presented here would still hold in this more general and more realistic framework. A
paragraph in the Discussion section goes in that direction; I would suggest pushing the reflection further. 
Another general remark concerns a possible confusion between “” and “ability to learn.” As I understand it, the model shows
that, given some diversity of self replicators in terms of (1) replication speed and (2) adaptation to diverse environments, we
observe an increase in productivity in a given environment. The underlying mechanism appears to be a form of natural
selection favouring the replicators that become more abundant over time in this environment. Yet the Discussion section
briefly extrapolates to the idea that systems that learn better would be advantaged. This issue is not explored here, and I
would suggest making this distinction clearer—perhaps earlier in the paper (abstract, introduction). 
I found the Discussion section a bit weak. In line with my comments above, I would suggest trying to anticipate more
thoroughly the implications of considering more complex patterns of autocatalysis in more realistic reaction networks and
also discussing the role of rudimentary natural selection as the mechanism underlying the gain in functional information in
these systems. The notion that more productive systems may be advantaged could also be more explicitly discussed, trying
to clarify what internal properties would make some systems better at generating functional information than others.

Response to Referee 1
General comments
This manuscript tackles an important issue, namely the need to quantify the ability of a chemical
system to “learn”, not merely to be correlated with the environment, but to acquire specific
properties that would make it better over time at “doing something”. Here, “doing something”
means “producing matter through autocatalysis, using given resources”. Yet I must say I am still
struggling to understand what “learning” means. I first expected that the paper would investigate
how much productivity increases across successive active–inactive cycles: learning would then
mean “getting closer to the optimal strategy”. In fact, I think I now understand that the paper
(in Part II) formalises the extent to which different patterns of environmental changes (with
different levels of predictability) translate into different optimal strategies and (in Part III) how
these different optimal strategies could be implemented in a real system, if one could tune the
kinetic rate constants.
I enjoyed reading this paper in depth. In a sense, the findings may seem a bit trivial at the
end, yet the path to understand them is interesting in itself. The framework is one where a
variety of replicators, differing in their replication speed and/or their “adaptation” to different
environments, are subject to active and inactive phases. During active phases, there is an inflow
of matter, translating into an output driven by autocatalysis. Environments (e.g. what matter
gets in) can vary among successive active phases. During inactive phases, the system gets back
to equilibrium, more or less fast depending on the kinetic rate constants. In brief, the analysis
establishes that the optimal amounts of replicators at the beginning of an active phase depend on
the duration and probability of the different environments encountered.
We thank the referee for their careful reading of our paper, positive evaluation, and detailed
comments. In the following, we address their comments in detail.
