(Remarks to the Author) 
There are two highlights of this work: 1. The fabrication of Janus-structured fibrous artificial muscles by bicomponent
extrusion spinning; the combination of polyurethane (PU) and liquid crystal elastomer (LCE) improves the mechanical
properties of the LCE. 2. Leveraging the soft elasticity of liquid crystal elastomers and the dynamic bond exchange of
polyurethane (PU), multi-modal shape programming of Janus fibers has been achieved. However, as a fibrous artificial
muscle, shape change is only one aspect; the actuation properties (strain, stress, energy density, etc.) are even more crucial.
The following revisions are suggested: 
1. For straight Janus LCE/PU fibers prepared by a continuous extrusion platform, please provide their actuation strain,

for shape-changing materials used as artificial muscles. 
2. For spring-like Janus fiber actuators, how about their loading capabilities? Can these spring-like soft actuators load
external load and cyclically lift the load like a soft spring upon thermal actuation? 
3. What factors influence the order parameter? What is the range of the order parameter for processed fiber?

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript describes a continuous extrusion method for fabricating Janus LCE fibers. The integration of dynamic
covalent bonds with programmable molecular alignment to create asymmetric, biomimetic fibers is a compelling approach,
and the work constitutes a meaningful contribution to the field. Applications in soft robotics and adaptive textiles effectively
demonstrate the potential of this methodology. 
That said, the study would benefit from more comprehensive quantitative data to firmly substantiate its main conclusions.
Several claims currently rely on qualitative demonstrations rather than systematic experimental validation. Mechanistic
explanations, particularly those concerning material interactions and actuation control, need further development to be fully
convincing. 
I recommend major revision to address the following points: 
1.The proposed reinforcement mechanism involving the PU network requires more direct evidence at the molecular level. A
deeper analysis of the LCE-PU interface and how dynamic covalent bonding specifically enhances mechanical properties
would strengthen this section. 
2.Although the material shows promising reversibility over 50 cycles, extended cycling tests (e.g., several hundred cycles)
are necessary to properly evaluate its long-term durability and fatigue resistance. 
3.The influence of extrusion parameters on mechanical performance needs quantitative support. Data establishing clear
correlations between extrusion speed and tensile properties, with statistical validation, should be included. 
4.The shape recovery behavior under different temperature gradients requires further systematic quantification. Recovery
rates and positional accuracy across a range of controlled thermal conditions would help clarify performance limits. 
5.The control of various motion modes via light-thermal stimuli needs clearer characterization. Quantitative relationships
between stimulus parameters (e.g., intensity, wavelength, duration) and the resulting actuation behavior should be
established. 
6.The motion mechanism of the quadruped robot needs clarification. Please explain the functional differences between the
two side feet and how each contributes to the distinct locomotion modes. 
7.There is an inconsistency in light source labeling between the main text (NIR, 808 nm) and Figure 5 (IR). The figure should
be corrected for accuracy and consistency. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a strategy for the continuous fabrication of Janus LCE fibers that integrate a stimuli-responsive
LCE network with a dynamically reconfigurable PU network. The authors demonstrate programmable and reprogrammable
actuation, bioinspired design, and scalable integration into functional textiles, representing a notable advance in the field of
soft robotics and adaptive materials. 
1.The success of co-extrusion relies on the viscosity matching between the two precursors. Could the authors provide
quantitative data or discussion on how small viscosity deviations affect the interface stability and fiber morphology? 
2.The PU component exhibits dynamic covalent bond exchange above 55 °C, while the T
ₙᵢ
 of the LCE is around 67 °C.
Figure 2i indicates that the PU network undergoes relatively fast stress relaxation upon heating. Could the authors elaborate
on how the rate of dynamic bond exchange affects the long-term actuation stability, as well as potential creep and fatigue
behaviors under repeated thermal cycling? 
3.Regarding the fabrication process of the Janus fibers, I have several technical questions: (1) The process involves a two-
step UV curing. How is the crosslinking degree controlled during the first UV exposure to ensure that further alignment and
secondary curing can proceed effectively? (2) During the first UV curing, does the partial polymerization already fix the
shear-induced orientation of the LCE domains, or is the alignment mainly achieved during the subsequent stretching step?
(3) Upon the final thermal treatment, will the pre-aligned LCE phase experience slight shrinkage that may influence the
morphology of the adjacent PU domain, even if the temperature does not reach T
ₙᵢ
? (4) Could the authors further elaborate
on the detailed mechanism by which heating promotes hydrogen-bond formation within the PU network?

4.What is the interfacial adhesion strength between the LCE and PU domains? Are these two materials prone to
delamination under mechanical deformation? For long-term use, could a mismatch in strain or modulus between the LCE
and PU layers lead to interfacial instability or fatigue? 
5.In Figure 3c, what is the surface temperature reached during NIR laser irradiation? Is the temperature distribution across
