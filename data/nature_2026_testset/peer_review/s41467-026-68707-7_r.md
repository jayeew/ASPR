Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Spatial Heterogeneity and Subtypes of Functional Connectivity Development in Youth——Comments 
Li and colleagues leverage large-sample fMRI data and machine learning-based brain age prediction to propose a regional
brain development index (RBD) and identify three individual-level subtypes of functional connectivity (FC) over
development. They further demonstrate how these subtypes relate to cognition, cortical hierarchy, myelination, and gene
expression—linking multiple biological levels. Notably, the P2 subtype (characterized by advanced association cortex
development) shows better cognitive performance across two independent cohorts, highlighting the robustness and
scientific value of their findings. This work pushes beyond traditional global metrics (like brain age gap, BAG), offering the
first demonstration of FC developmental spatial heterogeneity and its multi-level implications at the individual level. Despite
these strengths, I do have several comments and suggestions to improve the manuscript: 
1. It seems that the authors do not adequately account for critical confounders such as socioeconomic status (SES), parental
education, or health conditions. The large-scale cohort studies such as ABCD and IMAGEN routinely include these as
covariates, since they are tightly linked to brain structure, function, and cognitive development in youth (Jirsaraie et al., 2023
Nat Commun; Noble et al., 2015 Nature). I thus suggest that the authors include these variables as covariates in their main
or supplementary analyses, or at least stratifying their results accordingly. Doing so would substantially strengthen the
credibility and scientific validity of your conclusions. 
2. Although the three FC developmental subtypes differ in executive function, social cognition, and memory, the cognitive
battery is relatively narrow and does not capture a broader spectrum of behavioral and psychological abilities, such as
emotion processing, motivational regulation, or creative thinking. Given the multidimensional nature of adolescent behavior
and cognition—and their relevance to neurodevelopment (see Casey et al., 2018, Biol Psychiatry)—I encourage the authors
to more fully acknowledge this limitation and, where possible, connect with additional behavioral or affective dimensions
available in open datasets. 
3. The proposed three-subtype spatial clustering is mainly compared to the conventional whole-brain BAG, but there is no
direct benchmarking against other established spatial clustering methods—such as structural brain age subtyping (Wang et
al., 2019, Nat Commun), spectral clustering (Satterthwaite et al., 2015, Neuron), or GMM. Please consider providing a
systematic comparison of clustering performance (e.g., ARI, NMI, stability), cognitive associations, and spatial distribution
overlap to clarify the innovation and boundaries of your approach. It would be also helpful if the authors can provide further
justification for the chosen number of clusters (k), including cross-cohort reproducibility or biological interpretability. 
4. While the manuscript connects gene expression, myelination, FC, and cognition, most analyses are correlational. It is now
well accepted that relationships across biological scales are often complex, with mediation, moderation, and feedback loops
(see Fornito et al., 2015, Nat Rev Neurosci). I would suggest explicitly discussing potential mechanistic links between the
micro, meso, and macro levels (e.g., via multimodal causal modeling, SEM, network mediation analysis) and acknowledging
the current limitations of causal inference. 
5. The analyses rely on two large cross-sectional cohort studies (PNC and HCP-D), which are valuable for describing group-
level differences across age but cannot capture within-individual developmental trajectories. The field broadly recognizes
that longitudinal follow-up is necessary to truly understand developmental rate, sequence, and causality (see Kraemer et al.,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

2000, Am J Psychiatry; Tamnes et al., 2017, Nat Neurosci). Please discuss this limitation, and emphasize the need for future
longitudinal studies (e.g., ABCD, IMAGEN) that can follow the same individuals over time to more accurately characterize
brain developmental heterogeneity and its cognitive and molecular correlates. 
6. The introduction does not sufficiently relate classical neurodevelopmental theories (e.g., Felleman & Van Essen, 1991,
cortical hierarchy; Huttenlocher, 1979, synaptic pruning; Hebb, 1949, plasticity) to the novel aspects of spatial subtyping. I
would recommend more explicitly connecting the study’s approach to these foundational models in both the introduction and
discussion, which would help a broader audience appreciate the conceptual advance. 
7. The results are organized as spatial variability/heterogeneity, subtyping, cognition/gene associations, and replication.
However, the scientific rationale and linkage between each step could be more clearly articulated—for instance, why certain
molecular analyses were performed post-subtyping, and how each step is motivated by prior findings or hypotheses.
Explicitly stating the underlying logic for each major analysis will make the paper more cohesive and accessible. 
8. Figure 1 shows the RBD analysis pipeline, but does not clearly label model inputs and outputs, the formula for RBD, or
how RBD feeds into downstream clustering and association analyses. Please consider annotating workflow nodes, core
equations, and analytic steps to improve interpretability, especially for interdisciplinary readers. 
9. Currently, subtyping is based only on brain imaging. There is increasing interest in behavioral (e.g., LCA) or joint brain-
behavior subtyping approaches (see Drysdale et al., 2017, Am J Psychiatry; Smith et al., 2022, Trends Cogn Sci). I
encourage the authors to discuss prospects for integrating behavioral subtyping with brain-based subtyping, and perhaps
even attempt a preliminary behavioral subtyping analysis with available data to explore overlap and complementarity, which
could inform future stratified intervention strategies. 
10. All major analyses are correlational, and there is no direct causal chain established between FC subtype, cognition, or
gene expression. Please make a clear distinction between correlation and causality in the discussion and avoid over-
interpretation. Where possible, supplement with sensitivity analyses—such as subgroup analysis by age, sex, or SES;
mediation analysis or SEM to explore whether FC subtype mediates links between molecular/structural factors and cognitive
outcomes. Also, highlight that future work leveraging longitudinal data, intervention, or multi-omic integration will be needed
to clarify causal mechanisms. Such enhancements would greatly increase the scientific impact and robustness of findings.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Li and colleagues examined spatial patterns of functional brain development across two normative
samples and characterized data-driven subgroups by their cognitive profiles. The study is well motivated: deriving regional
brain age estimates addresses an important limitation of traditional brain age studies that rely on a single global metric. 
My primary concern relates to the authors' approach to replicating the data-driven analysis. The authors applied k-means
clustering to identify subgroups based on patterns of regional brain development (RBD) indices in their primary cohort (PNC)
and compared cognitive scores across subgroups. Because clustering will always produce a solution, independent
replication is critical to demonstrate that subgroups are meaningful rather than artifacts. To address this, the authors
classified individuals from their replication cohort (HCP-D) using PNC-derived centroids and concluded that subgroup
replicability was supported by similar cognitive differences across datasets. However, this approach does not validate
replicability. 
True replication would require rerunning clustering independently in both cohorts and demonstrating that (1) subgroup
patterns of RBD indices and (2) associated cognitive profiles are similar. Without establishing (1), it remains unclear whether
comparable subgroup structures exist. Classifying HCP-D participants based on PNC centroids assumes, rather than tests,
subgroup generalizability and risks overestimating replicability. For example, it is theoretically possible that multiple,
drastically different RBD patterns could yield similar distributions of cognitive scores without reflecting the same underlying
neurodevelopmental patterns. To convincingly demonstrate replicability, the authors should rerun clustering independently
in the HCP-D cohort and compare both subgroup structures and cognitive profiles to those observed in PNC. 
Separately, the cognitive measures differ substantially between the two datasets (executive functioning, social cognition,
and memory in PNC, versus fluid cognition, crystallized cognition, and total cognition in HCP-D). As a result, even if
subgroup differences were consistent in direction or magnitude, it would be difficult to interpret this as evidence of
replication, given that the constructs are not directly comparable. To strengthen their conclusions, the authors should
harmonize cognitive domains across cohorts or more explicitly acknowledge this limitation. 
Additional comments for improving the manuscript: 
• The use of cross-sectional data to study development is a major limitation that is not discussed in the manuscript. This
should be acknowledged. 
• Normative modeling, which similarly derives individualized regional deviations from expected trajectories (and can

incorporate non-linearity and covariates, stated limitations of the chosen approach), is not mentioned. While I do not expect a
change in approach, a brief rationale for choosing RBD over normative modeling would be helpful. 
• The choice of the Schaefer 400 parcellation should be justified, particularly given evidence suggesting that the 200-parcel
resolution produces more representative connectivity networks (Luppi et al., 2021). Additionally, the omission of the typically
paired Melbourne subcortical atlas is a missed opportunity to capture subcortical contributions. 
• Raw correlation values were used as inputs to the ridge regression model. Typically, Fisher r-to-z transformation is applied
to stabilize variance and normalize correlations; the rationale for deviating from this standard practice should be provided. 
• I appreciate the authors' approach to determining the optimal number of clusters. However, it would strengthen the
manuscript to include more standard clustering validity metrics (e.g., Silhouette coefficient) in the supplemental material for
comparison. 
• More detail is needed on the clustering methodology: Why was k-means chosen over alternative algorithms (e.g., spectral
clustering)? Why was the cosine distance metric used? 
• Complete statistical reporting is needed throughout the manuscript, including test statistics, exact p-values, and effect
sizes. Currently, many results are only partially reported (e.g., indicating p < 0.05 without further detail). 
• The manuscript should specify that Python and scikit-learn were used for the ridge regression modeling, including software
versions. 
• A table summarizing cohort characteristics (age, age range, sex, race/ethnicity, scanner information, motion parameters,
cognitive performance, etc.) should be included.

individual variability between P1 and P3. Based on all these quantitative and qualitative 
assessments, the number of clusters was set to 3 in our analysis.

development (RBD) indices in their primary cohort (PNC) and compared cognitive scores across 
subgroups. Because clustering will always produce a solution, independent replication is critical 
to demonstrate that subgroups are meaningful rather than artifacts. To address this, the authors 
classified individuals from their replication cohort (HCP-D) using PNC-derived centroids and 
concluded that subgroup replicability was supported by similar cognitive differences across 
datasets. However, this approach does not validate replicability. 
 
True replication would require rerunning clustering independently in both cohorts and 
demonstrating that (1) subgroup patterns of RBD indices and (2) associated cognitive profiles 
are similar. Without establishing (1), it remains unclear whether comparable subgroup structures 
exist. Classifying HCP-D participants based on PNC centroids assumes, rather than tests, 
subgroup generalizability and risks overestimating replicability. For example, it is theoretically 
possible that multiple, drastically different RBD patterns could yield similar distributions of 
cognitive scores without reflecting the same underlying neurodevelopmental patterns. To 
convincingly demonstrate replicability, the authors should rerun clustering independently in the 
HCP-D cohort and compare both subgroup structures and cognitive profiles to those observed 
in PNC. 
 
Separately, the cognitive measures differ substantially between the two datasets (executive 
functioning, social cognition, and memory in PNC, versus fluid cognition, crystallized cognition, 
and total cognition in HCP-D). As a result, even if subgroup differences were consistent in 
direction or magnitude, it would be difficult to interpret this as evidence of replication, given that 
the constructs are not directly comparable. To strengthen their conclusions, the authors should 
harmonize cognitive domains across cohorts or more explicitly acknowledge this limitation. 
Response: We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comments and constructive

Response: Thank you for this thoughtful comment. While normative modeling is 
increasingly used to derive deviation scores from normative trajectories for individuals, it is 
typically based on element-wise regression, which poses challenges for modeling FC due

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 18 -->

to its high dimensionality. Prior studies applying normative modeling to FC have either used 
summary measures such as FC strength [ref r12] or surrogate scores derived from 
dimension reduction techniques [ref r13], which may not fully capture the richness of FC 
profiles. Although multivariate normative modeling methods exist, their application to FC 
data remains relatively limited. 
In contrast, the predictive modeling of brain age provides an interpretable, composite score 
that reflects the development status of each cortical region based on its full FC profiles, 
without relying on FC strength or dimension reduction. Moreover, the calibration of 
predicted brain age against chronological age in the pipeline is an analogy to normative 
modeling, enabling the estimation of individual deviations in brain development. 
We have added a discussion regarding this in the revised manuscript.
