Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper by Sharp et al. is an interesting application study of hydroxyl radical footprinting, a relatively new technique for
structural proteomics, which highlights new approaches for structural labelling of whole cells (here, red blood cells). The
work presented is mostly novel, and although in part specific for the use of a commercial footprinting system most of the
findings should also be applicable to laser-based and synchrotron footprinting approaches. 
The work is of sufficient interest to a broader readership and I recommend the paper for publication subject to a number of
revisions as suggested below. 
The paper talks about labelling of mammalian tissue, but I would not call blood a tissue - these are cells. 
Can you speculate about the oxidation reaction with the sulfate radical anion, is that radical more stable than hydroxyl and
does it not also lead to other secondary reagent ions (i.e. ROS/RONS)? I would assume that more is known about the
mechanism here albeit maybe not in the context of footprinting. Is it important that persulfate rather than sulfate is used, given
that both could in theory generate a sulfate radical anion? This is a prominent outcome of the research presented here and
should be discussed in more detail. 
Figure 1 is suggestive of two samples being pooled, which I presume is not the case - please edit the figure to make it clear
that two different biological conditions are being compared and that readouts of the experiment are relative changes in
oxidation/modification at the peptide level. 
Unless I missed it, the specific modifications (e.g. +16 Da) which are detected and used for determining relative differences
are not specified. Which types of mass shifts occur with (per)sulfate and which ones form the basis of relative quantitation
here? The section on "Proteomic data processing" also lacks information on how those modifications considered here were
quantified. 
Most readers will not know what is meant by "secondary oxidation products". Are these oxidations mediated by secondary
reagents produced from (per)sulfate, e.g. ROS/RONS? or specific types of modifications found on amino acids, e.g. those
due to slow oxidation reactions due to incomplete quenching? Please provide a clear definition of the term, and why these
are potentially problematic (although it could be argued that slow labelling products are likely still structurally informative). 
Clarify why background oxidation is measured in the presence of reagent (but no UV) rather than in absence of reagent? 
Does imidazole really make a difference for the quench - I see hardly any significant difference apart from the one instance
which was highlighted already. 
Were the peptides of the two types of conditions (mice) also checked for changes in PTMs which could confound the results
of the peptide oxidation searches? 
The two proteins found to carry significant differences between wt/disease variant are interesting, can you suggest what type
of conformational change or aggregation could cause these, in the light of what is known from literature? It would be good to
follow these results up a bit more what they can tell us about the state of these proteins in the red blood cells (?), and how
that relates to disease mechanism in the pancreas or liver (?).

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors describe radical protein footprinting (RPF) with mass spectrometry detection on proteins in whole blood,
representing an expansion of footprinting methods to more biologically-relevant samples. The authors demonstrate that a
different radical precursor (i.e. persulfate) than hydrogen peroxide, which is typically used in RPF, is needed because of the
high concentration of catalase in blood that causes foaming in the presence of hydrogen peroxide. 
Overall, I think the manuscript should be published after some significant revisions. The ability to do RPF in whole blood is
important, but the manuscript needs some additions and changes, which are listed below, to make it suitable for publication. 
1. Because Figure 2 primarily reports the results of H2O2 treatment of blood, which is not used in the rest of the manuscript, I
recommend that this Figure be moved to the SI. This move would make room for an additional figure that I recommend
below. 
2. How is the 'Modification extent' shown on the y-axes of Figures 4, 6, 7, and 8 calculated? This information should be
included in the methods section. 
3. In discussing the data associated with Figure 6, the authors indicate that 20% of the peptides from extracellular proteins
exhibit an average modification extent of greater than 0.3, while only 1.5% of the intracellular proteins passed this threshold.
For some frame of reference, it would be helpful to indicate what percentages of peptides from a pure protein in buffered
solution would exceed an average modification extent of 0.3. 
4. The authors note that three peptides from intracellular proteins have significant differences in the extent of modification
between the WT and T2DM mice. Can the authors speculate about the structural or biological significance of these
observations? Or, are these changes simply method related (e.g. differential permeability of persulfate into blood cells from
the different samples)? If the authors are going to note this observation, then some comment on it would improve the
manuscript. 
5. In discussing the data in Figure 7, the authors note that "Oxidation coverage of the detected peptides ranged from sparse
to very high, which is consistent with reported findings…" I think that they mean that this observation is consistent with other
in vivo findings, correct? The ‘in vivo’ qualifier should be added to the text. 
6. It appears that there are 4 biological replicates for the data shown in Figures 6 and 7. I think that the methods section says
blood from 4 mice (of each type) were taken, but it would be good to note this value in the figure captions. 
7. In Figure 7, are the peptides indicated in parts A-E only those that are oxidized or are these all the detected peptides?
This information would be helpful for knowing if the information is limited by overall sequence coverage or are only certain
peptides oxidized (while more are not)? 
8. The structures shown in Figure 7G and F are small and difficult to see. Perhaps the authors could put these structures in
their own figure. If Figure 2 is moved to the SI, as suggested above, then the total number of figures would be the same. 
9. Any speculation as to what specific factors cause the alterations in the protein surface accessibility of complement c3 and

the paper. 
10. The comparison of the in vitro footprinting of complement c3 to the in-blood results is interesting and important; however,

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
The paper by Sharp et al. is an interesting application study of hydroxyl radical footprinting, a relatively new technique for 
structural proteomics, which highlights new approaches for structural labelling of whole cells (here, red blood cells). The 
work presented is mostly novel, and although in part specific for the use of a commercial footprinting system most of the 
findings should also be applicable to laser-based and synchrotron footprinting approaches. 
The work is of sufficient interest to a broader readership and I recommend the paper for publication subject to a number of 
revisions as suggested below. 
 
Comment: The paper talks about labelling of mammalian tissue, but I would not call blood a tissue - these are cells. 
Response: We respectfully disagree with the reviewer’s comment. Blood is widely classified as a specialized connective 
tissue in biological and medical literature (one representative citation given below), having its origin from mesenchyme and 
its function in transport and homeostasis. Blood is thus a connective tissue whose matrix consists of free-flowing ground 
substance (plasma) with no fibers. However, as this is not key to the manuscript, we are willing to change the wording at the 
editor’s discretion. 
Reference: 
Kamrani, P., Marston, G., Arbor, T. C. & Jan, A. Anatomy, Connective Tissue. In: StatPearls [Internet]. Treasure Island (FL): 
StatPearls Publishing; 2023 Mar 5. PMID: 30860769. 
Comment: Can you speculate about the oxidation reaction with the sulfate radical anion, is that radical more stable than 
hydroxyl and does it not also lead to other secondary reagent ions (i.e. ROS/RONS)? I would assume that more is known 
about the mechanism here albeit maybe not in the context of footprinting. Is it important that persulfate rather than sulfate is 
used, given that both could in theory generate a sulfate radical anion? This is a prominent outcome of the research presented 
here and should be discussed in more detail. 
Response: One reason for choosing SO₄⁻• over other potential radicals is its reduction potential is 2430 mV , which is higher 
than that of •OH (1900 mV). In vitro RPF studies with the sulfate anion radical show that the same level of global 
modification can be achieved as the hydroxyl radical at three times lower precursor concentration [1-3].  
Gross and coworkers [1] showed that the commonality of the sets of modifications produced by in vitro SO₄⁻• and •OH RPF 
method suggests that the dominant chemical pathways of SO4−• labeling are similar to the better understood •OH 
mechanisms. Gross and co-workers postulated that a likely explanation for these similar outcomes is that both processes 
begin with an initial hydrogen abstraction reaction at aliphatic sites. Additionally, formation of hydroxyl radicals by sulfate 
radical reaction with water (equation 1) may compete, giving rise to an •OH active radical [1]: 
SO₄⁻• + H₂O → SO₄²⁻ + H⁺ + •OH (1) 
This interpretation is supported by kinetic analysis from McElroy and Waygood [2]. 
In the present study, we describe why the more commonly used hydrogen peroxide is unsuitable for radical protein 
footprinting in blood due to the decreased stability of the radical precursor in blood. The lack of signaling activity of the 
sodium persulfate in cells is another of the major advantages. A caveat of using hydrogen peroxide is its preexisting role in 
signaling pathways which lead to biological changes within the cell [4].  
Sodium persulfate is chosen over sulfate because the peroxydisulfate bond enables efficient photochemical cleavage to 
generate potent, fast-reacting sulfate radicals under commonly used RPF laser wavelengths, whereas sulfate ions cannot be 
directly photoactivated at convenient wavelengths. This ensures effective and rapid protein labeling even in the highly 
complex, radical-scavenging environment of whole blood. Herrmann (2007) reviewed the photolysis of simple anions, 
including persulfate, confirming its properties for formation of sulfate radicals [5]. 
We have added a brief discussion of our choice of persulfate to the manuscript.

Comment: Most readers will not know what is meant by "secondary oxidation products". Are these oxidations mediated by 
secondary reagents produced from (per)sulfate, e.g. ROS/RONS? or specific types of modifications found on amino acids, 
e.g. those due to slow oxidation reactions due to incomplete quenching? Please provide a clear definition of the term, and 
why these are potentially problematic (although it could be argued that slow labelling products are likely still structurally 
informative). 
Response: We agree that “secondary oxidation products” should be defined more explicitly. In the revised manuscript, we 
now clarify that by “secondary oxidation products,” we refer to oxidative protein modifications that are not formed directly 
during the fast-labeling event by reaction of SO₄⁻• and •OH with the protein target, but instead arise from less reactive 
reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) generated as secondary intermediates from the initial radical chemistry. These 
products are most commonly created by insufficient scavenging of these slower-acting ROS/RNS and/or prolonged exposure 
to the photoactivatable precursor, which itself is a reactive species. 
Such modifications are problematic for our workflow because they may occur after protein conformations have changed post-
perturbation, leading to artifacts not reflective of the native structure. Discussion of this issue can be found throughout FPOP 
literature, but we cite Gau et al 2009 and Venkatesh, Tomer and Sharp 2007 where the issue of artifactual contributions of 
slow oxidation were approached quantitatively. 
This definition and rationale have been added to the revised manuscript for clarity.

Comment: Clarify why background oxidation is measured in the presence of reagent (but no UV) rather than in absence of 
reagent? 
Response: Persulfate can oxidize some organics directly, without involving radical species [1]. To account for background 
oxidation caused by the reagent itself and more accurately reflect the modifications resulting from the labeling chemistry, 
background oxidation was subtracted using control samples containing the reagent but no UV exposure. This captures both 
pre-existing protein oxidation products, as well as any persulfate-induced protein oxidation products generated independently 
of photo-activation (which are minimized by our workflow, but should nevertheless be included). This practice is in line with 
the current community-recommended best practices for radical protein footprinting [2]. However, as these recommendations 
are currently only available as a pre-print and are undergoing revision, we will not cite them in this manuscript.

Comment: Does imidazole really make a difference for the quench - I see hardly any significant difference apart from the

Response: Our results shown in Figure 4 are in agreement with anecdotal data: while traditional quench is sufficient for many 
peptides in persulfate-based RPF, there are a subset of peptides where we find the traditional quench insufficient. We 
identified two such peptides (α = 0.05) in our test system, and found imidazole to be sufficient to eliminate this background 
oxidation from persulfate. 
Comment: Were the peptides of the two types of conditions (mice) also checked for changes in PTMs which could confound 
the results of the peptide oxidation searches? 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this important comment. Adding a PTM search compounded with the RPF search 
quickly creates a problem with a search space that rapidly expands as each new potential differential modification is added to 
the search. We have included a brief discussion of this limitation to the manuscript. Additionally, we searched all proteins 
detected in this manuscript in UniProt for reported PTMs. We found that several intracellular proteins, particularly β-globin, 
α-globin, 60S ribosomal protein L40, and flavin reductase, contain extensive reported PTMs. In contrast, among the 
extracellular proteins investigated, no oxidized peptides from complement C3, serotransferrin, or apolipoproteins contain any 
reported PTMs. Only two peptides each from transthyretin and albumin contained previously reported phosphoserine 
modifications, which are unlikely to confound the significant conformational changes we observed in C3 and serotransferrin. 
Comment: The two proteins found to carry significant differences between wt/disease variant are interesting, can you 
suggest what type of conformational change or aggregation could cause these, in the light of what is known from literature? It 
would be good to follow these results up a bit more what they can tell us about the state of these proteins in the red blood

Response: In the revised manuscript, we demonstrate the conformational change of C3 detected by our RPF approach is 
consistent with complement activation in T2DM, which we assess by ELISA of C3 and its activated product C3b. The C3 
ELISA assay is specific for mouse C3, while the C3b ELISA kit uses antibodies raised against C3b, and these antibodies 
recognize epitopes that are preserved in C3b and its downstream fragments. As a result, the assay detects activated C3 species 
(C3b, iC3b, and C3c) but does not detect intact native C3. Thus, the measured signal reflects the overall level of C3 
activation, with C3b representing the major and immediate product and its derivatives contributing to the total detected 
activated C3 cascade. The result is shown in Figure 6. We also found that the conformational changes in transferrin is 
consistent with an increasing transferrin iron saturation in T2DM, which has been validated in the revised manuscript by 
measuring the serum iron and transferrin iron binding capability. The result is shown in Figure 7.
