Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Liu and co-workers proposes a NiFe LDH/FeMoO heterostructure catalyst for efficient and stable OER in
alkaline electrolysis. The catalyst exhibits a low overpotential of 316 mV at 2 A cm−2 and exceptional stability, driven by
LOM activation via oxygen vacancies and interfacial electric fields. The integrated system using the catalyst with a PV-
AEMWE system achieves solar to hydrogen efficiency of 20.15%. These findings will contribute significantly to the
development of OER catalysts for sustainable hydrogen production. While the work is innovative and methodologically
robust, several aspects require clarification to strengthen the scientific rigor and contextualize its impact. 
1. The authors have analyzed the valence states and coordination environments of Ni and Fe elements in the NiFe
LDH/FeMoO catalyst using XAF and XPS. However, no detailed analysis of the Mo element is provided. Given the
importance of FeMoO to the heterostructure, it is critical to understand the contribution of Mo to the catalyst. Such analysis
(chemical state and coordination of Mo) would be helpful in clarifying role of Mo in enhancing the catalytic performance. 
2. The one-step water bath synthesis method for the catalyst is ingenious. However, the manuscript does not mention
whether the proportions of raw materials in the precursor solution were adjusted to optimize the catalytic performance.
Different ratios of Ni, Fe, or Mo sources may cause a change in the catalyst’s structure and composition, which may affect its
activity and stability. Additionally, the reaction temperature plays an important role in the growth of the catalyst. 
3. The authors have explained the excellent stability of the NiFe LDH/FeMoO heterostructure catalyst very well. However, it
remains unclear whether the catalyst morphology changes after long-term stability testing. Given the importance of the
heterostructure in determining catalytic performance, it is necessary to investigate whether the heterostructure can still be
maintained. Characterization techniques such as SEM or TEM after long-term testing could provide valuable insights into
the morphological stability of the catalyst. 
4. The authors constructed a PV-AEMWE system to explore the application of the catalyst in hydrogen production through
renewable energy - powered water electrolysis, achieving a practical STH efficiency of 20.15%. Since this performance is
not currently being studied much, it would be more persuasive to compare it with other relevant studies. A table or figure
summarizing the key performance metrics of different PV-AEMWE systems, such as STH efficiency and current density,
would clearly show the advantages of the proposed catalyst system. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Liu et al. developed NiFe layered double hydroxide nanosheets on pyramid FeMoO for efficient and durable
oxygen evolution reactions. They demonstrated that the heterostructure catalyst achieves a lower overpotential at various
current densities compared to previously reported electrocatalysts. Through comprehensive analysis using in-situ
spectroscopy and DFT calculations, they provided mechanistic insights into the enhanced catalytic reactivity and structural
stability of the system. Furthermore, they demonstrated an anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer using NiFe
LDH/FeMoO anode and Y-NiMo/MoO2−x cathode, achieving a PV-EC system with a 20.1% STH efficiency when coupled
with a perovskite/Si tandem solar cell. In my opinion, the work aligns with the readership of Nature Communications and
should be considered for publication after minor revisions. 
1. The authors employed in-situ Raman spectroscopy to show the structural stability of the pyramid structure of the catalyst.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

They are encouraged to provide further morphological and crystallographic data following prolonged OER operation to
illustrate the structural integrity. 
2. The authors should include complete JV and EQE data, showing at least up to the VOC and JSC for both sweep
directions, and detailed PV parameters of the tandem solar cells. What are the areas of the PV and electrolysis cells? 
3. The STH efficiency of the integrated PV-AEMWE system appears to be limited by the current of the PV cell, when
compared to some state-of-the-art perovskite-on-silicon tandems. The authors are encouraged to discuss the potential for
improving the STH efficiency of water splitting systems using perovskite-based PV technologies. 
4. The NiFe LDH/FeMoO||Y-NiMo/MoO2−x electrolyzer demonstrated durability for 100 hours. However, for the integrated
PV-AEMWE system, only a 30-hour measurement was performed. The authors are encouraged to present results from
extended durability tests and compare these with reported PV-EC systems utilizing perovskite-based solar cells. Even if the
system is not yet stable, they should analyze the components that limit stability and potential solutions to enhance its
development for practical applications. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
General Comment 
This study introduces pyramidal Fe2(MoO4)3 into the benchmark alkaline OER catalyst NiFe LDH to promote the lattice
oxygen-mediated mechanism (LOM), thereby enhancing OER activity. The catalyst achieves an impressive current density
of 2 A cm
⁻
² at an overpotential of 316 mV and demonstrates a solar-to-hydrogen (STH) efficiency of 20.15% when integrated
with a perovskite/Si tandem solar cell. While the catalytic performance and durability are excellent, the manuscript lacks
sufficient discussion on the fundamental aspects of the catalyst and does not provide enough experimental detail for
reproducibility. In particular, substantial improvements are needed in the experimental data and interpretation related to in-
situ SEIRAS and Raman analyses. Without major revisions in these areas, I cannot support the publication of this
manuscript. 
Specific Comments 
1. The authors use RuO2 as a reference, but IrO2 is generally considered the commercial benchmark noble metal catalyst
for alkaline OER, especially due to its superior stability compared to RuO2. Please clarify this choice and, ideally, include
experimental data for IrO2. 
2. The manuscript primarily provides indirect evidence for LOM and AEM mechanisms (except for the theoretical calculation
data). Direct validation using isotope-labeling experiments (e.g., oxygen isotope mass spectroscopy, SEIRAS, or Raman
with H218O or D2O) is strongly recommended. 
3. The performance of in-situ SEIRAS is highly sensitive to catalyst film thickness and uniformity. Details such as ink
formulation, loading amount, and deposition method (e.g., whether to use spin coater, rpm settings) should be clearly
described to ensure reproducibility. 
4. There is a lack of essential information on potential step intervals and residence time during SEIRAS measurements.
Furthermore, Figures 3a and 3b do not clearly indicate at what potentials peaks emerge or whether their intensities increase
progressively. The authors must clarify and update this section with detailed discussion. 
5. Assignment of OO and OOH peaks: No experimental evidence is provided to support the assignment of SEIRAS peaks to
*OO or *OOH species. The authors should consider isotope-labeling experiments (e.g., with H218O and D2O-KOD) to
validate these peak assignments. 
6. *Ambiguity of OO origin: The manuscript claims that a higher *OO coverage correlates with LOM preference, but only
theoretical DFT evidence is provided. It remains unclear whether the observed *OO signal reflects a true lattice oxygen-
involved intermediate or a dual-site *OO species also seen in AEM. Further clarification and data are required. 
7. The interfacial H2O band at ~3280 cm-1 and bending H2O band at ~1640 cm-1 shown in Figures 3a and 3b lack
interpretation and do not appear to be critical to the current study. For clarity, the spectra should be rescaled to highlight only
the *OO and *OOH peaks. 
8. The authors attribute this O-H vibrational band (~3280 cm-1) to *OH adsorption, but such peaks are widely recognized as
arising from H2O species (e.g., 2HB-H2O, 4HB-H2O, and free H2O). If the authors intend to argue a link between water
adsorption and LOM preference, they must perform deconvolution and revise the interpretation. Otherwise, the current
explanation associating this band with *OH adsorption is not convincing. 
9. The authors mentioned that Mo–O species remain after OER (Figure 4b), which is not well supported. Mo is known to
leach under alkaline conditions even without applied potential, and even more so under OER conditions. The authors
should provide additional evidence (e.g., post-OER ICP analysis or ex-situ/OCP Raman data) to demonstrate that Mo–O
peaks persist with significant intensity. 
10. Typo in Figure 4f   The label “Fas telectron transport” should be corrected to “Fast electron transport.” 
11. Figure 4d presents Rct comparisons, but the corresponding description in the manuscript discusses ICP-OES results.
Please revise and clarify. 
12. A distinct peak near 760 cm-1 is observed in NiFe LDH/FeMoO but not in NiFe LDH. This feature should be identified,
especially since it disappears during OER. 
13. LDH-based samples often exhibit broad bands in the 900–1200 cm
⁻
¹ range, associated with O–O vibrations and linked
to LOM, which is namely “active oxygen”. The authors should provide high-frequency Raman data to discuss the potential-
dependent evolution of these species. 
14. Catalysts favoring LOM typically exhibit higher oxidation states in metal sites. However, the authors report delayed Ni

redox and suppressed Fe oxidation, which appears to contradict prior studies. A discussion reconciling this discrepancy is
needed.

Although the enhancement of M –O covalency via elevated oxidation states can 
facilitate the involvement of lattice oxygen in O –O bond formation, there are also 
inherent drawbacks. First, excessive consumption of lattice oxygen  can result in the 
uncontrolled accumulation of oxygen vacancies and disruption of the crystalline 
framework. Second, over-oxidation of Fe can lead to the formation of  soluble high-
valent ferrate species (e.g., FeO42−), which causes irreversible dissolution, depletion of 
lattice oxygen, interruption of the LOM cycle, and eventual loss of active sites . These 
issues highlight the well-recognized challenge of balancing high activity with long-term 
stability in LOM catalysts.  Additionally, recent studies by Shao et al. ( Nat. Energy 4, 
329 (2019); Nat. Commun.  12, 3992 (2021)) have revealed that the oxygen species 
involved in O –O coupling is not necessarily derived from the direct oxidation of 
transition-metal centers. Instead, electrophilic oxygen intermediates such as peroxo -
like (O 22−) or superoxo-like (O 2−) species can be generated through ligand -to-metal 
charge transfer, while the metal centers maintain relatively moderate oxidation states. 
These findings suggest that lattice oxygen participation can proceed without a 
pronounced increase in the formal valence of the metal cations, further supporting that 
high metal oxidation states are not an essential prerequisite for the LOM pathway. 
In the NiFe LDH/FeMoO heterostructure designed in this study, both experimental 
results and theoretical calculations reveal that interfacial interactions cause the O 2 p 
band centre to shift upwards. Furthermore, oxygen-vacancy-assisted electronic 
redistribution promotes the participation of lattice oxygen without triggering excessive 
Fe oxidation. This regulation arises from the synergistic effects of oxygen vacancies 
that tune the O 2p orbitals, and of interfacial built-in electric fields that modulate the Ni 
3d states. Consequently, the oxygen ligands accommodate a greater fraction of the 
oxidative charge, thereby lowering the barrier to lattice oxygen redox participation . 
Meanwhile, the oxidation of Ni and Fe cations is delayed rather than being forced into 
extremely high formal valence states under low overpotentials. In other words, it is
