Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Multi-year La Niña events occur frequently, and have a significant impact on global weather and climate. The present study
examined multi-year La Niña events, which are 
self-sustained by changes in rainfall and salinity. The authors revealed the
relationships between rainfall, salinity and the SST in both the central and eastern Pacific based on extensive data analysis
and model experiments. I particularly appreciated the authors’ efforts on model simulations. They conducted two sets of
ocean general circulation model experiments to illustrate the delayed effect of rainfall on salinity, and a series of well-
designed sensitivity experiments to examine the effect of rainfall on SST via salinity during multi-year La Niña events. The
model results provide valuable evidence to support effects of rainfall and salinity on multi-year La Niña events. The paper is
well-written, and the methods are convincing. Although the role of rainfall-salinity feedback in multi-year La Niña events is
high novel, some revisions are needed to clarify some issues before its publication. 
Specific comments: 
1. The abstract requires a clearer description of how salinity responds to multi-year La Niña events. It should also specify the
relative contributions of rainfall and oceanic dynamic processes during multi-year La Niña events. The explanation of the
slow response process to rainfall is overly simplistic and requires a more comprehensive introduction. Furthermore, the
implications presented in the abstract need to be strengthened to convey the impact of this study effectively. 
2. Model agreement and discrepancies in MLS simulation. 
I note that the simulated MLS variability in the GC model is weaker than that in ORAS5 reanalysis or IAP observational data.
While the authors have offered plausible explanations for this discrepancy, the potential implications of this model bias for
the subsequent sensitivity experiments require further clarification. For instance, how might this amplitude difference affect
the intensity of rainfall-salinity feedback? Regarding the ROMS simulation, although the amplitude of the modeled MLS
matches the observations well, notable differences exist in the spatial patterns. Specific reasons for these spatial
discrepancies should be addressed. 
3. In "Asynchronous effects of… on MLS" section, some of the texts overlaps with previous section and should be
condensed. While the significant role of zonal advection is acknowledged, a more detailed discussion of the impact of
vertical mixing processes is needed. A more detailed analysis of the balance between rainfall contribution and horizontal
advection in Figure 2a would be beneficial. Specifically, decomposing horizontal advection into its zonal and meridional
components during the NDJF period could reveal important underlying physical processes related to oceanic adjustments.
The current figure layout, while informative, could be enhanced through reorganization to better highlight these causal
relationships. 
4. The paper used ORAS5 data for the diagnostic analysis. Why not consider using IAP4 or EN4 based on the
observations? What differences might arise in the results? Additionally, I am wondering what role salinity plays in
stratification during a La Niña event in a single year, and how this compares with events spanning multiple years (double dip
or triple dip)? 
5. A more comprehensive discussion of the impact of rainfall and ocean salinity on ENSO diversity will benefit the
manuscript. In particular, the influence of rainfall on physical processes after it enters the ocean requires further clarification,
despite being mentioned briefly in the Methods section. 
6. I am unsure about the results presented in Extended Data Figure 3. The discussion is rather simplistic and lacks depth. To
improve clarity, I recommend incorporating this figure into the main text. 
7. Lines 76-86: The paper emphasizes that rainfall anomalies caused by multi-year La Niña events can have a lasting
impact on the ocean, which is further intensified due to the changes in ocean salinity. However, the paragraph does not
clearly explain their cumulative effect, which I believe is essential for readers to understand. 
8. Fig.S1: GODAS shows relatively weak interannual anomalies for MLS. What might be causing of this? Please investigate

“Multiyear La Niña events are becoming more frequent, as shown by historical

impacts on tropical Pacific rainfall variability, which subsequently affects mixed-layer

1. Robust effect of rainfall on multiyear La Nina in GC-OGCM experiments 
The experiments are divided into three types: applying rainfall anomalies in the 
first-year La Niña (1yr exp), applying rainfall anomalies in the second-year La Niña

La Niña (all exp). The rainfall anomaly is set at a fixed value of -9 mm/day to ensure 
comparability with the observed MLS amplitude, such that the impact of rainfall via 
the salinity pathway is reasonably represented. Nevertheless, these idealized 
experiments only emphasize the sensitivity of the rainfall effect to location and timing. 
On this basis, we have performed a zonal shift of the region where rainfall is applied to 
test the regional dependence of the rainfall feedback. Overall, eight sets of experiments 
are designed, each centered in the range of 150°E–140°W, with a zonal extent of 40°. 
For instance, the experiment centered at 150°E covers a spatial range of 130°E–170°E. 
Firstly, the experimental results show that in the 1yr exp, the impact of rainfall on 
MLS in the Niño4 region is more significant in the first-year La Niña (Figure R1); 
however, a distinctly positive anomaly persists in the second year, indicating that the 
initial rainfall can still affect MLS in the western-central equatorial Pacific in the 
subsequent year. Additionally, in the 2yr exp, the impact of rainfall on MLS in the 
second year is stronger than that of the 1yr exp on MLS in the first year, suggesting that 
the background field in the second year of multi-year La Niña is more conducive to 
amplifying salinity changes induced by rainfall. When considering the all exp, the 
impact of rainfall on MLS in the second year represents the superposition of the effects 
from the aforementioned two experiments, which further confirms the existence of a 
cumulative effect of rainfall on MLS during multiyear La Niña. Secondly, from the 
perspective of spatial differences, the mixed layer in the eastern equatorial Pacific is 
relatively shallow, making it prone to a stronger MLS response to rainfall anomalies;

2. Model dependency 
Regarding the potential model dependency issue arising from our use of a single 
model, since we are currently unable to perform unified experiments with multiple 
ocean models, we have summarized as comprehensively as possible the results from 
previous literature on the impacts of freshwater flux or rainfall on the tropical Pacific 
mean state and ENSO. Specifically, most studies have confirmed that when negative 
rainfall anomalies with an amplitude comparable to interannual variability are imposed 
on the western tropical Pacific, they can lead to a cooling of more than -0.3°C in the 
central-western equatorial Pacific (Kang et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2010)—this range is 
consistent with the SST changes observed in our current experiment. Alternatively, 
when interannually varying rainfall anomalies are incorporated into multiple ocean

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 26 -->

23 
 
models, it is found that they can increase the ENSO amplitude by 10-15%, a magnitude 
that aligns with the observational results in this study (Guan et al., 2022, 2023; Maes et 
al., 2002; Vialard et al., 2002; Yang et al., 1999; Zhu et al., 2014). 
Furthermore, we have also conducted experiments similar to the GC-OGCM using 
the ROMS. The results show that introducing negative rainfall anomalies in ROMS 
leads to a more pronounced cooling of the second-year La Niña (~0.1 °C), though the 
intensity is lower than that in the GC-OGCM.  The cooling effect of rainfall on SST in 
the ROMS is relatively weak, which may be attributed to the fact that its atmospheric 
forcing field is fully prescribed. In contrast, in the GC-OGCM, the key components of 
net heat flux (i.e., latent heat and sensible heat) are determined by an atmospheric mixed 
layer model (Refs. 9-10); thus, partial atmospheric feedbacks have been represented in 
GC-OGCM. Another factor is that the uppermost layer in GC-OGCM is the mixed layer, 
which results in the cooling effect of rainfall being more readily reflected in SST. 
Whereas in ROMS, rainfall is only added to the topmost layer (~5 m), so the impact of 
rainfall needs to propagate downward gradually through vertical diffusion. Therefore, 
combined with Table R1, we consider that our current conclusion—i.e., rainfall can 
maintain approximately 14-32% of the SST cold anomaly amplitude during multi-year 
La Niña events—is generally reasonable. However, in the discussion section, we also 
propose that more ocean models or climate models should be used to conduct the same 
numerical experiments to further consolidate this conclusion.
