REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author):

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

In the manuscript "Short-term Antarctic Ice-Sheet dynamics during the late Oligocene: multi-proxy records from ODP Site
689", the authors present a high-resolution, multi-proxy reconstruction of Antarctic Ice Sheet (AIS) dynamics between 26.2
and 25.2 Ma using benthic foraminiferal δ18O, Mg/Ca-derived bottom-water temperatures, and radiogenic Nd and Pb
isotopes from ODP Site 689. The study is methodologically robust and offers novel insights into short-term AIS variability
and associated weathering processes during the late Oligocene. They found eccentricity-paced ice volume fluctuations from
26.2 to 25.2 Ma with ice changes in the East Antarctica. However, several key issues need to be addressed before the
manuscript is suitable for publication. 
Major Comments 
1. Interpretation of δ18O and δ18O_SW: 
There appears to be some inconsistency in how the authors relate δ18O_SW to ice dynamics. In line 169, the manuscript
states that "the radiogenic isotope records also co-vary with δ18O_SW during most of the studied interval," yet the overall
correlation between δ18O_SW and the radiogenic isotope data seems weak. Furthermore, line 184 suggests that δ18O_SW
reflects factors beyond AIS dynamics. To strengthen the interpretation, I recommend the authors clarify the role of δ18O_SW
in reflecting ice-sheet variability. Is it viewed primarily as a proxy for ice-volume change, or do other processes such as local
salinity or water mass changes dominate? A more consistent conclusion throughout the text would improve clarity. 
Additionally, the statement that "δ18O_SW may reflect ice-sheet thinning/thickening, which would produce weathering
signals different from repeated ice-sheet advances/retreats" is unclear. Does this imply that thinning and thickening can
occur independently of advance/retreat cycles? If so, the physical distinction between these modes of ice-sheet change
needs to be explicitly explained. Otherwise, this could be misleading, as thinning/thickening and advance/retreat are
generally linked in ice-sheet dynamics. In my opinion, it is physically impossible for ice sheet thinning/thickening, while no
advance/retreat. 
2. Radiogenic Isotope Interpretations: 
The interpretation of authigenic Pb isotopes as reflecting subglacial weathering beneath an expanded East AIS is
compelling, but some points need clarification. Since changes in authigenic Pb isotopes can result from either source
composition or weathering intensity, why not also include a more detailed analysis of detrital Pb isotopes? Comparing
detrital Pb isotopes with εNd may help reduce uncertainty due to source change or weathering intensity change. 
Flowerdew et al. (2012, 2013) report continental Pb isotope data from East Antarctica, showing generally radiogenic
compositions. However, detrital 206Pb/204Pb values from ODP 689 appear lower than expected if East Antarctica is the
dominant source. The discrepancy warrants further discussion. Could there be contributions from other, less radiogenic
sources? 
In the interval between ~26.0 and 25.6 Ma, the offset between authigenic and detrital 206Pb/204Pb becomes more
pronounced, while no similar divergence is seen in 207Pb/204Pb and 208Pb/204Pb. An explanation for this selective
divergence would be valuable—does it reflect differential contributions from high-U/Pb versus low-U/Pb sources, or varying
weathering regimes? 
3. Long-term Trends in Isotope Data: 
The gradual increase in 206Pb/204Pb and simultaneous decrease in εNd is intriguing but only briefly discussed. Could this
trend reflect long-term AIS expansion or circulation shifts, such as increased influence of East Antarctic sources via the
Weddell Gyre? Since sediment supply (SS) also shows a long-term increasing trend, if the Weddell gyre is bringing more
east Antarctic material to the site, will it can the more radiogenic Pb and less radiogenic εNd? A more thorough exploration
of how water mass transport and provenance might co-evolve with ice-sheet dynamics would enhance the discussion. 
4. Sedimentological Constraints: 
Consider plotting both mean sediment supply (SS) and SS% to evaluate whether the observed sedimentation trends is
controlled by bottom-current strength. This could help assess whether changes in the isotope records are primarily linked to
provenance/weathering, or instead to transport processes. 
Minor Comments 
Avoid inserting spaces between text and citations throughout the manuscript. 
Figure 3: Consider labeling eccentricity maxima and minima directly on panel A to clarify their phase relationship with
δ18O_SW. 
Figure 4: Including correlation coefficients (R values) for panels B and C would help quantify relationships between proxies. 
Line 37: Replace "Milankovitch cycles" with "orbital forcing" for improved precision. 
Line 177: radiogenic Pb 
Line 245: Typo—please check the phrasing, particularly around "by ?".

Creac’h et al. present an impressive multi-proxy dataset from Southern Ocean ODP Site 689, providing critical new
constraints for our understanding of ice sheet behaviour during the late Oligocene. Debate over the timing and magnitude of
ice volume fluctuations during the early icehouse world have long persisted in the literature. However, using a combination
of d18Ob, benthic foraminiferal Mg/Ca (and calculated d18Osw ), and Nd and Pb isotopes, the authors provide further
support for large-scale waxing and wanning of the Antarctic ice sheet on eccentricity timescales. The authors are
commended for the impressive high-resolution multi-proxy records presented in this study which take a step forward in our
understanding of how dynamic the Antarctic ice sheet was during higher than present day pCO2 levels but within the range
projected for the end of this century and beyond. 
This is a timely study presenting important new results (and again, a very
impressive and hard-won set of records!), with well supported conclusions, There are however a few points that require
further clarification and/or discussion. 
My main concern with the manuscript in its present form concerns the discussion of d18Ob and d18Osw. Given the
importance of this records to the interpretations presented here, the factors driving d18Ob need to be clearly and properly
explained in the introduction and considered in full throughout the discussion. d18O 
is driven by temperature and d18Osw,
and in turn d18Osw is influenced globally by factors including ice volume and regionally by factors including salinity at sites
of deep water formation. Presently the interpretations in this manuscript are based on the assumption that ice volume is the
only factor driving changes in d18Osw. While, and especially on these timescales, it is likely to be the major driver of change
in d18Osw, it is incorrect to ignore these other factors which might vary by spatially and in relation to changes in ocean
circulation. These additional factors potentially driving d18Osw 
should not be ignored, but by combing the new d18Osw
reconstruction presented here with that of Brzelinksi et al., and compiling d18Ob records for the late Oligocene, (in addition
to Antarctic-proximal sedimentological evidence, including that presented here), the authors may be able to present an even
stronger argument for ice volume changes by showing whether or not comparison with other records suggests a role for
factors other than ice volume in driving d18Osw during some or all of their study interval. 
Furthermore, it is also important to acknowledge that even if the d18Osw change is driven solely by ice volume changes,
then it is difficult to translate this to an actual volume increase/decrease because of how the d18Oice changes throughout
the ice sheet growth. This being said, is it possible to estimate possible ice volume here and relate that to previous estimates
of sea level fall for this interval? 
A discussion of other factors influencing benthic foraminiferal Mg/Ca ratios should also be included when interrogating and
showing the robustness of the detailed Mg/Ca-derived BWT records presented here, which show little temperature
variability. Please also comment on foraminiferal preservation and whether any taphonomical gradient exists, especially
with respect to the eccentricity cycles targeted within this study. Several other deep sea sites (including 1263) experience
dissolution cycles. Are the same also seen at the shallower Site 689? And could this mask any temperature changes in the
Mg/Ca -derived BWTs? 
Please discuss what water mass is assumed to be bathing the study site and whether this has been consistent across the
study interval. It may also be helpful to comment on whether this is assumed to be the same water mass present at depth at
the other sites discussed with respect to pacing of waxing and waning of the Antarctic ice sheet (e.g., U1406 and 1263). 
If the assumption is that Site 689 is being bathed by waters sourced from the high southern latitudes, then a discussion is
missing on the fact that eccentricity is driving large-scale changes in the Antarctic ice sheet without impacting ocean
temperatures. Why are these changes not being ‘felt’ by ocean temperatures (surface ocean proximal to the ice sheet,
translating into deep ocean temperatures at the study site).

Assuming that (1) the isotopic composition of the late Oligocene ice-sheet was d18Oice = -42‰ (DeConto et al., 2008; Edgar et al., 2013; Eldrett et al., 2007; Gasson et al., 2016); (2) d18OSW = 0‰ reflects the volume of the modern AIS, i.e., about 26.9 x 106 km3 of ice (Fretwell et al., 2013; Paxman et al., 2019), and (3) a change in d18OSW of 0.1‰ infers an ice growth rate of 3.8 x 106 km3 (Bohaty et al., 2012; Liebrand et al., 2017).
