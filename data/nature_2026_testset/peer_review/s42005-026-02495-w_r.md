Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors used cluster expansion to determine the energetically stable configurations of two Lithium borocarbides,
namely, Li2B3C and Li3B4C2. They also carefully discussed the electronic behavior of their σ-bonding bands especially in
terms of phonon-mediated superconductivity and how they are tuned. Based on EPC calculations, the authors also
proposed a promising uniaxial strain-applying method to enhance the BCS superconductivity in Li2B3C. As a result, this
work may motivate researchers to explore the vast configuration space of lithium borocarbides as a promising and
competitive type of BCS superconductor. 
However, I am concerned that the following points need to be considered before the formal acceptance of this paper: 
1) Generally, MgB2 is not considered a high-temperature superconductor, since its TC is lower/close to the McMillan limit
40K. Some of the languages may need to be revised to be rigorous on this point. The current work is important since it
explores the feasible strategies for “achieving phonon-mediated high-temperature superconductivity”. However, "phonon-
mediated high-temperature superconductivity" is not an established concept in the field. Language use about it needs to be
cautious. 
2) In [20], only by totally ignoring the Coulomb pseudopotential can the Tc achieve as high as 55K. Therefore, the
corresponding results would be more persuasive if the author could consider whether the coarseness of the q-mesh may
lead to spurious enhancement in the low-frequency region of EPC parameter calculation. 
3) Abbreviations such as “TW” (this work) and “GLX” are not a common style in scientific literature. I would suggest to use
“ours” and “Gao et al.” instead. 
4) Using uniaxial straining (i.e. not ambient-pressure structure), the estimated TC of the LBC system is even lower than
MgB2 at ambient pressure. I suppose this may not be the fully optimized structure, following the light-element compound and
sigma-band metallization strategy. In this sense, I suggest the authors discuss the possibility of further optimizing/enhancing
TC at ambient pressure based on electron-phonon coupling in the discussion part. 
4) This article is a bit lengthy. I am sure that some pruning of the text would make it neater and more concise.

2. Why are the bonding WFs centered on the B-B or B-C bond centers s-like?
Reply: We appreciate the reviewer for his/her professional question about our Wannierization.
This is the key point of our Wannierization. In Bn+1Cn-1 plane, B/C’s 2s, 2px and 2py orbitals are
sp2 hybridized, forming ?-bonds between nearest-neighboring atoms. Those ?-bonds are very
strong, so the anti-bonding states are well above the Fermi level. As a result, we can use less WFs
to accurately describe the electronic physics by excluding those anti-bonding states (only use
pz-like WFs and s-like σ-bonding WFs).
The s-like WFs are the bonding states of sp2 hybridized orbitals: there is an increasing in wave
function of the bonding state between two nearest-neighboring atoms. On the other hand, the
anti-bonding states of sp2 hybridized orbitals are p-like: there is a nodal plane in wave function of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

the anti-bonding state between two nearest-neighboring atoms, resulting in a higher energy of
anti-bonding state.
3. The fillings of various σ-bonding WFs should be explained clearer. At least the first case must
be detailed. Why not considering self-doping from π bands? The authors wrote: "one hole is
doped into the nine σ-bonding bands of the B4C2(3-) layer (excluding contributions from
self-doping through π bands)." Why? Please explain the undoped case.
Reply: We thank the reviewer for this comment.
We did not consider self-doping from π bands because we wanted to build a foundation of
electronic structure analysis by simply counting electrons. We calculated the apparent electron
fillings of various σ-bonding orbitals without self-doping from ? orbitals (similar to calculate the
apparent chemical valence). This can effectively help us estimate the location of the Fermi level.
After that, the crystal field effect plays a secondary role on the electron filling: few holes are
always doped from π bands as cations are intercalated between layers in such MgB2-like structures.
For example, MgB2 is isoelectronic to graphene, whose σ-bonding orbitals should be totally filled
without considering self-doping from ? bands. As a result, after considering self-doping from π
bands, the Fermi level of MgB2 is slightly below the top of its B-B σ-bonding bands.
Turing to the first case Li2B3C (Gao et al.), it has one more hole compared with MgB2 and this
hole is doped into its B-B layer. As there are three B-B σ-bonding WFs in one B-B layer, the B-B
σ-bonding WFs should be 5/6 filled.
In the case Li3B4C2 (Gao et al.), it also has one more hole compared with MgB2, as Li3B4C2 can be
considered as LiB2+2LiBC. However, owing to its unique structure, the B-B σ-bonding WFs
strongly hybridize with the B-C σ-bonding WFs. As a result, it’s hard to determine the exact
doping level of the B-B σ-bonding bands while it is established that one hole is doped into the
B4C23- layer (three B-B bonds and six B-C bonds).
In our revised version, we modify the text to avoid misunderstanding:
“… one hole is doped in one B-B layer (three B-B σ-bonding WFs) , so B-B σ-bonding WFs are
nearly 5/6-filled.” (in section II.B)
“… one hole is doped into the nine σ-bonding bands of the B4C23- layer (without considering
self-doping from π bands)." (in section II.B)
We sincerely thank the reviewer again for bringing this to our attention and we have corrected the
filling number of Li2B3C (Gao et al.) in our revised version.
4. Why should we care about the specific filling numbers?
Reply: We thank the reviewer for raising this key point.
As mentioned in the previous question, we can estimate the filling numbers of σ-bonding bands by
ignoring self-doping from π bands. And this is the main concept in our electronic structure
analysis. As Bn+1Cn-1 layer can be regarded as one B-B zigzag chain and n-1 B-C zigzag chains,
LinBn+1Cn-1 always has one less electron than LiBC. Moreover, because the bridge B-B bonds are
shorter, the B-B σ-bonding bands in the bridge bonds are always totally filled. As a result, the hole
is always doped into those two B-B σ-bonding WFs in that B-B zigzag chain, causing the specific
filling number
3
4 across all LinBn+1Cn-1 compounds. As the self-doping plays a secondary role, this

filling number,
3
4, leads to the Fermi level lies at the dispersive part of B-B σ-bonding bands.
Those dispersive part are composed by non-bonding states, which are coupled with phonon
vibrations weakly. Conclusively, that specific filling number is the origin of the weak
electron-phonon coupling in LinBn+1Cn-1 under ambient pressure.

6. The authors wrote: "… leading to different doping levels across B-B σ-bonding WFs.
Conversely, the nearest-neighbouring hopping parameters exhibit similar magnitudes regardless
of whether the atomic configurations are optimized, suggesting that variations in hopping strength
do not significantly inﬂuence the electronic structures." Can we say that perturbations in atomic
positions and in the bond-stretching phonon modes mainly influence on-site energies rather than

Reply: We thank the reviewer for this interesting question.
First, in this part, the optimization of atomic configurations means the arrangement of B and C
atoms with the lowest energy, aka the optimal coloring. We want to state that, the variations in
hopping strengths play a secondary role in the change of electronic structures. On the other hand,
the most important difference is the variations if on-site energies, which leads to different doping
levels across different kinds of B-B σ-bonding WFs.
Second, we go back to the reviewer’s interesting question. If we choose atomic orbitals as our
local basis of TB model, perturbations of atomic positions in the bond-stretching phonon modes
have major influence on hopping strengths and minor influence on on-site energies. This is
because hopping strength comes from the overlap between two orbitals, while on-site energy
comes from the orbital energy in a lattice potential. As a result, when the distance R between two
atoms increases, hopping strength will decay exponentially (∝ ?−?) and on-site energies will
decay smoother (∝
1
?).
However, in our Wannierization, we choose pz-like WFs and s-like σ-bonding WFs as our local
basis. The pz-like WFs are insensitive to the in-plane phonon vibrations. For the s-like σ-bonding
WFs, the on-site energies not only depend on the energies of sp2-hybridized orbitals, but also
depends on the energy splitting between the bonding and antibonding states (proportional to the
hopping integral between atomic orbitals). When the perturbation is induced, the variation of

hopping integral will directly affect the on-site energies of s-like σ-bonding WFs. Hence,
perturbations of bond-stretching phonon modes mainly influence on the on-sites energies of
σ-bonding WFs at the sites where bonds stretch.
For example, in Li2B3C (ours), the perturbation of Ag phonon mode mainly influences on the
on-site energies of σ-bonding WFs at bridge sites, as B-C and B-B bridge bonds stretch in the Ag
phonon mode. We extract the TB parameters of Li2B3C (ours) after inducing the perturbation and
compare these parameters with the TB parameters before perturbation, as detailed in TABLE. R1.
The on-site energies of W1 and W4 vary most significantly, supporting our analysis. The
variations of other parameters, such as hopping strengths, are one order smaller.

7. Since Sec. II C, Li2B3C (GLX) is rarely mentioned. Why? Is it superconducting? Can its
superconductivity be explained by σ-bonding?

electrons, as discussed in Ref. 6.

11. The authors wrote: "This alignment generates a large deformation potential, leading to a
substantial boost in EPC." What is the alignment? Why does it generate a large deformation
potential? Why does the deformation potential decrease for larger strain?
Reply: We thank the reviewer for this helpful comment.
The “alignment” here refers to the bridge B-B ?-bonding bands crossing the Fermi level and
forming the elliptical cylindrical Fermi surfaces near the Γ point under -4% b-axis strain.
In this situation, the top of bridge B-B ?-bonding bands lies just above the Fermi level. Those
states of the new elliptical cylindrical Fermi surfaces are more antibonding and sensitive to
vibrations compared with the nonbonding dispersive part of zigzag B-B ?-bonding bands.
As the strain is further increased, more holes are doped in the bridge B-B σ-bonding WFs and the
states of the new elliptical cylindrical Fermi surfaces are more non-bonding (they are from more
dispersive part of bands). Consequently, The deformation potential decreases for larger strain.
In order to avoid misunderstanding and improve readability, we modify the text in our revised

“As a result, the states of the newly formed elliptical cylindrical Fermi surfaces are more

deformation potential, leading to a substantial boost in EPC.” (in section II.D)

EPW can calculate anisotropic superconductivity by solving Migdal-Eliashberg equations

phonon mode. The displacement of atom in our perturbation is the corresponding component in
the eigenvector of the specific phonon mode times 0.1 Å. The atomic movements will result in the
shifts of bands, especially the bands strongly coupled with that phonon mode. The patterns are the
phonon vibration modes we care about.
This a common method to directly show affections of a phonon mode on electronic structure,
especially on the bands near the Fermi level (Phys. Rev. Lett., 2001, 86(19): 4366; Phys. Rev. B,
2015, 91(4): 045132; Phys. Rev. B, 2020, 102(14): 144504). To improve readability, we add the
detail in our revised version: “we examine how the band structures are affected by perturbations in
the bond-stretching phonon modes with 0.1 Å amplitude.” (in section II.C)

Lithium borocarbides, namely, Li2B3C and Li3B4C2. They also carefully discussed the electronic
behavior of their σ-bonding bands especially in terms of phonon-mediated superconductivity and
how they are tuned. Based on EPC calculations, the authors also proposed a promising uniaxial
strain-applying method to enhance the BCS superconductivity in Li2B3C. As a result, this work
may motivate researchers to explore the vast configuration space of lithium borocarbides as a
promising and competitive type of BCS superconductor.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors wrote: "Notably, the top of these bridge B-B σ-bonding bands now are above the 
Fermi level, as highlighted by the dashed purple circles in Fig. 5(c). As a result, the states of the 
newly formed elliptical cylindrical Fermi surfaces are more antibonding and sensitive to 
vibrations. This change in electronic structure generates a large deformation potential, leading to 
a substantial boost in EPC." These bridge B-B σ-bonding bands are still composed of σ-
bonding states. Why did the authors say that they are more antibonding? Why do they sensitive to 
vibrations? Because of larger electronic DOS? Why does the change in electronic structure 
generate a large deformation potential? 
 
Reply: We thank the reviewer for pointing out this. This valuable question did push me to think 
more and improve the quality of our paper. 
Indeed, the bridge B-B σ-bonding bands are still composed of σ-bonding states (the antibonding 
bands are well above the Fermi level). However, we wanted to emphasize that the top of these 
bridge B-B σ-bonding bands are more antibonding than the dispersive part of the bridge B-B σ-
bonding bands (the antibonding state is usually with higher energy because they have more 
nodes).  
During the process of preparing the response, I noticed this key point: when a bond-stretching 
phonon mode vibration is introduced, always some bonds are elongated while other bonds 
are shortened. Furthermore, the elongated bands are weakened while the shortened ones are 
strengthened. Consequently, the elongated, weakened bonds lead to smaller energy splitting 
between the corresponding bonding and antibonding bands while the shortened, strengthened 
bonds lead to larger energy splitting. As a result, the top of the bonding bands of those elongated, 
weakened bonds is lifted up while that of those shortened, strengthened bonds is lowered down. 
Certainly, the bottom of the antibonding bands of those elongated, weakened bonds is lowered 
down while that of those shortened, strengthened bonds is raised (although these antibonding 
bands are usually well above the Fermi level and not considered in DFT calculations).  
Let us take Li2B3C (ours) as an example. For the Ag vibration mode in Fig. 4(a), the B-B bonds 
at the bridge sites are elongated while the B-B bonds in zigzag chains are shortened. After 
introducing the Ag mode perturbation, the top of bridge B-B σ-bonding bands is lifted up while 
the top of zigzag chain B-B σ-bonding bands is lowered down, as shown in Fig. 4(b). Meanwhile, 
the dispersive states remain nearly unaffected. This is why the top of the bridge B-B bonding 
bands are more sensitive to vibrations, consistent with our aforementioned analysis. 
The larger electronic DOS is beneficial to phonon-mediated superconductivity, but here it is not 
the main reason of the enhanced deformation potential. Through the uniaxial strain, the sensitive 
top of bridge B-B σ-bonding bands is lifted up, just over the Fermi level, and generates a large 
deformation potential. 
To be clearer and avoid misunderstanding, we modify the text in the revised version: 
“When the perturbation of a bond-stretching phonon mode is introduced, always some bonds 
are elongated while other bonds are shortened. Consequently, the elongated, weakened bonds 
lead to smaller energy splitting between the corresponding bonding and antibonding bands 
while the shortened, strengthened bonds lead to larger energy splitting. For example, in our 
case, the top of the bridge B-B σ-bonding bands is lifted up while the top of zigzag chain B-B
