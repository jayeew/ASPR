mechanical confinement for the alloying reactions of Sn-based anodes in Na-ion batteries. The integrated restrictions

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports an ingeniously designed Sn/SnO2-CN composite with in situ–formed C–N anchors that combine
mechanical confinement with chemical bonding. The well-designed anode delivers outstanding performance in terms of
cycling stability (>7,000 cycles at 2 C with ~0.0036% capacity decay per cycle) and rate capability, which represents the
state-of-the-art sodium anode performance. Moreover, pouch-cell validations further confirm the practical relevance of this
alloy anode design. The study provides deep mechanistic insights incorporating of advanced characterization techniques
(XRD, synchrotron TXM tomography, ss-NMR, AFM, etc.). In particular, the synchrotron TXM tomography provides high
spatial resolution towards the mechanical stability of the anode. Through those characterizations, this work identifies the key
role of C-N bonding in reshaping alloying pathways towards ultra-stable structure design. Overall, this work offers good
insights in terms of advanced characterizations and excellent performance for alloy anodes. Despite some technical issues,
this work could be considered for publication in Nature Communications after addressing those points.

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports on the self-construal tin anode containing in situ-formed C-N anchors for sodium-ion batteries, which
improves mechanical and chemical stability of anode materials that could overcome the major obstacles of alloy anodes
such as volume changes and unstable solid electrolyte interphases. The authors prepared the Sn/SnO2@CN material
through thermal reduction strategy and the resulting Sn/SnO2@CN electrode exhibited significantly improved
electrochemical performance in terms of a high capacity (750 mAh/g) and long-term cycling stability over 700 cycles at 2C. In
addition, the full cell combined with NVP/C cathode demonstrated the practical feasibility of the developed anode. The
claims are well supported by various structural/morphological analyses using both in situ and ex situ methods, as well as
electrochemical tests. While the enhanced performance of the developed anode is quite impressive, I believe that the
novelty of the manuscript and the analysis results are not sufficient for publication in Nature Communications, several
aspects requiring further clarification and additional evidence. The detailed comments are outlined below: 
1. The novelty of the work must be clearly addressed and discussed, please compare your research with existing research
findings on Sn-based anode materials (especially in the Introduction section) and highlight novelty of this work. 
2. The authors used L-tyrosine to synthesize the Sn/SnO2@CN anode material. What are the advantages of L-tyrosine and
why did the authors use it to realize the unique structure? The synthetic mechanism also needs to be explained. 
3. In Fig. 1g, the authors state that the final product exhibits a nanoscale phase distribution due to the influence of the C-N
skeleton on the growth behavior of Sn. Could the alternating phase separation be controlled through this synthetic method?
Please provide further explanation. 
4. In addition, while the electrochemical performance is promising, the material should also demonstrate scalability to be
considered viable as an anode for commercial applications. Please provide further discussion on the scalability aspect. 
5. The authors state that the Sn/SnO2@CN anode retains particle integrity without pulverization at the end of discharge
(lines 189-192, Fig. 3e). However, experimental evidence supporting the chemical binding effects of the C-N anchors is not
provided. Therefore, the authors should include relevant experimental data obtained during cycling. 
6. It is true that the authors conducted various advanced analyses and demonstrated the critical role of the C–N anchors in
enhancing the electrochemical performance. However, most of the characterization results (XRD, TXM, AFM, etc.) are
limited to the particle level. Therefore, I suggest that the authors provide additional electrode-level characterization to
substantiate the mitigation of electrode failure and performance degradation in alloy-based materials. 
7. In Fig. 5g-h, I am wondering about the effects of viscoelastic SEI layer on the mechanical robustness of the electrode. In
my opinion, a more elastic film would be expected to exhibit enhanced reversibility during charge-discharge reactions.
Please provide further explanation. 
8. Based on Fig. 2b, the initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) of the Sn/SnO2@CN anode appears to be around 80%. Since full-
cell performance is highly influenced by the ICE, and a low ICE generally leads to poor cycling stability, it is unclear how the
Sn/SnO2@CN full cell could exhibit excellent electrochemical performance without presodiation. 
9. The mass loading would be an important factor for practical electrochemical systems. Considering the relatively low mass
loading of the anode (1.5 mg/cm2), it would be valuable to provide electrochemical test results (e.g., charge/discharge
profiles, cycling stability, etc.) using electrodes with a higher loading mass (>3–4 mg/cm2).

4.In Figure 2b, the capacity of nano-Sn drops significantly at 5C, whereas Sn-CN maintains its 
performance. This difference should be explained.  
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment regarding the rate-performance 
difference observed at 5C. The pronounced capacity decay of nano-Sn, compared with the 
stable performance of Sn/SnO 2-CN, mainly arises from increased polarization and severe 
structural degradation of the nano-Sn electrode under high-rate conditions. As shown in the 
galvanostatic charge-discharge curves at 5C (Figure R1), nano-Sn exhibits markedly larger 
polarization than Sn/SnO2-CN, especially in the alloying plateau region near 0.25 V vs. Na⁺/Na. 
This elongated and sloped plateau reflects sluggish Na⁺ diffusion and sluggish reaction kinetics. 
In the absence of a continuous conductive framework, nano-Sn particles suffer from inefficient 
electron transport, and such a limitation becomes more pronounced at high current densities, 
resulting in large ohmic polarization and a sharp capacity drop. 
In contrast, the C-N anchored conductive network in Sn/SnO 2-CN ensures efficient charge 
transport, buffers volume variation to maintain structural integrity, and facilitates the formation 
of a thin, stable, and conductive SEI. These synergistic effects minimize interfacial impedance 
and polarization, thereby preserving high capacity even at 5C. A detailed discussion of this 
point has been added to the revised manuscript on page 8, line 140-142. We sincerely thank the 
reviewer for this valuable suggestion.

8.The manuscript mentions both amorphous and crystalline Na ₉Sn₄ phases. How was the 
presence of amorphous Na 9Sn4 confirmed? What is the mechanism for the transition during 
solidification? A similar question applies to Na₁₅Sn₄. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for the insightful questions concerning the identification and 
transformation mechanism of the amorphous Na-Sn phases. The presence of amorphous 
Na9Sn4 was confirmed through combined in situ XRD and ex situ solid-state ²³Na NMR 
analyses. As shown in Figure 3a-c in the manuscript, the absence of diffraction peaks at the 
intermediate discharge state (point C) indicates an amorphous structure, while the ²³Na ss-NMR 
spectrum displays an obvious characteristic resonance of Na 9Sn4 at ~218 ppm. These results 
together confirm that the product at point C is predominantly amorphous Na9Sn4 in the in-situ 
cell operation. The observed relatively sharp NMR peak likely originates from partial 
relaxation of the amorphous intermediate during the ex situ measurement.1  
A similar behavior applies to Na 15Sn4. Although crystalline Na₁₅Sn₄ is the 
thermodynamically stable phase, the C-N anchoring network imposes kinetic barriers that

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

inhibit full crystallization, resulting in a coexistence of amorphous and poorly crystalline 
Na₁₅Sn₄ domains. The increase in XRD intensity during early desodiation reflects delayed 
crystallization, further supporting the presence of amorphous Na₁₅Sn₄ at full discharge. 
Regarding the transition mechanism, the alloying reaction proceeds via a kinetically 
constrained multistep pathway, where amorphous intermediates may form prior to or coexist 
with crystalline counterparts. The rapid Na⁺ insertion and large volume expansion could induce 
substantial strain and suppress long-range atomic ordering, favoring amorphization as a stress-
adaptive route. In the Sn/SnO2-CN electrode, the embedded C-N anchors further regulate local 
kinetics, promote amorphous phase formation, and therefore practically accommodate large 
volume change and stabilize the electrode. These amorphous intermediate phases could 
subsequently evolve into crystalline phases (e.g., Na9Sn4, Na15Sn4) upon further (de)sodiation 
or relaxation, which is consistent with the stress ‐adaptive and kinetically limited alloying 
mechanism observed in alloy-based systems 2-4. We have added additional discussion on the 
phase transition processes in the revised manuscript on page 10, line 181-184.  
 
9.In line 167, why is there an unexpected increase of crystalline Na ₁₅Sn₄ during desodiation? 
How does the C-N anchor induce phase transition hysteresis? 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful question regarding the unusual 
crystallization behavior during desodiation. The apparent increase in the XRD intensity of 
crystalline Na₁₅Sn₄ could be primarily a kinetic phenomenon, arising from phase transition 
hysteresis induced by the C-N anchors.  
As shown in the ex situ ²³Na ss-NMR data in Figure 3c, a significant portion of the sodiated 
product persists in a metastable, poorly crystalline state at the end of discharge, due to the 
restricted atomic diffusion and phase growth imposed by the C-N network. At the initial stage 
of charging, Na⁺ ions are gradually extracted from these metastable domains. While the strong 
interaction with the C-N framework slows this process, creating a kinetic window that allows 
atoms within the Na-rich regions to reorganize into the more thermodynamically stable, long-
range ordered Na 15Sn4 structure. Therefore, the observed increase in XRD intensity reflects 
delayed crystallization, a “recrystallization” of previously amorphous or poorly crystalline

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

material, rather than the formation of new Na15Sn4. 
This hysteresis could be fundamentally enabled by the C-N anchors, which exert chemical 
and mechanical restrictions that alter the reaction pathway, suppress the typical crystallization 
kinetics during sodiation, and promote this unique electrochemical-driven structural ordering 
upon desodiation. We have added corresponding explanation and updated the discussion in the 
manuscript on page 10, line 187-191 and thank the reviewer for prompting this important 
clarification. 
 
10.It appears that SnO2 also undergoes phase changes. This should be discussed. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. As described in the manuscript, 
the Sn/SnO 2-CN composite contains a small amount of SnO 2 ( ∼3.7 at% O from elemental 
analysis). During the initial discharge, SnO2 may undergo a conversion reaction (SnO2 + 4Na⁺ 
+ 4e⁻ → Sn + 2Na2O), 5, 6 which is consistent with the observed decrease in XRD peak intensity 
of SnO₂. The resulting Sn may then further participate in alloying reactions with Na. Upon 
subsequent charging, SnO2 is largely recovered, as shown in Figure 3a of the manuscript. The 
SnO2 shell in the Sn/SnO 2-CN structure may collectively facilitate maintaining mechanical 
stability and enhance the structural durability of the alloy electrode. We have added 
corresponding discussion in the revised manuscript on page 10, line 192-193.  
 
11.In Figure 4, the changes in pore size during cycling should be analyzed and discussed. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this valuable comment. We have carefully analyzed the 
pore size evolution of Sn/SnO2-CN using 3D tomographic reconstructions. As noted, Figure 4 
presents the 3D morphologies of Sn/SnO 2-CN at different cycling stages, clearly revealing 
noticeable variations in pore structure. However, since these data were obtained from different 
particles after cycling, it is difficult to make a rigorous quantitative comparison of absolute 
pore sizes. This limitation arises from intrinsic particle-to-particle variations in volume change, 
which is common in conversion alloy-type anodes.7, 8 Nevertheless, the ex situ 3D tomography 
provides reliable qualitative insights. After the first alloying cycle, large pores (100-300 nm) 
form as a result of significant volume expansion. Upon further cycling, the C-N anchors 
promote a self-adaptive structural reorganization, leading to smaller and more uniform pores

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

(50-100 nm) that effectively accommodate strain and preserve structural integrity. These 
morphological trends directly visualize the self-stabilizing feature of Sn/SnO 2-CN during 
cycling in this study. While precise quantitative tracking of pore evolution within the same 
particle remains challenging, the overall evolution in pore structure and volume is 
unambiguously demonstrated by the 3D nano-tomography results. We have added 
corresponding discussion in the revised manuscript on page 14, line 255-261.

(3) Could the authors clarify if the 3-hour thermal reduction window (Fig. 1b) is strictly optimal 
across batch sizes? Are there sensitivity thresholds for H2 concentration or heating rates that 
might affect C-N anchor formation? 
Response: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point regarding the parameter 
sensitivity of our synthesis process. The 3-hour thermal reduction time was identified as 
optimal for the specific conditions and batch size described in our study (typically 0.2-1 gram 
per batch). This thermal treatment duration was determined to achieve an optimal balance 
between complete reduction of SnO 2 to Sn and the preservation of a sufficient C-N anchor 
framework derived from the tyrosine precursor, as evidenced by XRD, SEM, and 
electrochemical performance (Figure 1b, Supplementary Figures 2-3 in the manuscript). We 
note that scaling up the process may require further optimization, as larger batch sizes can 
influence heat and mass transfer dynamics, potentially affecting the homogeneity of reduction 
reactions.  
Regarding sensitivity, the H 2 concentration (5% in Ar) and heating rate (5 °C/min) were 
selected based on preliminary experiments and literature precedents to ensure controlled 
reduction without excessive carbonization or particle aggregation. Deviations from these 
conditions, such as substantially higher H2 concentrations or faster heating rates, could lead to 
over-reduction, degradation of the nitrogen-doped carbon structure, or sintering of Sn particles 
(as discussed in Figure S2 in the Supporting information of the manuscript), which would 
compromise the mechanical and chemical confinement. 
While the current parameters are robust for the laboratory-scale synthesis presented, we 
agree that detailed kinetic studies and scaling trials would be necessary for industrial translation. 
We have added additional description on the synthesis process in the Methods section of the 
revised manuscript.  
 
(4) The discharge curves in Fig. 2c exhibit a small voltage bump at low C-rates. What is the 
underlying cause of this feature? Does the baseline Sn anode also display a similar voltage

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 15 -->

bump? 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful observation regarding the small voltage 
bump in the discharge curves at low C-rates (Figure 2c). Although similar features have 
occasionally been reported for Sn-based anodes, 26 their origin has not been systematically 
clarified. Based on our in situ XRD and ²³Na ss-MAS NMR results (Figure 3 in the manuscript), 
this voltage bump likely arises from the relaxation and reorganization of metastable amorphous 
Na-Sn intermediates into more ordered crystalline Na₉Sn₄ during the multi-step alloying 
reaction process. This voltage bump in the discharge curve reflects a momentary reduction in 
overpotential as this crystallization initiates, therefore temporarily lowering the polarization 
voltage during alloying reactions.  
This feature is more pronounced in the Sn/SnO 2-CN electrode, which can be attributed to 
the regulating effect of the C-N anchoring framework on the ion transport and phase evolution. 
The nano-Sn electrode also shows a similar but less distinct bump, likely due to greater reaction 
heterogeneity in the electrode caused by uncontrolled volume changes. At higher C-rates, this 
feature gradually vanishes because the increased polarization voltage dominates at higher C 
rate. A brief discussion of this behavior has been added to the revised manuscript on page 9, 
line 170-175 and revised supporting information on page 26. 
 
(5) Please explain in detail the principle and basis for determining the existence of the C-N 
network and pores through X-ray attenuation rate. 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful question regarding the use of X-ray 
attenuation to identify the C-N network and pore structures. The determination is based on the 
fundamental principle of synchrotron-based X-ray nano-tomography, which relies on 
differences in X-ray attenuation properties of various components within the material. 
In this technique, 2D projection images are collected at different rotation angles, and the 
transmitted X-ray intensity follows Beer-Lambert’s law (I = I₀e-μt), where I0 is the incident 
intensity, I is the transmitted intensity, μ is the linear attenuation coefficient, and t is the sample 
thickness. The value of μ depends on the material’s density and atomic composition. Higher 
density and higher atomic number (Z) materials attenuate X-rays more strongly. 
For the Sn/SnO2-CN composite, Sn-containing phases exhibit high attenuation due to their

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 16 -->

high Z (Sn, Z = 50), whereas the C-N framework (composed of light elements C, N, O; Z ≤ 8) 
shows much weaker attenuation. Pores or voids filled with air or electrolytes display near-zero 
attenuation. Consequently, regions of high attenuation could correspond to Sn-containing phase 
in the reconstructed 3D tomography result, while low-attenuation regions can correspond either 
to the C-N network or pores. 
This distinction between Sn-containing phases and C-N framework is employed to for the 
morphological and structural correlation for Sn/SnO 2-CN. In the attenuation line profiles 
(Figures 3h, 3i in the manuscript), sharp drops in attenuation (valleys) could correspond to 
pores, while the broader, moderately low attenuation regions (shoulder peaks) can be attributed 
to the C-N framework. This interpretation follows established tomography analysis principles 
and is corroborated by complementary EDS, XPS, and TEM evidence. We have added 
corresponding descriptions in the Methods section of the revised manuscript.
