Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study focuses on detecting sleep apnea-related respiratory events through the use of a deep learning approach and up
to 7 PSG overnight signals (plus data from EEG, EOG, and EMG). The topic is of interest for sleep research, the objectives
are well-proposed, and the manuscript is well-written. The authors establish that their approach is useful in sleep apnea
detection, mainly because of its ability to properly differentiate the several types of respiratory events (including a new event
type), thus covering a gap in the related literature. Despite the interest of the proposal, it is my opinion that several major and
minor issues should be addressed prior to the publication of the manuscript: 
Major issues: 
1/ Apneic event length is 10 seconds or more. Therefore, the frequency associated with their occurrence is below 0.1 Hz.
Accordingly, respiratory signals being filtered between 0.1 Hz and 4 Hz are losing very important information. Several
studies report that the most useful sleep apnea-related information in respiratory signals is below 0.1 Hz. It is my opinion that
filtering should be changed to between 0.025 Hz and 4 Hz to improve the performance of the model, which is high but not
impressive. 
2/ I understand the advantages of the current proposal regarding previous approaches. However, it is my opinion that the
manuscript fails to compare the diagnostic performance (not the scoring performance) with that reached by many other
proposals. There exist previous studies using thousands of subjects but only using single channels like SpO2 that reach
diagnostic performances that cannot be seen as inferior to those reported here using 7 + 3 signals. As diagnosis is the major
conceptual goal of these automated approaches, the authors should compare and discuss these results with other relevant
proposals in this particular task. 
3/ As there are deep learning solutions that do not require an equal sample rate for the signals, and 8 Hz is below the
sampling rate recommended by the AASM for respiratory signals, this choice should be justified and acknowledged as a
limitation of the study. 
4/ I think that the choice of the particular deep learning architecture should be justified and discussed. The existence of
literature pointing to CNNs as reaching higher performances than RNNs, even in tasks involving signal processing (time
series), justifies clearly explaining the reasons behind the choice of the deep learning architecture (which in this case
includes a combination of both approaches via ResNet). 
5/ The intra-class correlation coefficient is better suited than Pearson’s correlation to measure agreement between different
scorers (with the predicted AHI being one of them). 
Minor issues: 
- "To simplify any annotated mixed apneas were labelled as OA due to there being an effort to breathe during the event." It is
my opinion that this is not a simplification but a limitation (probably due to an unavoidable class imbalance concerning this
type of event)

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present an assessment of a new automatic sleep disordered breathing event detector (ABED) for identifying
respiratory events in sleep disordered breathing. They report that generally the ABED performs as well as individual expert
scores, particularly for obstructive apneic events. Data from a number of different, existing cohorts were used to train and
validate the ABED, which is a strength of the study. 
The potential of this is that the very rapid inference time (14 seconds per average PSG is cited) can increase efficiency and
scope of sleep apnea testing. 
A number of possible areas for clinical translation are given. I think the most attractive is in easily identifying endotypes of
obstructive sleep apnea. Endotypic traits have been proposed for use in selecting patients that might benefit preferentially
from a range of non-CPAP alternative therapies including oral appliances, hypoglossal nerve stimulation, supplemental
oxygen, and pharmacotherapy. Given there is currently a one-size-fits-all management approach that predominates where
CPAP is first line treatment for almost all patients. Accurately identifying patients likely to respond to alternatives to CPAP to
avoid a trial and error approach to alternatives would be a major advance. Systems to identify endotypes from routine PSG
signals with automated modelling have been developed and some are referenced here (21-25). However, the authors have
not referenced further work that has demonstrated some of these systems are highly accurate in predicting, for example, oral
appliance treatment outcomes in obstructive sleep apnea (see Dutta et al, J Clin Sleep Med 2022), or that combine clinical
data and models from PSG data (see Eckert et al, Sleep 2024;47 (Suppl 1): A257) to identify successful candidates for Oral
Appliance Therapy (and vice versa). The authors assert that the addition of “apnotyping” via probabilistic detection of event
subtypes within each majority class could benefit sleep apnea endotyping. It remains an open question whether the ABED
will be as effective as these methods in clinical practice as this should be acknowledged in the limitations. Further research
is also needed to demonstrate if endotypes are useful in predicting which patients are at risk of adverse health
consequences of OSA. This is beyond the scope of the current study. 
The authors also do not reference previous work on the significance of isolated respiratory events without arousals or
desaturations (IRE). Previous work has shown non-apneic REs without cortical arousal or desaturation are associated with
significant respiratory and heart rate changes. And, that optimal CPAP and the reduction of resistive load are associated
with the normalization of heart rate indicating potential clinical benefit (for example see Sankari et al, Sleep 2016
;40:zsw024). Easy identification of these events would be useful but is not a novel finding. 
There are a number of other limitations I feel need to be considered in addition to those given in the Discussion. As the
authors point out in the Introduction, a number of novel metrics taken from the PSG have been identified that have shown to
be better predictors of downstream consequences of OSA than the AHI. These include hypoxic burden and a number of
metrics derived from heart rates. Further, the intensity of arousals from events are also likely to be clinically significant. It
does not appear that the ABED identifies any of these metrics. Consequently, the value of the ABED in identifying which
patients may benefit long-term from therapy is uncertain. 
Sleep studies also require scoring of sleep stages and identification
of arousals due to limb movements or that occur spontaneously. I presume the ABED would need 
to be utilised in addition to
automatic scoring software for these features. 
Overall, the paper presents a potentially useful addition to automate the scoring of sleep-disordered breathing events that
will likely be of value in sleep medicine.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This study focuses on detecting sleep apnea-related respiratory events through the use 
of a deep learning approach and up to 7 PSG overnight signals (plus data from EEG, 
EOG, and EMG). The topic is of interest for sleep research, the objectives are well-
proposed, and the manuscript is well-written. The authors establish that their approach 
is useful in sleep apnea detection, mainly because of its ability to properly differentiate 
the several types of respiratory events (including a new event type), thus covering a gap 
in the related literature. Despite the interest of the proposal, it is my opinion that several 
major and minor issues should be addressed prior to the publication of the manuscript: 
 
Major issues: 
1/ Apneic event length is 10 seconds or more. Therefore, the frequency associated with 
their occurrence is below 0.1 Hz. Accordingly, respiratory signals being filtered between 
0.1 Hz and 4 Hz are losing very important information. Several studies report that the 
most useful sleep apnea-related information in respiratory signals is below 0.1 Hz. It is 
my opinion that filtering should be changed to between 0.025 Hz and 4 Hz to improve

We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful and important comment regarding the low-
frequency content of respiratory signals. 
 
We would like to clarify that although apneic events typically last >= 10 seconds, their 
physiological signatures - particularly reductions in airflow or effort - are reflected in 
modulations of the signal envelope rather than the generation of new frequency 
components below 0.1 Hz. In our experience, these envelope changes remain 
detectable even after applying a 0.1 Hz high-pass filter, since the base breathing 
frequencies (typically ~0.2–0.4 Hz) are still present. 
 
To empirically assess the reviewer’s suggestion, we retrained and evaluated our model 
using a 0.025-4 Hz band-pass filter and compared the results to our original 0.1-4 Hz 
filtering. The results across four cohorts (MESA, MrOS, WSC, CFS) show minimal and 
inconsistent performance differences.    As shown in rebuttal tables 1 and 2, there is no

Finally, we note that the MrOS study applied a hardware high-pass filter at 0.15 Hz to 
most respiratory channels (except nasal flow), whereas MESA and CFS did not apply 
such filtering. To harmonize preprocessing across cohorts and avoid cohort-specific 
bias in model training, we chose a conservative high-pass cutoff of 0.1 Hz. This tradeoff 
supports generalization while still preserving the envelope information necessary for 
apnea detection and has minimal influence on results across populations.  
 
 
2/ I understand the advantages of the current proposal regarding previous approaches. 
However, it is my opinion that the manuscript fails to compare the diagnostic 
performance (not the scoring performance) with that reached by many other proposals. 
There exist previous studies using thousands of subjects but only using single channels 
like SpO2 that reach diagnostic performances that cannot be seen as inferior to those

reported here using 7 + 3 signals. As diagnosis is the major conceptual goal of these

We thank the reviewer for raising this important point regarding diagnostic performance. 
We agree that comparing our model’s diagnostic utility with simpler approaches is 
necessary to contextualize its relevance and impact. 
 
Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated strong diagnostic performance using 
single-channel inputs such as SpO2 [https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-
40604-3], achieving high ICC and F1 metrics for AHI estimation at (ICC: 0.92, F1: 0.78), 
(ICC: 0.94, F1: 0.74), and (ICC: 0.94, F1: 0.75) for CFS, MrOS, and MESA, 
respectively. Comparatively, we achieve ICC = 0.92 and F1 = 0.74. These models 
benefit from ease of use and scalability, and we agree that their contributions are 
valuable - especially in population-level screening contexts. 
 
In contrast, our approach is explicitly designed to go beyond estimation of AHI, which 
have been increasingly criticized as insufficient for characterizing apnea severity and 
guiding personalized treatment. Our model leverages multichannel PSG data to provide 
event-by-event classification, differentiation of apnea e vents (obstructive, central, 
hypopnea, and isolated respiratory events), and the ability to inform apnea endotyping.  
 
Rather than only predicting whether a patient exceeds an AHI threshold, our model 
characterizes the temporal structure and type of respiratory disturbances, which are 
more closely associated with physiological burden and clinical outcomes. Several 
studies emphasize the importance of phenotyping sleep apnea patients beyond the AHI 
- for instance, identifying arousability, hypoxic burden, or event clustering - which are 
not easily captured by single-channel or threshold-based methods. 
 
We have added a discussion of this comparison to the revised manuscript, highlighting 
the trade-offs between simplicity and diagnostic richness. Ultimately, we see our work 
as complementary: while single-channel systems are suitable for screening, detailed 
multi-channel models like ours offer higher resolution insights needed for personalized 
interventions and advancing our understanding of sleep apnea pathophysiology. 
 
3/ As there are deep learning solutions that do not require an equal sample rate for the 
signals, and 8 Hz is below the sampling rate recommended by the AASM for respiratory 
signals, this choice should be justified and acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. We have now added both the 
rationale for our choice and a corresponding limitation statement in the revised 
manuscript. 
 
Our choice of an 8 Hz sampling rate reflects a trade-off between computational 
efficiency and retaining signal information across a large, multi-cohort dataset. This 
sampling rate is sufficient to capture the primary respiratory dynamics relevant to sleep 
apnea detection, which predominantly occur in the 0.2-0.33 Hz range, corresponding to 
normal adult breathing rates (≈  12-20 breaths per minute) 
[https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8774258/]. For thoracic, abdominal, and oral 
thermistor signals - where respiratory effort and airflow patterns are relatively slow - 8 
Hz ensures adequate resolution while minimizing training costs. For nasal airflow, we 
note that although certain high-frequency components like snoring vibrations occur > 
100 Hz [https://www.nature.com/articles/srep30559], our approach does not rely on 
snoring acoustics, and the 8 Hz rate captures the signal envelope adequately for the 
purposes of apnea event classification. 
 
However, we acknowledge the reviewer’s point that the AASM recommends higher 
sampling rates (>= 25 Hz) for respiratory channels to ensure accurate waveform 
morphology, particularly in clinical diagnostic settings. While our approach performs well 
at 8 Hz, this limitation should be considered in contexts where finer temporal resolution 
may be required, especially for detecting subtle respiratory patterns or developing 
models for real-time clinical applications. 
 
We now acknowledge this as a limitation in the manuscript and recommend that future 
work explore whether using higher sampling rates could further enhance model 
performance, particularly in prospective datasets. 
 
4/ I think that the choice of the particular deep learning architecture should be justified 
and discussed. The existence of literature pointing to CNNs as reaching higher 
performances than RNNs, even in tasks involving signal processing (time series), 
justifies clearly explaining the reasons behind the choice of the deep learning 
architecture (which in this case includes a combination of both approaches via ResNet). 
 
We thank the reviewer for the helpful comment encouraging a deeper justification of our 
model architecture which we have added to the Manuscript. The ABED architecture was 
designed to leverage both spatial and temporal signal characteristics through a hybrid 
structure that combines a convolutional feature extractor (a modified ResNet50) with a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

temporal modeling layer (bi-directional LSTM). This design reflects the nature of the 
input data—multimodal time series from respiratory, oxygen saturation, and arousal-
related signals—where both short-term local features and longer-range dependencies 
are important. 
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly residual networks (ResNets), are 
known for their ability to efficiently learn spatial hierarchies in signal patterns and are 
less prone to overfitting compared to simpler CNNs or multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), 
especially when deep architectures are used thanks to residual connections [He et al., 
2016, Ref. 69]. We selected ResNet due to its scalability, stable gradient flow, and 
demonstrated success in prior applications [He et al., 2016, Ref. 69]. 
While some studies suggest that CNNs alone can outperform recurrent models for time 
series tasks [Van Steenkiste et al., 2019, Ref. 31], these often rely on single-modality 
input or short temporal contexts. In contrast, sleep apnea involves complex temporal 
dependencies - such as the delayed relationship between respiratory flow reduction, 
oxygen desaturation, and arousal - that are difficult to capture without explicit sequence 
modeling. The distinction between a hypopnea and an isolated respiratory event (IRE) 
relies not just on morphology, but on when these features co-occur. Therefore, temporal 
modeling is critical. 
 
To that end, we employed a bi-directional LSTM to capture both forward and backward 
temporal dependencies within 240-second windows, allowing the model to integrate 
context across time and across channels. This is essential in sleep breathing analysis, 
where the onset and consequence of an event may be separated by several tens of 
seconds. We also considered transformer-based models but found them less suitable 
for our mid-scale dataset due to their high parameter count and increased risk of 
overfitting without massive datasets. 
 
5/ The intra-class correlation coefficient is better suited than Pearson’s correlation to 
measure agreement between different scorers (with the predicted AHI being one of 
them). 
 
We thank the reviewer for this insightful suggestion. We agree that the intra-class 
correlation coefficient provides a more appropriate measure of agreement between true 
and predicted AHI values as it also takes into account the bias of the model. We have 
calculated the ICC of true and predicted AHI and get ICC = 0.92(95% CI: [0.90 0.93]). 
We have now added these to the revised manuscript in Figure 1e.

- "To simplify any annotated mixed apneas were labelled as OA due to there being an

We thank the reviewer for this observation and agree that the handling of mixed apneas

However, we acknowledge that mixed apneas may have distinct pathophysiological

Thank you for highlighting this omission. We have now included a reference to the study 
by Sankari et al. (2016), which demonstrated that non-apneic respiratory events without 
cortical arousal or desaturation are associated with significant respiratory and heart rate

● Novel Metrics: Although ABED describes the overall apnotypes of subjects, we
