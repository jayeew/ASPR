Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
OVERALL SUMMARY 
The authors have developed an optimized system to compare traction forces and molecular tension across the mechanical
linker protein vinculin. They assessed the correlations between these mechanical readouts at various length scales, which
include the whole cell, individual focal adhesions, and within individual focal adhesions. This optimized experimental
system and the multiple-scale comparisons represent significant advancements previously lacking in the field. However,
there are considerable issues with the interpretation of the experimental results and the statistical analyzes presented in the
manuscript. Specifically, the reported FRET efficiencies differ significantly from those in the existing literature, and there is a
lack of statistical analysis for the claims that underpin the biological insights discussed in the manuscript. 
MAJOR CONCERNS 
1. In line 73, the authors mention the vinculin tension sensor somewhat generically. Specifically, they did not use VinTS, as
originally described by Grashoff in Nature, 2010. Instead, they used a different sensor, VinTS-tCRMod-GGSGGS7, as
detailed in LaCroix's eLife, 2018. This sensor features different fluorescent proteins (FPs) and distinct extensible domains.
Given these differences, it is important to reference these sensors uniquely, as they likely possess different optical and
mechanical properties (more on this below). 
2. There is a significant technical issue with the manuscript. The unloaded FRET efficiency of VinTS-tCRMod-GGSGGS7,
as determined by measuring the associated TSMods, should be ~48% in cells and ~55% in solution (LaCroix, eLife, 2018).
Furthermore, the in-solution measurements have been independently repeated in another recent publication (Liu,
Angewandte Chemie, 2025). The unloaded FRET Efficiency measurements, which are supposed to be device independent,
of ~15% reported in this study are hard to understand. One explanation is that, while mRuby2 has been shown to be a good
FRET acceptor in fluorimetry and sensitized emission-based approaches, it has been shown to be a poor FRET acceptor in
FLIM (Martin, PLoS One, 2018). While the reasons for these differences have not been explored, it is possible that they
could be related to the previously described photochromism of mRuby2 (Bindels, Nat Meth, 2017). This behavior is
extremely problematic in FRET-based biosensors and accentuated by high light exposures, like those in FLIM. These
issues regarding differences in absolute FRET efficiencies between the various studies need to be acknowledged in the
manuscript. The authors may wish to perform fluorimetry measurements to show that VinTS-tCRMod-GGSGGS7 is behaving
as expected in their hands in a low light exposure system. 
3. Point 2 also raises concerns about how the FRET data should be present. While the unloaded FRET efficiency is
unexpectedly low, it appears very consistent and expected relative differences between controls and experiments are
observed. This suggests that FRET data can be normalized for presentation. This seems like a relatively small, but
important, change, given that some of the FRET data is already normalized. 
4. Also related to this point, the statement that force amplitudes were not calculated from the FRET efficiencies due to the
nature of calibration (line 145) should be removed. The difficulties in converting the observed FRET efficiencies are driven
by the apparent offset of the unloaded FRET efficiency and have nothing to do with the calibration. 
5. In many places, conclusions are drawn by assessing the correlations between variables. Some relationships are
considered relevant, and some are not. However, how these decisions were made is unclear. Some statistical analyzes,
potentially done by numerical bootstrapping, need to be completed. This pertains to figures 3d-k and 4d-f. 
6. In Figure 4, what is the justification for using a slope threshold of 0.1 and -0.1 to bin the data? Can a linear regression be

used to show slopes are statistically different from zero? This seems like a less arbitrary way to bin the cells. 
MINOR CONCERNS 
1. This might be stylistic, but the authors often discuss “important insights”, but these insights are not listed. It would make
the paper more impactful if these insights were explicitly stated, especially in the abstracts and the conclusion. 
2. At line 60 the authors state: “While TFM is instrumental in quantifying and visualizing the tractions transmitted via FAs, it
does not provide a direct readout of the molecular tension within FA proteins, as it captures tractions exerted at the substrate
level.” 
While not incorrect, this statement does not convey the entirety of the complexities when comparing traction force and
molecular tension. There are indeed differences in the z-position of the measurement (i.e. substrate vs. intracellular), but
there is another issue. TFM reports the entirety of the stress generation from the cell, while a molecular tension sensor on
reports the tension across a specific protein. As there are multiple independent mechanical linkage between F-actin and
integrins (see review by Roca-Cusachs, JCS, 2012, for instance), this is an equally important issue that needs to be
mentioned. 
3. On line 75, imprecise language is used. The authors state, “VinTS reveals the magnitude and spatial distribution of forces
within FAs.” A more accurate statement is “VinTS reveals the magnitude and spatial distribution of tensions experienced by
vinculin within FAs.” 
As there are a number of mechanical linker proteins in FAs, it is not possible for a single molecular
tension sensor to report all the forces within FAs. 
4. Line 111: what is meant by “double knock out” is unclear. I think the authors mean homozygous KO MEFs (vinc -/-), as
opposed to heterozygous KO MEFS (vinc +/-). 
5. Line 167: The authors use the term FA tension. What is meant by this term is unclear. However, I believe that the authors
are attempting to describe vinculin tension averaged at the FA level. However, the forces/tension/stresses at the FA level are
often discussed in micropost based traction force microscopy (see the work of Chris Chen and others). For clarity, the
authors should retain the notion that tensions that are measured are only across vinculin. This reviewer suggests FA-
average vinculin tension, but other options that the authors prefer are likely sufficient. 
6. The sentence starting at line 304 is imprecise. The authors state “By improving experimental sample preparation,
streamlining data acquisition, and developing custom software, we achieved a highly detailed analysis of FA forces.” A more
accurate statement is “By improving experimental sample preparation, streamlining data acquisition, and developing custom
software, we achieved a highly detailed comparison of FA forces and the tension experience by vinculin.” 
7. The sentence starting at 313 is imprecise. The authors state: “FAs with very low vinculin tension, on the other hand, were
mainly located at the cell center, where FAs are typically smaller and subject to compression forces”. Compressive forces
have mainly been observed in cells on micropatterns (Rothenberg, CMBE, 2015). In unconfined cells, it is typical that the
forces on the central FAs are close to zero. This sentence should be edited to more accurately reflect the previously
published work. 
8. Line 336: The authors state, “This variability likely reflects differences in the way cells interact with the ECM.” The
plasticity of the relationship between FA characteristics and vinculin has been investigated by Hernández-Varas, Nat
Comm, 2015. This work is not cited in the manuscript, and it should be acknowledged. 
9. Line 374: The authors state, “Tractions likely depend on the combined effect of vinculin tension, density and activation,
rather than vinculin tension alone.” There appears to be an assumption that all traction forces are solely dependent on the
tension through vinculin. It would be more precise to state, “Tractions likely depend on the combined effect of vinculin
tension, density and activation as well as tension transmitted through other proteins, rather than vinculin tension alone.” 
10. Line 378: The authors state: “Vinculin tension fluctuates on short timescales, while tractions, as large-scale outputs, take
longer to adjust.” It is unclear what literature the authors are referencing to place a limit on the dynamics of the traction force.
For instance, in Plotnikov, Cell, 2012, it is shown that tractions fluctuate at their temporal resolution of 5 sec. Given the multi-
scale in this manuscript, some clarification seems pertinent. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a study on the integration of Traction Force Microscopy (TFM) and Fluorescence Resonance
Energy Transfer (FRET)-based vinculin tension sensors to explore focal adhesion mechanics. The authors provide a
multiscale approach that links molecular forces to whole-cell traction mechanics, addressing an important gap in
understanding how vinculin-mediated molecular forces contribute to larger cellular mechanics. While the study has the
potential to advance knowledge in the field, several aspects require further experimental validation, the inclusion of
appropriate controls, and further discussion of methodological limitations before the manuscript can be considered for
publication. 
The use of FRET-based vinculin tension sensors is well-established; however, the authors have not employed an
independent validation method, such as optical tweezers, atomic force microscopy (AFM), or Förster distance
measurements, to confirm their force calibration. The assumption that FRET efficiency changes exclusively reflect vinculin
tension is problematic, as factors such as fluorophore photobleaching, environmental pH variations, and sensor crowding
could also influence the observed FRET signals. Addressing these potential confounding variables through additional
controls or experimental refinements would strengthen the validity of the conclusions. 
A key limitation of the study is the assumed direct correlation between vinculin tension and whole-cell traction forces.
Traction force microscopy provides an integrated readout of cellular forces, which are modulated by multiple adhesion
proteins beyond vinculin, including talin, paxillin, and FAK. The manuscript would benefit from a more detailed validation
and discussion on how these proteins interact with vinculin and contribute to the observed forces. Additionally, the absence
of a full rescue GFP-vinculin control, as well as a negative control for the TFM experiments, limits the ability to determine the
specificity of vinculin’s role in force generation. The rationale for performing force measurements exclusively at the 24-hour
time point is unclear, and earlier time points should be considered, particularly during focal adhesion formation when cellular

mechanics are still dynamically evolving. 
Further experimental refinements, such as drug-based perturbations to modulate contractility, would provide a more
comprehensive understanding of how vinculin interacts with the cytoskeleton to regulate forces at both molecular and
cellular scales. Several images in the manuscript suggest that the observed effects may arise from increased contractility
due to vinculin localization to stress fibers and extracellular matrix assembly. Targeted pharmacological interventions could
help disentangle vinculin’s specific contribution to these processes. Addressing these concerns would significantly improve
the robustness of the study and enhance its contribution to the field.

MINOR CONCERNS 
1. The authors may wish to dampen their claims about the differences in the FRET efficiency being due to “modality
differences”. A three-fold difference in FRET efficiency is difficult to explain simply in terms of a bias towards faster photons.
That would be a very large bias. Also, the paper that is cited as basis for this claim does not really address this point, in the
opinion of this reviewer. There is no direct comparison between phasor FRET and sensitized emission based FRET
efficiency measurements, and there is no mention of potential bias between the measurements. What is discussed is the
importance of getting a high number of photons for FLIM measurements. The authors may wish to give a variety of reasons
for the potential differences, as to not direct the field in one particular direction prematurely. In addition to the previous
discussion about the issues with mRuby2, the authors may wish to discuss about the potential difficulties in using tension
sensors with FLIM measurements. For instance, most analyzes on phasor FRET focus on scenarios with a mixture of 2 or at
most several FRET states (i.e. bound or unbound receptors). The FRET efficiency variation of tension sensor with an
unstructured polypeptide as a deformable element, such as the VinTS used here, may essentially be continuous and have a
very high number of states. It seems likely that this could cause issues in analyses and require higher photon counts for
accurate measurements. Additionally, it might be worth noting that most instances of FLIM used in conjunction with tension
sensors have used “digital sensors” (see work of Carsten Grashoff) that are likely to have a lower number of states and may
require less photons to accurately represent. 
2. This sentence should like be re-written: “As a result, absolute FRET values cannot be accurately determined from imaging
data alone, as each pixel may contain a mixture of molecules with different FRET states.” This is a common misconception
in the field. With sufficient measurement events, an average of multiple states is not “inaccurate”. It is simply an average. It is
true that the average value of the underlying distribution may not correspond to the FRET of specific, well-populate
molecular state. For instance, the mean of a bimodal distribution is well defined, even known that value likely does not
correspond to a strongly populated state, as is it would for a Gaussian distribution (for instance). Therefore, while average
FRET values can be readily misinterpreted, but they are not de facto “inaccurate” as stated in the text. They are simply an
ensemble average and subject to the same constraints as any other measurement involving an ensemble average and not
the measurement of the entire distribution itself.

We thank the reviewer for this comment regarding the discrepancy between the unloaded FRET efficiency values 
reported for tCRMod-GGSGGS7 in the literature and those obtained in our study using FLIM.  
We respectfully note that while FRET efficiency measurements can be device-independent, they are not 
necessarily modality-independent. In particular, although measurements tend to be consistent across modalities 
at the single-molecule level, this equivalence often breaks down in imaging-based, pixel-level analyses. Differences 
between lifetime-based (FLIM) and intensity -based (e.g., SE -FRET) methods can arise due to how mixed FRET 
populations within a pixel are handled. FLIM calculates photon-weighted lifetimes, which means that lower-FRET 
species, being more photon-rich, can disproportionately influence the measured lifetime, skewing the apparent 
FRET efficiency toward lower values in heterogeneous samples. 
To confirm that the lower FLIM-derived FRET values observed in our study were not due to sensor malfunction or 
errors in sample preparation, we performed additional control experiments using a sensitized-emission (SE) FRET 
approach, following the protocol described by Gates et al. (2019). As described in the newly added Supplementary 
Note 2 and shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, SE-FRET measurements of cells expressing tCRMod-GGSGGS7 yielded 
an unloaded FRET efficiency of 48.6 ± 3.3%, in agreement with published values. In contrast, FLIM measurements 
of the same samples gave an average efficiency of 14.2 ± 1.1%. These results confirm that the discrepancy arises 
from the analytical method, not from experimental handling or sensor performance. 
We discuss this discrepancy explicitly in the Results section of the revised manuscript , where we clarify that 
absolute FRET values from FLIM are lower than SE-FRET, and provide a possible explanation. 
Lines 201–217: “Of note, the absolute FRET efficiencies obtained using FLIM phasor analysis were lower than those 
reported for the same Clover –mRuby2 pair in the literature (REF). To confirm that the sensor itself performed 
correctly, we quantified the unloaded FRET effic iency of TSMod using a conventional sensitised emission (SE) 
approach, as in LaCroix et al. (REF) (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 9). In this ratiometric, intensity-
based method, TSMod exhibited an unloaded FRET efficiency of 48.6 ± 3.3% (Supp lementary Fig. 3), closely 
matching the 49.6% as reported by LaCroix et al., confirming that the sensor folds, matures and transfers energy 
as expected in our system. This difference in absolute FRET efficiency values arises from the measurement modality

pixel-level analysis is influenced by the heterogeneity of FRET species within each pixel. As a result, absolute FRET 
values cannot be accurately determined from imaging data alone, as each pixel may contain a mixture of molecules

a single molecular state. In FLIM -based analysis, this ensemble averaging is additionally photon -weighted. As a 
consequence, low-FRET or donor-only species, which emit more donor photons, disproportionately influence the 
calculated lifetime and systematically shift the apparent FRET efficiency toward lower values (Coucke et al. 2023). 
In the specific case of the sensor used here, this modality -dependent shift is likely further amplified by the 
photophysical properties of the acceptor. Previous studies using FLIM showed that mRuby2 gives unexpectedly low 
FRET when used as the acceptor fo r Clover or EGFP in tandem constructs (Martin et al., 2018), and similar 
behaviour was reported for mRuby3 with mNeonGreen (McCullock et al., 2020). Although the underlying reasons 
remain unclear, the unusually low apparent FRET efficiencies have been link ed to slow and incomplete 
chromophore maturation as well as pronounced photochromic behavior. Both of these properties result in a 
significant population of acceptor molecules that are either non -emissive or transiently switched to dark states, 
which in tu rn increases the relative fraction of donor -only or weakly FRETing species within a pixel. In photon -
weighted FLIM analysis, this leads to an even stronger overrepresentation of low-FRET species and a corresponding 
downward shift in the apparent FRET efficiency. 
Additionally, gradient-response tension sensors such as VinTS report force through a near-continuous distribution 
of FRET states rather than discrete levels. Accurate lifetime averaging in such systems requires substantially higher 
photon counts, an d limited photon statistics may therefore represent an additional contributing factor to the 
observed discrepancy (Esposito, 2020).” 
 
2. This sentence should like be re -written: “As a result, absolute FRET values cannot be accurately determined 
from imaging data alone, as each pixel may contain a mixture of molecules with different FRET states. ” This is a 
common misconception in the field. With sufficient measurement events, an average of multiple states is not 
“inaccurate”. It is simply an average. It is t rue that the average value of the underlying distribution may not 
correspond to the FRET of specific, well-populate molecular state. For instance, the mean of a bimodal distribution 
is well defined, even known that value likely does not correspond to a strongly populated state, as is it would for 
a Gaussian distribution (for instance). Therefore, while average FRET values can be readily misinterpreted, but they 
are not de facto “inaccurate” as stated in the text. They are simply an ensemble average and subject to the same 
constraints as any other measurement involving an ensemble average and not the measurement of the entire 
distribution itself. 
We agree that ensemble averaging does not imply inaccuracy. To address this, we revised the sentence at Line 184 
to read: 
“In this case, the measured FRET signal represents an ensemble average over mixed FRET populations and 
therefore does not reflect a single molecular state.”

Overall summary 
The manuscript presents a study integrating Traction Force Microscopy (TFM) with FRET-
based Vinculin Tension Sensors (VinTS) to investigate focal adhesion (FA) mechanics 
across multiple scales, linking molecular forces to cellular mechanics. This approach 
provides valuable insights into the multiscale dynamics of FA mechanotransduction and 
addresses an important gap in understanding how molecular forces contribute to overall 
cellular mechanics. The authors have largely addressed the reviewers’ comments through 
additional controls and methodological clarifications. However, some points, such as the 
inclusion of negative TFM controls, could further strengthen the conclusions. Below, I 
provide a point-by-point summary of the authors’ responses to the reviewers’ comments.
