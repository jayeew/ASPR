Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Fedel et al., found an anomalous SOT in structures with an ultrathin Pt layer (less than 1 nm). The anomalous SOT is
revealed by decrease of the critical current density for the magnetization switching. The authors attempted to the TEM
observation and found the formation of isolated grains when the nominal Pt thickness is less than 1 nm. The authors claimed
that this grain formation is an origin of the anomalous SOT, since they would provide additional scattering sources. The
formation of the grains is supported by the MATLAB simulation. Additionally, the authors observed increase of the SOT
when an additional light metal layer (Ti, or Mn) is located on the Pt layer. The authors claimed that this comes from the
orbital Hall effect of the light metal layer. 
1. The finding of the anomalous SOT is interesting, and it is worthy to be considered. However, I don’t feel that the authors’
claim for the origin of this SOT is robust. Particularly, the authors excluded a possibility of the REE, mentioning the expected
thickness dependence of the SOT. I think that this is based on an identical interfacial state regardless of the Pt thickness. But
it is not true, as shown the formation of the grains in the ultrathin regime. Moreover, both the Pt and/or TbIG layers are
exposed to atmosphere if the Pt layers are very thin. A possible influence by oxygen cannot be excluded although Pt is an
inactive material and TbIG is already an oxide material. All these make complex for the analysis. Here, I encourage to
discuss the oxidation issue by their EELS observation data. 
To this end, I don’t think that the influence of the interfacial effect is completely excluded, in terms of the spin current
generation. Also, I’m not sure whether mentioning the scattering-mediated extrinsic spin Hall contribution in abstract is
proper, considering the discussion is not sufficiently fruited. 
2. The authors assumed 1.2 nm of spin diffusion length for Pt, referring a literature. But I’m not fully convinced. The spin
diffusion length and spin Hall effect of Pt varies as a function of the resistance of Pt (Sagasta et al., Phys. Rev. B 94, 060412
(2016)). The comparison of the resistance is required to refer a value in a literature. I’m concerning that the authors main
claim could persist even though the assumption on the spin diffusion length doesn’t hold. 
3. The resistance and resistivity of the Pt layer is shown in Fig. 3(a), and 6(b), respectively. Between them, the main claim of
this manuscript, the anomalous SOT by the qualitative change in the ultrathin Pt regime, is discussed, supported by the
resistance data shown in Fig. 3(a). Indeed, the resistance is inversely proportional to the layer thickness if the resistivity is
constant, thus Fig. 3(a) is insufficient to discuss the qualitative change in the ultrathin regime. I suggest including the
resistivity in Fig. 3(a) or somewhere nearby. 
4. As I understand, the main claim of this manuscript is the anomalous SOT from the ultrathin Pt. The authors additionally
show the results from the samples including a light metal. In these samples, the thickness of Pt layer is 1.2 nm, out of the
ultrathin regime. Do the second part have any correlation with the main claim? 
5. Minor one: There is no description for Fig. 2(f) in figure caption. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents a novel and significant contribution to the field of spin–orbitronics by demonstrating morphology-
driven extrinsic spin Hall contributions in ultrathin Pt films. Through a comprehensive combination of transport
measurements, magneto-optical switching, structural STEM–EELS analysis, and morphology simulations, the authors
provide strong evidence that grain-boundary scattering in nanogranular Pt enhances spin–charge conversion well beyond

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

conventional mechanisms. The additional demonstration that Ti and Mn overlayers actively contribute orbital torques is
highly impactful, opening new routes toward low-power and energy-efficient spin–orbitronic devices. The work is clearly
written, supported by well-prepared figures, and will be of broad interest to the spintronics and orbitronics communities. 
There are, however, several points that should be addressed before publication. First, while the attribution of enhanced
efficiency to morphology-driven scattering is compelling, the evidence remains indirect; it would strengthen the paper to
clarify how this mechanism can be distinguished quantitatively from known extrinsic spin Hall contributions, and to
acknowledge residual uncertainties. Second, the EELS data show notable Ti intermixing into TbIG, which likely degrades
the interfacial magnetic quality. The manuscript should more clearly demonstrate the consistency between this apparent
detrimental effect and the overall improvement in switching efficiency attributable to orbital torque generation by titanium.
Third, although the comparison between millisecond and nanosecond current pulses reduces the role of Joule heating, a
brief quantitative estimate of the expected temperature rise would more firmly exclude thermally assisted switching as the
dominant mechanism. Fourth, the authors are encouraged to discuss the generality of their findings beyond TbIG,
considering whether similar morphology-driven effects and orbital contributions might be expected in other garnets or
metallic ferromagnets. Finally, the discussion could be further strengthened by connecting the results more explicitly to the
most recent literature on the orbital Hall effect, including its experimental detection in light metals such as Ti and Cr. 
Overall, the manuscript is of high quality and presents results that are both novel and impactful. Addressing the points above
will improve the clarity and robustness of the conclusions, and I recommend acceptance after minor revision. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Fedel and Avci systematically investigated current-induced spin-orbit torque (SOT) switching in TbIG/Pt based
heterostructures grown by sputtering. They found that a Pt dusting layer as thin as 0.6nm can improve the SOT switching
efficiency of the adjacent TbIG. Next, adding Ti layer on top of Pt can further enhance the switching efficiency, as long as the
Pt layer is sufficiently thick (>=1.2nm) to prevent TbIG-Ti intermixing. They attributed these results to: (1) morphology-
enhanced extrinsic spin Hall effect, (2) efficient orbital (from Ti layer) to spin current conversion by the Pt layer. Overall, the
manuscript reports on a novel mechanism of SOT switching which is of high interest for the spintronics community. The
study is original, timely and I would be happy to recommend publication of the manuscript in Communications Physics if the
authors can address the following comments: 
1) Via extensive characterizations, the authors convincingly showed that the nominally 0.6nm-thick Pt dusting layer is
indeed highly non-uniform, possibly due to poor wetting of Pt on TbIG. Yet, the authors calculated an averaged critical
current density jc using the nominal thickness and compared jc in this regime with jc in thicker, rather uniform Pt structures.
Instead of the proposed enhanced extrinsic spin Hall effect, I would like the authors to consider the following competing
scenario: 
For ultrathin Pt layer with non-uniform thickness, since the resistivity of Pt strongly depends on the thickness, the actual
spatial-resolved current density will be highly non uniform. Most current will shunt through the conducting paths, leading to
higher local current density (sufficient to induce SOT switching without involving new charge-spin conversion mechanism)
than the calculated averaged current density. Once the nucleation process is done underneath thicker Pt, full switching can
be achieved either by (1) propagation of the opposite domains to the whole device with a lower jc or (2) if we consider the
lateral dimensions of the current/thickness non-uniformity (a few nm) is smaller than the domain wall width of TbIG, then the
structure might be forced to switch fully. 
2) To distinguish current non-uniform scenario and extra extrinsic scattering, perhaps temperature dependence may give us
a clue. Can the authors comment on this? 
3) The authors have chosen to estimate “critical switching current”. Since the spin Hall effect of Pt is mostly intrinsic, for Pt
with varying resistivity, would it be more relevant to plot/discuss in terms of “critical electric field” instead? 
4) That would be nice to show that the magnetic properties (Hc and Hk) of TbIG underneath Pt does not change with Pt
coverage. 
5) Other minor issues/typos: 
a) Page 12: “…in the MOKE setup, following the procedure described previously” << proper citation will help 
b) Figure caption for Fig. 2(F) is missing 
c) It would be better to clearly state that the switching data in Fig.4 are measured with nanosecond pulses.

feedback and the opportunity to address the comments in detail. Below, we provide our responses

We appreciate the reviewer’s comments regarding the possible role of the REE and the influence of 
oxidation in ultrathin Pt films. We address these concerns below. 
Rashba-Edelstein Effect: We initially ruled out REE as the dominant mechanism for the enhanced 
switching efficiency in ultrathin Pt based on the assumption of comparable Pt/TbIG interface across 
different Pt thicknesses due to in-situ growth. In this simplified scenario, the REE contribution should 
remain largely independent of Pt thickness or become less prominent in thinner films due to partial 
surface coverage, contradicting the increased switching efficiency in sub-nm Pt coverage. However, 
we acknowledge the reviewer’s point that a more complex scenario might be at play and a more in-
depth discussion on the potential role of REE is required.   
The collective understanding based on electrical characterization (finite conductivity), switching 
characteristics (100% switching), structural analysis, and support from simulations lead to the 
conclusion that even in the thinnest Pt sample (0.6 nm nominal thickness), the entire TbIG surface is 
covered by at least one monolayer of Pt which interconnects thicker islands/grains. If the REE occurs

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

at the very first monolayer of Pt, then the effect would be approximately constant across all 
thicknesses. If it were to extend beyond the first monolayer then the effect would be smaller in 
thinner Pt and saturate as the surface becomes continuously covered by the thickness required for 
the REE to have the maximum effect to generate charge-spin conversion. Neither scenario correlates 
with the switching efficiency trend observed in Fig. 3 in our manuscript, hence the REE is deemed not 
critical or negligible to explain the improved charge-spin conversion and switching efficiency in 
ultrathin Pt. 
Pt oxidation: We agree that granular films are more susceptible to environmental exposure due to 
higher surface/volume ratio. However, several factors suggest that oxidation is unlikely to play a 
significant role in our samples: 
 TbIG is already an oxide, grown in an oxygen-rich environment, and is therefore not expected 
to undergo further oxidation, as noted by the reviewer. 
 Pt is a noble metal and highly resistant to oxidation under ambient conditions. Literature 
reports (e.g., Van Spronsen, M. A., Frenken, J. W., & Groot, I. M. (2017). Nature 
communications, 8(1), 429) show that Pt oxidation typically requires elevated temperatures or 
pressures. At room temperature and atmospheric pressure, only a sub-monolayer of 
chemisorbed oxygen (up to ~0.25 ML) has been reported on Pt(111) surfaces (e.g., Pang, Q., 
Zhang, Y., Zhang, J. M., & Xu, K. W. (2011). Applied Surface Science, 257(7), 3047-3054). 
Indeed, in our experience, even intentionally incorporated O2 into Pt is unstable and tend to 
diffuse to nearby layers or air over time (Feng, J., Grimaldi, E., Avci, C. O., Baumgartner, M., 
Cossu, G., Rossi, A., & Gambardella, P. (2020). Physical Review Applied, 13(4), 044029), which 
further supports that Pt alone cannot noticeably oxidize to give rise to a significant spin-
charge conversion effects. 
 Our Pt films are polycrystalline, not exclusively (111)-oriented, and thus would be even less 
favorable for oxygen adsorption reported in Pang et al., Appl. Surf. Sci. 2011. 
 Most importantly, our EELS analysis does not show any oxygen signal at the Pt region (both 
bottom and top surface) suggesting that no significant oxidation occurred during or after 
deposition. 
While we cannot completely rule out the possibility of oxygen adsorption in ultrathin Pt whose 
detection goes outside the experimental limits, we find no other transport evidence supporting its 
presence or influence in our samples. If such adsorption were to occur, it might introduce a Rashba-
type spin–orbit coupling channel, but again, this would likely be spatially inhomogeneous and not 
sufficient to explain the observed enhancement in switching efficiency. 
Therefore, while interfacial effects such as REE and oxygen-related modifications cannot be entirely 
excluded a priori, we believe they are unlikely to be the dominant contributors. We have extended 
the discussion in the relevant part of the main text based on the above response.

convinced. The spin diffusion length and spin Hall effect of Pt varies as a function of the resistance of 
Pt (Sagasta et al., Phys. Rev. B 94, 060412 (2016)). The comparison of the resistance is required to

refer a value in a literature. I’m concerning that the authors main claim could persist even though the 
assumption on the spin diffusion length doesn’t hold. 
We thank the reviewer for raising this important point regarding the spin diffusion length (λ_SD) of 
Pt and its dependence on resistivity. We fully agree that λ_SD is not a universal constant and can vary 
significantly with the microstructure and resistivity of the Pt film, as shown in Sagasta et al., Phys. Rev. 
B 94, 060412 (2016). 
In our manuscript, we are careful not to extract or rely on a precise value of λ_SD using the 
conventional drift-diffusion framework. This is because our ultrathin Pt films are nanogranular and 
laterally inhomogeneous, as confirmed by STEM and EELS analysis. In such a morphology, the 
assumptions underlying the drift-diffusion model, such as uniform conductivity and well-defined 
interfaces, are not valid. A more appropriate treatment would require a Boltzmann transport 
approach or atomistic simulations, which are beyond the scope of this work. 
We use the literature value of λ_SD ≈ 1.2 nm only as a qualitative reference to define three regimes 
in our data: thick (t_Pt > λ_SD), intermediate (t_Pt ≈ λ_SD), and ultrathin (t_Pt < λ_SD). This 
classification helps us interpret the observed trends in switching efficiency, but our main conclusions 
do not rely on the exact value of λ_SD. 
Importantly, the key observation in our work is that switching efficiency increases as the Pt becomes 
thinner and more resistive, which is consistent with the trend reported by Sagasta et al.—namely, 
that increased resistivity correlates with shorter λ_SD and enhanced spin Hall angle. However, in our 
case, the increased resistivity arises not from impurity scattering or disorder but from morphology-
induced scattering at grain boundaries. Therefore, it is not clear if the expected trend reported in 
Sagasta et al. would be applicable in the sub-nm Pt regime. 
We have clarified this point in the revised manuscript and emphasized that our conclusions are 
robust even if the exact value of λ_SD becomes thickness-dependent and/or differs from the 
literature values.

We thank the reviewer for this important question regarding the connection between the two parts 
of our study. 
The overarching goal of our work is to explore new strategies for achieving efficient SOT switching in 
REIG/metal heterostructures. The first part of the manuscript focuses on identifying the most efficient 
and thinnest Pt for SOT generation in TbIG/Pt bilayers. This investigation revealed that ultrathin, 
nanogranular Pt layers (0.6 nm) exhibit anomalously high switching efficiency due to morphology-
enhanced spin–charge conversion. 
The second part of the study builds on this foundation by introducing light metal overlayers (Ti and 
Mn) to explore the role of orbital torques. Our intention was to use the most efficient bilayer (TbIG/Pt 
0.6 nm) as the base structure. However, we found that capping this ultrathin Pt with Ti led to strong 
intermixing of Ti with TbIG and complete suppression of the spin-dependent (SMR) signal due to 
degradation of the magnetic interface. On the other hand, using a thicker Pt layer (e.g., 4 nm) would 
suppress orbital contributions from the light metal due to dephasing and loss of spin current 
(converted from incoming orbital current from Ti) due to short spin diffusion length of Pt. 
We therefore selected Pt 1.2 nm as a compromise: although it is not the most efficient for SOT 
generation on its own, it is thick enough to preserve a reasonably intact Pt/TbIG interface and 
maintain measurable SOT switching, and thin enough to allow orbital-to-spin conversion without 
being limited by the spin diffusion length issue described above. 
Thus, the second part of the study is directly connected to the first: the ultrathin Pt analysis is 
required to identify the contribution from Pt alone without going through the assumption that the 
torques would be small/negligible because of its thickness comparable to SDL. It also motivates the 
choice for the optimal base layer for exploring orbital torque contributions. This progression reflects 
a broader strategy of combining morphology-driven spin Hall enhancement with orbital torque 
engineering to achieve efficient switching in REIG-based devices. 
We have revised the manuscript to better highlight the continuity between the two parts of the work.

The manuscript presents a novel and significant contribution to the field of spin–orbitronics by 
demonstrating morphology-driven extrinsic spin Hall contributions in ultrathin Pt films. Through a 
comprehensive combination of transport measurements, magneto-optical switching, structural 
STEM–EELS analysis, and morphology simulations, the authors provide strong evidence that grain-

boundary scattering in nanogranular Pt enhances spin–charge conversion well beyond conventional 
mechanisms. The additional demonstration that Ti and Mn overlayers actively contribute orbital 
torques is highly impactful, opening new routes toward low-power and energy-efficient spin–
orbitronic devices. The work is clearly written, supported by well-prepared figures, and will be of 
broad interest to the spintronics and orbitronics communities. 
There are, however, several points that should be addressed before publication. 
We thank the referee for their careful reading of our manuscript. We appreciate the constructive 
feedback and the opportunity to address the comments in detail. Below, we provide our responses 
to the points raised. 
First, while the attribution of enhanced efficiency to morphology-driven scattering is compelling, the 
evidence remains indirect; it would strengthen the paper to clarify how this mechanism can be 
distinguished quantitatively from known extrinsic spin Hall contributions, and to acknowledge 
residual uncertainties.  
We thank the reviewer for highlighting the need to clarify the nature of the proposed morphology-
enhanced spin Hall effect (SHE). To be precise, we do not claim that the charge-to-spin conversion 
mechanism itself is fundamentally different from known intrinsic or extrinsic SHE mechanisms. 
Rather, our key point is that the origin of the scattering events responsible for spin current 
generation is different than those typically considered in other experiments. 
In Pt, scattering is generally attributed to impurities or defects within a uniform metallic matrix. In 
contrast, our ultrathin Pt films exhibit a nanogranular morphology, where grain boundaries and 
thickness inhomogeneities dominate the electron transport landscape. These morphological features 
introduce additional scattering centers, which we propose act as previously unexplored sources of 
extrinsic spin Hall generation or enhance the intrinsic SHE. 
Thus, while the underlying mechanism of spin–orbit coupling and spin current generation remains 
the same, the resistivity increase is morphology-driven rather than impurity-driven. This distinction is 
important because it opens a new regime in which structural disorder, rather than chemical doping, 
can be harnessed to enhance spin–charge conversion. 
We have revised the manuscript to clarify this interpretation and to acknowledge that, although the 
evidence is indirect, it is consistent with a morphology-enhanced SHE scenario. We also explicitly 
note that further theoretical modeling or measurements would be required to quantitatively 
disentangle the intrinsic and extrinsic contributions. 
Second, the EELS data show notable Ti intermixing into TbIG, which likely degrades the interfacial 
magnetic quality. The manuscript should more clearly demonstrate the consistency between this 
apparent detrimental effect and the overall improvement in switching efficiency attributable to 
orbital torque generation by titanium.  
We thank the reviewer for highlighting the apparent contradiction between the observed Ti 
intermixing at the Pt/TbIG interface and the enhanced switching efficiency in the Ti-capped samples.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

As shown in our EELS analysis (Fig. 2E–F), Ti intermixes with the underlying Pt and partially diffuses 
toward the TbIG interface, leading to some degree of Ti–Fe substitution. This intermixing is indeed 
expected to degrade the interfacial magnetic quality and reduce spin transparency. However, the 
extent of this degradation is strongly dependent on the Pt thickness. In the case of the thinnest Pt 
layer (0.6 nm), the interfacial degradation is severe enough to completely suppress the SOT signal, as 
we do not observe any spin-dependent transport effect (SMR, AHE) or SOT switching. This suggests 
that the interface is no longer spin-transparent, likely due to significant disruption of the magnetic 
order at the TbIG surface. In contrast, for the 1.2 nm Pt samples, although some intermixing is still 
present, the Pt layer is thick enough to partially preserve the integrity of the Pt/TbIG interface. This is 
evidenced by the presence of a measurable AHE signal and robust SOT-induced switching. The 
observed enhancement in switching efficiency with increasing Ti thickness, despite the interfacial 
degradation, indicates that the orbital torque contribution from Ti is strong enough to overcome the 
partial loss in spin transparency. 
Therefore, the improved switching efficiency in these samples reflects a competition between two 
effects: 
1. A detrimental effect due to interfacial degradation from Ti intermixing, which reduces spin 
mixing conductance. 
2. A beneficial effect from orbital torque generation in Ti, which contributes additional spin 
current via orbital-to-spin conversion in the adjacent Pt. 
Our results suggest that at intermediate Pt thicknesses (1.2 nm), this trade-off reaches a favorable 
balance: the Pt is thin enough to allow efficient orbital-to-spin conversion, yet thick enough to 
maintain sufficient interfacial quality for spin current transmission. We have clarified this 
interpretation in the revised manuscript to better reflect the nuanced interplay between interfacial 
degradation and orbital torque enhancement.

firmly exclude thermally assisted switching as the dominant mechanism.

We appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comment and fully acknowledge that in ultrathin Pt layers

thickness dependence of Pt resistivity. In such cases, current preferentially flows through well-
connected grains, potentially leading to higher local current densities than the average value 
obtained using the nominal thickness. From this perspective, the granular morphology plays a key 
role, not by introducing new scattering mechanisms, but by enabling localized regions with elevated 
current density where standard SHE can operate efficiently. Once domain nucleation occurs beneath
