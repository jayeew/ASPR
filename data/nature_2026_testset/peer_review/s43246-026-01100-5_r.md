Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This manuscript establishes an electrolyte additive screening framework integrating Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Large
Language Models, and aqueous zinc-ion batteries. The proposed workflow and verified conclusions are of significant
methodological and practical value, supported by experimental validation. Therefore, I recommend publication in
Communications Materials after revision. Specific comments are as follows: 
1. In the field of aqueous zinc-ion batteries, more than 100 papers are published annually, with highly diverse and unevenly
reliable data regarding electrolyte compositions, microscopic mechanisms, experimental characterizations, and
computational results. Why do the authors choose only 50 papers as the input for the RAG–LLM model? 
2. During the quantitative molecular screening process, the fundamental analysis criteria align with established chemical
strategies, demonstrating good chemical interpretability. However, in the iterative optimization stage, the authors manually
introduce several quantitative thresholds that significantly influence the LLM’s analysis and selection results. Please provide
the reason for these quantitative criteria and a chemical explanation. 
3. The authors randomly select 22,000 molecules from a public database. Please provide the specific random sampling
strategy. Moreover, how do the authors evaluate the commercial availability of these molecules? 
4. Previous studies have shown that imidazole-based additives possess strong reducibility due to their low LUMO energy
levels. Upon introducing MI into the solvation structure, why does the system exhibit enhanced Zn²
⁺
 reduction, rather than
the overall LUMO decrease being dominated by the intrinsic reducibility of MI itself? (Page 9, Line 303) 
5. Although the authors have conducted extensive comparative computational analyses, the experimental section lacks
direct electrochemical validation for MI to fully support the discussion (Figure 7f–h). 
6. Considering the difference in reducibility between the two molecules and their solvation clusters, how do the authors
distinguish the respective influences of these additives on SEI formation and composition? 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This article presents a system that integrates Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) technology and large language
models to enable efficient molecular screening of over 20000 molecules. It also explores the effect of molecular structure on
Zn2+ solvation shell, ion migration, and hydrogen bond network. The AI-assisted screening approach is inspiring, and the
theoretical analysis is comprehensive. I recommend the paper to be published after minor revisions, with specific comments
listed below: 
1. In Figure 2, the authors screen molecular pairs based on binding energy and molecular weight. As noted in Figure 4f,
certain molecules (e.g., AP) may exhibit different binding energies with Zn2+ depending on the coordinating atoms.
However, the binding energy data in Figure 2 appear to assign only one value per molecule. Please clarify this point. 
2. It is beneficial to provide some experimental characterization to verify the conclusion derived from theoretical analysis. For
example, in “Dynamic evolution of the interfacial hydrogen bond network and polarization behavior” section, the authors are
recommended to include Raman, FTIR or NMR spectroscopy. 
3. The information about the addition amount of the additives is missing, please make a supplement. 
4. The RAG retrieval results by large language models indicate that surface adsorption is a key mechanism affecting battery
performance (Supplementary Figure 9-10), The reviewer would suggest the authors consider this point into the regulation
mechanism of the selected additives. 
5. In Figure 7a, the symmetric cell with MI-based electrolyte shows a shorter lifespan but a lower overpotential compared to
the AP-based electrolyte. What might explain this phenomenon? 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
1. The manuscript is generally well written and technically sound. However, several sentences are lengthy and could be
simplified to enhance readability. Please reduce redundancy between the main text and figure descriptions and ensure that
figure captions provide concise supporting explanations.

2. The introduction could better highlight the specific challenges of electrolyte additive design in zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs) and
clearly justify why an AI-driven approach is needed compared to conventional strategies. 
3. The selection and validation of the two additives: rigid 2-methylimidazole (MI) and flexible 3-aminopropanol (AP) are
compelling. Please elaborate on why these two were chosen. Were they the top candidates based on binding energy,
molecular weight, or other screening parameters? 
4. Several figures, especially Fig. 1 and those showing coordination modes, angle/distribution data, and hydrogen bond
network evolution, are complex and difficult to read. Consider improving figure clarity and labeling for better interpretation. 
5. Since the study focuses on only two additives, please discuss the generalizability of your findings. Can the derived
insights or molecular descriptors guide the design of other additive classes? It would strengthen the manuscript to include a
discussion on the scalability of your workflow; can it handle datasets larger than 20,000 molecules or adapt to different
electrolyte systems? 
6. The statement that this is the first microscopic-level elucidation of steric effects on ZIB electrolytes is somewhat
overstated. Previous works (e.g., with sucrose, cyclodextrin, or amino acid additives) have examined similar effects using
MD simulations. Please rephrase to appropriately acknowledge earlier studies. 
7. The phrase “screening accuracy = 100%” should be replaced with “screening precision under defined criteria reached
100% in benchmark tests” to avoid misinterpretation. 
8. Adding a concise comparative table summarizing MI vs. AP effects (solvation shell volume, hydrogen bond count,
diffusion coefficient, cycle life, and corrosion current density) would make the results easier to follow. 
9. Some terminology (e.g., “entry barrier,” “internal packing effect”) should be clearly defined or briefly explained for better
clarity. 
10. Considering the growing focus on sustainable materials, a short comment on the environmental or cost aspects of the
proposed additives would broaden the work’s relevance. 
11. Given the novelty of the AI-assisted workflow, please consider adding a brief section on future directions, such as
expanding to machine learning–based property predictions or adaptive real-time screening.

1. In the field of aqueous zinc -ion batteries, more than 100 papers are published annually, with

Response: Thank you for raising this important methodological question. The selection of 50 papers 
was based on rigorous screening criteria and systematic capability validation, rather than an arbitrary 
decision. 
I. Rigorous Literature Screening Criteria 
Although the aqueous zinc-ion battery field publishes 100+ papers annually, we applied strict four-tier 
screening standards: 
1. Publication timeliness: Published between 202 0-2025 to capture the latest mechanistic 
understanding 
2. Journal quality: Impact factor >10 (Nature series, Science, JACS, Angew. Chem., EES, Adv. 
Mater.) 
3. Topical relevance:  Strict focus on additive molecular design, solvation regulation, and 
interfacial stabilization mechanisms, excluding studies solely focused on cathode materials or 
battery engineering 
4. Data completeness: Requiring comprehensive experimental data, computational validation, 
and in-depth mechanistic insights 
Literature identification was completed through Web of Science database searches using keywords 
"zinc-ion battery", "electrolyte additive", "solvation structure", and "interfacial stability", followed by 
manual screening based on the above criteria. Among annually published papers, only a limited subset 
simultaneously meets all four rigorous standards. Complete bibliographic information for all 50 papers 
is listed in Supplementary Table S1. 
II. Quantitative Capability Saturation Validation 
To rigorously verify the sufficiency of 50 papers, we conducted controlled capability testing of the RAG 
system at different corpus sizes (10, 30, 40, and 50 papers) using the same query: 
"As an electrochemical energy storage researcher, I am designing a systematic screening scheme for 
zinc battery electrolyte additives. From a molecular perspective, what structural and chemical 
properties can ensure that additives effectively regulate Zn²⁺  solvation behavior while stabilizing the 
electrode/electrolyte interface in aqueous electrolytes?" 
Key findings (Figure R1-R4): 
• 10 papers: RAG system identified 3 basic functional group types ( -OH, -COOH, -NH₂) and 3 
property dimensions, with relatively generic mechanistic descriptions 
• 30 papers: Functional group identification significantly expanded to 7 types (adding -SO₃⁻, -O-, 
-S-, -C=O), property dimensions expanded to 4 (adding "chemical stability"), mechanistic

Knowledge Bases）[R1-R13] 
1. XLogP range (-3.0 to 3.0) 
Based on the atom contribution method for calculating octanol/water partition coefficients. This range 
balances adequate water solubility with other desirable physicochemical properties, ensuring good 
solubility in the aqueous phase while maintaining appropriate interactions with the zinc surface . 
2. TPSA range (0.0 to 80.0 Å²) 
Based on the relationship between topological polar surface area and molecular polarity. This range 
ensures moderate polarity, achieving a balance between "dissolution in water" and "adsorption to zinc 
surface" . 
3. Hydrogen bonding parameter ranges 
Ranges for hydrogen bond donors (HBD: 0 -3) and acceptors (HBA: 0 -6) ensure sufficient polar 
functional groups to interact with Zn²⁺ and H₂O while avoiding excessive hydration that reduces 
interfacial activity . 
4. Rotatable bond count (monocyclic: 0-3; short chain: 1-5) 
Based on definitions of molecular flexibility parameters and structural classification standards. 
Moderate molecular flexibility facilitates conformational adaptive adsorption of additives on the zinc 
surface . 
5. Molecular weight limitation (≤300 Da) 
Based on molecular weight optimization standards for small functional compounds. Small molecule 
additives can rapidly diffuse to the zinc surface and form effective protective layers.

1. Energy level matching and reaction kinetics

potential of Zn²⁺/Zn (-0.76 V vs. SHE) is within the electrochemical window of aqueous electrolytes,
