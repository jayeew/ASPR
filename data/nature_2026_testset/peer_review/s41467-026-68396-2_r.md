Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Brzoka and colleagues present data to indicate that (i) the increased presence of CD39+ extracellular vesicles (EVs) impairs
ADP-induced platelet aggregation in murine and human sickle cell disease (SCD); (ii) a polymorphism in the gene encoding
CD39 restores ADP-induced platelet activation in SCD patients, thereby impairing the protection conferred by increased
CD39+EVs against pulmonary thrombosis in this patient subset. A limitation of the study is that CD39 is expressed by some
leukocytes, in addition to endothelial cells, which may mean that these effects are not necessarily exclusively endothelial
cell–derived EV dependent. However, this is fully acknowledged by the authors, and results are not overinterpreted. 
These important and very novel data go some way to clarify previous and conflicting evidence of alterations in ADP-
dependent platelet aggregation in SCD PRP. Findings may also explain why some SCD patients are at more risk of
pulmonary thrombosis. Indeed, authors suggest that SCD patients presenting ENTPD1 polymorphisms may be candidates
for purinergic signalling inhibitor therapy. 
The study is elegantly designed, and the manuscript is well written and very easy to follow. I have some suggestions for
some additional data to support the study’s findings. 
Comments:
 
- Confirmation that pulmonary thrombosis can be induced in SCD mice through ADP-independent mechanisms (e.g., via
collagen/epinephrine induction) would verify that this effect is specific to the ADP pathway. 
- In Figure 4(g-n), it would be important to confirm that the intravascular haemolytic rate did not differ significantly between
the patient groups (e.g., by measuring plasma cell-free heme or describing other haemolytic marker parameters), thereby
ensuring that this variable was not influencing EV release from the endothelium. 
- As I understand it, data in Figure 4m and n show platelet aggregation for just one patient per group, while Extended Data
Figure 2 presents platelet aggregation data from three more patients (one in each group). For the extended figure, the ADP-
dependent aggregation for the GG genotype appears to be partially inhibited. Data from more patients in each group should
be provided. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Centrifugation of anticoagulated blood does not remove all cells, and at least in human blood always residual platelets
remain present. Since SEC is a size-based separation method, SEC effectively separates soluble proteins from particles
such as EVs (with 2B cut off of about 70 nm), but SEC cannot separate EVs from similar-sized non-EV particles, which
include platelets, protein aggregates and large lipoproteins. Although the authors claim they pool fractions 4-7 because
these fractions “contained the vast majority of EVs”, evidence for this statement is not provided. 
The authors use NTA to detect EVs. NTA is a single particle detection method that detects all particles (platelets,
lipoproteins, EVs, protein aggregates, etc.), and the light scattering signal of the detected particles depends on both the size
(diameter) and refractive index of the particles. In plasma, but also in SEC fractions of plasma, EVs are (still) outnumbered
by lipoprotein particles. For example, in human plasma less than 1% of all detected particles are detected by EVs (paper

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

from 2013, Dragovic), and detection of EVs by NTA requires labelling and detection of fluorescence, which is not standard
on commercial NTA. The lower limit of detection of NTA is usually about 80-100 nm, so it seems unlikely that the size range
of detected “EVs” is 50-400 nm. Similarly, adjusting concentrations of particles as determined by NTA does not make sense,
as the true fractions of EVs is unknown. 
I see no evidence showing that the antibody against CD39 really detects CD39 (and only CD39), and as explained for SEC,
it should be excluded that detected CD39 is present in platelets, lipoproteins or other co-isolates as protein aggregates. 
There are international guidelines to publish flow cytometry data on EVs. This reporting framework, called MiFlowCYT EV,
ensures that indeed EVs are being detected, etc., and this framework includes a series of essential controls. This is
completely missing here, making the imaging flow cytometry impossible to judge from my perspective. 
CD31 is also abundantly present on platelets. 
Finally, EVs from cultured human endothelial cells are isolated from medium containing 1% (not specified) serum. Serum
contains a very high concentration of platelets and EVs, these serum-derived EVs will co-isolate with endothelial EVs, thus
possibly hampering the interpretation of downstream results. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Pulmonary microvascular thrombosis is a well-known complication of sickle cell disease (SCD) and is associated with
significant morbidity and mortality but it is not clear why only a sub-set of patients develop pulmonary thrombosis evidenced
by autopsy and CT studies. It has also been noted that the pulmonary arteriole thrombi were platelet-rich and associated
with development of thrombocytopenia in such patients with pulmonary thrombi. ADP is well known as a potent platelet
agonist and released in high levels during hemolysis. Using the Townes SCD mouse model, the authors observed ADP
challenge led to in-situ thrombosis in pulmonary arterioles and eventually occlusion in control mice. In contrast, IV ADP
failed to trigger pulmonary thrombosis in HbSS mice. The authors then propose that reduction of the ADP-specific platelet
aggregation in SCD mice, was due to an increase in extracellular vesicles (EVs) of endothelial origin that express CD39.
The CD39+ EVs released by lung endothelium prevented ADP-induced platelet aggregation and lung thrombosis by
degrading ADP to AMP in SCD mice. Pre-treatment of the EVs with CD39 inhibitor POM-1 abrogated the ADP-dependent
platelet aggregation in SCD mice. 
Following on a recent report that SNP rs3176891 in CD39 was associated with a modest risk of thrombosis in people of
European ancestry, the authors investigated if this could underlie the susceptibility of pulmonary thrombosis in a sub-set of
SCD patients. The allele-frequency of CD39 rs3176891A→G in TOPMed population of 
African descent was ~50%, but not
associated with risk of pulmonary thrombosis in contrast to SCD patients. To provide a biological basis for the association,
the authors showed that ADPase activity was two-fold lower in EVs of SCD patients with the rs3176891 AG and GG risk
alleles compared with EVs of SCD patients with rs3176891 AA. 
This is a compelling and study that provides a basis for differential complication of pulmonary thrombosis in SCD patients
adding another layer to the genetic modifiers of sub-phenotypes. The study was well designed and leverages the use of data
available in the public domain. 
Comments: 
1. Can the authors suggest why SNP rs3176891 is associated with a modest risk of thrombosis in people of European
ancestry but not in peoples of African descent (without SCD)? 
2. A major argument in this study is that circulating EVs with CD39 degrades ADP to AMP. However, no measurements of
the levels of ADP and AMP were provided in the paper. For example, what’s the level of ADP in control and SCD mice?
What’s the change in ADP levels after adding control or SCD EVs, as well as CD39 inhibitor such as POM-1? What’s the
ADP level in patients with rs3176891G risk allele in comparison with A allele? These results could further support authors’
conclusion.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I still have concerns about the separation and detection of platelets, extracellular vesicles (EVs) and lipoproteins. 
C1/R1. I agree with the authors that the most commonly used and recommended protocol to prepare platelet-free plasma
(PFP) from human blood (for EV research) involves two sequential centrifugation steps. However, when different
laboratories used this protocol recently, the concentration of platelets in the PFP differed >50-fold between the laboratories
(different operators, centrifuges, how much plasma is collected, etc.; please see https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36043239/).
The MISEV and MiBlood EV guidelines recommend to measure the concentration of remaining platelets by e.g. a routine
hematology analyser or flow cytometry, but it is also recommended to report the lower limits of detection (LoD). The
concentration of remaining platelets can be below the LoD, but that does not mean there are not platelets. 
When looking at the provided Figure for R3, the LoD seems to be around 5 x 10E3 platelets/uL, which equals 10E6
platelets/mL. That may seem a negligible concentration of remaining platelets when compared to the starting material, but
the volume fraction of remaining platelets easily equals or exceeds that of EVs per plasma volume, and removal of even
such an apparently negligible platelet fraction also removed 50-75% of miRNAs thought to be associated with “plasma EVs”
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36788785/). So, it is easy to overestimate the contribution of (platelet) EVs. 
Measuring platelets by flow cytometry is indeed a good option, but more details should be provided regarded gating, volume
analysed, etc., and also here an LoD should be reported. 
Regarding Figure 3B. The concentration remaining platelets compared to EVs and lipoproteins is so small, and the
analysed volume so small, that it is unlikely to record platelets at all. This also holds true for large EVs: because their
concentration is so low, they do not seem to be present, but this simply is a matter of their relative abundancy/concentrations.
C2. There seems to be a misunderstanding regarding how SEC works. All particles (whether commercial beads, cells
including platelets, or even very large multimeric proteins such as VWF) will all c0-elute from the column, and hence end up
in the same fraction(s). Only particles and soluble molecules with a diameter smaller than the cut off of the resins’ pore size
will elute later. Thus, platelets, EVs and (large) lipoproteins will always co-elute. The authors conclusion that “platelets
cannot be present in the EV fractions” is wrong. Again, NTA detects mainly lipoproteins (Supplementary Figure 11), and this
does not say much about the presence of EVs in SEC fractions.
