Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors investigate the interaction between nimbolide (NIM) and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Using molecular docking,
mutagenesis, and cellular assays, they propose that NIM modulates NLRP3 activity by targeting the NACHT domain.
Functional studies in HEK293T and inflammasome-competent cells support its inhibitory role, with evidence of reduced
inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. 
This manuscript addresses an important and timely question in inflammasome biology: whether nimbolide can directly
modulate NLRP3 activity. Given the growing interest in therapeutic targeting of NLRP3 in inflammation-driven diseases, this
study is of potential significance to both immunologists and the broader biomedical community. The work is ambitious and
could have translational impact. However, there are issues in data presentation, methodological reporting, and experimental
consistency that currently limit the strength of the conclusions. Addressing these will improve clarity, reproducibility, and
overall rigor. 
Several technical and methodological concerns should be carefully considered. These include limitations in the in vivo
model used, insufficient experimental detail, incomplete quantification of immunoblots, and inconsistencies across figures. I
refer the authors to the detailed comments in the attached Word file, which highlight these points in greater depth and
provide specific recommendations for revision. 
To strengthen the manuscript and make it suitable for publication in Communications Biology, I recommend: 
In vivo studies: Justify dosing strategies with consideration of route-dependent bioavailability. Replace intraperitoneal LPS
with a more appropriate ARDS model (e.g., intratracheal LPS, CLP, or acid aspiration), and resolve the inconsistency in the
Figure 5 legend regarding route of LPS administration. 
Complete methodological reporting: Provide missing details such as cell numbers, drug concentrations, assay readouts,
buffer compositions, and manufacturer information. 
Figure 1A: Reformat to show compound names, clarify the number of biological replicates, and discuss other compounds
identified in the heatmap. 
Immunoblots: Include densitometric quantification and address inconsistencies between replicate experiments. 
Mechanistic validation: Add experiments confirming inhibition at both priming (e.g., IκB and ERK signaling) and assembly
(e.g., ASC speck formation by confocal microscopy) stages. 
In summary, the manuscript tackles a question of high relevance with potentially novel findings, but substantial clarifications
and additional experimental rigor are required before the work can reach its full impact.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript by Xu et al. reports the identification of Nimbolide as a novel inhibitor of NLRP3. Nimbolide, derived from
the Indian neem tree, was shown to specifically target the NACHT domain of NLRP3 and disrupt inflammasome formation.
With prolonged incubation, Nimbolide also inhibited the NFκB pathway, thereby reducing priming of the NLRP3
inflammasome. The authors further demonstrated therapeutic potential of Nimbolide in vivo, using LPS-induced acute
respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and DSS-induced colitis mouse models, where treatment alleviated disease
phenotypes through NLRP3 inhibition. Overall, this study highlights Nimbolide as a promising therapeutic candidate for
NLRP3-related diseases. However, additional lines of evidence are required to strengthen the conclusions and to establish
the specificity and translational relevance of the findings. 
Major comments: 
In Fig. 4G, the authors propose that Nimbolide interacts with NLRP3 at residue K565. However, the current evidence is not
sufficient to support this conclusion. It would be important to test the K565A mutant in HEK293T cells co-expressing ASC, or
in THP-1 cells or BMDMs, to determine whether K565A is resistant to Nimbolide treatment. 
To further support that the NACHT domain is the primary target of Nimbolide, the authors could examine the effect of
Nimbolide on auto-active NLRP3 variants with mutations in different domains, for example, D21H (pyrin domain), F304C
(NACHT domain), and E690K (LRR domain). Since these mutations activate NLRP3 via distinct mechanisms, one would
expect pyrin- and LRR-domain variants to be relatively resistant to Nimbolide inhibition, whereas NACHT-domain variants
may remain sensitive. 
In addition, the K565E variant is a strongly active NLRP3 mutant (PMID: 39930093). It is important to test whether K565E is
resistant to Nimbolide treatment, ideally alongside MCC950 as a positive control. 
Finally, all assays presented lack a positive control for NLRP3 inhibition. Including MCC950 as a reference inhibitor would
strengthen the validity of the findings. 
Minor comments: 
• The title “Nimbolide as a selective NLRP3 inhibitor ameliorates associated inflammatory diseases by disrupting the
activation of inflammasome” is vague. It is not clear which “associated inflammatory diseases” the authors are referring to.
Please specify the diseases tested in this study (e.g., ARDS, colitis) to make the title more precise and informative. 
• Line 57, if Nimbolide targets the NACHT domain rather than the pyrin domain, it would likely inhibit the NLRP3–ASC
interaction indirectly by altering the overall conformation of NLRP3. 
• Line 59, The abbreviation ARDS appears for the first time here. Please spell it out in full at first mention. 
• Line 106-108, CAPS is autoinflammatory disease, not autoimmune disease. 
• In the Methods section, key details are missing for the experiments on NLRP3, NLRC4, and AIM2 inflammasome
activation, as well as for the SDD-AGE assay. In addition, catalogue numbers for several reagents (e.g., the CCK-8 kit)
should be provided to ensure reproducibility. 
• Line 307, The phrase “overactivation of NLRP3” is used frequently, but this is not accurate without a defined comparison
between basal activation and activation above basal levels. Please clarify or use a more precise term. 
• Line 319, mean “NIM treatment”? 
• Line 413, hydrogen bonds are transient and weak individually, can’t say it’s stable. 
• Fig 1A, the compounds tested are not labeled, making the data difficult to interpret. In addition, several compounds appear
to show inhibition efficiency comparable to Nimbolide. Please provide a clear rationale or experimental validation to explain
why these other compounds were not investigated further. 
• Fig 1D, please explain why pro-IL-1β is detected in the untreated samples at levels comparable to those in LPS-primed
cells. 
• Across all figures, key details are missing regarding the duration of Nimbolide treatment. In addition, it is unclear whether
the data shown represent technical replicates or independent biological repeats. Please clarify both to ensure reproducibility
and proper interpretation. 
• Fig 3H, please explain why NLRP3 already interact with ASC in untreated condition. Suggest including a negative control
using NLRP3 mutation, D31V, whose interaction with ASC is abolished (PMID: 35559676). 
• Fig 4A-C, typo in “proase,” which should be “pronase”. Please also provide quantification of band intensity to demonstrate
the changes after pronase treatment, based on at least three independent experiments. In addition, the efficiency of pronase
appears to vary across proteins or experiments—for example, it seems more effective in panel B than in panels A and C.
Please clarify or discuss potential reasons for this variability. 
• Fig 4B, two bands at different size shown in the NEK7 blot, please specify which band refers to NEK7. 
• Fig 4E, it is unclear what the authors want to show here, quantification for experiments from three independent repeats is
required. 
• Fig 5C, it is unclear how lung injury was scored. Please describe the scoring criteria in detail and indicate whether the
assessments were performed in a blinded manner. 
• Fig 5, what is ArtB? 
• Fig 5 & 6, “normal” means untreated mice? 
• Fig S3, The ATPase activity of the NACHT domain is essential for NLRP3 function and inflammasome assembly. Could
the authors clarify how Nimbolide targets the NACHT domain without impairing ATPase activity? For example, is it possible
that Nimbolide alters domain conformation or inter-domain interactions rather than directly interfering with ATP hydrolysis?

This study investigates Nimbolide (NIM), a natural compound that acts as a specific inhibitor of NLRP3. NIM suppresses
both the priming and activation of NLRP3, leading to reduced caspase-1 activation and IL-1β release. This research is
significant in that NIM is a natural product that selectively binds to the NLRP3 NACHT domain, interfering the its
oligomerization. Furthermore, the major findings of this study suggest that NIM holds therapeutic potential for conditions
associated with excessive NLRP3 activation, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and ulcerative colitis.
The study could be further strengthened through several suggestions below. 
Major comments 
1. In the introduction, please highlight some commercially available NLRP3 inhibitors and their limitations, as well as
emerging inhibitors with distinct mechanisms compared to existing ones (PMID: 40930090). 
2. In Fig. 2A, C, and Fig. 3A, B, NLRP3 expression in BMDMs does not appear to increase following LPS+ATP treatment
compared to untreated controls, which is unexpected. The authors should clarify this result or provide additional data to
confirm NLRP3 induction. 
3. To fully confirm the specificity of NIM to NLRP3 inflammasome, it is recommended to try LPS plus Imiquimod, which is
known to activate potassium efflux-independent NLRP3 inflammasome (PMID: 40930090). 
4. The results showing that long-term NIM treatment inhibits NF-κB priming while short-term treatment suppresses
inflammasome assembly (Fig. 3A-B) are interesting, but the explanation is somewhat compressed. A clearer breakdown of
these dual actions and their clinical relevance, including potential off-target effects of NF-κB modulation, would strengthen
the manuscript. 
5. In the DARTS assays (Fig. 4A, C), enzymatic degradation of NLRP3 (as well as some Casp1, NEK7, and ASC) without
NIM treatment appears incomplete, and, in some cases, the NLRP3 level looks comparable with the condition with NIM.
Increasing the pronase concentration to achieve full degradation of these proteins would strengthen the robustness of the
results. 
6. The discussion briefly mentions pharmacokinetics and structural biology gaps, but it would be stronger with more
reflection on potential off-target effects, challenges in clinical translation, and comparison with benchmark inhibitors like
MCC950. 
7. There should be some data that compares the effectiveness of NIM with well-established NLRP3 inhibitors such as
CRID3. 
Minor comments: 
1. Although CCK-8 assays showed cell viability >85% up to 5 μM (Fig. 1C), systemic toxicity in mice (body weight,
liver/kidney function) was not described. Including toxicity data or at least a statement that no adverse effects were observed
would increase confidence in NIM’s therapeutic potential. 
2. Some legends (e.g., Fig. 6D-E) repeat “lung injury score” in a colon model, which seems like a copy-editing error. Ensure
legends are precise, consistent, and specify what statistical tests were used for each panel. 
3. There are multiple grammatical issues (e.g., “3.4. NIM bind to NLRP3’s Lys565 sites” should be “3.4. NIM binds to
NLRP3’s Lys565 site”). Careful copyediting for grammar, tense, and word choice would improve readability. 
4. Typo in line 319, ‘NIN’ to ‘NIM’. Typo in Figure 1I Y axis. 
5. In Figure 1H, there is a very minimal level of cell death in the LPS+ATP condition itself, which is hard to tell that NIM
protected cells from undergoing death. 
6. In Figures 3A and 3B, the long-term and short-term show significant differences in terms of protection levels. The authors
are recommended to mention those changes in the manuscript. 
7. The description ‘degradation caused by increased temperatures in BMDMs’ in line 397 could provoke some confusion, as
it sounds like not pronase but temperature leads to degradation. 
8. More information regarding Figure 4G is required in line 416. 
9. Additional information about those two markers and why the authors used them is needed in line 436. 
10. In Figure 5C, what is ArtB? And in other bar graphs, do they all share the same legend? 
11. In Figures 6D to G, the colors should be labeled properly to help the readers easily check the data. 
12. In line 494, it is recommended to clarify why NIM has strength compared to other natural products or other chemical-
based compounds. Additionally, the examples of natural products in clinical use are too redundant. 
13. References are needed in lines 322, 335, 378, and 385.
