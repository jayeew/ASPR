Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a study on the development of a biomimetic electrochemical interface based on cancer cell
membranes for the subtype-specific detection and characterization of breast cancer-derived EVs. The authors employ breast
cancer cell membranes to coat a gold substrate, creating a biomimetic interface capable of selectively capturing homologous
EVs, followed by silver nanoparticle-labeled anti-CD47 antibodies for signal amplification. The platform demonstrates strong
subtype selectivity (ER+, HER2+, and TNBC) and excellent analytical performance, including a wide linear detection range
and low detection limit (239 particles/mL). Additionally, the integration of this approach into a microfluidic device provides
parallel, high-throughput analysis of clinical samples, illustrating significant potential for precise breast cancer diagnosis and
personalized treatment. However, several important issues must be addressed prior to publication: 
1. Given that the biosensing interface relies heavily on membranes derived from cell cultures (MCF-7, BT-474, and MDA-
MB-231), the authors should clarify batch-to-batch variations and reproducibility. Please provide statistical evaluation (e.g.,
relative standard deviation, RSD) of membrane preparations from different batches and discuss strategies for
standardization. 
2. The manuscript employs a 50 °C pretreatment step to reduce the interference of endogenous CD47 on cell membranes.
Please clearly discuss the rationale behind selecting this temperature, potential impacts on other membrane proteins or
epitopes critical for EV binding, and provide experimental evidence (e.g., proteomic analysis or binding assays) confirming
minimal impairment of subtype-specific binding capability. 
3. The current approach tests only three representative breast cancer subtypes (ER+, HER2+, TNBC) with a single cell line
for each subtype. Considering breast cancer’s intrinsic heterogeneity, please discuss the applicability and potential
limitations of this strategy when detecting EVs derived from other clinically relevant breast cancer subtypes (e.g., Luminal B,
basal-like) or heterogeneous patient samples. Moreover, the authors should experimentally verify whether the biomimetic
interface retains its subtype-specific recognition capability when alternative breast cancer cell lines from the same subtype
are used for membrane preparation (e.g., replacing BT-474 with SK-BR-3 for HER2+ subtype). Such cross-validation
experiments are crucial to demonstrate the broader reliability and robustness of the proposed approach in accurately

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

identifying breast cancer EV subtypes. 
4. Although the authors evaluated clinical plasma samples, the number of samples is relatively small (n=37 initially, and
n=60 for microfluidic experiments). For robust clinical validation, it is crucial to expand the sample size, employ rigorous
statistical methods (including ROC curves, AUC, sensitivity/specificity calculations), and explicitly address statistical power
and significance after multiple testing corrections. 
5. The authors should verify specificity of the developed assay by including EVs isolated from other cancer types (e.g., lung,
ovarian cancer) and benign breast diseases (e.g., fibroadenoma). Clarify the platform’s capability to differentiate these EVs
from breast cancer-derived EVs. 
6. The manuscript currently lacks information regarding the long-term stability and storage conditions of the cell membrane-
coated electrodes. Authors should evaluate performance after prolonged storage under various conditions (dry, refrigerated,
or frozen) to facilitate practical application. 
7. Detection relies on CD47 expression, potentially limiting diagnostic accuracy if CD47 levels vary significantly among
patients. Please discuss possibilities for multi-marker detection or demonstrate a complementary approach to minimize
dependence on a single biomarker, thereby enhancing robustness and reliability. 
8. It is necessary to evaluate the purity of EV preparations to rule out potential non-specific signals. 
9. The current clinical validation was performed within a single institution. To further confirm the robustness, clinical utility,
and reproducibility of the proposed method, the authors should perform multicenter clinical cohort studies involving
independent patient populations across different laboratories or hospitals. Such studies will critically demonstrate the
platform’s stability and practicality in real-world clinical scenarios. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript demonstrates promising technical innovation but requires significantly stronger mechanistic validation,
clinical benchmarking, and generalizability data to warrant consideration in a high-impact journal. The platform is creative,
but its translational claims are premature without further refinement and robust comparative evaluation. While the use of cell
membrane-coated surfaces for homotypic binding is well-established in drug delivery and nanocarrier design, applying this
to EV capture for subtype-specific diagnostics is presented as novel. However, this approach is an incremental rather than
fundamental advance. 
1) The assumption that homotypic binding is subtype-specific is insufficiently mechanistically grounded. There is no
demonstration that the cell membrane properties driving the selective binding are conserved or exclusive to the EVs of
interest. While the authors show reduced binding from heterologous EVs, selectivity may stem from bulk physicochemical
features (size, charge, general membrane protein content) rather than subtype-specific molecular interactions. Subtype-
specific EV composition is known to be heterogeneous and overlapping across cancer subtypes. Demonstrating functional
binding specificity in cell-line-derived EVs does not sufficiently prove robustness in real-world clinical heterogeneity. To
show this, the authors should include direct molecular profiling (e.g., proteomics or glycomics) of the captured EVs to confirm
they are subtype-distinct in composition, not merely captured by chance or affinity artifacts. 
Could candidate surface ligands
be mutated or enzymatically removed to show loss of binding? Or perhaps can perform competition assays with excess
unlabeled EVs or soluble membrane components to test for receptor–ligand mediation. Even any single-vesicle imaging or
flow cytometry for higher resolution binding analyses would be helpfl. 
2) Clinical sample testing is promising but lacks sufficient detail on patient heterogeneity, treatment status, tumor burden,
and time of sampling. Furthermore, the reliance on plasma EVs from known subtype diagnoses is a weak validation
standard unless compared with gold-standard molecular subtyping. Blinded concordance is necessary but not sufficient.
There’s no benchmark comparison (e.g., current clinical diagnostics) to determine added value. The authors should include
ROC curves comparing this method vs. ELISA, IHC, or RNA-seq gold standards, and stratify patients by clinical parameters
(stage, treatment) and analyze performance variation. 
3) The claimed limit of detection (239 particles/mL) may not be biologically meaningful without establishing the fraction of
disease-relevant EVs in that pool. Also, the specificity of subtype discrimination is overasserted based on few comparisons
and lack of molecular confirmation. Without assessing molecular cargo or testing in mixed-subtype scenarios (e.g., patients
with multiple lesions or clonal evolution), real-world diagnostic precision remains uncertain. Suggest to perform spike-in of
mixed EV populations from different subtypes to test discriminatory power or correlate capture signal with bulk or single-EV
RNA/protein subtype markers. 
4) EV characterization is minimal and largely inferred. The use of commercial dyes and nanoparticle tracking lacks rigor in
distinguishing EVs from co-isolated contaminants (e.g., lipoproteins, protein aggregates). The authors show NTA, TEM, and
CD47 staining, arguing consistency with EV standards, but there is no use of MISEV-recommended characterization
standards (e.g., multiple markers, negative markers, protein/lipid ratio). The authors should quantify EV yield, protein
content, and purity using BCA or BCA plus nanoparticle counts. 
5) The electrochemical signal is presumed to derive exclusively from AgNP-labeled CD47 on EVs bound via homotypic
interactions. However, potential contributions from nonspecifically bound nanoparticles or artifacts from surface chemistry
are not ruled out. While the authors show control signals on bare gold and nonhomologous interfaces are lower, antibody-
independent interactions or aggregation of AgNPs could still produce artifactual signal. The authors should use isotype
control antibodies conjugated to AgNPs. They could also include surfactant or blocking agent titrations to determine optimal
stringency.

6) Statistical testing is limited to basic t-tests and ANOVA with no mention of effect size, confidence intervals, or correction
for multiple comparisons across groups. Some plots (e.g., heatmaps) lack scale bars or error bars. Suggest to add ROC
curves and AUC values for classification, and report inter-assay CVs and replicate numbers in all figures. Also, please
indicate number of independent experiments and biological vs technical replicates. 
7) Finally, the title and claims imply a broadly applicable diagnostic method, but the study focuses only on breast cancer. It
remains untested whether other cancer types can be similarly subtyped or if this is breast cancer–specific. While they
authors might argue that this is a model system and future generalizability, the method’s reliance on tumor-specific
membrane signatures may not hold in more immunologically or molecularly heterogeneous cancers (e.g., lung, melanoma,
glioma). The authors should re-scope the title and claims, while discussing limitations and assumptions around cancer types
where homotypic membrane binding may not apply. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zou et al. aim to develop a platform for the efficient analysis of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in liquid biopsies. To this end, the
authors construct biomimetic interfaces coated with breast cancer cell membranes, enabling the homotypic capture of breast
cancer EVs. This approach is based on the principle that EVs share membrane characteristics with their cells of origin. EVs
bound to the biomimetic interface are subsequently detected using CD47 antibodies conjugated to silver nanoparticles,
which generate an electrochemical signal for quantitative analysis. The concept of the study is interesting but there are
multiple major comments. 
Major Comments 
1. The extraction of cell membranes using a freeze–thaw method is a critical component of the proposed platform. However,
the protocol is insufficiently described in the Methods section. How specific and reproducible is this procedure for isolating
pure plasma membranes? Are intracellular organelles effectively removed during the process? Moreover, how is the correct
orientation of the membranes on the interface ensured? 
2. The procedure for isolating EVs from the various breast cancer cell lines is unclear. The authors state that “EVs were
initially pelleted at 300 g for 10 min.” However, EVs do not pellet at such low speeds, raising concerns about whether the
authors are indeed working with EVs. Additionally, the downstream characterization data do not convincingly demonstrate
the presence of EVs in the samples. Only limited data for MCF-7-derived EVs are shown; no characterization is provided for
EVs from the other cell lines mentioned. 
3. The authors coat the interface with MCF-7 membranes and show selective binding of MCF-7-derived EVs over EVs from
other breast cancer cell lines, suggesting a homotypic interaction. However, they then apply the platform to patient plasma
samples and report binding of plasma-derived EVs. Given that only a small fraction of plasma EVs likely originates from
tumor cells, and considering the heterogeneity of patient samples—even those with ER-positive breast cancer—this result
seems surprising. The authors should clarify this discrepancy and include appropriate controls. For example, would EVs
from other ER-positive cell lines also bind to MCF-7 membrane-coated interfaces? Would EVs from other cancer types bind
as well? 
4. The authors state that their method allows for the retrieval of molecular information. However, only CD47 is evaluated.
Can the authors clarify what they mean by "molecular information"? Is it possible to adapt this assay to detect other EV
markers, such as the tetraspanins CD9, CD63, or CD81? 
5. CD47 is not specific to tumor-derived EVs; it is also expressed by platelets for example. The blood processing method
described does not appear to deplete platelets. Therefore, it is plausible that the detected signal may originate from platelet-
derived EVs rather than tumor-derived EVs. The authors should address this possibility and provide more detailed
information on how blood samples were processed to minimise such confounding effects. 
In conclusion, while the manuscript presents a potentially valuable approach, the authors must clarify the methodological
aspects, improve the characterisation of EVs, further validate the results in patient plasma and include appropriate controls
to validate the specificity.
