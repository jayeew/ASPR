REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
This is an interesting and well-described study of satellite observations of deformation for the 2025 Myanmar earthquake, for
which a geodetic slip model is produced (Fig. 2), along with dynamic modeling for the same geometry (Fig. 3), informed by
two near-fault observations of ground deformation as the rupture passed a CCTV site and a strong motion site. 
The authors
do not analyze additional seismic data available for the event, so their models have very limited control of the kinematics,
and both the geodetic and dynamic model slip functions are smoother than obtained in seismic analyses, so the models
likely provide limited predictability of other observations. For example, the ~75° dip of the hypocentral segment likely is too
steep to account for the ~60° dip of the Global Centroid Moment Tensor solution or the ~50° dip of the updated W-phase
solutions presented by Ye et al. (TSR, 2025), both of which should be mentioned (the USGS-NEIC W-phase solution with
82° dip is for an unreasonable centroid depth of 40.5 km). As a result many distant P wave first-motions will be violated by
the geodetic model. But, as a solution based on only surface deformation observations, the geodetic model is what it is, and I
believe it is very competently determined. One has to wonder, looking at Figure 1, why there is no discussion of the
aftershocks all shifted eastward from the fault outcrop when the model dips can account for only a small fraction of that, and
why is the pattern of aftershock broader in the northern segment(s). This is a weakness of the paper any reader will note. 
Constructing a dynamic rupture model entails specifying many poorly constrained parameters (including the fault segment
geometry, given the limitations of the geodetic model), but especially the prediction of pre-stress from regional stress
averages without detailed knowledge of prior event slip distributions, fluid variations, structural variations, etc., and great
uncertainty in frictional parameters everywhere. 
Some of this uncertainty is discussed, but perhaps without much caution as
to how uncertainties limit the reliability of the final models. 
The dynamic models give very smooth slip distributions along
strike totally due to the input assumptions (Fig. 3), with only the NPW signals (and later, the camera inferred motions)
providing rupture constraint. 
Good predictions are achieved with the depth-varying rupture speed model, but the data
constraints are still very limited and the slip model is not explicitly reconciled with the geodetic model, nor with published slip
models from seismo-geodetic inversions like that posted by NEIC-USGS, Ye et al. (2025), Melgar et al. (2025), etc., and the

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

camera analysis of Kearse and Kaneko (TSR, 2025) is not referenced (perhaps these appeared after this submission, but at
least these references should be included in revision). So, while I think good fit is provided to NPW (other works on-line do
equally well in fitting this station) and I like the interpretation of the camera recordings (it is sensible and better discussed
than some preprint results and consistent with published work), the models are not fully satisfying in that the kinematics and
details of the slip distribution are not well constrained. An example is the inference of supershear at the northern end of the
fault, which is pretty much totally a result of a priori assumptions and is not documented by data in this paper (some results
from seismology appear to support this possibility, but that is not shown here). 
Overall, the manuscript is OK with me for publication, as the discussion about deeper supershear and shallow sub-shear,
even while initiation of supershear may have been facilitated by a sediment layer, is interesting. That the whole rupture was
not subshear or was all supershear is not surprising, it is just that the regions where supershear occurred are not well
resolved (not at all by the geodetic (static) model; and not by having two local observations along the 450 km long rupture). I
found the paper well prepared and thorough, with good description of the procedures and choices made in the
parameterization. The models are OK for what they are, but are seen through a limited lens of observations. The results will
still be of interest to the broad community working to use seismic, geodetic, camera and surface rupture data together to
constrain this important event, so I support publication (with minor revisions to update references and to perhaps add in more
nuanced discussion of the limitations of the data and model assumptions in this work). 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The submitted manuscript presents source-process analyses for the 2025 Myanmar earthquake. The authors use geodetic
datasets (SAR images) to estimate the static slip distribution, and subsequently perform a dynamic rupture simulation to
further investigate the rupture process. The simulation results are compared with near-field strong-motion records from the
NPW station and with the CCTV recording. By integrating these observations and simulations, the authors primarily argue
that the 2025 Myanmar earthquake exhibits depth-variable rupture speeds, which could be associated with the thick shallow
sediment layer. 
I find this study to be a significant contribution, as it provides an interesting perspective on heterogeneous rupture behaviour
and their possible relation to depth-variable material property in the source region. However, I have some concerns
regarding the evaluation of the rupture behavior in the authors’ dynamic rupture simulation. While I understand that the
available near-field seismic data are limited (e.g., only the NPW station is available), I believe the rupture behavior inferred
from the simulation should have been examined more rigorously, or at least compared with other kinematic source models
(e.g., finite-fault solutions). There appear to be several observational studies in the literature that could provide useful
benchmarks for comparison, which I list below. For instance, it would be informative to evaluate the rupture propagation
northward from the epicenter, as well as the rupture termination toward the southern end of the model space; features that
might be limitedly constrained by only the recordings from the NPW station and the CCTV site.

REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The revision of this paper makes some improvements prompted by the reviewer comments. It remains unfortunate that the
authors do not consider constraints from seismology, unlike the excellent paper they cite by Wei et al. (2025) in Science,
which exploits the complementary attributes of satellite and seismic data for resolving faulting geometry and kinematics. 
As I
said before, that is what it is, but it relegates this paper to secondary importance. 
Wei et al. (2025) place direct seismological
constraints on supershear rupture, preferring a model with subshear rupture to the north of the epicenter (albeit not well-
resolved) and a transition from subshear to supershear to the south, along with demonstrating that the northern segment has

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 7 -->

shallower east dipping geometry (robustly indicated by first-motion mechanisms for teleseismic data, as well as asymmetry
in the E-W and vertical surface satellite displacements). So, the solution presented in this paper still will produce significant
misfit of teleseismic first-motions due to having too steep of a fault in the north (the high sensitivity to very shallow dislocation
limits the resolution of deeper geometry). Dismissing the value of seismic data arbitrarily is an unfortunate limitation. This
paper also infers supershear in the north purely on the basis of a poorly resolved initial stress distribution (both geometry
and very simple stress regime, ignoring the historical faulting) for the dynamic model, with only the southern portion having
direct video and seismic observations, whereas other studies that have been published (e.g., Ye et al, 2025; Xu et al., 2025;
Wei et al, 2025; Goldberg et al., 2025) all exploit both teleseismic, strong motion, and geodetic observations to resolve the
rupture kinematics directly. 
The paper remains acceptable in my opinion, but I cannot muster great enthusiasm for its
purported results when there is a clear path toward obtaining better resolution. 
Wei et al. (2025) compare their limited
resolution geodetic inversion with the high resolution inversions from multiple point-source and finite-fault inversions of joint
data, demonstrating the gains that can be had.
