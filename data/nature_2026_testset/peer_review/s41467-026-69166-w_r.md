Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
I have carefully read the manuscript submitted by Amrutha et al. 
Assuming a virialized distribution of line-emitting "clouds" within the 
broad emission line regions of AGN, this authors investigate the 
repeatability of single-epoch (SE) supermassive black hole (SMBH) mass 
estimates in a new and volume-complete AGN sample (6-degree Field Galaxy 
Survey, 6dFGS). 324 AGN with z < 0.1 were observed at least twice with 
a mean time separation of ~20 years to measure changes in the broad 
Hbeta and 5100A continuum luminosities as well as that in the broad 
Hbeta full-width-half-maximum (FWHM). The broad Hbeta to the narrow 
emission line luminosity ratio (assuming a constant value in the latter) 
was also measured. 
Two additional interesting features brought into this paper's analysis 
are that (1) optical spectral measurements of the type 1.5 AGN NGC 5548 
spanning nearly 40 years were similarly analyzed, and (2) SE SMBH mass 
estimates were determined from optical spectra (acquired on the same 
spectrograph) of 13 AGN that have appeared in the reverberation-mapping 
literature with mass estimates. 
The overall goal is to determine which measurable AGN luminosity 
quantity is the most stable / robust estimator of the characteristic 
size R(BLR) (as associated with the Hbeta broad emission line), which 
then appears within the virial SMBH mass estimation equation. Such 
would hopefully minimize the systematic scatter in our SMBH mass 
estimates. The authors' analyses find that L([OIII}) is a more 
stable/robust estimator of the characteristic size of R(BLR), and 
suggest a few reasons as to why, relative to L(5100) and L(Hbeta). 
With a unique approach, this is an important contribution to the 
AGN field pertaining to estimating the masses of SMBH, particularly 
as associated with single-epoch estimates (in contrast to those 
arising from reverberation-mapping observing campaigns). 
I find 
the general methodology and analyses to be sufficiently explained 
and sound. 
However, 
before I can recommend this work for publication, is one 
issue of significance that I would like to see addressed (part A) 
There are also a few general predictions of photoionization 
models of BLRs that I hope the authors' would find helpful in their 
interpretations of their findings (part B). I also have some comments, 
suggestions and requests related to improvements in clarity in

B) Issues pertaining to photoionization models and reverberation 
within the BLR 
1) A measured broad emission line (Hbeta) lag (c*tau) relative to 
the 5100A continuum flux variations is *not* a simple measure (or 
even a constant scale factor) of some physical, characteristic size 
of the line-emitting region -- such as one that might be plugged 
into: 
f*v^{2}*R = G*M_{SMBH}. 
This topic arises within the text on pages 1, 2 of the manuscript. 
In addition to the fact that surfaces of constant time delay cut 
through different geometries differently (for a fixed line of sight): 
The cross-correlation function between emission line and continuum 
light curves has long been known to be equivalent to the convolution 
of the transfer function with the continuum (driver) auto-correlation 
function. Thus the broad emission line's measured amplitude of response 
and lag depend on the *temporal / amplitude characteristics of the 
driving continuum light curve* within the observing window of an 
observational campaign -- in addition to the radially-distributed 
responsivity and responsivity-weighted radius of that particular 
emission line within a given BLR geometry. 
(The above is analogous to driving a mechanical system consisting 
of 1 or more natural vibrational frequencies.) 
A continuum driver whose flux variations are temporally short 
relative to an emission line's responsivity-weighted radius (and 
associated light travel time given a particular geometry) will

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

produce emission line variations of lower amplitude and shorter 
delays (relative to that expected from the transfer function 
centroid or approximately the responsivity-weighted radius, 
for example). See, for example, the discussion surrounding 
Figure 9 in Goad & Korista (2014) 
https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.444...43G/abstract 
and the noted references related to this topic therein. 
Summarizing.... 
A particular broad emission line emanating from a BLR with fixed 
boundaries and geometry and "average" responsivity will reverberate 
with differing amplitudes and lags depending on the temporal/amplitude 
nature of the driving continuum (of the same average luminosity) 
spanned within the observational campaign's duration. In general 
a measured lag tau does not map simply to some physical size R/c. 
---> This certainly must contribute to scatter in the Hbeta lag-luminosity 
relationship, as well as to the "size inertia" discussed in this paper 
and is also related to Figure 6. 
2) The "breathing" of a broad emission line's characteristic size R 
with variations of an AGN's continuum luminosity, R ~ L^{beta}, is 
related to the emission line's radial responsivity distribution in the 
BLR: eta(r) = dlog(F_line)(r) / dlog(Phi_H), 
where F_line is the local radial (r) emission line flux and Phi_H is 
the flux of ionizing photons. First, as noted in O'Brien et al. (1995) 
and Korista & Goad (2004) (see references, below), "breathing" occurs 
if the emission line's responsivity varies radially within the BLR 
(a constant responsivity in space and wrt source luminosity level would 
result in no breathing). Second, and more importantly, the expectation 
that a physical radius (e.g., the responsivity-weighted radius) associated 
with the size of the region emitting a particular emission line scales as 
the square-root of the ionizing source luminosity (aka "breathing") is 
expected ONLY for emission lines with responsivities near unity (1) 
(linearly responding) over all but the inner-most regions of the BLR. 
This would include the HeII recombination lines, as well as the UV 
intercombination lines, such as C III] 1909. 
But emission lines with less than linear responsivities, such as the 
Hydrogen Balmer emission lines will "breath" more shallowly, in that 
the responsivity-weighted radius will grow more slowly than the 
square-root of the magnitude of the source's ionizing photon luminosity 
Q(H). 
(This dependence of breathing amplitude on the emission line's 
responsivity can be understood with the following example -- 
an emission line with a very small responsivity, e.g., MgII 2800, will 
not only vary with a small amplitude in flux, but is also not expected 
to undergo significant breathing, either. In an unrealistic limit of 
zero emission line responsivity, nothing about the emission line 
changes due to changes in the source ionizing photon luminosity.) 
Two implications to the present paper: 
(i) Wang et al. (2020) and the present work assume erroneously that 
"standard" breathing necessarily means R ~ sqrt(L) within an individual 
AGN, and thus the resulting "standard" virial line width W should scale 
as: W ~ L^{-0.25}. 
{By the way, as originally defined in the literature (see references below), 
breathing describes the behavior of a characteristic size of a line-emitting 
region as the source ionizing luminosity changes, R ~ L^{beta}. What the 
width of a broad emission line does depends at minimum on the particular 
velocity field of the line-emitting gas, which may or may not be 
virial-dominated. I do not understand why Wang et al. (2020) and possibly 
others have associated breathing with W and L.} 
(ii) Additionally, the UV continuum, energetically closer to the ionizing 
Lyman continuum responsible for most of Q(H), is known to vary with a 
greater amplitude than the optical 5100A continuum. 
In the well-studied NGC 5548:

https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015MNRAS.453.3662G/abstract

In any case it is not surprising that broad Hbeta shows a significantly 
shallower breathing amplitude in log W vs. log L (Figure 3) than

https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ApJ...365L...5N/abstract .

O'Brien et al. (1995) 
https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995MNRAS.275.1125O/abstract

The term "breathing" in this context may have been introduced to 
the literature in Korista & Goad (2004) 
https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2004ApJ...606..749K/abstract 
which explores the responsivities of the broad H, He optical emission 
lines, and the relationship of an emission line's responsivity to 
breathing and an emission line's intrinsic Baldwin Effect. 
The intrinsic Baldwin Effect in an emission line merely requires 
that an emission line's responsivity is non-linear with respect to 
the driving continuum variations. 
"Breathing" requires that the responsivity varies radially within the 
BLR and thus in time as the source of ionizing photons changes luminosity

Referee Report 
Title: Correcting Black Hole Masses Biased by Size Inertia in Broad Line Regions 
Recommendation: Reject 
Summary 
This manuscript presents a study based on a two-epoch spectroscopic analysis of a sample of low-redshift AGNs, with the
goal of improving single-epoch black hole mass estimates. The approach centers on using [O III] luminosity as a proxy for
the ionizing luminosity, under the assumption that it provides a more stable indicator of the BLR radius over time. 
The topic is timely and relevant, as improving the reliability of mass estimators is crucial for understanding AGN
demographics and evolution. However, there are serious concerns about both the methodology and the physical
interpretation adopted by the authors. The manuscript does not, in its current form, provide sufficient justification for its core
assumptions and does not meet the standard for publication in Nature Communications. 
Major Comments 
Use of FWHM and Line Width Assumptions 
The most fundamental issue is the use of FWHM as a velocity width indicator in the virial mass formula, and the assumption
that black hole mass scales with the square of FWHM. This is particularly problematic in the context of a study on variability,
where FWHM responds differently than the line dispersion (sigma_line) to continuum flux changes. Since line width enters
the mass estimate as a power-law (typically squared), a poorly behaved velocity measure like FWHM introduces significant
uncertainty. The authors do not explore how FWHM compares to sigma_line under varying luminosity, nor do they justify
their choice. Simply assuming FWHM is an appropriate measure in this context is not acceptable. 
Use of [O III] as a Proxy for Ionizing Flux 
The use of [O III] flux as a proxy for the ionizing flux also raises concerns. While the authors mention potential advantages,
they do not consider significant drawbacks. First, [O III] luminosity reflects the time-averaged state of the continuum over
years to decades, due to both light-travel and recombination timescales. In contrast, H-beta responds on reverberation
timescales, which are much shorter and more relevant for BLR dynamics. Second, [O III] is produced by much higher energy
photons than those driving Balmer line emission. Empirically, the study by Greene et al. (2010) shows that the BLR radius

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 8 -->

scales more tightly with H-beta luminosity than with [O III], and with less scatter. The authors have not provided any evidence
that [O III] is a better or even comparable proxy to H-beta. 
Conceptual Clarifications 
In the introduction, the manuscript refers to virial breathing as an “assumption,” when it is actually a predicted consequence
of the standard reverberation mapping interpretation under virial dynamics. 
The statement that the variable part of the line may have a different velocity distribution than the mean line is technically
correct, but empirical studies show that line widths in mean and variable spectra are highly correlated. 
-------------- 
Presentation of NGC 5548 and Figures 
The analysis of NGC 5548 is reduced to a single data point in Figure 1, despite the long history of monitoring and variability.
It would be more informative to show a range or distribution. 
Figure 2 shows changes in H-beta to [O III] ratios over a 20-year interval. The figure is difficult to interpret quantitatively as
the x-axis is not clearly defined. Moreover, a change of 0.26 dex in flux over two decades is not surprising and likely
common among AGNs. 
On [O III] Variability Timescales 
The claim that [O III] luminosity remains constant over human timescales is incorrect. Long-term monitoring has shown that
[O III] can vary. The more accurate assumption in prior work is that [O III] remains approximately constant over BLR
reverberation timescales, not over decades. 
Terminology and References 
Terminology should follow standard spectroscopic conventions. For example, the line should be referred to as [O III]
lambda5007, not lambda 5007 [O III]. 
Table 2 relies on outdated virial product estimates. More recent results are available in Dalla Bonta et al. (2020) for most
objects, as well as in Bentz et al. (2023, ApJ, 944, 29) for IC 4329A, Du et al. (2015, ApJ, 806, 22) for Mrk 1044, and Bentz et
al. (2021, ApJ, 906, 50) for NGC 3783. 
Conclusion 
The manuscript does not provide a convincing justification for its methodological choices, particularly the use of FWHM and
[O III] luminosity as primary inputs to the mass scaling relation. The results do not offer clear new insight into the physics of
AGN mass estimation and do not meet the threshold of novelty or rigor expected for this journal.

(Remarks to the Author) 
We have read “Correcting Black Hole Masses Biased by Size Inertia in Broad Line Regions” by Amrutha et al. This
manuscript reports analysis of spectral data of active galactic nuclei (AGN) that had previously been observed roughly two
decades earlier. The authors study the joint variability in the width of the H-beta line and the bolometric luminosity (as
measured by 5100 Angstrom continuum measurement and others). Based on their analysis the authors find that the joint
variability is not well described by the prevailing model (the “breathing” broad line region [BLR] model). This, in particular,
has implications for mass estimations of the AGN black holes. The authors then advocate for use of the [O III] luminosity
instead of other luminosity indicators (5100 continuum, H-beta, or combinations with Fe line complex luminosities). 
We feel that the potential result is interesting, but there are a number of concerns that prevent us from being able to
determine whether the conclusions are sound. The authors will need to address our major concerns before we can
recommend publication. 
*** MAJOR CONCERNS *** 
A key concern is the experimental setup. The authors are comparing 6dFGS spectra taken on Schmidt with WiFeS spectra
taken on the ANU 2.3-m telescope. Small systematic differences in the different data sets (even though they were reduced
and fitted with the same procedure) could lead to the appearance of a problem in the model when it is in the data. Is it
possible to directly compare the instrumental setups for consistency? While the authors have the NGC 5548 data that should
be self-consistent, the trend within NGC 5548 is far less convincing. 
Related to the above, measurement uncertainties are not addressed in the manuscript at all. It is essential to understand
how measurement uncertainties are incorporated in the analysis. This is especially important when some of the effects that
are being reported are slight (e.g., the difference between 5100 and [O III] scatter in mass measurements is only 0.035 dex).
The correlations of uncertainties between continuum and line widths is a particularly problematic aspect that needs to be
addressed. The fact that the Baldwin effect was not mentioned nor any attempt to mitigate it is particularly worrisome. It is not
possible to publish this work without a thorough examination of how this effect plays a role in the analysis. 
A key test of whether what the authors see is real would be to simulate observations with realistic correlated uncertainties

that vary according to the R–L relation with an intrinsic scatter. How often does the simulated Delta FWHM vs Delta L
correlation show the same trend (or more extreme) than the data do?

Title: Correcting Black Hole Masses Biased by Size Inertia in Broad Line Regions
Recommendation: Reject
Summary
This manuscript presents a study based on a two-epoch spectroscopic analysis of a sample of low-redshift
AGNs, with the goal of improving single-epoch black hole mass estimates. The approach centers on using
[O III] luminosity as a proxy for the ionizing luminosity, under the assumption that it provides a more
stable indicator of the BLR radius over time.
The topic is timely and relevant, as improving the reliability of mass estimators is crucial for understanding
AGN demographics and evolution. However, there are serious concerns about both the methodology and
the physical interpretation adopted by the authors. The manuscript does not, in its current form, provide
sufficient justification for its core assumptions and does not meet the standard for publication in Nature
Communications.
Major Comments
Use of FWHM and Line Width Assumptions
The most fundamental issue is the use of FWHM as a velocity width indicator in the virial mass formula,
and the assumption that black hole mass scales with the square of FWHM. This is particularly problematic
in the context of a study on variability, where FWHM responds differently than the line dispersion
(sigma_line) to continuum flux changes. Since line width enters the mass estimate as a power-law
(typically squared), a poorly behaved velocity measure like FWHM introduces significant uncertainty.
The authors do not explore how FWHM compares to sigma_line under varying luminosity, nor do they
justify their choice. Simply assuming FWHM is an appropriate measure in this context is not acceptable.
In the sub-section of methods pertaining to SE mass estimates, we have added a paragraph justifying the
use the FWHM as a line width in our SE estimates. The FWHM is the most commonly used velocity
width indicator for SE mass estimates, although the σline,rms is used for RM mass estimates. Work by
Dalla Bont` a et al. (2020) compares the two, and finds that although σline works better at estimating
masses, using FWHM is not unreasonable. For comparison, we present a version of Figure 3 in the
manuscript below, but using σline instead, and our results remain largely the same. We note that the
goal of this work is to examine how SE mass estimates depend on variability, and to present evidence for
the resulting mass differences in observations separated by 20 years. Since FWHM is the most widely
adopted line-width measure in existing SE mass prescriptions, we use FWHM consistently in our estimates
to ensure direct comparability with standard practice.
Use of [O III] as a Proxy for Ionizing Flux
The use of [O III] flux as a proxy for the ionizing flux also raises concerns. While the authors mention
potential advantages, they do not consider significant drawbacks. First, [O III] luminosity reflects the
time-averaged state of the continuum over years to decades, due to both light-travel and recombination
timescales. In contrast, H-beta responds on reverberation timescales, which are much shorter and more
relevant for BLR dynamics. Second, [O III] is produced by much higher energy photons than those
driving Balmer line emission. Empirically, the study by Greene et al. (2010) shows that the BLR radius
scales more tightly with H-beta luminosity than with [O III], and with less scatter. The authors have not
provided any evidence that [O III] is a better or even comparable proxy to H-beta.
