Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript details the very exciting confirmation of a lava tube entrance on Venus. Over the last few decades, orbital
data has strongly suggested (or even confirmed) the existence and nature of lava tubes on the moon and Mars, and this new
confirmation for Venus just goes to show that these are ubiquitous features of planetary surfaces with histories of basaltic
volcanism. It uses an approach developed and previously applied by the authors for characterizing lunar lava tube entrances
using radar, and includes a comparison of radar data for a terrestrial analog lava tube, and the manuscript convincingly rules
out other potential features. As such it is worthy of publication. 
However, there is definitely room for improvement. My main criticism of this manuscript is its repeated exaggeration- of the
novelty of this work (e.g. there being no confirmed Martian detections) and its importance (e.g. relevance to future robotic
missions, access to layered deposits), however this could be remedied by more measured language, better referencing, and
more realistic (but still important!) implications. The core of the manuscript is strong enough to support publication without
the hyperbole. 
Here are some specific recommendations: 
The manuscript misses out on some early studies of crater chains (and prospective lava tubes) from the early post-Magellan
mission days. Citations of those works would help show how long this question has been out there, and that this work is
using a novel SAR interpretation approach (and new analog comparisons) to answer a question where only speculation
existed before. 
This one is only a thesis but has extensive coverage of the topic: 
Melville, Graeme P (1994). Lava tubes and channels of the Earth, Venus, Moon and Mars. University of Wollongong. Thesis.
https://hdl.handle.net/10779/uow.27649461.v1 
This one is shorter but mentions Venus lava tubes (co-authored by the author of the thesis listed above): 
Publications of the Astronomical Society of Australia, Volume 10, Issue 3, 1993, pp. 200 – 202. DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1017/S1323358000025662 
The introduction says that “…from optical images (e.g., by photoclinometry) it is very difficult to assess whether a
subterranean cave extends from the skylight.” I will agree that this is true, but it also undersells some of the quality work that
has been done trying to do just that- e.g. in Haruyama et al. (2009), a paper you’re already citing elsewhere. You make the
stronger case for your work in the next sentence (e.g. you couldn’t use imagery for Venus anyways). 
A sentence later in the Intro, you say that “Magellan is a spaceborne side-looking SAR…” Do you mean that Magellan HAD
a spaceborne side-looking SAR? Or more precisely that it had a radar system (the RDRS) that could operate in a SAR
mode? 
Page 7, last sentence of the first paragraph states “These deposits tend to increase the radar backscattering uniformly
around the feature” in reference to impact craters. The reference cited relates to radar imaging of craters on the Moon- could
you cite a Venus-specific source? Venus craters have much different ejecta patterns than lunar ones, often involving more
impact melt, and not as “rough” in radar as lunar craters (and can even be brighter than their surroundings in radar images).

See https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2023/pdf/1052.pdf and other refs regarding Venus ejecta. 
Page 7, second paragraph, second sentence: “Given that there are no confirmed detections of intact lava tubes for the
Martian case…” Are you sure you want to say this? Do you mean confirmed using the SAR method? I’m sure there are
members of the HiRISE team (MRO) who would contend that their lava tube entrances on Alba and Arsia Mons are
“confirmed.” Sauro et al. (2020), which you are already citing, discusses this. With no other discussion in this manuscript
about what you mean by “confirmed,” this statement is unsupported. 
Same paragraph, in reference to the critical factors influencing lava tube stability- the reference cited is great but makes no
mention of Venus. You should make it clearer that this reference does not mention Venus (or Venus-specific conditions). I
don’t know but I’d be curious if factors such as high atmospheric pressure, or prolonged cooling times (given high surface
temperatures) could affect their stability, or the tensional strength of the lava material. As you’ve just stated ahead of this
sentence, there is currently no info about the structural stability of lava tubes on Venus. While it is clearly beyond the scope
of this paper to model this, it might be good to acknowledge that Venus surface conditions could potentially influence this
(and perhaps that further work is needed to assess whether the general conditions for lava tube stability developed in the
paper cited can be applied to Venus). 
Final paragraph, page 8. Some of the applications seem wildly fanciful to me and should be scaled back. That this skylight
“could also be of great interest as targets for a possible lander robotic mission” with a citation to a DAVINCI mission paper is
way off- that is not a robotic mission and will at best only return data for minutes if it as a soft landing, and it already has a
target area. The next sentence talks about “preserved layers within the conduit walls could serve as a detailed record of the
planet’s volcanic history, enabling the reconstruction of geological timelines.” I’ve spent many weeks in lava tubes on
multiple continents (and islands) and I have never seen layered deposits, except for maybe one flow covering another flow
or two (but likely separated in time by years at most). It’s just not the kind of environment that preserves a long record. 
I’ve made multiple minor corrections and suggestions for more concise language, for example replacing “in the proximity of”
with “near” throughout. I’m attaching an annotated PDF with my suggestions. 
Good luck! 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present an argument that a geologic feature on the western flank of Nyx Mons, Venus, is a lava tube. This would
be a new identification of a lava tube on Venus. Lava tubes have been discussed briefly in the Venus volcanology literature
but little to no evidence has been presented identifying one. The primary evidence that the feature is a lava tube is the
unique radar return pattern and a qualitative comparison to a terrestrial example at Lanzarote, Spain. The authors do an
excellent job using radar remote sensing to present an argument that the feature on Venus is a lava tube. However, the
manuscript is lacking in several essential areas that I explain below. Identifying a lava tube on Venus is an exciting
possibility, however as this manuscript is currently written I cannot recommend it be published. The majority of my comments
can be addressed with text edits. I recommend major revisions and look forward to seeing the revised manuscript. -Ian T.W.
Flynn 
Major Comments: 
• A general major comment is that the authors rely on the planetary cave literature rather than the planetary volcanology
literature. This by itself would not be an issue, but the authors do not include accurate information about lava tube formation
and similar volcanology processes. I make several line edits and other major comments related to this issue. As the
manuscript focuses on the identification of a lava tube, a volcanic feature, I encourage the authors to revisit some of their
citations to make sure they are appropriate. 
• The authors do not cover the full spectrum of volcanology processes that could form a lava tube. I made a note of this in the
introduction (see the attached PDF) and below are some references that could be used to help the authors expand the
introduction. Only a few additional sentences would be needed to explain the other lava tube formation processes in the
introduction. As written, the authors give the impression lava tubes can only form under one mechanism, and this is not
correct. 
o Peterson, D. W., Holcomb, R. T., Tilling, R. I., & Christiansen, R. L. (1994). Development of lava tubes in the light of
observations at Mauna Ulu, Kilauea Volcano, Hawaii. Bulletin of Volcanology, 56, 343-360. 
o Valerio, A., Tallarico, A., & Dragoni, M. (2008). Mechanisms of formation of lava tubes. Journal of Geophysical Research:
Solid Earth, 113(B8). 
o Calvari, S., & Pinkerton, H. (1999). Lava tube morphology on Etna and evidence for lava flow emplacement mechanisms.
Journal of Volcanology and Geothermal Research, 90(3-4), 263-280. 
• Similar to the above comments, the authors should add some discussion either in the introduction or discussion as to if the
formation of lava tubes on Venus is expected. Given the extreme environmental conditions on Venus, it is unclear on how
lava tubes will form on the planet. Head and Wilson, (1988) briefly describe that lava tube formation on Venus should be
more common than on Earth. Flynn et al., (2023) discusses how the reduced gravity on Venus should promote the increased
formation of crust cover, which could promote the formation of lava tubes. There needs to be some discussion in the
manuscript about whether the formation of lava tubes is expected or could occur on Venus.

• The main text of the manuscript mentions the “unique radar backscatter” associated with lava tube skylights. This is the
primary result used in the manuscript to indicate the Venusian feature is a lava tube. However, the specific radar return
pattern is not described in quantitative detail in the main text. There is a minor description of the unique radar return pattern
in a figure caption, but the supposed diagnostic radar return pattern for a lava tube is not described in the main text. The
“unique radar backscatter” used to identify a Venusian lava tube needs to be described in the main text in detail so that
readers can evaluate the identification criteria. At present this description lacks significant detail and needs to be greatly
expanded. 
Minor Comments: 
• All my line edits are made within the PDF. I made comments over specific locations where sentences needed revisions.
Text with lines through it indicate it should be removed or substantially rephrased. 
• Line numbers in a manuscript are standard formatting practice for Nature articles and most journals. It was frustrating to not
have them and made making specific comments more difficult. Please make sure to include line numbers in the future. 
• A rille is not a lava tube. A rille is believed to form through a primarily deconstructive process (thermos- or mechanical-
erosion of the substrate during a high-volume effusive eruption). Lava tubes are primarily a constructive process. Please
update this detail throughout the manuscript. 
• I agree with the authors that the feature may be a lava tube. Simila features are observed at Api Mons, Uti Hiata Mons, and
Kali Mons. It would be useful in the discussion section or a conclusion paragraph to highlight the possibility of identifying
lava tubes elsewhere on Venus. 
• The manuscript switches between refereeing to the feature as a cave and skylight. This is confusing at times, as caves can
have different formation processes and geologic implications. Please use consistent terminology throughout the manuscript.
Since you are focused on volcanic features I suggest using volcanology terms.

literature to suggest that graben-fissure fracture systems (with

This manuscript details the very exciting confirmation of a lava tube entrance on Venus. Over 
the last few decades, orbital data has strongly suggested (or even confirmed) the existence 
and nature of lava tubes on the moon and Mars, and this new confirmation for Venus just goes 
to show that these are ubiquitous features of planetary surfaces with histories of basaltic 
volcanism. It uses an approach developed and previously applied by the authors for 
characterizing lunar lava tube entrances using radar, and includes a comparison of radar data 
for a terrestrial analog lava tube, and the manuscript convincingly rules out other potential 
features. As such it is worthy of publication. 
However, there is definitely room for improvement. My main criticism of this manuscript is its 
repeated exaggeration- of the novelty of this work (e.g. there being no confirmed Martian 
detections) and its importance (e.g. relevance to future robotic missio ns, access to layered 
deposits), however this could be remedied by more measured language, better referencing, 
and more realistic (but still important!) implications. The core of the manuscript is strong 
enough to support publication without the hyperbole.

Discussion on the Layover effect: 
The methodology is independent of surface layover effects  especially with the modest 
topography present in the study area, which is notably smooth (see Supplementary Fig. 1c) . 
Indeed, it explicitly accounts for the layover of the tube interior when computing its depth. 
Variations in surface elevation do not appreciably affect the results  as surface roughness 
does, as the measurement specifically targets the interior of the tube (analyzing the echo 
returns from those specific features) rather than the surface above it [Ref -4], [Ref-5], [Ref-6], 
[Ref-7]. To make this explicit, we added the following statement to the discussion section: 
“Previous studies have demonstrated that the methodology is not adversely affected by 
surface layover for small to moderate topographic variations, such as those present in this 
case” (refs. 14, 21, 24, 33, see Supplementary Fig. 1c).
