Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors developed chiral copper(I)-catalyzed enantioselective radical N-alkylation of hydrazines with racemic 2-
chlorocaroxamides to give enantioriched hydrazines. The strategy is new, results are excellent, and products are highly
useful. Therefore theses results may be acceptable as a paper in Nat. Commun. However the following revisions are
requested before further consideration. 
(1) According to the experiments with BHT and TEMPO shown in Fig. 6a, racemic products 43 and 44 were produced
together with enantioriched hydrazines 1. Therefore, enantioselective N-alkylation of hydrazines may proceed through SN2
or SN1 pathway via intermediate III. If the authors propose such a mechanism shown in Fig. 6c, “radical N-alkylation” in the
title is not suitable. If this is “radical N-alkylation”, products 43 and 45 should also be enantioriched. 
(2) Please show a plausible mechanism about asymmetric induction. 
(3) If any experimental evidence of intermediate III is shown, the proposed mechanism is more reliable. 
(4) Fig. 1b: As examples of the asymmetric nucleophilic addition to hydrazones, refs. 23–29 are cited. The following paper
should be added as a more recent example. K. Nishimura, Y. Ogura, K. Takeda, W. Guo, K. Ishihara Org. Lett. 2022, 24,
7685–7689. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Liu, Chen, and co-workers describes an enantioconvergent radical N-alkylation of racemic α-haloamides
with hydrazine derivatives. This strategy broadens the synthetic toolbox for accessing enantioenriched chiral hydrazines,
with the key to success attributed to the careful choice of a suitable hydrazine nucleophile. The authors further demonstrate
the synthetic utility of this method through the enantioselective synthesis of aza-heterocycles as well as the stereodivergent
synthesis of N-amino dipeptides. Overall, I recommend publication of this work after the following issues are addressed: 
1) Can this strategy be extended to α-tertiary haloamides? 
2) The substrate scope appears somewhat limited. For instance, it would be informative to examine more sterically hindered
substituents at the ortho position of the α-phenyl ring, such as alkyl or aryl groups. In addition, only one example (compound
18) bearing an α-alkyl substituent was investigated, and the obtained 86% ee is a promising result. It would strengthen the
work to include additional examples featuring longer or bulkier alkyl groups. 
3) With respect to the scope of hydrazine derivatives, how do N-Et-N-Boc hydrazine or N-iPr-N-Boc hydrazine perform under
the reaction conditions? 
4) Since the authors propose a radical–polar crossover process, a Hammett analysis would be highly desirable to support
the mechanistic proposal.

Comment 1: The authors developed chiral copper(I)-catalyzed enantioselective 
radical N-alkylation of hydrazines with racemic 2-chlorocaroxamides to give 
enantioriched hydrazines. The strategy is new, results are excellent, and products are

Our response: We greatly thank the reviewer for the positive comments on our work. 
We also thank the reviewer for their reconsideration of its publication in Nat. Commun.
following appropriate revisions. We have made great efforts in carrying out additional 
studies to fully address all the concerns of the reviewer. Accordingly, we have carefully 
revised the manuscript according to the reviewer’s suggestions.
Comment 2: According to the experiments with BHT and TEMPO shown in Fig. 6a, 
racemic products 43 and 44 were produced together with enantioriched hydrazines 1. 
Therefore, enantioselective N-alkylation of hydrazines may proceed through SN2 or 
SN1 pathway via intermediate III. If the authors propose such a mechanism shown in 
Fig. 6c, “radical N-alkylation” in the title is not suitable. If this is “radical N-
alkylation”, products 43 and 45 should also be enantioriched.
Our response: We greatly thank the reviewer for bringing this issue to our attention. 
We agree with the reviewer that our current data does not fully support the original title 
and feel very sorry for it. Accordingly, we have rephrased the manuscript title to 
“Copper-catalysed enantioconvergent N-alkylation of hydrazines with racemic α-
haloamides to access enantioenriched hydrazines” to describe the strategy more 
accuracy. In addition, we have rephrased the term “radical N-alkylation” with “N-
alkylation” in many places of the revised manuscript.
Regarding the radical trapping experiments, we have supplemented the determination 
of enantiomeric excess (e.e.) values for the BHT-trapped product 60 (originally labeled 
as 43) and TEMPO-trapped product S10 (originally labeled as 44). Notably, these 
results reveal that compound 60 is obtained as a racemic mixture (0% e.e), whereas 
compound S10 exhibits a moderate e.e. value. To elucidate the origin of this 
stereochemical discrepancy, we have performed in-depth mechanistic analysis 
combined with relevant literature precedents (e.g., Nat. Commun. 16, 4922 (2025); Nat. 
Commun. 15, 7313 (2024); Nat. Catal. 7, 1372 (2024); Nature 618, 294 (2023).). For 
product 60, we propose that the addition of the generated radical II to BHT proceeds 
via a non-stereoselective radical addition pathway, which accounts for the formation of

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 5 -->

racemic 60 (Fig. 1 in this letter). In contrast, the moderate e.e. value observed for 
product S10 can be rationalized by two plausible reaction pathways: (1) a radical-
radical coupling between the intermediate carbon-centered radical and TEMPO, which 
proceeds without chiral induction ; and (2)  the outer-sphere nucleophilic attack of the 
TEMPO− species on the Cu III intermediate III—analogous to the stereocontrolled 
formation of enantioenriched hydrazines—thereby further supporting our proposed 
outer-sphere nucleophilic attack mechanism (Fig. 2 in this letter). Collectively, the 
coexistence of these two pathways explains the moderate e.e. value of product S10.
Accordingly, we have retained the BHT trapping experiments in the revised manuscript, 
while the TEMPO trapping experiments (for which two plausible pathways exist) have 
been included in the Supporting Information with corresponding clarifications. Thanks 
again for your valuable suggestions.

Comment 3: Please show a plausible mechanism about asymmetric induction.
Our response:  We greatly appreciate the reviewer’s constructive comment on

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 6 -->

clarifying the details of asymmetric induction. Building upon the mechanistic 
framework for the key enantiodetermining C(sp3)−N bond formation established in our 
previous work (Nature 618, 294 (2023)), we propose two transition states ( re-TS and 
si-TS) that correspond to the formation of the two product enantiomers, respectively 
(Fig. 3 in this letter). We identified that the steric clash between the ligand sulfonyl 
group and the relatively bulky R 1group in si-TS would be expected to render this 
transition state energetically disfavored. In contrast, the energetically favorable re-TS
thus favors the formation of the desired products with the S absolute configuration, 
which is consistent with the experimental results. Accordingly, this discussion has been 
incorporated into the revised manuscript. 
Fig. 3 | Proposed transition states for asymmetric induction.
Comment 4: If any experimental evidence of intermediate III is shown, the proposed 
mechanism is more reliable.
Our response:  We greatly appreciate the reviewer for bringing this issue to our 
attention. The proposed direct outer-sphere attack by hydrazine avoids the 
thermodynamically unfavorable direct deprotonation of hydrazine by mild bases, which 
is likely a key factor contributing to the success of our current N-alkylation reaction. 
Furthermore, the stereocontrolled formation of product 44 provides additional support 
for our proposed outer-sphere nucleophilic mechanism on the CuIII intermediate III (Fig. 
2 in this letter). 
As suggested by Reviewer 2, we have also conducted Hammett analysis by using para-
substituted phenylhydrazines with varied electronic properties in conjunction with α-
haloamide E1. We found a negative linear relationship between the log(kAr/kPh) and the 
substituent constants σp of the phenylhydrazines (Fig. 4 in this letter). These findings 
are consistent with a transition state bearing partial positive charge in the rate-
determining step, which presumably suggest the generation of the key electrophilic 
CuIII intermediate. Accordingly, we have added all these results to the revised 
manuscript.

Comment 2: Can this strategy be extended to α-tertiary haloamides?
Our response: We greatly appreciate the reviewer’s valuable suggestion regarding the 
extension of this strategy to α-tertiary haloamides. Accordingly, we have first 
investigated the enantioconvergent N-alkylation of N,N-bis-Boc hydrazine N7 with 
racemic α-tertiary haloamide E50 using our previously reported anionic N,N,N-ligands 
(L*11–L*14) (Fig. 5 in this letter). These initial trials afforded the desired product S1
in low to moderate yields and low enantiomeric excess (e.e.). To further optimize the 
outcome, we evaluated the enantioconvergent N-alkylation of N7 with racemic α-
tertiary haloamide E51 under the same ligand screening conditions. For ligands L*13
and L*14, modest improvements in both yield and e.e. were observed, though these 
results remained unsatisfactory. In a further effort to address this challenge, we explored 
the enantioconvergent N-alkylation of N,N-bis-Ph hydrazine N8 with racemic α-tertiary 
haloamide E50 and E51 using ligands L*11–L*14 (Fig. 5). These reactions afforded 
moderate yields but persistently low e.e. values, presumably arising from the strong 
nucleophilicity of N8, which promotes non-selective background reactions. We are 
actively continuing to explore this promising direction and anticipate reporting our 
progress in due course.

Comment 5: Since the authors propose a radical–polar crossover process, a Hammett

the polarity requirements in the C−N bond forming process, we have conducted 
Hammett analysis by using para-substituted phenylhydrazines with varied electronic

of the key electrophilic CuIII intermediate. Accordingly, we have added all these results 
to the revised manuscript.
