Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors analyzed the global change in synchronous spatially dry and hot (DH) events and their impacts on gross
primary production (GPP). This topic is potentially interesting, as concurrent climate extremes can induce simultaneous
production shocks across regions, disrupting the stability of food production. However, I have several concerns that should
be addressed to strengthen the study: 
Major comments: 
(1) The authors define DH events using two criteria: SPI < -1 and Tmax > the 90th percentile (based on weekly and grid-
level values). While this approach is reasonable, the results could be sensitive to the selection of thresholds. To enhance
the robustness of the findings, I recommend to test alternative definitions or thresholds for DH events. 
(2) A regional DH event is defined as a week when at least 5% of a region experiences a DH event. However, a 5%
threshold seems relatively low, and it is hard to represent DH event for that region. With such a small proportion, even a
localized DH event could qualify as a regional event, potentially overstating the spatial extent of synchronicity. 
(3) 
The authors estimate GPP loss by comparing GPP during DH weeks with that during ‘normal’ weeks, normalized by the
GPP of normal weeks. However, this approach may not fully isolate the effect of DH events. Other extreme conditions, such
as excessive precipitation or flooding, can also reduce GPP, yet such weeks may be incorrectly labeled as ‘normal,’ thereby
introducing bias. Moreover, it is unclear how the authors accounted for long-term technological improvements or other non-
climatic drivers of GPP trends over time. 
(4) The authors mention the impacts of DH events on the yields of rice, maize, and wheat, but no detailed methods are
provided. This part requires elaboration. Additionally, I am not sure whether authors also account for phenological factors
when estimating the impact of DH on major crop yields. For example, maize in the Northern Hemisphere is typically planted
in spring and harvested in autumn. Using annual DH statistics may not accurately capture the crop’s exposure to DH events
during sensitive growth stages. 
Minor comments: 
(1) Overall, the manuscript requires improve writing clarity. For example, Lines 26-28 state: “These widespread events
reduce global ecosystem productivity by ~0.75%, with a 10% increase in synchronous area lowering productivity by ~1.2%
and maize and wheat yields by 10–15%.” This sentence is difficult to interpret. Consider rewriting it for better readability and
clarity. Similarly, the sentence in Lines 97-99 is also unclear or grammatically incorrect. Please check carefully other
sentences to improve the quality of manuscript. 
(2) The use of bold font for certain words (e.g., Line 298: “global”; Line 372: “DH weeks”) appears inconsistent and
unnecessary. Please revise these for consistent formatting, using normal font unless emphasis is warranted. 
(3) The GPP data used in the study spans from 2000 to 2019. However, in one example, the authors refer to using data from
42 years, stating: “For example, if the first week of June (1–7 June) at a grid cell is a DH event in the years 2010 and 2015,
but is found to be normal in 30 other years (with the remaining 12 years experiencing either drought or heatwave)...” This is
inconsistent with the available 20-year GPP dataset and requires clarification. 
(4) Line 356 contains a grammatical issue: “are” and “is” are used together incorrectly. Please revise the sentence to ensure
subject–verb agreement.

REVIEWERS' COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Thank you to the authors for responding thoughtfully to my earlier comments. I still think that characterizing the incidence of
exposure to the combination of dry and hot conditions across the globe is useful, as is attributing the increase in incidence to
warming. The authors continue to provide a useful contribution on that front. The paper could focus solely on that and be
publishable, and I would suggest narrowing the paper somewhat (if not completely) toward that contribution. 
The component of the paper focused on GPP and yield remains a bit perplexing to me. The authors present the DH->GPP
and DH->yield links as a third main contribution of the paper, after documenting changes in DH incidence and attributing
those changes in incidence to warming. I do not see a compelling argument in the paper as to why yield impacts across
space should be anything other than additive in location-specific losses. I do agree that there is a nonlinear relationship
between yield losses and food affordability, but the authors only directly investigate the DH->yield relationship. 
Consider that global yield is just an area-weighted average of location-specific yields. Let’s assume that area planted is
unaffected by DH events (that seems like a bad assumption in the long run, but I do not think that is the focus of/accounted
for in this paper). From many existing studies, we know that exposure to dry and/or hot events in a given location is often bad
for yields in that location, and that longer or more frequent exposure to such conditions exacerbates yield declines. If DH
conditions are spatially synchronized, clearly that can increase the number of affected locations, and hence the number of
terms in the area-weighted average that are affected by local DH conditions. That link is by construction, and while
practically important, not particularly interesting. Do the authors think that spatial synchrony also has some differential effect
on location-specific yields? I didn’t find the results in Table S2 to really address this — they separate affects of average area
from the number of synchronized DH events, but the number of synchronized DH events in that analysis is also increasing in
the number of weeks each location within the event is exposed to DH conditions, and we know that location-specific stress
is bad for location-specific yields. That analysis doesn’t seem convincing in terms of demonstrating more than just a scaling
up of location-specific effects with a more widespread occurrence of DH conditions. Is there any reason to suspect super-
additivity of losses across space? I still could see reasons why spatial synchrony could have a distinct effect (e.g., if it
depletes water resources shared across regions or drives up the prices of compensatory inputs), but the paper doesn't
investigate any of those mechanisms. 
In sum, I fully agree that more hot, dry weather is bad for yield, especially if it occurs during sensitive crop periods. But I don’t
find the statistical analysis of the *spatially synchronized* DH->yield link (or DH->GPP link) link to be convincing evidence
that those synchronized events do more than additively scale up what we already know about location-specific DH-yield
relationships (even though there could be reasons for nonlinear scaling). I do think that those synchronized events are
especially concerning for food security, as simultaneous losses can challenge the ability to smooth losses over time (through
exhaustion of limited storage) or space (through limits to trade), as the authors allude to in their paper. But the paper doesn’t
directly investigate relationships with prices or other indicators of food insecurity that I think would be more likely to exhibit a
nonlinear response to the spatial synchrony of DH exposure. I would suggest recasting the yield/GPP components of the
paper as a simple illustration of the scale of losses that occur under widespread DH exposure, and devoting less space to
those analyses.

My main concern about this paper is that the motivation for studying the impacts of synchronized events on GPP and yield is less clear, and some of the associations may have little to do with synchrony. I’ll express these concerns in terms of yield, but I think they apply in an analogous fashion to some of the GPP results.  From a crop yield standpoint, it’s not clear why we should be specifically concerned about DH events occurring in the same week in different locations across the globe. One possible reason might be if we think that there are times of heightened crop sensitivity (e.g., pollination) and those times are likely to be identical in different regions. In that case, a simultaneous DH event during pollination everywhere could be devastating, and far more concerning than the same number of DH events occurring at different times during the growing season in different regions. Thank you for the interesting insight. Our central motivation is that synchronization reflects a simultaneous occurrence of extremes across multiple breadbasket regions which creates fundamentally different risks compared to dispersed or staggered events. When several regions experience drought-heatwave (DH) events at the same time, the global buffering capacity, where stable yields in unaffected regions offset localized losses, is substantially weakened. Thus, synchronization is not simply a function of total area affected by DH events, but an indication of systemic exposure where simultaneous shocks undermine resilience at regional, national, and global scales. For motivation we added following text in the introduction section Lines 95-115:  “While regional extreme events can disrupt local agricultural production, their impacts are often partially absorbed through trade and redistribution. For instance, multi-year droughts cause prolonged and severe food security challenges, but losses in one region can be offset by trade from other unaffected regions. In contrast, when such extreme events occur simultaneously across multiple major crop-producing regions, particularly the global breadbaskets, even at the time scale of weeks to months, the effects are amplified and can severely stress international markets. Recent studies indicate that widespread droughts and/or heatwaves can reduce crop production by 4–10% at both global and national scales (Kornhuber et al 2023, 2020, Gaupp et al 2020, Mehrabi and Ramankutty 2019, Lesk et al 2022), underscoring the severity of spatially

synchronized hazards. Further, synchrony increases the likelihood of concurrent yield shocks in staple crops, diminishes the buffer capacity of international trade. These events not only reduce yields across multiple breadbasket regions but also amplify systemic risks by triggering heightened media attention, speculative activity, and market volatility (Bellemare 2015). Thus, they pose a dual threat, direct production losses and indirect amplification through food price dynamics, indicating their unique importance for global food security. Beyond agriculture, synchronous DH events also suppress terrestrial carbon uptake on a global scale (Zscheischler et al 2014a), thereby weakening one of the most critical ecosystem services that regulate the climate. Despite these far-reaching consequences, little is known about how synchronized DH events simultaneously affect vegetation productivity and crop yields across regions, even though their losses may exceed the sum of individual regional impacts. Quantifying these impacts is essential to capture the true scale of risk that synchronous DH events pose to both global ecosystem functioning and agricultural production.” We acknowledge that strict “same week” alignment could overstate the importance of calendar coincidence, given variations in crop phenology, planting schedules, and varietal differences across regions. To explicitly address this, we refined our methodology as follows: 1. From weekly to monthly synchronization: Instead of relying solely on weekly coincidence, we defined synchronized events at the monthly scale. This allows for small lags in crop calendars (e.g., differences of 1-4 weeks in flowering or pollination) while still capturing periods where vulnerable phases overlap across regions. This approach smooths out phenological noise while retaining the signal of broad-scale synchronization. 2. Critical growth-phase focus: Synchronization was assessed only during each region’s critical 90-day window (flowering, pollination, maturation), rather than assuming uniform crop calendars. This ensures that we measure synchronization when crops are most yield-sensitive, not merely when DH events coincide on the calendar. 3. Regional thresholding to avoid spurious overlap: A cropping region was only classified as “DH-affected” if at least 250 km² and 5% of its cropland was impacted, ensuring that synchronization reflects substantial agricultural exposure rather than small, localized anomalies. These refinements produce a robust measure of synchronization that is not overly sensitive to staggered planting or crop varietal differences. Importantly, our results remain consistent: we find a negative association between widespread synchronized DH months and crop yield anomalies during the critical growth phases across all three major crops. In summary, synchronization captures systemic risk that cannot be explained by total affected area alone. By expanding our framework from the weekly to the monthly scale, and embedding it within crop-sensitive periods, we demonstrate that synchronization is not an artifact of phenological variation but a genuine feature of agricultural risk under climate extremes. To capture the role of spatial synchronization, we concentrated on the critical phases of the cropland. First, we identified dominant crop-growing regions for each calendar month (shown in Figure S17-S19), defined as regions with at least 2,500 km² of cropland in the critical phase. The threshold of 2,500 km2 corresponds to the area of one grid cell at the equator, ensuring that only regions with substantial crop presence are considered. Second, a dominant crop growing region was classified as DH-affected if the maximum monthly affected crop area exceeded both 250 km² and 5% of its cropland extent. Third, we defined a regional DH month if at least one week within that month qualified as a regional DH week (see Section 6.3). These thresholds ensure that at least part of the cropping area is significantly impacted: the 250 km² (representing 10% of the areal threshold used to define regional cropping zones) sets the lower limit for the 5% criterion preventing very localized events from qualifying as regional DH events in crop areas, and in capturing cases where extremes occur at slightly different weeks within the same month. By combining these three conditions, we produced a monthly binary dataset indicating whether a crop-growing region experienced a DH event (1) or not (0). From this dataset, we counted the number of DH affected regions per month for the three selected crops. Months with at least five affected regions for wheat and maize, or three for rice (reflecting fewer dominant rice regions), were defined as widespread synchronized events/months. Finally, we examined the association between the annual frequency of these synchronized months and annual yield anomalies using regression and correlation analyses. This approach allowed us to distinguish whether yield losses were primarily driven by large DH-affected areas within single regions or by simultaneous impacts across multiple crop-growing regions. Furthermore, to enhance the robustness of our analysis, we fitted a generalized linear regression model between yield anomalies and synchronization, considering the total DH-area affected (Area affected) and the number of widespread synchronized events (Synchronized events) as predictors. We observed a strong interdependence between the total DH-area affected and the number of regions synchronously affected, which motivated the inclusion of an additional interaction term in the GLM. Since the predictors were not normally distributed, and to address potential multicollinearity, we standardized the predictand (i.e., yield) and predictor variables prior to model fitting to minimize bias (Marquardt, 1980).
