Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
COMMSBIO-24-7961 
“The gut metabolite indole-3-acetic acid aggravates neuropsychiatric lupus via the AHR/STAT3 pathway in microglia” 
In this study, the authors investigated the possibility that gut microbiota specific to NPSLE patients may induce activation of
microglia and neuropsychiatric symptoms through the disruption of the blood-brain barrier. By analyzing the gut microbiota of
NPSLE patients and transplanting it into mice, they elucidate the resulting intracellular signal changes in microglia, thereby
providing important insights in this field. I have several concerns regarding the interpretation of the experiments and the finer
details below. 
1. In Fig.1, the rationale for the interpretation that FMT-NPSLE causes BBB dysfunction appears somewhat weak. To better
interpret the data and clarify, I recommend adding supplementary experimental data or discussing relevant literature.
Although FMT-SLE is used as a control compared to FMT-NPSLE, more precise control of the experiment would be better.
For example, gut dysbiosis has been suggested to affect BBB permeability (e.g., PMID: 25411471 and 26923630). To what
extent does the antibiotic pretreatment, as part of FMT, influence the experimental results? Could it be interpreted that
increased BBB permeability due to pretreatment is rescued in FMT-SLE but not in FMT-NPSLE? 
2. Please spell out what "FMT" stands for in the main text. 
3. Regarding the behavior data in Fig.1, although the experiment is designed to assess neuropsychiatric symptoms, is there
no difference in locomotor activity? It would be better to have supplementary data to conclude that the results of the OFT,
NOR, and MWM tests are due to decreases in cognitive and memory functions rather than a decline in motor functions. Also,
is the BBB dysfunction observed in FMT-NPSLE limited to the hippocampus? 
4. Please provide the rationale for the two-week interval between behavioral tests and sacrifice. What is the basis for
determining this timeline? Additionally, are the changes in gut microbiota due to FMT sustained two weeks later, or have
they returned to baseline? 
5. For Fig. 2E, please clarify at which stage of the timeline the samples for the mouse data were collected. Additionally, PCA
analysis is not shown for patient data as it is for mouse data. Will patient data show similar results? In Fig. 2B, the variability
among samples appears large. If there is no significant difference between groups, does this mean that transplantation into
mice reduces this variability? 
6. Regarding lines 292-293, please briefly describe in the main text, along with references, why this species (L. reuteri) has
garnered considerable research interest. This will clarify the rationale for focusing on this species in the study. 
7. Despite FMT-NPSLE causing phenotypic changes in WT mice, please more clearly indicate the reason for proceeding to
examine using MRL/lpr mice. Additionally, it might be beneficial to also examine the original gut microbiota of MRL/lpr mice.
If MRL/lpr mice exhibit NPSLE-like behavioral changes, does their gut microbiota resemble that of NPSLE patients, or is L.
reuteri increased? 
8. Comparing Fig. 4A and 4B, the concentration of IAA in feces is at pg/kg levels, while in CSF it is at ng/ml levels, indicating
significant concentration. Please explain how this concentration difference occurs. Additionally, the oral administration dose
of 100 mg/kg for MRL/lpr mice seems much higher than physiological levels. Please provide the rationale for choosing this

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

concentration. Have lower concentrations been tested? 
9. In the citations on lines 401 and 421, would it be more appropriate to list the first author rather than the corresponding
author? 
10. As mentioned in lines 401-403, oral administration of Lactobacillus in MRL/lpr mice has been reported to reduce
intestinal barrier damage. This seems contradictory to the current study’s result that IAA administration disrupts the BBB in
MRL/lpr mice. It would be better to discuss why such inconsistencies occur even with the same genetic background.
Furthermore, in Fig. S2 of this study, IAA administration adversely affects the intestine. Does this paper consistently suggest
detrimental effects of L. reuteri, or does it imply that different conditions from previous reports are responsible? Please
discuss this more clearly or provide supporting data. 
11. Please clearly discuss the conceptual mechanism: does the activation of microglia by IAA, which crosses the BBB,
cause BBB disruption, or does IAA directly disrupt the BBB? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, the authors investigated the role of gut microbiota in neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE).
They showed that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from NPSLE patients into healthy mice induced anxiety, cognitive
impairments, hippocampal microglial activation, and disrupted blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, suggesting that gut
microbiota contributes to brain injury. Furthermore, they revealed that both NPSLE patients and FMT-NPSLE mice exhibited
elevated levels of Lactobacillus, particularly Lactobacillus reuteri, which correlated with increased levels of pro-inflammatory
cytokines. The authors then investigated the role of L. reuteri in lupus-prone MRL/lpr mice. They showed that administration
of L. reuteri exacerbated hippocampal damage, neuroinflammation, and BBB disruption. L. reuteri produces the metabolite
indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), which was elevated in NPSLE patients and mice treated with L. reuteri. The study further revealed
that IAA promotes neuroinflammation, leading to a BBB and intestinal barrier disruption, and worsens behavioral and
cognitive impairments in lupus-prone mice. Mechanistically, IAA was shown to activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)
in microglia, leading to downstream STAT3 phosphorylation and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.
Inhibition of AHR suppressed these effects, reducing neuroinflammation and behavioral deficits. 
This study provides a novel mechanism in which L. reuteri and its metabolite IAA exacerbate NPSLE through AHR/STAT3-
mediated microglial activation, offering new insights into the pathophysiology of NPSLE. While the study is well-designed,
there are several points that require clarification or additional experimentation before publication. Indeed, several
methodological and interpretational issues need to be addressed, including additional controls, detailed experimental
descriptions, and improved imaging and cytokine analyses. These revisions will strengthen the study's conclusions and
ensure its scientific rigor. 
General comments: 
1- Behavioral tests: 
- The authors used the Open Field Test (OFT) and Elevated Plus Maze to assess anxiety and depression-like behaviors.
However, anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors are distinct disorders, and depression cannot be evaluated solely
using these tests. Additional tests, such as sucrse preference test, coast and splash tests, should be performed to properly
assess depression-like behaviors. For guidance, see Party et al., 2019. 
- In the OFT, did the authors assess general locomotor activity? It is critical to ensure that the mice did not have impaired
locomotion that could confound results. 
- For the Morris Water Maze (MWM), the time spent in the target quadrant during the probe trial should be reported to
strengthen the conclusions. The authors should specify in the methods the number of trials per day. 
- The authors should describe in more detailed the figure 3C-3H. 
2- Statistical Analysis 
The authors did not describe whether they performed normality tests before applying Student's t-tests. Without this, the
validity of the statistical comparisons is unclear. Additionally, significant differences were observed despite a small sample
size (e.g., n = 3). Did the authors account for variance homogeneity? These points should be clarified. 
3- Microglial Activation: 
The authors assessed microglial activation using Iba1 immunostaining. However, Iba1 only indicates the presence of
microglia, not their activation state. Co-staining with additional markers such as CD68 or TLR4 is necessary to confirm
activation. This omission limits the interpretation of microglial activation. 
4- Methods Details 
- Tissue collection procedures (e.g., perfusion or non-perfusion) were not described. This information is essential to evaluate
potential biases in the results. 
- Details about image acquisition (e.g., type of microscope, magnification, areas of interest, number of slides, animals, and
quantification methods) were omitted and should be added. 
- The authors focused exclusively on the hippocampus but did not justify why this region was selected. They should specify
the subregions examined (e.g., CA1, CA3, or dentate gyrus). Did they authors also look at the cortex? 
- In Figure 4D, the authors reported serum data but did not describe how serum was collected. This should be included in
the methods. 
- The solvent of L. Reuteris should be mentioned.

5- Cytokine analysis 
- While the authors used qPCR to evaluate cytokine expression, ELISA would have been a more accurate method for
quantifying cytokine levels. Additionally, the focus was solely on pro-inflammatory cytokines. It would be relevant to include
anti-inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10 to provide a more comprehensive understanding. 
- The use of a single housekeeping gene for qPCR analysis is no longer considered robust. Multiple reference genes should
be used for normalization. 
6- Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) Disruption 
The authors inferred BBB disruption based on reduced tight junction protein levels and albumin. However, this alone is
insufficient to confirm BBB disruption. Additional experiments, such as immunostaining for fibrinogen or IgG leakage or
using Evan’s Blue dye, are needed to validate BBB permeability. 
7- Figure and Data Presentation 
- In Figure 1I, cytokine levels analyzed by qPCR are expressed in pg/ml, which is incorrect for this method. 
- Legends are inconsistent—some are well-explained, while others lack detail. All figure legends should be revised for
precision and clarity. 
- Immunohistochemistry images appear blurry (expect Figure 1). Higher-quality images should be provided, and whole-
hippocampus views at lower magnification would help contextualize the data. 
- The authors should ensure that all figures and visualizations, particularly those involving color-coded data, are designed to
be color-blind-friendly. 
- The graph in Figure 6C shows discrepancies between the p-STAT3/STAT3 levels and the corresponding quantification,
which should be corrected. 
- There is a mistake in the legend axis of the figure 3D. 
8- In Vitro Experiments 
- Details about the correlation between intensity and cytokine levels (in pg/ml) are missing. This connection must be better
explained. 
- The in vitro experiments need to be more detailed. 
9- Intestinal analysis 
The part of the intestine studied was not specified. It is assumed to be the proximal colon, but this should be explicitly stated.
The specificity of the immunostaining for tight junction proteins in the intestine is questionable and needs further validation.
Furthermore, higher-quality images should be provided. 
Intestinal permeability should also be assessed using methods other than Western blot. 
10- Control Groups for AHR Antagonist Experiments 
The AHR antagonist was dissolved in DMSO, but the control group received PBS. Since DMSO can induce inflammation,
experiments should include a control group treated with DMSO alone to account for this effect. 
11- Additional comments 
The authors mention that IAA can cross the BBB (line 327) but did not provide references. This claim should be supported
with citations. 
The authors talk about the brain damage but they looked only in the hippocampus. I think they should be more cautious
about their conclusion and/or study other brain regions such as cortex. 
In the NPSLE, the patients have some seizures. Does this preclinical model present this characteristic and does this
treatment can decrease this parameter? 
The authors used female in their study. Did they test on male mice? 
In the cohort, does the NPSLE patients under pharmaceutical treatment? This could impact directly the microbiota. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Yi Feng, Lijuan Zheng, Wenli Tang, and colleagues investigate the relationship between gut microbiota and
neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE), which is characterized by neurological and psychiatric symptoms
such as cognitive dysfunction, seizures, and depression. This study specifically highlights the role of Lactobacillus reuteri (L.
reuteri) and its metabolite indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) in the progression of NPSLE. The findings indicate that IAA activates
the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) in hippocampal microglia, triggering inflammatory pathways, including the activation of
STAT3, which contributes to the development of NPSLE. This research suggests that targeting the gut microbiota could offer
potential therapeutic avenues for managing NPSLE. Overall, the materials and methods used by the authors appear
appropriate for addressing the research question. 
• In Figure 1, the authors present results from comparing SLE and NPSLE in fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) studies,
finding that FMT-NPSLE mice exhibit increased anxiety and depression-like behaviors compared to FMT-SLE mice. Since
the treated animals are C57BL/6J mice, do these animals develop lupus symptoms in addition to the evaluated behavioral
symptoms? Is there any observed difference in the behavior of animals between FMT-treated (SLE or NPSLE) and untreated
wild-type animals? 
• In Figure 1H, the authors show an increase in IBA1+ expressing cells in the hippocampal region. Could the authors confirm
this observation through western blot analysis? In Figure 1I, the authors mention increased levels of IL-1B, IL-6, and TNF-α

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 4 -->

mRNA, yet the "y" axes of these graphs are labeled in pg/mL. Are these measurements for protein or mRNA levels? 
• In Figure 2, the authors compare microbiota composition. How does the microbiota of healthy donors differ from that of SLE
or NPSLE patients? Have the authors conducted this comparison? 
• What is the explanation for the absence of Prevotellaceae and Alistipes in human samples, while these families are
enriched in mice after FMT? 
• Does L. reuteri induce SLE-like damage when administered to a wild-type animal? 
• Does L. reuteri exacerbate other lupus symptoms in MRL lupus-prone mice, such as the production of autoantibodies? 
• Does treatment of wild-type animals with L. reuteri result in behavioral changes? Previous reports have indicated that
indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathway, potentially regulating and reducing
inflammation. How do the authors explain their findings of an opposite effect, where AHR activation induces the production
of pro-inflammatory cytokines? 
• The authors do not cite the article titled “Lupus gut microbiota transplants cause autoimmunity and inflammation” by
Yiyangzi Ma, which appears relevant to the topic and identifies gut microbiota contributing to the pathogenesis of SLE.

Comment 1: In Fig.1, the rationale for the interpretation that FMT-NPSLE causes 
BBB dysfunction appears somewhat weak. To better interpret the data and clarify, I 
recommend adding supplementary experimental data or discussing relevant literature. 
Although FMT-SLE is used as a control compared to FMT-NPSLE, more precise 
control of the experiment would be better. For example, gut dysbiosis has been 
suggested to affect BBB permeability (e.g., PMID: 25411471 and 26923630). To what 
extent does the antibiotic pretreatment, as part of FMT, influence the experimental 
results? Could it be interpreted that increased BBB permeability due to pretreatment is 
rescued in FMT-SLE but not in FMT-NPSLE? 
Response: We sincerely appreciate your insightful comment and helpful suggestions. 
To further clarify the interpretation that FMT-NPSLE contributes to BBB dysfunction 
and to assess the potential impact of antibiotic pretreatment, we performed additional 
experiments including four groups: Control, Antibiotics only, FMT-SLE, and 
FMT-NPSLE.  
BBB permeability was evaluated by assessing albumin and MMP9 expression in the 
hippocampus via western blot, as well as IgG leakage in both the hippocampus and 
cortex using immunofluorescence (Fig. A-B, below). Our results demonstrated that 
mice in the Antibiotics-only group exhibited elevated levels of albumin and MMP9 in 
the hippocampus, along with increased IgG immunofluorescence intensity in both the 
hippocampus and cortex, compared to the Control group. These findings confirm that 
antibiotic pretreatment alone can compromise BBB integrity. 
Importantly, the FMT-SLE group showed no significant improvement compared to 
the Antibiotics-only group, indicating that FMT from SLE patients did not reverse the 
BBB changes induced by antibiotics. In contrast, mice in the FMT-NPSLE group 
displayed the most severe BBB disruption, as evidenced by the highest levels of 
albumin and MMP9 expression and the most pronounced IgG leakage. 
There was no significant difference between the FMT-SLE and antibiotics-only 
groups, indicating that FMT-SLE did not mitigate the BBB alterations induced by 
antibiotics. In contrast, mice in the FMT-NPSLE group exhibited the most 
pronounced BBB disruption, as evidenced by the highest levels of albumin and 
MMP9 expression and IgG leakage. These findings suggest that while antibiotic 
pretreatment can compromise BBB integrity, the gut microbiota from NPSLE patients 
may further exacerbate this effect, supporting our hypothesis that NPSLE-associated 
dysbiosis contributes to BBB dysfunction. We hope these additions adequately 
address your concern and help clarify the interpretation of our findings.

behavioral tests and sacrifice. What is the basis for determining this timeline?

Response: Thank you for your valuable comments. The two-week interval between 
the initiation of behavioral testing and tissue collection was based on the experimental 
timeline commonly used in previously published study (PMID: 37118092). 
Conducting the full battery of behavioral tests, including OFT, NOR, and MWM,

were maintained throughout the behavioral testing phase and up to the point of 
sacrifice, thereby strengthening the link between gut dysbiosis and the observed 
neuropsychiatric phenotypes.

As shown in Fig. 2B, the fecal microbiota of SLE and NPSLE patients exhibited 
considerable inter-individual variability. Interestingly, after FMT into recipient mice,

the gut microbial profiles became more consistent within each group (Fig. 2E), with 
clearer differences between groups. The results may be attributed to the following 
reasons. First, the mouse model offers a controlled genetic and environmental 
background, which minimizes host-related noise. Second, FMT does not result in a

Comment 6: Regarding line 292-293, please briefly describe in the main text, along 
with references, why this species (L. reuteri) has garnered considerable research 
interest. This will clarify the rationale for focusing on this species in the study. 
Response: Thank you for your helpful suggestion. As one of the most widely studied 
and utilized probiotics, L. reuteri has garnered significant research interest due to its 
ability to modulate host immunity and its strain-specific immunoregulatory effects. L. 
reuteri plays an important role in regulating the differentiation and function of 
immune cells (PMID: 37638038). For example, L. reuteri produces indole derivatives 
that lead to the reprogramming of gut CD4⁺ intraepithelial lymphocytes (PMID: 
28775213). In a TLR7-induced mouse model of lupus, L. reuteri was found to activate 
plasmacytoid dendritic cells and type I interferon signaling, thereby exacerbating 
lupus-like pathology (PMID: 30581114). Moreover, tryptophan-derived metabolites 
from L. reuteri have been shown to increase susceptibility to experimental 
autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) (PMID: 36419205). These findings support the 
relevance of L. reuteri in the context of immune regulation and autoimmune diseases, 
providing a rationale for focusing on this species in our study. We have added a brief 
explanation and relevant references in the revised manuscript to clarify the rationale 
for focusing on L. reuteri (lines 375–377).

we then employed MRL/lpr mice as a disease-relevant model to further explore how 
gut microbiota and their metabolites influence NPSLE in a genetically susceptible 
background—thereby better mimicking the clinical context.  
To investigate whether MRL/lpr mice exhibit spontaneous gut dysbiosis that may be
