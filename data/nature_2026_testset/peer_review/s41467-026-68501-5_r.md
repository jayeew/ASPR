Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a nice study that reports the chemical production and experimental assessment of two new prodrugs of bictegravir
with improved pharmacokinetics, as longer half-life and shorter tails. In animal models, NMXBIC provided plasma inhibitory
concentrations enough to be administered twice a year. Furthermore, its short PK tail would allow late dose forgiveness, a
concern with the currently available ultra-long acting cabotegravir. Given that bictegravir has a good safety profile and
greater resistance barrier than cabotegravir, NMXBIC would be more appealing than cabotegravir for both HIV prevention
and treatment. Thus, further clinical development of NMXBIC is warranted. The research team has long experience in the
development of ultra-long acting drugs and has pioneered several formulations in the HIV and hepatitis fields. 
The Discussion is quite simple and would benefit from expanding the scenarios for HIV Treatment and prevention with the
adevent of a 6-month potent antiviral such as NMXBIC. Specific comments for the Discussion section: 
1. Drug resistance. From the clinical standpoint, two major differences exist between cabotegravir and bictegravir. The first is
resistance barrier by HIV. Whereas HIV integrase mutations Q148R and N155H may compromise the activity of
cabotegravir, they have not demonstrated to reduce bictegravir efficacy in vivo. 
2. Drug interactions. A second difference between cabotegravir and bictegravir refers to its metabolism. Cabotegravir is
largely catabolized in the liver by UGT1A1 and in less extent by CYP3A4. Rifampin may induce its metabolism. Overall the
potential for drug interactions is lower with bictegravir. 
3. Drug volume. The new bictegravir prodrug NMXBIC needs low volume nanosuspensions and may overcome one of the
major side effects of current parenteral long-acting cabotegravir formulations, which is local pain at injection sites. They may
extent for a few days, with common development of induration and nodules. The data presented by the author’s testing 1 ml
injection site reactions of NMXBIC in the New Zealand rabbit model support its favorable safety profile. 
4. PK tail. The long PK tail of long-acting cabotegravir accounts for prolonged subtherapeutic drug concentrations when
timely dosing is forgotten, favoring development of drug resistance. In contrast, the new bictegravir prodrug NMXBIC depicts
a shorter PK tail and may allow dosing forgiveness with lesser risk. 
5. Combination options. The opportunity to combine the new bictegravir prodrug NMXBIC with lenacapavir, which is
prescribed subcutaneously every 6 months as HIV treatment, opens a further clinical scenario for NMXBIC. Since HIV
treatment with long-acting cabotegravir-rilpivirine every 2-months has revolutionized HIV therapeutics, the advent of a new
treatment option every 6 months will definitively transform the HIV field and improve patient’s management. 
6. Pre-exposure prophylaxis. The new NMXBIC formulation given twice a year should be more appealing than currently
approved options, making easier PrEP indication in some developing regions with high HIV endemicity, as in Sub-Saharan
Africa, where 25 out of the 40 million HIV-positive persons live. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author)

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

This manuscript by Nayan et al describes the design, synthesis, and in vitro and in vivo evaluation of novel monomeric and
dimeric bictegravir (BIC) ester prodrugs formulated as injectable nanosuspensions. The primary focus is on the dimeric
prodrug (NMXBIC), which achieves ultra-long-acting (ULA) antiretroviral drug exposure with a short pharmacokinetic (PK)
tail, addressing a key limitation of current long-acting HIV therapies. The study employs comprehensive in vitro and in vivo
methods, including pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, efficacy, and safety assessments in rodent and rabbit models. The
development of a dimeric BIC prodrug (NMXBIC) with a short PK tail is a significant advancement, potentially overcoming
the issue of prolonged subtherapeutic drug exposure seen with existing long-acting injectables. The authors thoroughly
characterize the synthesized prodrugs using NMR, FT-IR, mass spectrometry, and XRD, and assess their physicochemical
properties, hydrolysis profiles, and antiretroviral activity in vitro. 
Notwithstanding the promising data of the prodrugs shown by the authors, a few key experiments are missing to ensure that
the conclusions drawn by the authors are valid: 
- Statistical tests are completely absent from the manuscript. These need to be conducted, without them any comparative
statements should be removed throughout. 
- While the manuscript demonstrates sustained BIC plasma levels and in vitro antiviral activity, there is no in vivo efficacy
data (e.g., viral load reduction in infected animal models). Such data would strengthen the translational impact and provide
direct evidence of therapeutic benefit 
- The manuscript references other long-acting BIC formulations but does not provide direct head-to-head experimental
comparisons in their models. Including such data is necessary to contextualize the advantages of the here developed
prodrugs more clearly. 
- While the study infers hydrolysis mechanisms from plasma/tissue BIC levels, this is insufficient evidence for the metabolic
fate and prodrug release. Identification of hydrolysis intermediates (e.g., monomeric BIC-lipid conjugates for MXBIC) should
be conducted. Enzyme-specific hydrolysis assays (e.g., carboxylesterase knockdown models) would further delineate
esterase specificity of prodrug release and indicate tissue selectivity of the prodrugs. Biliary/fecal excretion profiles should
be conducted in vivo to rule out intact prodrug elimination and therefore incomplete prodrug conversion in vivo. 
- While prodrug stability was assessed room temperature, it should be investigated at accelerated conditions (e.g.,
50°C/75% RH) to further delineate stability. It would also be important to assess stability under clinical-grade sterilization
(e.g., autoclaving, filtration) to assess the clinical translatability of the developed prodrugs and formulations. 
- While injection site reactions were minimal to moderate in rabbits, the long-term implications of repeated dosing, especially
in humans, warrant further discussion in the manuscript. 
Minor comments: 
- The brackets indicating the extended tail seem to be missing in Figure 1B for MXBIC. 
- Error bars should be shown as SD instead of SEM in several of the line plots to more clearly indicate experimental
variability, e.g. Fig 1E, 1F, 2A, 2B, 3A, 3B, 4A, 4B, 5A, 5B. 
- Standard deviations of PK parameters should be indicated in the Tables in Fig 4C and 5C.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have made substantial improvements to the manuscript. The addition of in vivo efficacy data and the inclusion
of statistical analyses throughout the figures and results markedly improve the transparency, rigor, and interpretability of the
work. These additions strengthen the manuscript and provide much-needed validation for the claims of antiviral activity and
pharmacokinetic performance. 
However, several areas where the authors indicate that additional work will be required are not sufficiently caveated or
acknowledged in the main text. In particular: 
1. Comparative claims — While the rebuttal appropriately explains that head-to-head testing against other long-acting
bictegravir formulations was not feasible, the manuscript still contains statements that could be interpreted as implying
superiority of the current formulation. These claims should be tempered or explicitly qualified to clarify that no direct
comparative studies were performed. 
2. Mechanistic interpretation of prodrug release — The manuscript continues to describe prodrug conversion and release as
fully efficient, without adequately acknowledging the complexity of in vivo metabolic pathways and the absence of direct
data on intermediates or enzyme-specific hydrolysis. The Discussion should explicitly note that while hydrolysis is
supported by indirect evidence, a full mechanistic and metabolic characterization remains future work. 
3. Stability and CMC development — The authors’ rebuttal mentions that further accelerated stability, sterilization
compatibility, and CMC characterization are planned, but these are not discussed in the manuscript. To present a balanced
view of translational readiness, the text should acknowledge that such studies are essential future steps to fully derisk the
compound for clinical development. 
Overall, the manuscript is now much improved in data quality and presentation, but it would benefit from clearer self-

containment and transparency in delineating what has been demonstrated versus what remains to be established in future
studies.

Point 2 . Drug resistance . “From a clinical standpoint, there are two major differences between 
cabotegravir (CAB) and bictegravir (BIC). The first is the barrier to resistance caused by HIV-1 infection.

Response: We agree! CAB has been reported to have a lower genetic barrier to resistance compared

Drug interactions. “The second difference between CAB and BIC is their metabolism. CAB is largely catabolized in the liver by UGT1A1, and to a lesser extent by CYP3A4. Rifampin may induce its metabolism. Overall the potential for drug interactions is lower with BIC.”  Response: We agree with the reviewer’s comment on the differences in metabolic pathways of CAB and BIC, which affect their susceptibility to drug-drug interactions such as CYP3A4 inducer. Several studies have shown that BIC is metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP)3A and uridine diphosphate- glucuronosyltransferase UGT1A1. Co-administration of BIC with strong CYP3A and UGT1A1 inducers such as atazanavir, nilotinib, and rifampicin is contraindicated [5]. Strong UGT1A1 inducers likewise decrease CAB concentrations [6]. Thus, although the metabolic mechanisms for BIC (dual CYP3A + UGT1A1) and CAB (predominantly UGT1A1) slightly differ, co-administration of rifampin with these agents significantly lowers their concentration and dose adjustment is required. In contrast, the new bictegravir prodrug NMXBIC depicts a shorter PK tail and may allow dosing forgiveness with lesser risk.” Response: We agree! The prolonged PK tail of ULA formulations is a concern in chronic viral infections such as HIV, where subtherapeutic exposure may facilitate resistance selection. Contemporary LA design efforts therefore aim to limit tail duration or otherwise mitigate tail-related risk. The novelty of NMXBIC is its ability to maintain biannual therapeutic concentrations while exhibiting a shorter terminal tail. For context, LA CAB has reported tail durations of approximately 43.7 weeks in males and 67.3 weeks in females, which can complicate missed-dose management and regimen switches [7]. By contrast, the shorter tail observed with NMXBIC may offer added flexibility for adherence lapses and therapeutic transitions, without compromising LA coverage. We have revised the discussion section to reflect this strength (please refer to lines 402-411 on page 18 of the main manuscript text).  Point 6. Our prodrugs are transient and are efficiently hydrolyzed into bictegravir upon dissolution from the particle. Extensive hydrolysis studies on similar prodrug nanocrystals from our laboratory have demonstrated equal involvement of both enzymatic and chemical hydrolysis in our ultra-long-acting prodrug activation to release parent drugs with known metabolic pathway and biocompatible linkages [8-10].
