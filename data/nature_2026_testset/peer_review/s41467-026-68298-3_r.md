Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The paper presents a geometric design framework for programmable three-dimensional woven metamaterials, encoding
woven topology in a graph structure. This approach significantly expands the design space, enabling tunable architectures,
functional gradients, and arbitrary heterogeneity. However, several critical issues prevent this reviewer to recommend its
publication in current version. 
Comment 1: The unit cell side length L (mentioned in Figure 1) should be explicitly labeled in the figure to improve clarity
and facilitate reader comprehension. 
Comment 2: While the manuscript thoroughly examines short-term mechanical properties, long-term performance and
durability are essential for practical applications. The authors should incorporate a preliminary analysis of fatigue resistance
under cyclic loading and stability under varying environmental conditions. 
Comment 3: Mechanical testing is currently limited to 2×2×2 microscale lattices (L = 60 μm). Experimental validation is
lacking for functionally graded designs (Fig. 1b) and larger tessellations (e.g., 4×4×4), which are crucial for demonstrating
scalability. 
Comment 4: Programmable failure patterns (Fig. 5b) are only supported by simulations. Experimental verification of
controlled fracture paths is necessary to strengthen the findings. 
Comment 5: The discussion section highlights potential applications (e.g., biological stents, impact protection) but lacks
quantitative evidence for key performance metrics, such as permeability and specific energy absorption rates. These should
be substantiated with relevant data. 
Comment 6: The authors must clarify the fundamental distinctions between their work and the closely related study
(10.1038/s41563-025-02219-5) to better position their contributions. 
(Remarks on code availability) 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents an interesting investigation about design strategy of three-dimensional woven metamaterials. A
series of programmable woven metamaterials with functional gradients and arbitrary heterogeneity are presented and
analyzed systematically by parameter study. Mechanical properties of proposed structures are tested and discussed in detail
by experiments and simulations. I believe that the work will be sufficient to merit publication after some minor modifications.
Some particular comments are listed as follow: 
1. The geometric configuration of woven metamaterials can be controlled by two parameters, Reff and nrev. For a given
lattice configuration, considering fabrication and performance, is there a relatively optimal solution for the two parameters?
More discussion is needed on the selection of parameters within the design space.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

2. In compression experiments, both the fiber diameter and cell size are selected at the micrometer scale. At this scale, when
altering design parameters, has the impact of material size effects on structural mechanical properties been considered? 
3. Figure 3 compares the specific energy absorption and specific modulus of woven metamaterials with different design
parameters across various lattice configurations. However, it is essential and intuitive to benchmark these properties against
existing literature on woven metamaterials and other lightweight structures. A more detailed Ashby plot is recommended. 
4. Despite the forward design methods discussed in the manuscript, inverse design strategies for energy-absorbing
metamaterials have concurrently emerged. A brief discussion of these advances is therefore encouraged, for example
adma.202405567, advs.202204977 and s42256-025-01067-x. 
5. In Figure 4c, the peak values of the simulation curve exhibit a significant discrepancy compared to the experimental
results. The statements about “absence of manufacturing defects in our models which are inevitable in experimental
specimens” is too vague. It is suggested to carefully verify the comparison of the experimental and simulation results. 
6. The regulatory effect on deformation modes and failure paths is remarkably significant. However, only simulation fitting
results are presented in the paper. Have experimental attempts been made to achieve the specified failure paths? Under
manufacturing tolerances, substantial deviations from idealized results may occur. 
(Remarks on code availability) 
I have carefully reviewed the code and can confirm that all results presented in the manuscript are reproducible. The
codebase is well-structured and will serve as a valuable resource for the broader metamaterials community in future
research. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript focuses on the framework to design woven structures and demonstrates their tunable mechanical properties
and programmable failure patterns. Generally, it is interesting by exploring the stretchable regime of mechanical
metamaterials. In contrast, plenty of works have been done regarding the compressive mechanical behavior while ignoring
the stretching counterparts. This manuscript paths a way for researchers to the compliance and stretchability of mechanical
metamaterials. I have no doubts on the correctness of this work, but have some concerns on the superiority, applicability and
engineering potentials. Before recommending for publication, I have following questions. 
1) The node design is interesting. Is the number of strands in woven beams pre-determined by the parent monolithic-beam
lattice? For example, the beams lattices in fig 1a can only be replaced by woven structures with 4 fibers due to connectivity.
Are there cases where three or other different number of fibers is applied? 
2) In page 10, the authors demonstrated that “all tested woven lattices retained remarkable stretchability, sustaining
stretches of at least 2 and up to 4 before complete failure.” This is remarkable. However, it is not solid convincing to compare
with other structures in literature. The comparative study should be: How good is the large-deformation performance
compared with conventional monolithic beam lattices counterparts that occupy identical institutive material and relative
density? 
3) Similar to the former question, is the energy absorption performance better or worse than their monolithic beam-and-
junction-based counterparts? Especially in the case of stretching conditions. 
4) With regard to energy absorption, is it derived from tension-displacement curves for woven lattices while obtained from
compression-displacement curves for beam lattices, respectively? 
5) It is for sure that woven lattices have significantly improved the extensibility than monolithic beam lattices. However, the
latter was mainly designed for superior compressive behavior which woven lattices fall short of. This is like a coin that has
two sides. I am concerned on the negative side of woven lattices, i.e., the compression performances. 
6) In fig. 3a, is the peak position of the stress-strain curve where fiber fracture starts to appear? 
7) The lattice microstructure is to some extent complicated in its geometry, where each beam is replaced by three or four
helical fibers. There has been reported structures with only one fiber and demonstrates 230% extension, i.e., (2020)
11:1180, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-14996-5. What is the unique advantage or significant progress of this proposed
structure? 
8) It is easy to envisage that monolithic metamaterials can have superior energy absorption behavior applied in crushing and
impact of protective equipment, automotive, civil structures, et al. What are the major potential engineering applications for
such stretching-featured mechanical metamaterials? 
9) As far as I understand, the different fiber strands are detached and not in contact with each other in their original state. In
the very initial stage, the contact pressure should be zero. Can the authors present the approximate contact pressure (real
value or magnitude, not max/min ratio) during the stretching process? 
(Remarks on code availability) 
Finite Element Modeling is well presented in the Methods section. The simulaiton is completed using commercial available
software ABAQUS/Explicit 2023. Details like periodic boundary conditions, mesh types and mesh sizes are all depicted.

Figure R2: (Figure S8) Woven lattice behavior under cyclic compression and tension. 4×4×4 lattices
show low plastic deformation after 10 cycles to 15% strain. a, 4×4×4 octahedron lattice with unit cell size
60 μm, nrev = 4/3 and Reff = 4 . First-cycle-normalized energy absorption shows moderate degradation
corresponding to low damage. b, 4 ×4×4 BCC lattice with unit cell size 60 μm, nrev = 4/3 and Reff =
4 demonstrates similar degradation over 10 cycles in compression. In tension, deformation response is
consistent over 10 cycles, demonstrating almost complete recovery. Scale bars, 50 μm.
In compression, the octahedron architecture exhibited a modest degradation in energy dissi-
pation, dropping by 45% over ten cycles, while the BCC architecture exhibited a similar reduction
of 47% though ten cycles. This is consistent with behavior previously reported for woven lattices1,
where the octahedron lattice, when subjected to cyclic tension and compression, exhibited moder-
ate reduction in normalized energy absorption without catastrophic failure. However, in tension,
the newly introduced BCC architecture exhibited minimal degradation. This difference is likely
due to the lower entanglement of the woven BCC lattice unit cell for the same parameters, deter-
mined by its lower number of constituent beams compared to the octahedron lattice. This reduction
in entanglement allows the fibers to reconfigure more readily in tension, aligning with the tensile
load but returning to close to their undeformed configuration upon unloading, and thus reducing
permanent deformation.

Response:
We appreciate the reviewer’s request for quantitive, application-related metrics. Our intention with
mentioning these potential applications in the last sentences of the manuscript is to highlight fu-
ture research directions—some of which are ongoing—based on the property and design spaces
that this work maps. To clarify this intention, highlighting to the reader that the primary contribu-
tion of the manuscript the the foundational framework for applications to come, we have revised
the wording in that last paragraph of the manuscript. We believe it is important to connect the
framework to future applications, even though these endeavors are not at a level of completion that
would merit their inclusion in the manuscript.
Regarding biological applications, we believe that the woven scaffolds’ low density, high perme-
ability, and spatial tunability make them well suited for fundamental mechanobiological studies.
They could also enable applications requiring precise control of mechanical properties, such as
promoting cell polarization and alignment during wound healing through stiffness gradients. We
further highlight that the stiffness range attainable by these scaffolds ( ∼ 30 − 500 kPa) is repre-
sentative of a wide range of biological tissues. As an example of this particular realization, we
would like to share unpublished (ongoing) cell culture experiments on woven and monolithic
lattices, successfully demonstrating mouse fibroblast attachment on each (Fig. R6). The scaffolds
were first treated with Poly-L-Lysine to enhance cell adhesion, after which BALB/3T3 mouse fi-
broblasts were introduced and cultured for two days. Following the culture period, the cells were
fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde for SEM imaging. The results revealed that, after the same cul-
ture duration, a significantly higher number of cells adhered to and proliferated on the woven than
the monolithic scaffolds. This highlights that the mechanical tunability of our woven scaffolds can
indeed significantly influence cellular behavior.
Regarding impact protection, though all experiments in the present study were performed quasi-
statically, the specific energy dissipation of our scaffolds in compression (see Fig. R2) ranges from
6–13 Jg−1, which is on par with traditional structural and energy-absorbing foams albeit at a much
lower density. Exploration in this direction of high-rate impact experiments is a key research focus
of our group 4, 5, and we intend to answer these questions in future work.

Response:
We thank the reviewer for their attention to our prior work on double-network-inspired (DNI)

metamaterials. Several key differences exist between these works:
• Universal design framework : double-networks are merely a subset of the design space
presented in this work—i.e., this work’s proposed framework generalizes to the design of
monolithic lattices (Reff → 0) and woven latices with arbitrary geometry. The DNI work did
not explore the role of geometric parameters within a given DNI network—instead, a single
manual CAD design was presented for each of the two DNI concepts.
• Novel topologies: The present work expands the design library of woven metamaterials to
new topologies (unit cells) and also enables the generation of a bespoke unit cell beyond the
ones proposed in Fig. 2. In prior woven-lattice works over the last 5 years 3, 6, 7—including
the DNI manuscript— the same two topologies had been employed.
• Open source code for automated generation : By inclusion of the open-source Python
code, this manuscript is the first to present a truly scalable framework for the design of 3D
woven metamaterials at any length scale.
• Functional variation: This work is the first to present a route to smoothly vary parameters
throughout a tessellation, enabled by the simplicity of the design framework (and easily
implemented via the code).
• Design of failure: This manuscript also is the first to propose the design of failure paths in
3D woven metamaterials. This design-by-failure approach leverages the systematic study of
the effect of geometric parameters on the response, two concepts that are non-existent in the
DNI manuscript.
To clarify this point to the reader, we have expanded on the comparison with this related work in
the manuscript. The following is an excerpt from the revised manuscript:
”Although these two architectures have been demonstrated to achieve significant toughness when
integrated with truss lattices3, these double-networks alone offer limited options for tailoring lat-
tice behavior. In particular, they are not well suited for achieving spatially graded or locally
anisotropic stiffness distributions, nor for programming targeted deformation or failure pathways. ”

Comment 2.1:
The geometric configuration of woven metamaterials can be controlled by two parameters, Reff
and nrev. For a given lattice configuration, considering fabrication and performance, is there a
relatively optimal solution for the two parameters? More discussion is needed on the selection of
parameters within the design space.
Response:
We thank the reviewer for this suggestion, which prompted us to create a new supplementary figure
that provides the reader a better sense of the manufacturable bounds for some morphologies (see
Fig. R7, reproduced below).
First, within the design space of a unit cell, there are indeed limits to both Reff and nrev beyond
which fabrication is infeasible due to strands fusing or crossing. In particular, because of the fab-
rication constraints of the two-photon lithography process used to create the samples in this work,
the strands of the lattice must be sufficiently spaced to prevent unintentional fusing—about one
strand’s diameter apart. This limits both Reff and nrev, in a coupled manner. For example, in the
case of 60 μm unit cells with a strand diameter of 2μm, Reff is limited to about a minimum of 2μm
and a maximum of 8 μm. However, both Reff and nrev affect this spacing; due to the expansion of
nodes required by larger Reff (see, for example, the rightmost column of unit cells in Fig. R7),nrev
is more strictly limited for larger Reff, as increasing nrev packs more strands into the remaining
effective beam length between nodes.

Response:
We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. The structure reported in the cited study
indeed achieves large tensile extension by using single helically arranged fibers. However, the wo-
ven lattices proposed in our work provide several distinct advantages that represent a meaningful
step forward.
First, woven lattices eliminate the nodal stress concentrations that arise when individual fibers
terminate or merge at discrete junctions. In our architecture, fibers continuously pass through the
lattice without forming traditional nodes, which avoids localized stress buildup and promotes more
uniform load distribution throughout the structure.
Second, the interlocking of fibers at the nominal “nodes” produces beneficial self-contact during
deformation. This interaction introduces frictional energy dissipation through sliding and entangle-
ments and diffuses damage over a broader region, as demonstrated in Fig. 4 and Fig. S15. These
mechanisms, which enable enhanced energy dissipation, are absent in architectures constructed
from single independent fibers.
Third, the redundant network created by fibers that traverse multiple unit cells enables extreme
extensibility in the very large deformation regime. When the lattice is highly stretched or partially
damaged, long continuous fibers are able to slide through the structure while remaining engaged.
This behavior supports extensions greater than 200 percent and is evident in both our simulations
(Fig. 4 and Fig. S15) and experiments (Fig. 3). The ability to maintain structural connectivity
under severe deformation is a key differentiator from single-fiber designs, which lack this type
of redundancy. This feature could be particularly valuable in functional applications that require
uninterrupted conductive pathways.
Taken together, these features show that the proposed woven architecture introduces important
advantages in stress distribution, energy dissipation, and large-strain robustness that complement
and extend beyond the capabilities of previously reported single-fiber systems.

Figure R15: (Figure S15) Evolution of contact pressure in beam-element simulation of 4 ×4×4 BCC
lattice. In the initial state, fibers are not in contact and contact pressures are zero. Contact begins at a stretch
of about 1.6, at which point contact pressure appears at the nodes between and in the center of each unit
cell. This pressure increases as entanglement causes knotting of fibers at these nodes and straightening of
the intervening fibers; the contact pressure is relatively consistent at each of these nodes, reaching maximum
far from the boundaries where deformation is greatest. Failure begins at a stretch of about 3, after which
contact pressure drops and delocalizes as failed strands slip.

REVIEWER #2
Remarks to the Author: The authors have addressed most of my comments in their revised
manuscript. Regarding the discussion of size effect in comment 2.2, it seems insufficiently rigorous
to conclude that the material exhibits no size effect based on tensile tests. For instance, size effect
might become apparent under bending conditions. For example JMPS 51(2003)1477-1508. The
reviewer’s intention is not to ask for additional experiments, but to encourage the authors to revise
the wording so that to make the discussion more rigorous.
Response:
We thank the reviewer for highlighting this remaining point. We have carefully considered the re-
viewer’s comment as well as the suggested work. Although we agree that unconsidered size effects
may be present at the scale of our material, because the fiber radius is kept constant throughout
the experiments, these effects should not affect the relative behavior and the conclusions in the
manuscript. However, this is a point that we should determine rigorously in subsequent work,
specifically in the presence of strain gradients and not simple uniaxial loading—we thank the re-
viewer for bringing this to our attention.
To address this point and improve the rigor of our discussion, we have revised the main text to
include the following when introducing the samples we fabricated:
