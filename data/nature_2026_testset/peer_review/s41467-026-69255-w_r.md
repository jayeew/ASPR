Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This exciting paper presents the most complete set of biologically relevant carbon oxides synthesized from H2 and CO2
reported to date. It is almost too good to be true from some standpoints, because it provides perhaps the most
comprehensive set of observations indicating that the core of central carbon metabolism unfolds spontaneously from H2 and
CO2, given suitable conditions and catalysts. What they show is that MoS2 converts H2 and CO2 into 32 (!) biologically
relevant compounds, mostly organic acids of CO2 fixation pathways, many of which have been reported from previous, well-
cited work of others, but many of which are new. Two compounds, acetaldehyde and glyoxylate, caught my attention
because aldehydes are reactive and might be short lived under these conditions. This is not to say that metabolism and life
arose directly from MoS2 catalysts, but it does say that MoS2 under hydrothermal conditions certainly could have helped, in
a surprisingly big way, which is welcome news. 
I am sure it is in the supplemental, but many readers will not find the energy to look through Supplemental figures 83–147,
so it would really be helpful if the authors could indicate in a main text Table how much they found (M, mM, µM, nM
concentrations) of these 32 compounds under some selected conditions. For specific values of interest, readers can then go
to the Supplemental. The information is provided for some compounds in other figures, but they should show an overview of
the concentrations for the compounds in Figure 1 for some important conditions in a main text table, is my suggestion. We
see in the text that some compounds preferentially accumulate at low temperature, which is more biocompatible than 250°C.
Figure 1 direly needs to cite [Fuchs, G. 
Alternative pathways of carbon dioxide fixation: Insights into the early evolution of
life? Annual Review of Microbiology 65, 631–658 (2011).] as he elucidated several of the pathways shown in the figure and
drew them in that paper in a manner similar to what we see here. 
Molybdenum is particularly interesting here because it is only involved in CO2 to formate conversion in primary metabolism.
Its efficient synthesis of C5–C6 backbones here is surprising and interesting. I am reminded of the side activities of Mo-
nitrogenase, which can produce hydrocarbons and other compounds under some conditions (work by M. Ribbe). Ribbe’s
papers might deserve some brief mention, as they provide precedent for longer backbones being synthesized by a Mo-
containing (MoS2-containing) enzyme. This connection makes the present work even more compelling and interesting. 
Line 96f. The use of H2 as the reductant should be mentioned early in the results discussion section. I find first mention of
H2, 10 bar or 1 MPa, in the legend to Figure 2. 
Figure 1 does not make sense without naming the reductant. Assuming that
H2 was the reductant for those experiments summarized in Figure 1, it is an impressive set of findings and an intuitive way to
present the results. 
Please make the reductant clear.

on axis label

would aid understanding for some of us. 
Line 379, Mo in enzymes is usually as molybdenum cofactor (MoCo), Mo-nitrogenase is an exception. Perhaps a simplified
structure comparison of the Mo environment here and in the MoS2 containing 
cofactor or Mo-nitrogenase would be
instructive in the main text. 
Figure 6 is useful, and the legend indicates H2 being produced by serpentinization, it should therefore show H2 emerging

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have claimed the formation of a large variety of metabolites from central carbon fixation pathways using the
twisted 1T’-MoS2 polymorph, which mimics the distorted geometry found in certain molybdenum metalloenzymes. I focus my
comments on the characterization and quantification of the small organic compounds and metabolites reported in this study,
where several issues preclude the proper analysis of the data. 
Most of the compounds have been identified only by GC-MS using comparison with MS libraries. This identification process
typically affords several scores to evaluate the reliability of the identification. These scores are not provided by the authors,
which does not leave the reader with any confidence to justify the characterization. The authors only provide a visual
comparison of the experimental/library MS traces. This is especially an issue when significant additional peaks with higher
m/z are detected and/or the relative intensity of peaks differ significantly (the latter could indicate different fragmentation
patterns, Fig. S81 is an example of both). 
Accordingly, the identification of several compounds reported in this manuscript
appears doubtful. 
Concerning the products only identified by GC-MS, another issue is that the authors have not evaluated their derivatization
methods on authentic standards: some important intermediates arise from dehydration and have been only observed after
concentration of the reaction mixture. Although it’s not likely that freeze-drying will produce those, it is still an important
control. The authors should treat authentic samples of the compounds they claim are detected with their derivatization
methods and show the resulting GC-MS traces in a way that allows for easy comparison with the results reported in the
present study. 
I would additionally recommend to spike the reaction mixture with some authentic sample of the supposed products to
confirm their retention time and fragmentation pattern in the GC-MS (in case of co-elution, for example, complicate the
analysis), either because they are essential to the authors’ argumentation, or because their MS spectra does not match
unambiguously. 
Finally, the use of authentic standards would also enable the authors to provide some evaluation concerning the
quantification: in the present state of the work, we do not know on which order of magnitude the products are detected after
concentration of the mixture. Is it 10x lower than the products detected by NMR before concentration? 100x lower? 100,000x
lower? 
The authors should also have a deeper look if an experimental catalytic activity can indeed be demonstrated: they have
used 0.1 mol/L NaHCO3 with a 0.5 mol/L 1-T’MoS2 loading: even if there is DFT calc. and IR analysis showing the potential
for catalysis, it is not the same as experimentally demonstrating catalysis: deactivation/inhibition (and other processes) can
result in a lack of catalytic turnover. This would not be catalysis. The authors must show experimental catalytic turnover (i.e.
that more products are formed than the amount of “catalyst” used) to claim that the 1-T’MoS2 is a catalyst. If this cannot be
demonstrated, the authors cannot claim “catalysis”. Rather, they should refer to it as a promoter or a reagent. 
Based on the above points, further work is required such that the authors can properly justify their claims that certain
compounds have been produced and well quantified. As these claims are central to the manuscript, I must recommend
major revisions.
