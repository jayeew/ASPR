Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript describes a transfer method for carbon nanotube arrays that achieves a non-destructive transfer of
nanotubes from the growing substrate to the receiving substrate by water condensation and sublimation, which not only
preserves the complex and fine structure and properties of the arrays but also avoids array damage by the capillary action of
water evaporation. However, the reviewers note that this idea has been reported in references 42 and 43. Although they
contain the authors' previously published work while improving the transfer accuracy of the arrays and excluding the effect of
water evaporation, the core parameters of the three are similar, resulting in the present work appearing to be less innovative
and failing to meet the standard of Nature Communications papers. In addition, the reviewers have concerns about other
aspects of the work and would like the authors to answer. 
1. In the experimental section, the authors show that plasma with oxygen and carbon tetrafluoride was used to remove the
layer of entangled nanotubes and to hydrophobicise them before transfer. The reviewer believes that the gas-phase
treatment is not selective, and since it removes some regions of nanotubes, it must cause structural damage to other
nanotubes, and would like the authors to provide data to refute this. The reviewer is also curious whether the treatment has
weakened the bonding strength of the nanotubes to the growth substrate, thus making it easier to peel off, and would like the
authors to explain this from the data. 
2. The reviewers note that the authors used receiving substrates of different materials and flexibilities during the
demonstration of array transfer, and also deposited chromium and gold on the silicon substrate. The reviewers would like the
authors to provide the results of the contact angle tests of these substrates and information on whether wettability
improvement treatments was made before transfer, considering the appearance of liquid water film during the process, and
that the wettability of the substrate affects the homogeneity of the ice film on the substrate as well as the strength of the bond
to the array. 
3. The authors mention that the ice sublimation method can transfer arrays with sizes ranging from 10 µm to 1 cm. The
reviewer is curious if the authors have tried to transfer larger-sized arrays, and also what the difficulties are for transferring
larger arrays. 
4. In Fig. 3, the authors state that the transfer yield of the array can be controlled by controlling the thickness of the ice. The
reviewer is curious as to how the thickness of the ice layer is controlled, how uniform it is, and how this thickness was
derived in this work. Also, why did such an amount of ice melting not cause the array to shrink? 
5. In Fig. 2, the authors show that the arrays maintained good contact with the receiving substrate after transferring to PET
and Ecoflex in bending and stretching states, while also showing heat transfer capability. The reviewer is curious about the
strength of this contact and how it is achieved, and the authors should provide data to explain this. 
6. The reviewers note that the manuscript focuses on reporting the macroscopic properties of the transferred arrays, such as
the fineness, completeness, and efficiency of the transferred arrays, but does not describe the adaptability of the method to

the arrays, such as the range of heights over which the arrays can be transferred, the density and structure of the nanotubes,
etc., as well as the changes in these properties after transfer. It is recommended that the authors add such information. 
7. Finally, there are a few details in the manuscript that the reviewer hopes the authors should pay attention to. For example,
Fig. S7 is not mentioned in the main text; the edges of the array in Fig. 3a are not clear, the authors can add auxiliary lines to
strengthen them; the surface temperature of the array in Fig. 5d is higher than that of the commercial thermally conductive
pads, whereas the surface temperatures of the two in f are opposite, and the results are not in agreement with those of Fig.
S11 in c and d.

2. Can it be applied to single-walled V ACNT? 
 
[Response #1-2] 
We appreciate the reviewer ’s question regarding the applicability of our ice -
sublimation-based transfer process (ISTP) to single -walled V ACNTs (SW-V ACNTs). 
Since the ISTP relies on interfacial adhesion between the ice layer and the CNT tips, 
followed by sublimation that avoids liquid -phase capillary forces, the underlying 
mechanism is independent of the CNT wall number. Therefore, in principle, ISTP can 
be applied to SW-V ACNTs as long as the forests maintain sufficient vertical alignment 
and areal density. However, our current CVD system does not support the synthesis of 
vertically aligned SWCNT forests, so we were unable to experimentally verify this 
within the scope of this work. We believe that applying ISTP to SW-V ACNTs would be 
a valuable extension of this study and plan to investigate this in future work by 
optimizing process parameters for the mechanical properties of SWCNT forests.  
 
3. How much is the longest length of V ACNTs that can be transferred? 
 
[Response #1-3] 
We thank the reviewer for the question regarding the maximum length of V ACNTs 
transferable using the ISTP. The success of the transfer primarily depends on whether 
the adhesion formed at the ice –CNT interface exceeds the bonding strength between 
the V ACNT base and the growth substrate. When this condition is satisfied, the transfer 
efficiency remains largely independent of CNT length. In our experiments, V ACNTs 
with heights up to approximately 200 µm were successfully transferred without 
noticeable degradation in yield or structural integrity (Fig. R1). This length reflects the 
maximum achievable height in our CVD system rather than a limitation of the ISTP 
itself, suggesting that even longer V ACNTs could, in principle, be transferred. 
We note, however, that when the lateral dimensions of the V ACNT pattern become 
very small compared to its height, growth -induced bending can occur during CVD, 
which inherently limits the attainable length of vertically aligned CNTs [R2].  
Therefore, while the ISTP does not impose a strict limit on CNT length, 
maintaining suitable pattern dimensions during growth is important to ensure reliable 
transfer yields. 
[R2] Mo, Jiarui, et al. "A high aspect ratio surface micromachined 
accelerometer based on a SiC -CNT composite material." Microsystems & 
Nanoengineering 10.1 (2024): 42.

We thank the reviewer for this valuable suggestion. We have added a quantitative

(1) Single CNT transfer condition 
During the melt–refreeze cycle, the acceptor is briefly warmed so the thin ice layer 
becomes a transient water film. Because V ACNTs are intrinsically hydrophobic and 
remain in a Cassie –Baxter wetting state, liquid water does not invade the inter -strand 
pores; instead, it forms a thin interfacial layer at the tips. When the acceptor is recooled, 
water refreezes around the protruding CNT tips and sidewalls, partially embedding 
them. (Supplementary Fig. 20) Upon separation, the embedded segment of a CNT 
experiences an adhesive shear from the ice (vdW + hydrogen bonding + mechanical 
interlocking in the frozen cap). If that adhesive shear overcomes the root anchoring at 
the catalyst/oxide on the donor, the CNT transfers.

To clarify the mechanism underlying the dependence of transfer yield on ice 
thickness, we developed a simplified physical model describing the behavior of a 
single CNT  strand in the ice -mediated transfer process. The analysis shows that 
transfer occurs when the adhesive force generated by the re frozen ice exceeds the 
anchoring force at the CNT –catalyst interface, defining a critical embedded depth 
and a corresponding threshold ice thickness for detachment (Supplementary Note 1, 
Supplementary Fig. 20). Building on this single -CNT framework, we ext ended the 
model to the array level by incorporating the statistical distribution of CNT heights, 
as the V ACNT forest exhibits microscale height variations rather than a perfectly flat 
top surface. The array-level model predicts a gradual increase in transf er yield with 
ice thickness and its eventual saturation once most CNTs exceed the critical 
embedding depth, which agrees well with the experimental results (Supplementary 
Note 2, Supplementary Fig. 21, Supplementary Fig. 22). However, if the ice becomes 
excessively thick–significantly exceeding the V ACNT length–the melted water can 
also contact the donor substrate and re-freeze, potentially causing the donor substrate 
to remain bonded to the ice layer. This sets a practical upper limit for the ice thickness 
in the transfer process.

thin-ice formation followed by sublimation. Specifically, water vapor from the 
ambient environment condenses on the cooled acceptor substrate and temporarily

The authors developed an ice layer sublimation -based V ACNT transfer process . By 
implementing a substrate cooling system, a thin ice layer was formed on the acceptor 
substrate and the strong adhesion between the ice and the V ACNTs was leveraged to 
detach them from the growth substrate. Under low-pressure conditions, the ice layer 
sublimated without passing through the liquid  phase, thereby preserving the structural 
integrity and functional properties of the V ACNTs. The V ACNTs were then integrated 
as thermal interface materials for smartphones and infrared sensor absorbers.  The 
findings presented in the paper are both valuable and novel, I recommend its publication 
contingent upon addressing the following concerns: 
1. The structure of V ACNT including diameter, wall number, G/D ratio, etc, should be 
provided. 
2. Can it be applied to single-walled V ACNT? 
3. How much is the longest length of V ACNTs that can be transferred. 
4. The authors should carry out modeling work to validate the transfer mechanism as 
well as transfer yield. 
5. The authors should add comments on the novelty of this work compared with 
references 42 and 43.
