Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Wang and co-workers reported a novel strategy that employs directional interactions to weaken the original ones, thereby
inducing the crystal to transition from a slipped to bent state. This approach has exhibited a universality in a wide range of
cocrystal systems, enabling the successful fabrication of bent crystals in a variety of luminous colors, with bending angles
ranging from 61.8° to 85.0°. Notably, this strategy has also been effectively utilized to construct hierarchical bent
microstructures. Given that these bent microstructures possess anisotropic optical properties, the team has successfully
demonstrated their potential for photonic switching functionality. This study opens up a new avenue in the field of organic
crystal engineering, the potential of the reported method of crystal preparation and the crystals themselves can be quite
interesting to the general chemistry community. I recommend acceptance of the manuscript after the minor issues and
comments below have been addressed in full: 
1.In this paper, given the crystallographic parameters and crystal structure have been clarified, it is necessary to provide
detailed information about the preparation of bulk crystals suitable for X-ray single crystal diffraction analysis next. 
2.In this work, theoretical calculations method of the crystal structure in Figure 2 and detailed structural characterization
should be explained. 
3.How about the thermal stability of the bent structures? 
4.The illustrations in Figures 2b and 2f should be rearranged so that their angles match those of the experimental evaluation
objects. 
5.The authors should provide the spectrum shown in Figure 3o. 
6.The author analyzed the transition dipole moment of the cocrystal. However, why did the experimental results in Figure 5
show that the directions of the transition dipole moments at the two ends of the bent structure were different? 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Ma et al demonstrates the emergence of a route to grow molecular crystals that are inherently bent. The
team presents evidence that the degree of bending can be tuned by changing the characteristics of the crystallisation
procedure, including through change in the rate of crystallisation and the saturation levels. The team proposes a mechanism
for the bending of the crystal as being due to an inherent desire to reduce interfacial strain energy, for which they have
sought support through some molecular dynamics simulations. The authors subsequently demonstrate how multiple bends
can be introduced into the same crystal, leading to extended hierarchical constructions. Finally, the authors provide some
evidence that the bent crystals can be used as optical waveguides, suggesting that these bent crystals could be suitable in
nano-optical devices. In principle the study is an interesting concept with potential to out-compete the need to develop
mechanically flexible crystals for this purpose. The ability to selectively grow bent crystals, rather then bend crystals
mechanically, could lead to higher integrity in the optical components. 
The study seems generally to be well documented with some convincing measurements and analysis of the crystal structure
and behaviours. However, the paper is itself quite a difficult read, making it somewhat challenging to fully follow the logic
that is being presented. Clarity needs to be significantly improved prior to any publication. In particular, it is quite difficult to
follow what the model actually is (seemingly dubbed slip-rotation). I am also uncertain as to the reproducibility or generality
of many of the datasets being presented. For example, in the microphotographs (and associated discussions) on the

‘proportion of slip vs bent’ crystals, there is no indication as to how representative the small area of analysis is, how
reproducible the proportions are, or even what the relative proportion are (noting that each analysis is done on drastically
different numbers of crystals). The same holds true for the crystal angles being measured/presented for the different types of
crystals/bent states: how meaningful are these angles, or do they vary between crystals? 
The data availability statement indicating that data are available from the authors on request is extremely disappointing. I
would strongly encourage the authors to ensure that the data are available with publication in support of open science,
especially if they aim to publish in a high impact journal where results should be open to scrutiny. This is particularly true
given the low level of data presented in the ESI and main manuscript, especially around the simulations, where no
information is actually available on their outputs. 
In addition to these broader criticisms, I would like the authors to strongly consider a number of additional points, which I
summarise here in order of reading: 
- In the introduction (p2) the authors write about a ‘spontaneous transition from slippage to bending’. At this point of the
manuscript it is not clear what this means. 
- In the introduction the authors discuss a strategy towards self-assembled crystals to induce spontaneous transition from
slippage to bending without destroying the crystal integrity. It is not clear what this means, or what is being criticised here.
There are many mechanically flexible, plastic, etc crystals that bend without affecting the overall integrity of the crystal, and
which do not affect the optical wave guiding nature of the crystals. 
- 
The authors write ‘’ upon a substrate with a relatively high temperature of 30 
℃
,’’ but it is not clear what the meaning is. 
- It is not clear what mechanism the authors are proposing when they write: 
‘’The continuous growth of the assembled
molecules at both ends of the slippage microstructure causes the stress on the slipping surface to increase continuously,
ultimately leading to spontaneous rotation as a means of stress relief’’ or when they propose ‘’ proposed in this work
undergoes a controlled bending at a specific angle through a rotation process, maintaining its original molecular
arrangement without undergoing stretching or compressive deformation (as illustrated on the right side of Figure 1A’’. I think
it is particularly difficult to understand what is being proposed as the model the authors seem to be contrasting in Figure 1A
is an old (and out-dated) model that is proposed for mechanical bending of crystals, but the present manuscript discusses
growth of bent crystals; not post-growth bending. This is a confusing comparison 
- The authors have suggested that ‘’ The detailed experimental procedures along with the resultant crystal structures are
presented in Supplementary Materials’’ However no such details are actually given. 
- In Figure S3, the predicted morphologies are absolutely not consistent with the morphologies observed by microscopy. The
simulated morphologies are block-like, whilst those observed experimentally are rods. It is also not clear what the 3 different
figures in S3 refer to. The same holds for all later morphology predictions as well. 
- In Figure S5 it is not clear what feature the authors are referring to as being indicative of slip. The same shadowing is seen
in the ‘non-bent’ crystal in S5. A similar confusion is around Figure S11 and the insets. 
- Video S1 certainly shows that the crystal bends during evaporation of solvent, but it is not clear how the video suggests
evidence of a slip-bend mechanism. 
- In S6 the authors are comparing ratios alpha 1 and alpha 2. These measurements are done manually on a TEM figure, but
there is no indication of the accuracy or reproducibility of these measurements, or associated errors. It is difficult to be
convinced that the very small offset is real, rather than an aberration, slip of the hand during measuring, or even due to some
small crystal fragment agglomerating to the side of the larger crystal. 
- Throughout the manuscript, there is no discussion around whether the ‘bent’ crystals are in fact a single crystal, or if they
result from agglomeration of two crystals through a disordered interface. What evidence do the authors have for the bent
crystals being a single crystal? 
- In Figure S11 it is not clear if the pre- and post-bent crystals are the same crystal. If not, how useful is the comparison? 
- I am quite confused about the comparison of ‘simulated’ and experimental PXRD patterns in the ESI for the range of
cocrystals. First, it is not clear where the experimental or simulated data came from. Second, it is quite concerning that for
almost none of the crystals do the experimental and simulated data match. This suggests a potentially significant error in the
data, or perhaps that powder diffraction was measured on single crystals (but why?), which results in major texturing. This
needs to be explained. 
- It is unfortunately impossible to comment on the molecular dynamics trajectories in the present case, given the lack of
analysis presented in the current manuscript, aside from some difficult-to-understand snap shots. Moreover, it is odd to see
such smooth energy profiles unless there is some significant steering in the MD trajectories. If the authors are going to
present MD data, there needs to be much more analysis (e.g. some analysis based on RDFs and cluster growth analysis,
free energy analyses, etc) that could bother clarify the nature of the study that was done, and also lend some quantification to
the data sets. 
- The experimental and simulation details are far from being sufficient to understand, let alone reproduce, the results in the
paper. There is little-to-no description of how the multiple hierarchical bends were introduced into the crystals 
- Stating simulations were performed ‘by materials studio’ is not sufficient. What algorithms were used, and what were the
settings? Moreover the authors need to clarify that they did not perform crystal structure prediction, but rather crystal
morphology predictions. It is not clear how the authors have modelled their crystals using Gaussian16 (a gas phase code),
or what these simulations actually were. It is not clear what algorithm was selected to drive nucleation in MD simulations;
nucleation being a rare event usually needs a bias in the simulations so I presume something similar was needed here. One
would not normally expect an NVT simulation to result in nucleation/crystal growth unless a driver or other thermodynamic
trajectory were to be introduced into the simulation (e.g. slowly remove solvent molecules, drive temperature, etc). See e.g:
doi/10.1002/wcms.1697.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In my view the authors have done a nice job of addressing the reviewer comments, which have drastically improved the
manuscript. I am happy with the modifications that they have made, but have a final query, which I think is essential in order
to justify the paper's publication in nature commun. 
The idea of growing bent crystals is certainly not new, and has been reported a number of times. This is often due to the
emergent growth of a twin (e.g., https://gelogia.com/crystal-twinning/;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/002202489500419X), which gives rise to macroscopic appearance of a
bent (or twisted) single crystals, for example: 
* Growth-actuated twisting/bending (2014): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/anie.201301223 
* Tautomerism induces bending and twisting of biogenic crystals (2025):
https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2426814122 
* Anomalous twin boundaries in two dimensional materials (2018): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-06074-8 
In fact, the exact orientation of relative domains in such 'bent' crystals have been studied in some detail using advanced
imaging methods in some cases (e.g., 2016: https://www.nature.com/articles/srep28651) 
I would like to see the authors justify their findings in light of previous similar findings, either to solidify the proposed
mechanisms or to establish the novelty of their own findings.

raising this important point regarding the relationship between our work and prior 
studies on twin-mediated crystal bending. We agree that crystal bending via twinning

While previous studies , such as the classical Wagner –Hamilton–Seidensticker

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 44 -->

(WHS) model for FCC crystals, growth -actuated twisting in biogenic systems, and 
twin-boundary characterization in 2D materials, have demonstrated that twin formation 
can yield macroscopic bending, these works largely focus on the morphological 
outcomes or idealized models of twin interfaces. In contra st, our study provides an 
nanoscale mechanistic understanding of bending in molecular organic crystals, 
achieved through a non-twinning pathway that has not been previously reported. 
Specifically, our work advances the field in the following key aspects: 
(i) A Non -Twinning Slip –Rotation Mechanism:  Unlike classical twin -mediated 
bending, where the bending arises from symmetrical growth along a pre -existing twin 
plane, the bending in our system results from an artificially triggered slip –rotation 
process. We  deliberately exploited anisotropic non -covalent interactions and used a 
curved substrate under controlled thermal conditions to induce asymmetric growth and 
progressive slip accumulation. Once the interfacial stress reached a critical threshold, 
the accumulated strain was released by spontaneous block rotation along predefined 
slip planes, leading to a stable bent configuration with a well -defined angle. This 
process begins with a straight one-dimensional single crystal and ends with a controlled 
bend, fundamentally differing from growth patterns initiated by twin boundaries. 
(ii) Lattice Preservation:  In conventional twinning, lattice deformation or 
reorientation at the boundary often occurs, which can compromise functional properties. 
In our system, bend ing proceeds via the rotation of rigid molecular blocks without 
continuous lattice distortion. This preserves the original molecular packing within each 
block and avoids introducing strain. As a result, the crystal lattice at the bending 
interface remains coherent and undeformed, enabling the retention of uniform optical 
properties, a crucial advantage for applications in organic optoelectronics. 
(iii) Bridging the Gap Between Organic and Inorganic Crystals: Our findings reveal 
that crystal bending can be achieved in organic materials through a mechanism distinct 
from those observed in inorganic or biogenic systems. While established 
crystallographic concepts (e.g., twinning) remain relevant, the manifestation in

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 45 -->

molecular crystals is uniquely governed by anisotropic intermolecular interactions and 
molecular flexibility. This offers a new mechanistic framework for designing organic 
semiconductors, where strain -free bending is desirable for retaining electronic and 
optical performance.

molecular crystals that is fundamentally different from twin-mediated deformation. By 
providing real-space mechanistic insight into a slip - and rotation-driven process, and 
demonstrating its benefit for functional property retention , we establish a novel 
principle for achieving and tailoring curvature in crystalline materials, with 
implications for the design of molecular photonic/electronic devices.
