Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors developed a CuCo bimetallic catalyst for electrochemical synthesis of urea via the co-reduction of CO2 and
nitrite (NO2-). The catalyst was comprehensively characterized using a series of microscopic and spectroscopic
approaches. In particular, in situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), Raman, and infrared spectroscopy were employed to
probe the dynamic chemical state and adsorbed species under reaction conditions. These experimental results, combined
with theoretical calculations, provide insights into the reaction mechanism. Several control experiments further confirm that a
Cu:Co ratio of 1:1 delivers the best performance in terms of urea Faradaic efficiency and yield. The authors also explored the
pH effect, highlighting the role of proton balance in modulating surface *H coverage and enhancing proton-coupled electron
transfer. Overall, this is a systematic study for the newly developed catalyst material for the complicated electrochemical co-
reduction of CO2 and NO2-. However, several key aspects regarding the nature of the active sites and the interaction
between Cu and Co remain insufficiently explored. Moreover, some data were not properly explained and clarified. Specific
comments are listed as below: 
1. The Introduction Part is too lengthy, making the readers not clearly understand the core message of the manuscript. Some
contents are related to noble metal catalysts, which are irrelevant to this study as the manuscript is focusing on non-noble
metal catalysts. 
2. The manuscript states negligible urea activity for pure Cu, which contradicts several recent studies showing urea
production from CO2 and nitrate/nitrite using Cu or Cu2O (Nat. Commun. 2025, 16, 3271; Applied Catalysis B: Environ.
2024, 340, 123265). The authors should provide more detailed discussion or justification regarding to this issue. 
3. It is interesting that only Cu and Co together with a ratio or 1:1 works well for urea production. However, the detailed
interaction between Cu and Co was not well demonstrated in the manuscript. 
In addition, the linear combination analysis of
XAS data shows the catalyst is not fully reduced under negative applied potential, with the co-existence of oxidized Cu or
Co species. Their amount is also varied at different reaction conditions. Therefore, the oxidized species might play important
role for catalysis, which should also be further explored. 
4. Cu 2p XPS is not sufficient to clearly distinguish Cu(0) and Cu(I). Therefore, the conclusion of existing of Cu(0), Cu(II), but
not Cu(I), is questionable. The peak deconvolution of CuLMM Auger spectra in Fig. 
S10e is also not accurate. Please check
the relevant literatures (e.g. Surf. Interface Anal. 2017, 49, 1325) for more accurate XPS data fitting. 
5. The authors conducted a lot of post-reaction characterization, including XAS, XPS and Raman. However, the detailed
information of the reaction condition was not clearly provided. The phenomena of further oxidation of the catalysts compared
to the pre-catalysts after electrochemical reduction conditions are strange. It is highly probable that the catalyst materials
were extensively oxidized in air after electrochemical tests. Given that the authors performed in situ experiments in the
following section, the post-reaction characterization seems not necessary and it may mislead the readers regarding the real
active species. 
6. From Line 195 to 216, this section contains repetitive statements and should be condensed for clarity. 
7. Line 222-224, the authors attributed detected Co3O4 in Raman (but not observed in XAS) to laser-induced oxidation.
However, this statement might be incorrect, as XAS is more bulk sensitive and may not detect near surface Co3O4,
particularly in small quantities. The LCA data in Fig. S20 also shows the presence of Co3O4 for the other control CuCo
samples, which may have higher amount of Co3O4. 
8. Regarding Raman and infrared spectroscopy data, in addition to *CO, peaks between 2000–2100 cm

correspond to *NO (see ACS Energy Lett. 2022, 7, 1187). This is because CO and NO are chemically similar. Isotope

(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a compelling and thorough investigation into the co-electroreduction of CO2 and NO2– to urea
using co-sputtered bimetallic Cu-Co catalysts. In situ synchrotron X-ray absorption spectroscopy was employed to
demonstrate dynamic stability of metallic Cu and Co phases under operating conditions. Besides, the authors further
proposed synchrotron-based infrared spectroscopy to investigate the specific reaction mechanism. DFT calculations provide
further evidence for the potential determining step. Finally, they achieve a high urea yield rate of 61 ± 6 mmol h
‒
1 gcat

–1.2 V vs. RHE. The work is well-structured, given the growing interest in sustainable nitrogen chemistry, and offers
meaningful contributions to the understanding of C-N coupling mechanisms. However, before it could be accepted, some
questions still need to answer. 
1. The tandem mechanism is attributed to atomic-level mixing via co-sputtering, but it is unclear whether such synergy is
superior to a physical Cu + Co composite. Additional sample synthesized from physically mixed Cu/Co electrodes or
sequential Cu-on-Co deposition should be discussed. 
2. The Co 2p XPS spectra appear to exhibit a broad peak that overlaps with a smaller underlying feature. The peaks should
be re-fitted with appropriate constraints to ensure accurate deconvolution. 
3. The authors emphasize that neutral pH favors urea formation, yet the mechanistic rationale is only superficially discussed,
which should be strengthened. 
4. It’s recommended to normalize current and yield rate to electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) to isolate intrinsic
catalytic activity and decouple surface area effects. 
5. The authors seem to construct a Cu(111)/Co(111) plane model for DFT calculations. I am wondering whether the authors
consider the defects or amorphous regions formed during sputtering, which may influence adsorption energetics. 
6. While stability tests are provided, the post-reaction TEM/XRD characterization is missing.

“Urea (CO(NH2)2) is the most utilized fertilizer globally with market size around $129 billion in 2023, 
which it is projected to grow to around $161 billion by 2032. 1 Currently, the primary method for 
commercial urea production is the Bosch-Meiser process, which is involves the reaction of ammonia 
(NH3) and CO 2 under elevated temperature (150 – 200 ⁰C) and pressure (100 – 200 bar). 2 NH3 is 
typically derived from the Haber -Bosch process, which relies on hydrogen sourced from fossil fuels, 
making upstream hydrogen production the main contributor to energy consumption an d carbon 
emissions.2 As a result, the overall urea production remains carbon -intensive, generating 
approximately 910 kg of CO2 for every ton of urea produced.3,4  
 
Electrochemical co -reduction of CO 2 and NO x⁻, such as NO 2⁻ or NO 3⁻, offers a promising, more 
sustainable alternative. These NOx⁻ species can be sourced from exhaust flue gas, chemical production 
by-product, wastewater streams, or synthesized via cold plasma oxidation of N 2.5-10 Such approaches 
could enable decentralized and potentially carbon -neutral urea production. However, urea 
electrosynthesis from CO2 and NOx⁻ is a complex multistep process involving multiple electron transfer 
(Table S1 -S2) and competing with mechanistically simpler reduction processes, viz. the hydrogen 
evolution reaction (HER), CO 2 reduction reactions (CO 2RR), and NO 2⁻/NO3⁻ reduction reactions 
(NO2⁻RR/NO3⁻RR or NO x⁻RR).2,11 Notable challenges include limited CO 2 adsorption capacity, 12 
sluggish dissociation of the C=O bond, 12,13 and difficulty in balancing adsorption and activation of 
both carbon- and nitrogen-derived species.2,14  
 
At the core of these challenges lies the C–N coupling step, whose mechanism remains poorly resolved 
due to interplay of multiple surface -bound species and dynamic changes in catalyst structure under 
reaction conditions. Proposed C –N coupling pathways involve intermediates such as *CO, *NO, 
*NH₂, and *NH, with key steps often limited by proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) barriers.11,15-

17 Elucidating these pathways —and understanding how the catalyst surface evolves during 
operation—requires in situ spectroelectrochemical characterization techniques, such as Raman, 
infrared, and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), which can directly capture transient intermediates 
and structural changes. 12,13 Achieving the most energetically favorable pathway requires precise 
tuning of intermediate adsorption energies, a task that may be inherently challenging for a 
monometallic catalyst. This has led to growing interest in multimetallic designs, where distinct active 
sites can preferentially stabilize different intermediates and facilitate sequential steps. Indeed, such 
strategy has been successfully applied in the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) 18,19 and CO 2RR20,21 
fields, offering a blueprint for tackling the complexity of urea electrosynthesis. 
 
Copper has been widely studied due to  its ability to facilitate CO 2 and NO x⁻ activation, moderate 
hydrogen binding, and tendency to form key intermediates such as *CO and *NH 2.22-26 However, Cu 
suffers from limited urea selectivity and deactivation caused by strong adsorption of nitrogen 
intermediates.27-31 Cobalt, on the other hand, offers high conductivity, favorable *CO binding, and the 
ability to stabilize *H intermediates, thereby promoting downstream protonation or hydrogenation 
steps.30,32-35 We hypothesize that combining Cu and Co could couple their complementary strengths—
enhancing NOx⁻ reduction to reactive nitrogen species on Cu, while stabilizing *CO and *H on Co—
to accelerate C–N bond formation.30,36,37 Supporting this concept, recent work on amorphous Cu–Co 
boride (a-Cu0.1CoBx) demonstrated alleviation of strong N -based and *CO intermediates binding on 
the catalyst surface and achieved a faradaic efficiency (FE) of ca 28% with a urea yield rate of ca 310 
μg h-1 mgcat-1 (5.2 mmol h-1 gcat-1) from CO2 and NO3⁻.38  
 
Building upon the hypotheses discussed above, we explored tandem Cu-Co catalysts prepared via co-
sputtering of the two metals to facilitate C‒N coupling through the provision of different sites that 
facilitate different key step s on this complicated process. We expected that the atomic -level mixing 
achieved through co-sputtering of Cu and Co can enhance PCET and facilitate the formation of key 
intermediates, thereby optimizing the C‒N coupling process. 39-41 We first performed systematic 
electrochemical studies under varying electrolyte conditions—including pH and nitrogen -source 
species—to evaluate urea formation and identify optimal conditions. Guided by these results, we 
employed in situ spectroelectrochemical techniques —in situ Raman, synchrotron radiation Fourier -
transform infrared s pectroscopy (SR -FTIR), and XAS —to monitor the formation and evolution of 
reaction intermediates directly on the catalyst surface under operating conditions. Finally, density 
functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed under the optimized reaction c onditions to 
provide atomic -scale insights into the roles of Cu and Co in facilitating key reaction steps and 
stabilizing crucial intermediates such as *CO, *NH 2, and *COOH. This integrated experimental –
theoretical approach aims to unravel the C –N coupling mechanism and guide the rational design of 
efficient catalysts for sustainable urea electrosynthesis.”

Comment 2: The manuscript states negligible urea activity for pure Cu, which contradicts several 
recent studies showing urea production from CO2 and nitrate/nitrite using Cu or Cu2O (Nat. Commun. 
2025, 16, 3271; Applied Catalysis B: Environ. 2024, 340, 123265). The authors should provide more

Reply: We appreciate the reviewer drawing attention to recent reports of urea formation on Cu/Cu2O. 
Our statement of negligible urea activity refers specifically to metallic Cu prepared through sputtering 
method under our operating conditions, and we would like to reiterate to the Reviewer  that this does 
not contradict studies that employ oxide-derived Cu (OD-Cu) or Cu2O under different regimes.16,42-45 
 
The surface state of Cu is critical for C –N coupling. Under cathodic bias, bulk Cu2O/CuO typically 
reconstructs into oxide-derived Cu (OD-Cu)—metallic Cu enriched with residual Cu⁺ sites, subsurface 
oxygen, defect structures, and increased surface roughness. 42-44 Such features enhance *CO and 
nitrogen-intermediate co-adsorption, thereby promoting C –N bond formation. 42 In contrast, the pure 
Cu catalyst in this work was prepared by sputtering, which generates thin, relatively smooth films with 
fewer defects that we aken its ability to stabilize intermediates. 45 Enhancement of C ‒N coupling in 
sputtered Cu systems is therefore typically achieved by introducing a co -catalyst to create binary 
alloys, which tune the adsorption energetics and selectivity.16  
 
Linear combination fitting analysis of in situ XAS data for the pure Cu catalyst indeed indicated the 
presence of minor Cu 2O and Cu(OH) 2 phases alongside metallic Cu. However, because linear 
combination fitting reflects bulk-averaged phase fractions, it cannot resolve whether these oxides are 
surface-localized, catalytically accessible, or stable under reaction conditions. 46,47 In parallel, our in 
situ synchrotron Fourier transform infrared (SR -FTIR) measurements further reveal that the 
electroreduction of CO2 and NOx⁻ on pure Cu is dominated by CO2RR, showing intense *CO/*COOH 
bands but only weak *CO (carbonyl) and *CN signals, characteristic of urea (Fig. R1, adapted as Fig. 
S42 in the revised Supporting Information ). Together, these findings suggest that while trace Cu 2O 
and Cu(OH)2 are present, their coverage a nd lifetime are insufficient to stabilize polar intermediates 
(*NCO, *NH₂CO) necessary for effective C –N coupling. Consequently, the catalyst behaves 
essentially as clean metallic Cu, consistent with the absence of measurable urea formation in 1H-NMR 
or GC-MS analysis. 
 
To clarify this point, we have added control experiments directly comparing in situ electroreduction 
on Cu1Co1, pure Cu, and pure Co ( Fig. R1) and expanded the discussion to explain the origin of this 
discrepancy with literature results in detail and have added control experiments for clarity in main 
manuscript (Page 10, Lines 208-218) and (Page 11 and Lines 232-236).

Reply: We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. To better understand the role of each Cu 
and Co, we conducted additional in situ SR-FTIR measurements on pure Cu and pure Co catalyst as 
controls (Fig. R1, depicted as Fig. S42 in the revised Supporting Information). The results shows that 
pure Cu exhibits higher activity towards CO 2RR, as evidenced by the formation of *CO bands 
associated with CO (g), *COOH associated with formate, and *CO stretchi ng related to methanol 
formation.50-52 Carbonyl (*CO) and *CN bands related to urea were only very weakly detected, 
indicating that urea concentration was below detection limit of 1H-NMR or GC-MS. In contrast, in situ 
spectra for pure Co, revealed no *CN features. Only weak *CO/*COOH bands were observed, while 
strong signals corresponding to *NH 3 were detected, suggesting dominant activity for NO 2⁻RR (Fig. 
R1). These controls indicate that while both Cu and Co individually show activity for  CO2RR and 
NO2⁻RR, respectively, neither is sufficient to drive C–N coupling efficiently.  
 
We acknowledge the reviewer’s point that linear combination fitting analysis of in situ XAS revealed 
minor contributions from Cu 2O/Cu(OH)2 and Co3O4/Co(OH)2. Such oxides/hydroxides have indeed 
been reported to stabilize polar intermediates ( e.g.,*NCO)53-55 and to promote NO 2⁻ activation and 
hydrogenation, repectively.30,56 However, when compared with the in situ SR-FTIR results for pure 
Cu, only weak carbonyl and *CN bands were observed. It is clear that these minority oxide/hydroxide 
phases, although present, are insufficient in coverage and/or lifetime to stabilize the intermediates 
necessary for effective C–N coupling. Moreover, our CV measurements (Fig. R2, depicted as Fig. S24 
in the revised Supporting Information) also suggest the reduction of Cu and Co into their metallic 
states at applied potential more negative than  −1 V vs. RHE. Since linear combination fitting XAS 
provides only bulk -averaged phase fractions and cannot resolve whether these minor phases are 
surface-localized, catalytically accessible, or persistent under reaction conditions, 57 we regard these 
values as indicative rather than definitive. 
 
Collectively, the evidence indicates that the predominant active states under cathodic bias are metallic 
Cu and Co. Thus, urea formation is governed not by the minor oxidized species, but by the tandem 
Cu–Co interface, where Cu facilitates *CO/*COOH generation and Co promotes *NH 2 formation in 
close proximity—greatly increasing the likelihood of C–N bond coupling. This cooperative behavior 
likely arises from electronic and structural interactions at the Cu –Co interface. Supporting this, 
electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) reveals that  Cu1Co1 exhibits a lower charge transfer 
resistance than pure Cu or Co ( Fig. R3, depicted as Fig. S27 in the revised Supporting Information), 
underscoring the enhanced electronic synergy that facilitates CO 2 and NO2⁻ and underpins the C –N 
coupling performance of the bimetallic catalyst.

Comment 3: The authors compared the CV features of different catalysts under different reaction 
conditions (Fig. S24). Please explain why the CV features corresponding to quasi -reversible catalyst 
redox peaks (not related to catalytic reactions) change under different electrolytes. Given that the same 
catalysts are used, why do peak shapes, positions, and areas vary? 
 
Reply: We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. As noted, the quasi-reversible peaks in 
the CVs correspond to surface redox transitions of Cu/Cu –O(H) and Co/Co–O(H) species in the Cu–
Co catalyst , rather than catalytic current. Although the catalysts are the same, the quasi -reversible 
redox peaks observed in CV are sensitive to several factors: 
 
1. pH-dependent proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) . The redox couples of transition 
metals ( e.g., Co 2⁺/Co3⁺, Cu⁺/Cu 2⁺) are proton -coupled and therefore highly sensitive to pH  
according to Nernst equation .35-37 Variations in bulk electrolyte pH or buffering capacity 
directly alter the equilibrium potential (Eeq). For example, switching from Ar-saturated KHCO3 
+ KNO2 (pH 8, NO2⁻RR) to CO2-saturated KHCO3 + KNO2 (pH 7, electroreduction CO2 and 
NO2⁻) lowers the interfacial pH, causing  the reduction peaks to shift to less negative  (more 
positive) potentials, as can be seen in Fig. R2I & Fig. R2L (depicted as Fig. S24I & Fig. S24L 
in revised Supporting Information). Furthermore, both CO2RR and NO2⁻RR consume H+ and 
H2O, and generate OH⁻ ions at the cathode surface. This creates a significant interfacial pH 
gradient that further shifts the Cu/Co redox equilibria from their bulk -derived values, directly 
accounting for the systematic potential shifts observed.38 
2. Specific anion adsorption and competitive binding. Electrolyte anions (HCO3⁻, NO2⁻, OH⁻) 
exhibit specific adsorption on the  Cu and Co sites, modifying the electric double layer and 
redistributing charge within the bimetallic interface .39-41 Moreover, the anions compete  with 
water molecules or protons for surface sites, affecting the fraction of the surface area available 
for the intrinsic metal redox process. This leads to electrolyte-dependent changes to the peak-
to-peak separation (ΔEp), peak symmetry, and peak intensity.42,43 
3. Electrochemical–chemical (EC) coupling and reconstruction.  The redox peaks reflect a 
dynamic, potential-dependent surface phase transition that is coupled to the solution chemistry. 
For instance, the presence of NO 2⁻ (a mild oxidant) close to zero  current densities can 
chemically re-oxidize and thus stabilize the higher oxidation state (Co3+ or Cu2+), affecting the 
starting surface state for the Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) scan.44,45 Moreover, the rapid local OH⁻ 
generation facilitates the formation and dissolution of  Cu(OH)2/Co(OH)2 phases. These 
processes render the waves quasi -reversible and cause peak broadening or area changes 
depending on the electrolyte.26  
4. Tandem stabilization  and interfacial effects . The Cu –Co interface modifies the 
dissolution/redeposition behavior of Co and stabilizes Cu⁺/Co 2⁺ hydroxide species. 46,47 
However, the degree of stabilization depends on the electrolyte: in CO 2-saturated KHCO 3 
(CO2RR), Cu–Co tends to maintain more reduced Cu sites, while in Ar-saturated KHCO3 + 
NO2⁻ (NO2⁻RR), Cu–Co tandem stabilizes and suppress Co dissolution (Fig. R2F & Fig. R2I, 
depicted as Fig. S24F & Fig. S24I  in revised Supporting Information) . This leads to the 
observed variations in peak intensities and integrated charges.

In summary, the electrolyte composition strongly modulates the surface redox behavior of Cu –Co 
catalysts by tuning pH, adsorbate binding,  local chemical reactions, and interfacial stabilization. The 
variations in quasi-reversible redox peaks are therefore expected, even when the same catalyst is used, 
and they reflect electrolyte -dependent surface chemistry rather than changes in the bulk c atalyst 
identity. 
 
Thus, while the intrinsic redox nature of the catalyst remains the same, the electrochemical 
environment provided by different electrolytes significantly impacts how these redox features are 
manifested in CV. We have revised the Supporting Information to c larify this point and included 
additional explanation in the caption of Fig. R2 (depicted as Fig. S24 in the revised Supporting 
Information).
