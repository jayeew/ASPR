Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, Song and coworkers perform structure prediction calculations on the barium-rhenium-hydride system at high
pressures. The general hope of this paper is to predict new high-temperature hydride superconductors, falling into a general
research trend that is now 10 years old and saw its first success with sulfur hydride. Their approach is based on the
observation that BaReH9 is an experimentally-known compound, so they guess that there might be more compounds with a
higher hydrogen content under pressure. They use standard and well-established techniques, although I am concerned
about some methodological details (see the rest of the report). 
It has become not so easy to review all this kind of papers, because of a general lack of innovation in them. To simplify the
process, I will divide the review into an objective part, and a subjective part. 
Objective part: 
This work raises a few methodological questions that need to be addressed. In particular, there are multiple statements that
are not supported by evidence. Some sound reasonable, but that does not make them justified. 
1. The authors write that "as pressure increasing, coordination number of Re in HTM unit transforms from 9 to 12 and then to
14". This is inaccurate. In their Fig. 1 we can see that BaReH9 and BaReH12 coexist on the hull. I think the authors should
be looking for a softer statement of the concept that the hydrogen content tends to increase with pressure. 
2. "Odd numbers occupied orbital generally drives metallicity and triggers superconductivity" is inaccurate. Odd occupation
implies metallicity (unless exotic electronic states occur), which is a *prerequisite* but does not trigger nor implies
superconductivity. 
3. "In the second transition of coordination number from 12 to 14, Tc decreases from 128 K to 42 K. Because the high
symmetry of 12-coordination unit [ReH12] in Pm3-BaReH12 was broken in P2/m-BaReH12 as well as weak bonding of
hydrogens inter HTM units". This statement is vague. Symmetry and bonding can affect Tc only indirectly. What does
decrease? The coupling? The phonon frequencies? Why? Does the DOS at the Fermi energy decrease, or the matrix
element? Each of this statement should be justified by referring to figures and calculated quties. 
4. "Because additional Ba cation, which increases the distance between HTM unit [ReH8] suppressed coupling of
hydrogens inter HTM units". Where is evidence for this statement? 
5. "Our results suggest three key factors for high-temperature superconductivity hydrides with HTM units". Are these design
principles? They are not justified. Point i) is wrong/incomplete. There can be a superconductor with an even number of
electrons. How does an odd number of electrons increase Tc? Do the authors have a general argument as to why? Their
compound Ba2ReH8 has an odd number of electrons and a low Tc of 19 K. Isn't this a counterexample to their own
argument? Point ii) is fine, although very vague. Point iii) is the conclusion of [Francesco Belli et al., Nature Communications
volume 12, Article number: 5381 (2021)] which the authors fail to cite. The authors, on the other hand, do not show any
observable that qufies the bonding strength in their structures. Again, their statement is not backed by evidence, and already
present in an older article. 
6. Fig. 1 should show the depth of the convex hull as a color scale, or multiple perspectives. Like this it is impossible to
understand how deep it is. There are python packages like pymatgen that can assist in making such plots. 
7. There is no way in which 12 hydrogens can arrange in a quasi-regular dodecahedron, because a dodecahedron has 20
vertices (and 12 *faces*). It has to be a different solid. 
8. The path chosen for the band structures in figure 3(a), 3(b) is unusual. Y-Gamma-S and R-Z are not high-symmetry lines.

So is the path for 3(d). I suspect this originates from a mistake, rather than a choice. 
9. The DOS in Fig. 3(c) shows signs of underconvergence compared to the bands on the side. Moreover, there seems to be
a pseudogap right at the Fermi energy. I urge the authors to compare the DOS with their and a denser grid to ensure this. 
10. "In general a "flat band-steep band" feature appearing near the Fermi level indicates a strong electron-phonon coupling
(EPC) and high-Tc superconductivity". The "flat band-steep band" argument is not established, and while may have been
used in some compounds, there is no general proof or argument showing that this mechanism leads to strong electron-
phonon coupling. 
11. The presence of a pseudogap near the Fermi energy for Pm3-BaReH12 implies that convergence of the integrals of the
epc near the Fermi energy is *crucial*. The authors did not report which smearing they employed to perform the double-delta
integral at the Fermi energy. They should, and they should report a convergence test for the electron-phonon coupling of
their high-Tc compound. 
12. An Ultrasoft pseudopotential together with a cutoff of 90 Ry is probably overconverged.. 
13. Looking into the Supplemental Material, I see that BaReH12 actually has elongated H2 molecules. Those are definitely
not "weak" bonds. On the other hand, coexistence of H2 molecules and superconductivity is not so common (only NaH6 as
far as I know), so that might be a more interesting angle for the authors to explore. 
Subjective part: 
1. The authors refer (in their title/abstract/conclusion) to "coordination number engineering" as a sort of new method to
discover ternary hydride superconductors. They write "And pressure provide an effective approach which controls the
coordination number of HTM units in stable hydrides". Calling this engineering is a stretch. The authors *hope* that since
there is the stable BaReH9, then there will be more, and in this case it's true. They, however, have no control whatsoever
over it, nor can they show that statistically this happens for multiple hydrides, nor can they *justify* that there is a specific
mechanism at play in BaReHx. 
2. This work falls into the bucket of a bunch of articles which are essentially picking three elements, running structure
prediction under pressure hoping to find a sizable Tc, and making up a story a posteriori. With the rare exceptions of those
that are able to find truly promising superconductors (e.g. the recent arXiv:2406.09538), all the other articles do not innovate
one bit. I believe that people *should* be able to still publish their honest results in some form, possibly making structures
open-access so that it may be useful to a general effort to construct a "database of everything". However, the efforts to
"pump" these articles in a neverending recipe of do-prediction-publish-superconductor is stale and inadequate to a serious
journal. 
Given all of the above considerations, I would advise the authors to drop the "design" argument, and present their result in a
longer, toned-down paper, where they present a more systematic analysis of Ba-Re-H structures. They could find a rationale
as to *why* the hydrogen content increases with pressure, and discuss why Ba-Re-H is/isn't as good as others (e.g.
Li2MgH16, Mg2IrH5, LaBH8/LaBeH8) and propose some other ternary that might be better. If they also make their results
available to the community through a joint submission to one of the many databases (e.g. NOMAD, AFLOW,
MaterialsProject), they can offset the other weaknesses of their results. I would not recommend its publication without a
major revision of the work. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper, inspired by experimentally observed Ba2ReH9, the authors explored thermodynamically stable phases and
superconducting phases in the ternary Ba-Re-H system under high pressure. Using the CALYPSO and AIRSS codes, they
predicted that BaReH12 emerges as a thermodynamically stable compound at 100 GPa and shows the superconductivity at
128 K, in which 12 hydrogen atoms forms quasi-regular dodecahedron. Their results suggest three key factors for high-
temperature superconductivity hydrides with hydrogen and transition metal (HTM) units: i) the number of electrons in HTM
units; ii) high symmetry of HTM units; iii) weak bonding of hydrogens inter HTM units. 
The current manuscript is well-crafted and technically sound. I have no reservations in endorsing this work for publication in
Communications Physics, after the authors consider and clarify the points that I raise below. 
(1) On convex hull diagrams shown in Fig.1 and Fig.S1, the definitions of “metastable phase” and “unstable phase” are
ambiguous. Does the “metastable” and “unstable” mean “thermodynamically unstable but dynamically stable” and
“thermodynamically and dynamically unstable”, respectively? If so, the number of unstable phases (gray points) seems to be
too large. The authors should explain it more clearly. In the first place, what is the composition of the metastable phase
colored by blue in Fig. 1? I was not able to find it in the main text and the supplemental information. In addition, they should
show the distance to the convex hull (enthalpy difference to the convex hull) for all the metastable phases. 
(2) The experimental results by Muramatsu in 2015 show that BaReH9 is the superconductor at 7 K at 100 GPa, whereas
the calculated results in this manuscript shows that BaReH9 is an insulator at the same pressure as mentioned on the line
98. The authors should discuss about this difference in the main text, comparing the experimental results and the calculated
ones. This information might be helpful for not only theoretical but also experimental researchers. If BaReH9 becomes a
superconductor at 100 GPa or higher pressure, I recommend that the authors add the results to Table 1.

(3) On the line 146, the unit of the distance between hydrogens is missing. Is it 1.0 angstrom? 
(4) On the DOS data of Cmmm Ba2ReH8 shown in Fig. 3, in spite of the same structure, the peak intensity at around the
Fermi level decreases owing to the shifting of the flat bands near the high symmetry point Z when the pressure increases
from (a) 100 to (b) 300 GPa. What does the shifting come from? If the authors have any idea, they should comment on this

(5) As the authors are stating in the manuscript, it is also important to explore high-Tc superconductivity at moderate

(6) In Table 1, the authors should add the superconductivity data of P2/m BaReH12 at 100 GPa if the structure is
dynamically stable at this pressure, which makes the comparison between Pm-3 and P2/m clearer. In addition, the authors
should explain in more detail
　
why the symmetry breaking from Pm-3 to P2/m causes the decrease of the superconductivity
because this point is one of the key factors for the design of high-Tc superconducting hydrides. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Observing a trend that many superconducting ternary hydride predictions result from mixing transition metal with other
elements, the authors present computational work on the Ba-Re-H system. 
They show that the simple-cubic phase of BaReH12, is stable and has a superconducting transition temperature of around
100K at 100GPa. 
They then propose rules for the design of superconducting hydrides; the HTMs should have an odd number of electrons,
high symmetry and weak H-H bonding. 
The main result is solid, and well founded by established methods, and I recommend it for publication. 
However, the text will require some modifications to improve the English, and some minor technical clarifications outlined
below. 
1. Line 146 is missing a unit of distance. 
2. To further solidify the assertion that odd-numbered electron counts lead to superconductivity, the authors should explicitly
provide some examples of even-numbered electron count structures. 
3. In general, I found it difficult to follow the elector counting. Particularly, it may help to remind the readers how many
electrons are in (e.g.) [ReH8]4-. 
4. On this point, the authors should clarify further. How can one design a superconductor by targeting odd-electron count
HTMs when the electron count depends on both the structure of the HTM and the chemistry of the ‘spectator’ atom, in the
language of Zurek and co-workers in ref.33. 
5. The argument supporting high symmetry as a requirement is not explained very much. Perhaps specific degeneracies in
the band structure in Figure 3. should be highlighted. 
6. Line 150: The authors should clarify what they mean by ‘disorderd’. 
7. It would be nice if the authors could explain and clarify further the transition between the Pm-3 and P2m phases of
BaReH12, as it sees a dramatic decrease in the Tc, despite a relatively small structural changes. Is the SC surpressed by
H2 molecules forming, or by breaking the symmetry, or by both? 
8. Line 170: Is there more specific examples of the ‘flat-band-steep-band’ rule the authors can point to?
