Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This work by She et al. provides a comprehensive longitudinal examination of S. aureus (SA) colonization and infection
among patients in the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia NICU. Progress in infection control and mitigation has stalled over
the past 10 years pointing to severe knowledge gaps in our understanding of carriage and transmission. This work provides
a significant contribution to that end. The rationale for the study is sound, the methods are solid, and the results and
conclusion are mostly appropriate. 
Methodological strengths of this work include a large number of children sampled over multiple time points, collection from
asymptomatic carriage as well as infections, focus on all SA (not just MRSA), extensive patient metadata data, and use of
whole genome sequencing (WGS) for high-resolution assessment of evolutionary links. Such large-scale sampling that
includes longitudinal samples and WGS is extremely rare! 
The attempt to tie strain types to transmission, invasive disease, and persistence is noteworthy with extremely valuable
potential to translate surveillance efforts into mitigation, and disease prevention. While there have been a small number of
studies that explored genetic features associated with the transition from colonization to invasion, pathogenicity, and
infection persistence (https://doi.org/10.1101/gr.165415.113, https://doi.org/10.1128/msystems.00378-22,
https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.77195, https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.01000-07, https://doi.org/10.1016/J.CHOM.2023.03.009), I am
not aware of studies that link strains (or their components) to asymptomatic persistence and transmission. This is an
important line of current research efforts to which this work will make a valuable contribution. 
Inclusion of spatial data is also a strength (but see my next point) as it adds to evidence that patient-patient transmission is
occurring, although it is difficult to understand how direct patient-to-patient transmission occurs among infants in such an
environment (with no direct interactions). I appreciate the authors pointing out that transmission could be mediated by
contaminated environments/equipment, and caregivers, but what other mechanisms of transmission seem possible? Could
direct transmission be occurring or transition from a parent to infants that are not theirs? 
In my opinion, author’s use of spatial data to further parse the effects of distance on transmission is not warranted or needed.
While the distance of spread within each NICU section may provide some biological insight into mechanisms of spread, it is
hard for me to imagine why distances between NICU sections are considered when an infant may be transported just as
easily between distant and adjacent sections. As such, while the presence of a colonized/infected individual within a section
increases risk to others in the same section, the impact of larger distances (between sections) on transmission is likely
completely anthropogenic. I suggest that the authors remove these additional analyses (including the rescaled phylogeny)
as they complicated this work and don’t seem to be needed to support the conclusions – Alternatively, provide better
justification for their inclusion. 
Perhaps the most important weakness of this work is that there is limited discussion of an alternative (null) hypothesis: By
chance alone (not due to any intrinsic genetic characteristic or adaptive evolution), some clones will have better fitness (at
some point they were able to proliferate – perhaps shed in higher numbers by a single source, or were in an infant that was
placed near others – in other words, they were in the right place at the right time). A high-fitness clone would be expected to
be more likely to colonize additional persons, be shed into the environment, and persist for longer periods of time - even in
the absence of any adaptive differences between strains. Importantly, such clones would also be more likely to cause
invasive disease simply by persisting in an environment with many immunosuppressed individuals subject to invasive
procedures. As such, while transmission seems to predict invasive disease (as stated in the title), this may not be due to the
presence of certain strains or genes, but rather because SA is widely circulating. Authors need to better modulate the

discussion of their results in light of this – the single sentence in line 270 is not sufficient. If indeed this null hypothesis is
correct, then simply monitoring the load of SA around the NICU may be easier and more useful than monitoring for specific
strains or genes. These two scenarios are not mutually exclusive. 
A few comments: 
About SNP thresholds: I see these as being a guide for identifying transmission events or even intra-host diversity. It is
unfortunate that the term “threshold” is commonly used as it suggests a hard cut-off rather than a guide. It is widely
recognized that the resolution in any clade is maximized when SNP discovery and phylogenetic analysis is restricted to
members of that clade, and thus a hierarchical approach to zoom in on a given clade is widely practiced. However, the
application of this concept has been slow in guiding transmission determination. While this method is not novel, I have never
seen such a thorough demonstration of this concept – and this will be of value to a broad set of readers. In short, the authors
show that the concept of a “threshold” is not valid. I would thus encourage the authors to use their analyses as a justification
for moving beyond the “threshold” concept. Can the authors think of a more appropriate descriptor than a “threshold
approach”? I think that authors can state that the number of SNPs found within a clone varied between x and y and this was
in line with previous work, instead of applying a “threshold”. Also, your hierarchical snp threshold approach may be better
described as being “clade-specific” rather than “corrected for phylogenetic structure” (Line 113). 
The recovery of an isolate in the environment that belongs to the same clone as a person does not strongly implicate an
environmental reservoir as claimed in line 187 or indicate the resilience of potential environmental reservoirs (line 190). A
more likely scenario is that colonized persons are shedding SA into the environment – explaining why SA can be found in
the environment despite persistent cleaning. 
In the discussion of potential limitations (line 280), authors could include the limited number of isolates sampled from each
individual which does not provide a representative sample of the within-host population. Population sequencing is becoming
more attainable and can show directionality of spread, identification of sources, and knowledge of missing transmission
links. Recent work has shown that mixed strains within a single host are more common than previously thought and
sampling only a single isolate to infer transmission will underestimate transmission. 
Minor points: 
Why use the term “geospatial” instead of just “spatial”? 
Line 253 and 406 – define what is meant by a “conservative” cutoff to eliminate any ambiguity. I presume that this is a low
number? 
Line 388 – “any of the rest members” should presumably be “any of the other members”.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Thank you for the opportunity to review the manuscript submitted by Planet and colleagues that reports on the genomic
relationships between environmental, human colonisation and human infection isolates of Staphylococcus aureus in a high-
volume two-centre Neonatal Intensive Care Unit over a three-year period. As the authors report, whole-genome sequencing
(WGS) has been a useful tool to investigate outbreaks involving transmission of Staphylococcus aureus, though there are
fewer data available on the transmission dynamics involving colonisation isolates outside of identified outbreak contexts. 
I will acknowledge that I am a clinician who oversees our hospital infection control program and have extensive experience
using genomics to infer transmission, although I have not assessed the bioinformatic methods in detail by downloading the
code and source data to replicate the analyses. However, the authors have presented sufficient detail in their methods that I
was able to follow how their analysis was undertaken and bioinformatic tools used. 
My main criticism is that the aim or the purpose of the study was not explicitly stated and consequently, there is a sense that
the study could have been better designed had the research questions been clearly defined at the start of the study.
Although the authors pose some interesting thoughts for consideration, it is not clear what new information from this study
could be directly used to inform clinical practice or subsequent genomics investigations. In the discussion, the authors point
to their inclusion of both MRSA and MSSA as a novel aspect of this study, though the analyses were not particularly targeted
towards comparisons between MRSA and MSSA and similar to other studies, seemed to be intended to be generalisable to
all isolates of Staphylococcus aureus. 
I have included some other points for consideration intended to help improve the quality of the manuscript. 
Major comments: 
1. I could not find a statement of approval from an ethics committee or IRB for this study - could this please be provided in
line with journal policy? 
2. The sampling strategy was possibly designed out of convenience rather than directed towards answering a particular

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

research question - possibly due to the unclear aims of the study. For example, if aiming to compare persistence and
invasive isolates, this would be better assessed with a systematic sampling strategy to rigorously identify all colonisation at
a minimum (e.g. all patients at regular intervals, multiple sample collection sites), with inferences around transmission
including all potential sources of incursion (patients, staff, visitors, environment), but could have been undertaken over a
shorter period of time. The limitations of the sampling strategy resulting in a large amount of missing data should be
acknowledged and discussed in detail: 
a) Due to the intermittent (3-monthly or monthly) sampling, it is difficult to know how many index patients were unsampled, or
whether the transmission could have been introduced through alternative sources e.g. staff, visitors, environmental sources. 
b) The interesting aspects around clusters involving invasive isolates being more persistent than pure colonisation clusters
is possibly influenced by ascertainment bias - given colonisation isolates were only obtained monthly, many of the clusters
may have only contained a few isolates as shown in Figure 6a and 6b. More routine sampling for colonisation may have
identified additional patients to inform persistence. 
c) From an epidemiological perspective, it is difficult to compare and interpret colonisation rates when surveillance/sampling
rates differ. The authors have presented colonisation rates as a number of colonised patients per 1000 patient-days.
However, this is typically only done when there is routine longitudinal surveillance of every patient or where every incidence
is captured rather than intermittent point-prevalence surveys - these cross-sectional data would be better presented as a
proportion of admitted patients colonised instead. 
d) Some studies have previously attempted to impute missing data to understand transmission e.g.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40256930/ 
3. The inference of transmission from the date of their most recent common genomic ancestor for transmission clusters
(Figure 2) is based on the assumption that genomic emergence is equivalent to epidemiological emergence - however,
phylogenetic trees are not transmission trees (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28100788/). For example, the authors have
mapped dates of the most recent common ancestor (tMRCA) for transmission clusters against admission dates for putative
index cases for each cluster. However, the date a bacterial strain "emerges" through genetic evolution is independent of the
movement of the bacterium's host (the patient). Furthermore, the first patient detected with S aureus colonisation is not
necessarily the source of transmission in an outbreak. Given the timespan, how would transmission within the NICU be
distinguished from vertical transmission (e.g. two neonates acquiring S aureus from the same mother, or transmission
between mothers rather than between neonates)? 
4. The underlying message that patients who are closer in proximity and in time are more likely to have the same strain is not
a particularly novel message. I think the more interesting messages lie in the exceptions to the rule - why were some isolates
from different patients separated in time and space (e.g. by 3 years in different sections) part of the same clone? Were these
patients related to others in the cluster or were there opportunities for transmission outside the NICU? 
5. More detail is needed on sample collection: How were environmental surfaces sampled? Was this after routine cleaning?
Were surfaces immediately adjacent to current patients excluded? It is interesting that these strains were isolated from the
environment so long after patients colonised with isolates - e.g. Mar 2024 (patient) -> May 2024 (environmental), Jan 2024
(patient) -> May 2024 (environmental). Many ICU surfaces are cleaned on a regular basis daily and persistent detection
suggests another undetected persistent reservoir e.g. staff, other patients. I think a result subheading that "Transmission
clusters CAN persist in environmental reservoirs" would be better representation of the data - <10% of of isolates recovered
from environmental sampling were S aureus, and only 3/69 (<5%) of transmission clusters were represented. 
6. More information on the infection prevention and control measures used within the NICUs during the study would assist
readers for context and interpretation. Were there any changes in policy during the study? For patients identified with
MSSA/MRSA colonisation, what measures were undertaken? In the discussion, lines 272-274 suggest that infection
prevention and control measures could reduce the risk of invasive infection - this should be the risk of transmission. The only
measure that has been shown to reduce the risk of invasive infection is decolonisation (in adults colonised with S aureus),
though to my knowledge this has not been demonstrated in neonates. Note there are also risks associated with
chlorhexidine administration in neonates. 
7. From an implementation in clinical practice perspective, what was the median time from colonisation to invasive infection
and from colonisation to onwards transmission? Would it have been feasible to identify colonisation, sequence the isolate
and implement decolonisation measures to reduce the risk of invasive infection, or control measures to reduce the risk of
onwards transmission? If not feasible, some of the discussion could be reframed around more realistic implementations of
the approach - for example, triggering infection control interventions such as point prevalence surveys across the unit,
universal decolonisation, enhanced unit cleaning and staff re-education around hand hygiene to interrupt persistence of
particular strains associated with a high incidence of invasive disease. 
8. Figures - in general the figures are detailed and contain a great deal of information. However, I feel the details are lost in
the interpretation of the figures. Firstly, many of the panels were too small at standard resolution to read the text or discern
differences between colours or points on plots. I took a long time to interpret the meaning of several panels within the figures
and how they contributed to the main messages of the manuscript. For example, Figure 3 appears interesting, but I am
struggling to understand panel 3e and what conclusions should be drawn from this. I understand the concept is that over
time with repeated transmission events, there is greater dispersion of a strain/clone, but I think this is already implied. The
depiction of this in the figure as a cumulative distance seems a bit farcical given the dimensions of the NICU physical
environment, and inaccurate due to the inability to track the colonisation status of individual patients post discharge from the
NICU.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Thank you for the opportunity to review the revised manuscript submitted by Planet and colleagues. The authors have made
significant revisions with additional analyses and details of methods to enhance the manuscript and I commend them for
their significant efforts. I also thank them for responding to my comments and providing further details around the study
design, sampling methods, and the data they present in their manuscript. 
However, I would respectfully disagree with this statement (abstract): "specific clusters of S. aureus strains that were
associated with invasive infection had higher rates of transmission" and the subtitle in the results: "Enhanced transmissibility
predicts risk of invasive infections": 
1. To conclude there was a higher rate of transmission would require comparable denominators – for example, in
epidemiology, rates of transmission would be assessed by comparing the number of new infections per number of
susceptible people exposed. However, the sampling denominators for invasive and colonising clusters is different -
colonising-cluster isolates were only captured monthly, but invasive-cluster isolates were obtained continuously. To
highlight this imbalance with a more extreme example, would the same conclusions have been drawn (that invasive-cluster
isolates are associated with higher rates of transmission) if the screening had only been performed annually? I believe that
the likelihood of many colonising-cluster isolates being missed between annual screening samples would have clouded
those conclusions – the same applies for 3-monthly and monthly screening. Did invasive-cluster isolates really transmit to
more patients, or could patients with colonisation-only isolates have transmitted to the same number of patients but not get
picked up as frequently because they stayed for shorter periods in the NICU, were sampled less frequently, and were
discharged home without being sampled? 
2. There is also a temporal bias and confounder that needs to be accounted for in epidemiological studies of transmission
rates. Under the definitions used in the study, any cluster that included a single BSI isolate was considered an "invasive
cluster". Multiple studies have shown an association between bacteraemia and length of stay in NICU - infants who stay
longer provide more opportunities to transmit to other individuals in the same environment who will be sampled as part of the
study, compared to those who are discharged early from the NICU and transmit to other individuals, but who are not
captured in the study. Furthermore, the larger a cluster grows, the greater the chance of a single invasive isolate – consider a
scenario where there were 99 colonising isolates in a cluster that result in 100 transmission events, but the 100th isolate is
an invasive isolate. Following the 100th isolate, there are no further transmission events because the neonate is
commenced on antibiotics and discharged from NICU. In this scenario, the cluster is labelled an "invasive cluster", even
though transmission resulted from predominantly colonising isolates. Perhaps another approach to consider would be to
define clusters as "invasive" or "colonising" on proportion of bloodstream isolates in the cluster with clusters having a higher
proportion of invasive isolates labelled as "invasive clusters". Although this will still miss some unsampled colonising
isolates, there will be less temporal bias. 
3. Although I recognise the additional analyses undertaken by the authors to try and demonstrate the absence of bias in
response to my previous comment #6, I think these analyses raise additional questions: 
i. Firstly, regarding the use of TransPhylo to compare unsampled populations - as the main aim of TransPhylo is to infer the
transmission tree between sampled individuals, any unsampled individual that does not lead to one sampled individual is
pruned from the transmission tree (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33617114/). Given the genetic variation among
colonisation isolates is similar to the variation among invasive isolates over a similar period of time, I would expect
TransPhylo to underestimate the proportion of unsampled individuals contributing to transmission. 
ii. Secondly, although I am a clinician and not familiar with the msm package in R, I presume that the HMM depends heavily
on the priors supplied to the model. Would this mean that the model performance is dependent on the observed data, and
thus difficult to accurately infer the influence of unsampled patients? 
4. The wording used in several sections is biased towards these conclusions and overstates the findings based on the data
presented. For example in the discussion, the text states "In our analyses, strains that colonized or infected more patients
over time were more likely to be invasive." However, this particular wording is misleading. My interpretation of the data is
that isolates that were part of a cluster with "invasive potential" were more frequently detected in the NICU, than isolates part
of clusters that were not observed to have "invasive potential". 
Unfortunately, I think it is difficult to completely avoid the limitations of the study design (retrospective observational cohort
study), but I believe the accuracy of the manuscript would be improved by acknowledging the sources of bias, adding
caution against over-interpretation of the data, and proposing more rigorous assessment in appropriately designed studies.
The genomic and transmission analyses are very comprehensive and should be commended, but these cannot completely
cover for limitations introduced by sampling methodology. Given the issues outlined above with assessing and comparing
rates of transmission with the reported study design, I believe the title, discussion and conclusions of the manuscript should
be tempered to better match the data presented.

Response to Reviewers for Nature Communications manuscript

We have thoroughly revised the manuscript to address all concerns raised.

acknowledged sampling limitations; (2) incorporated transmission tree analysis to substantiate our inference of 
transmission cluster sources (as suggested by Reviewer 1); (3) moved the spatial distance rescaled phylogeny 
analysis to the supplementary materials with accompanying discussion, following both Reviewers' 
recommendations; (4) enhanced figure clarity throughout the manuscript; and (5) described comparative 
analysis of SNP thresholds and within-cluster SNP distance distributions against published studies, providing 
preliminary validation for our Thresher approaches.

caregivers of patients, and shared equipment that is moved around the NICU from patient to patient

to explicitly address alternative transmission routes.
