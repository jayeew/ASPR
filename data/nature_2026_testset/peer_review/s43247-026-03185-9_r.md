REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors use historical DTR changes as an emergent constraint on future DTR changes across CMIP6 models. The
proposed EC yields a substantial reduction in the range of future projections, and the discussion of potential mechanisms
could lead to valuable insights on the drivers of DTR changes. However, the manuscript does not meet the standards
required for publication in several important aspects. 
1. Too much data is only shown and discussed on a very aggregated level – for example, there is no scatter plot of the
supposed correlation that forms the core of the proposed EC. This is of particular importance here, since the observed global
mean trend over the analysed historical period (not shown or quantified in the manuscript) is possibly positive, whereas
models agree on a negative trend. 
Radiative and turbulent heat fluxes are analysed as monthly means, but the turbulent fluxes change sign over the diurnal
cycle, and the magnitude of their diurnal cycle should be more relevant to DTR than their mean. 
2. The manuscript lacks clarity in the description of methods and results. 
For example, in Figure 1c: Is the normalized or full DTR regressed onto the trend? And is the unit given actually for the
quantity on the plot or for the trend onto which the DTR change is regressed? 
l. 381, is this the multi-model mean or the ensemble of one specific model? 
Where is the so-derived externally forced signal used, and which analyses use the full trends? 
3. The discussion of proposed mechanisms underlying the EC makes some interesting points, but this crucial part of the
work appears unfinished. In line 318, the authors note that models with larger historical trends project a smaller reduction in
the future. As noted under 1, it would be crucial to see the actual values this refers to – larger absolute trends, or does larger
mean smaller reductions? 
The manuscript does not suggest a clear mechanism explaining what properties and processes in models generate this
constraint, i.e. why models with a given historical signal produce a certain trend. This physical understanding is a crucial
point in building robust ECs.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

4. The robustness of the EC itself could be tested more thoroughly. One option would be to use the first ten years of scenario
runs to extend the historical record (and vary the historical timeframe), another would be to check if also holds in CMIP5
data. 
In addition to these issues, I often found the manuscript not to be very clear, and would recommend careful language editing
to ensure that each sentences precisely expresses a clear scientific point. I recommend to reject the manuscript in its present
state, but encourage resubmission to give the authors the opportunity to do justice to the potential of this research. 
Minor points: 
- The composited map-like region plots are difficult to read. Why not plot actual maps of quantities of interest? 
- Sensible and latent heat fluxes are turbulent, not radiative fluxes. (line 248 and elsewhere) 
- Line 290: Could the seasonal and regional signal be related to the melting point effect?
(https://doi.org/10.1029/2024GL109751) 
- Line 330: There is also a thermodynamic argument for reduced variability at mid-high latitudes in a warming climate
https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate2268 
- Line 373: why winter? 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
General comments: 
CMIP6 models are known to underestimate DTR at regional level when comparing to observations, and they do not capture
very well the observed spatio-temporal changes in DTR. In particular, they do not capture the recently observed increase in
DTR, and project a decrease instead. This implies that a study proposing an emergent constraint on models in regards to the
future projections of DTR is important. The article is well-written and the main arguments and conclusions are supported.
However, I have a few issues that I would like to see addressed before I can accept the manuscript for publication. 
Methods: 
Can the authors support better the use of the CRU TS observational data set or at least confirm the spatiotemporal features
that they have found by exploring another data set such as the 
Hadley Center Integrated Surface Data for example? 
Can the authors give an overview of how trends were estimated in this study, as this information is currently missing from the
methodology section of the paper and it is highly relevant in this case? 
Results: 
Figure 2: Can you comment on why the emergent constraint suggested in this study does not seem to work for the rest of the
IPCC regions? 27 regions out of 44 is a majority, but there are still a number of regions for which the proposed constraint
seems to not be effective or applicable, and this is important to explain, especially considering the implications for the global
results. 
Missing citations: 
The authors need to cite the following relevant study by Huang et al. (2023) in GRL “Increasing Global Terrestrial Diurnal
Temperature Range for 1980-2021”. Results in this study appear to agree with results in the proposed study, and given the
fact that it also proposes an emergent constraint of CMIP6 models based on historical observations, it is important that the
authors highlight the differences in their proposed methods for emergent constraints, as well as the potential advantages of
their method. 
Additionally, given the overlap of the analysis of DTR changes based on CMIP6 models, the authors need to also cite Wang
and Clow (2020) https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/clim/33/19/jcliD190897.xml, and discuss their results in the
context of results presented therein. 
Minor comments: 
Line 102: change to “high-emissions scenario”

Specifically, the emergent relationship arises from the cloud –radiation–DTR linkage. In the 
historical period, we observe that a notable decrease in cloud cover is associated with increasing

influence DTR primarily through their control on surface shortwave radiation (RSDS), models

DTR both historically and in the future.
