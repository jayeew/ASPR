Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this study, Liu et al. report an analysis of single cell data of hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPSCC) patients
receiving TPF chemotherapy, aiming at the identification of the cellular populations that mostly influence the response to
therapy. They identify a specific NK subset with ZNF683 high expression enriched in HPSCC patients responsive to
chemotherapy. By cell
‒
cell interaction analysis, they find that ZNF683+ NK cells exhibit the most pronounced cellular
crosstalk with effector memory T cells (Tem). Additionally, they also find an increase in GZMK+CD8+ T cells proportion after
treatment in responders compared with non-responders. Overall, these findings could help us understand the mechanisms
underlying chemoresistance in HPSCC. However, the manuscript suffers from major limitations. The analysis is overall
superficial, and current data presented are insufficient to support their conclusions, with limited insights adding to current
knowledge. 
Major points: 
1. The identification of ZNF683+ NK population via flow cytometry is problematic, with a presumption that double negative of
CX3CR1 and CD117 are recognized as ZNF683+ NK cells without measuring ZNF683 expression itself or any other
reliable surface markers. It’s quite important to validate this NK population to claim it’s unique roles in contributing patient’s
responsiveness to therapy. Thus, more supported data are required to address this point. 
2. The authors claim that ZNF683+ NK cells are able to promote CD8+ T-cell expansion by recruiting and activating CD8+
Tem cells. If this assumption is true, why there is no change in CD8+ T-cell proportion in responders with increased
ZNF683+ NK cells? (Supplementary Fig. 8A) Besides, current data is insufficient to support the modulating role of ZNF683+
NK cells on T-cell function, which is merely based on bioinformatic cell-cell interaction analysis. More evidences are
definitely needed to strengthen the conclusion. 
3. It’s entirely unclear how ZNF683+ NK cells recruit and modulate function of CD8+ T cells. What possible signaling
pathway or specific molecule might be involved? More exploring and validating experiments are required to demonstrate
their interactions. 
4. ZNF683+ NK cells are generally considered as tissue-resident NK cells, which are actually functionally competent with
capacity to produce cytokines (PMID: 31451696). This NK subpopulation has adaptive immune features with robust
preferential expansion in response to viral infection, and is shown to exert antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)
towards tumor or viral infection (PMID: 29166586, 28794018, 33602683). However, in present study, the authors claim that
ZNF683+ NK cells are non-cytotoxic, but instead exhibit an immune regulating function through their interaction with T cells.
Such contradiction makes their statement unconvincing regarding the exact function of this NK subset. 
5. The authors have employed a mouse model to demonstrate that depleting NK cells by anti-NK1.1 antibody abrogates
TPF chemotherapy-induced tumour regression. As ZNF683+ NK cells are the only subset significantly associated with
patients’ response to therapy, the authors should consider conduct similar in vivo experiment to test whether this NK
subpopulation is required for chemotherapy efficacy. 
6. Based on the potential role of ZNF683+ NK cells on activating CD8+ Tem cells, the authors aim to confirm the T-cell
functional status. But why the authors turn to examine PD-1+GZMK+CD8+ T cell phenotype, as PD-1 is generally
considered as a marker of exhausted T cells?

Minor points: 
1. How many samples were used in validation cohort? The authors should add the sample size in Fig.1B. 
2. In line 764, the authors wrote, “GZMK+CD8+ T and CXCL13 Tex CD8+ T cells exhibited significantly enhanced inter
cellular communication with ZNF683+CD8+ T cells”. It should be “ZNF683+ NK cells” according to the context. 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript presents interesting and potentially significant findings about the immune dynamics within HPV-positive
squamous cell carcinoma (HPSCC). However, several areas require substantial improvement to enhance the clarity,
validity, and reproducibility of the study. My comments are detailed below, organized by specific feedback on figures and the
text, followed by broader recommendations concerning methodology and interpretation. 
Specific Comments: 
Line 122-126: The population of HPV-specific CD8 T cells mentioned requires a more in-depth explanation regarding its
significance in HPSCC. I recommend providing context as to why these CD8 T cells are critical in this particular carcinoma
and clearly articulating how they differ from other T-cell subtypes. 
Lines 128-130: Previous studies are mentioned without appropriate citations. Including references to support these
statements will enhance the rigor of your arguments. Please add these references for accuracy and completeness. 
Line 343: It is essential to indicate the specific company source for each antibody used. Please include this information to
maintain transparency and reproducibility. I couldn’t find a key resource table. 
Lines 347-352: There is a mention of homemade antibodies, but it remains unclear how their effectiveness was validated.
More detail is needed on the evaluation process. Additionally, the source for Granzyme B should be indicated, as the
company origin is not provided. 
Figure 1A: The term "paired" is ambiguous. Please clarify. 
Figure 1C: The timing between radiographies is not indicated. Please specify the duration between both images for proper
interpretation. 
Figure 2A: There is a typo here: "Lymphoidcytes" should be corrected. 
Figure 2B: The colors in the image lack proper legend descriptions. Please specify which cell type each color represents. 
Figure 2C: The visualization is unclear, and the combined pre- and post-clustering analysis does not provide an easy-to-
interpret distinction. Consider presenting this figure differently, perhaps with separated visualizations or clearer labeling, to
improve the interpretability. 
Figure 3C: A notable NKT cell population is present, but it is not addressed in the discussion. Do these cells play a role in
tumor shrinkage? Investigating and including this would add value. 
Figure 3D: There are visible differences in NK cell localization between responders and non-responders. The responder
group shows NK cells spread throughout the tissue, while in non-responders, NK cells cluster around the tumor area. This
may imply functional differences—such as impaired migration or activity—between groups. More discussion on the
implications of these observations, including a potential follow-up post-treatment, would improve depth. 
Figure 3E: Typo: "protocol." 
Figure 4I: The presence of non-enriched immune profiles among some responders is evident. Discuss the variation
observed within responders, particularly patients with low immune cell enrichment. 
Figure 4I and 4J: There seems to be a clear distinction between responder subgroups. This requires further discussion,
particularly regarding variations in immune profiles and NK cell scores. Please provide additional analysis and elaborate on
these variations. 
Figure 5C: The NK cell population in responders appears to have subgroups. A more in-depth analysis of these subgroups
could help elucidate the differences between high and low NK responders. 
Figure 5D: Colocalization of green and white fluorescence is often absent. If these markers are meant to be on the same NK
cells, should they not colocalize? Please address whether this is expected or represents a methodological limitation.
Additionally, explain why validation was performed on only ZNF683+ NK cells and not on other subtypes. 
Figure 7B: The immune cells are not in direct contact with the tumor cells, raising questions about the mechanism of
cytotoxicity. More discussion is needed on how immune cells exert cytotoxic effects without direct contact. 
General major Comments: 
• Supplementary S4B: A significant reduction in weight in the NK-depleted group is noted, but no statistical analysis is
provided. Please include an appropriate statistical test between TPF and TPF+anti-NK1.1. 
• Role of T Cells: It would strengthen the study to deplete T cells alone and in combination with NK cells to confirm their
respective contributions to tumor dynamics. 
• ZNF683 and CX3CR1 Identification: The identification of ZNF683 as a canonical marker for NK cells is unclear. Additional
details on how these markers were selected are necessary. 
• RNA Velocity Analysis: The RNA velocity analysis does not convincingly support the suggested developmental trajectory.
Provide more evidence or elaborate on these conclusions. 
• Figure S6B: The labeling mismatch between IL7R and KIT needs to be corrected, as it currently does not align with the
main text. 
• Statistical Analysis: Each figure legend must specify the statistical methods used to validate the findings. 
• Supplementary S12B: Highlighting the interaction between ZNF683+ NK cells and GZMK+CD8+ cells lacks support, given
the absence of evidence of interaction. This should be revisited, and additional evidence should be provided if this
interaction is indeed crucial. 
• Data Analysis and Conclusion: The conclusions appear inconsistent regarding the roles of T and NK cells. Early on, T

cells are implied to have no effect, while later they are seen as crucial. This contradiction should be resolved or clarified. 
Summary 
The findings presented have the potential to significantly impact the field by advancing our understanding of the immune
dynamics in HPSCC, particularly with respect to NK cells and T cells. However, there are notable gaps in clarity,
reproducibility, and data interpretation that need to be addressed before the manuscript is ready for publication. 
Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
TPF induction chemotherapy is recognized as the standard treatment protocol for HPSCC; however, a subset of patients still
displays resistance to chemotherapy. The challenge of accurately predicting the response to induction chemotherapy
remains a significant issue in clinical practice. This article offers a novel perspective on sensitivity prediction assessed by
the NK baseline. The authors used paired pre- and post-chemotherapy samples for scRNA-sq. The results provide critical
insights into the temporal immune changes, particularly in relation to the dynamics of GZMK+ T cells that are closely
associated with the ZNF683+ NK baseline. These findings have not been reported elsewhere. In this respect, the findings in
this paper are intriguing, innovative and enlightening. 
The following concerns are still required to be addressed: 
1. In Figure 1, the authors present representative imaging of both the chemotherapy response group and the non-response
group, which is impressive. In order to provide readers with a better understanding of the treatment information for these
patients, it is recommended that the authors include the number of chemotherapy cycles (typically 2 cycles), details of the
dosages used and the timing of imaging assessments (generally 1 month post-treatment) in the methodology section. 
2. Figure 1E showed the detail of patient information, and there are four patients missing post-chemotherapy samples. The
reason for this absence should be clarified, whether it is due to complete remission after chemotherapy or issues with
sample quality?? 
3. The experiment involving tumor-bearing mice clearly demonstrates the essential role of NK cells in mediating the effects
of TPF treatment. NK cells have long been recognized for their innate immune function in limiting tumor progression and
development, and recent studies have begun to reveal their regulatory effects on the tumor microenvironment. The rationale
for the timing point of NK antibody injection in tumor-bearing mice should be clarified to enhance readers' understanding of
the aim of the experimental design. Why not use the blocking antibody at the beginning of tumor establishment?? 
4. The authors validated their innovative findings using FACS and mIHC. This finding is attractive, as obtaining samples
related to chemotherapy resistance and post-chemotherapy conditions can be quite challenging. Nevertheless, from a
rigorous scientific standpoint, I recommend that the authors consider adding approximately five more samples to each group
to enhance the reliability of their validation data. 
5. The author investigates the chemosensitivity of HPSCC from the perspective of NK cells, which is an intriguing viewpoint.
Beyond predicting sensitivity to TPF treatment, NK cells—supported by animal model experiments—showed potential
therapeutic value in the treatment of HNSCC in the future. The therapeutic value of NK cells should be strengthened in the
discussion section. 
6. Adding more precise annotations to the figures could help readers better grasp the article's structure and flow. For
instance, in Figures 3C, 7C, and Supplementary Figure 13C, it would be helpful to specify "gated on CD45+ live cells." 
7. In Supplementary Figures 9-11, the authors use pathway scores represented by a dot heatmap on a UMAP framework,
which effectively illustrates the functional characteristics of each cell type in a clear, visual format. However, this approach is
not described in the methods section. 
8. Supplementary Figure 7 utilizes data from public databases. Please provide details on the data sources in both the
methods section and figure legends. 
9. Li et al. revealed the immune microenvironment of hypopharyngeal cancer for the first time by using single-cell sequences
(PMID: 37503341), which is suggested to be cited in the background. 
10. It is recommended to add the biological validation of ZNF683+NK cells increase the killing effect on tumor cells by
regulating GZMK+ CD8+ T cells. 
11. At present, there are some authoritative literature reports on ZNF683+ NK cells (PMID: 37738974), and they are
suggested to cite them in the discussion section.

(Remarks to the Author) 
This is a well-conceived and timely manuscript presenting a novel role for ZNF683
⁺
 NK cells in licensing CD8
⁺
 T cell
responses to chemotherapy in hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma 
(HPSCC). The authors integrate scRNA-seq,
spatial analysis, and functional validation in both human and murine models to support their conclusions. This revised
manuscript version presents a significantly improved study detailing the immunological mechanisms underlying
chemotherapy response in HPSCC. Using single-cell RNA-seq profiling, the authors uncover the role of ZNF683
⁺
 NK cells
as regulators of chemotherapy efficacy and establish a mechanistic link between these NK cells and the activation of
GZMK
⁺
 CD8
⁺
 effector memory T cells. Notably, the authors perform longitudinal profiling of paired pre-/post-TPF
chemotherapy samples, supported by multiplex immunohistochemistry, mouse models, and in vitro co-culture experiments.
The added validation and clearer methodology considerably enhance the manuscript’s clarity and impact. However, several
critical issues need to be addressed before the manuscript can be considered for publication. 
Major Concerns 
1. Unclear origin of NK Cells in Figure 4A 
The manuscript does not explicitly state whether the 7,539 NK cells used to define the NK1/NK2/NK3 subsets in Figure 4A
were derived from human HPSCC tumor samples or from the murine model. 
This must be clearly clarified in both the Results text and figure legend, as this data forms the basis for downstream
functional and clinical interpretation. 
2. Lack of immunological annotation for NK1/NK2/NK3 subsets 
The authors define transcriptional clusters NK1 (ZNF683
⁺
), NK2 (CX3CR1
⁺
), and NK3 (KIT
⁺
), but do not adequately explain
what these subsets represent immunologically. 
NK1 = likely tissue-resident/memory-like NK cells 
NK2 = likely terminally differentiated cytotoxic NK cells 
NK3 = likely progenitor-like or immature NK cells 
This clarification is essential for contextualizing their relevance to known NK cell biology and the proposed mechanism. 
3. The authors report a significant difference in NK1 infiltration between responders (Rs) and non-responders (NRs), but not
for NK2 or NK3. Given the small discovery cohort (n = 12, including only 5 NRs), the study may be underpowered to detect
more subtle differences. NK3, representing immature NK cells, is known to infiltrate tumors. The authors should
acknowledge this limitation and avoid overinterpreting the exclusivity of NK1. 
4. In Figure 5A, the same or similar colors appear to be used for tumor and non-tumor compartments, making spatial
interpretation difficult. Use clearly distinguishable colors to label tumor nests vs. stroma. Quantitative analysis of ZNF683
⁺
and ZNF683- NK cell infiltration in tumor vs. stromal compartments should be performed, stratified by response group (Rs vs.
NRs). This spatial quantification is critical to substantiate the claim that ZNF683
⁺
 NK cells localize primarily in tumors of
responders. 
5. ZNF683 is used as a marker of NK1 cells, but it remains unclear whether ZNF683 itself plays a functional role in NK cell
licensing of CD8
⁺
 T cells. The authors should discuss this limitation and, ideally, support their claims with loss- or gain-of-
function data (e.g., ZNF683 knockdown in NK cells and impact on T cell activation).

3. Statistical Reporting. The manuscript frequently reports significant differences without precise p-values or effect sizes. 
4. Ensure consistent use of terms such as “ZNF683
