Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript entitled, "Leveraging trained immunity for the epigenetic and metabolic rewiring of macrophages to enhance
colorectal cancer vaccination by inducing an NK-cDC1 axis" reported that combined trained immunity and tumor vaccine led
to significant tumor killing using both mouse colon cancer models and human colon cancer organoids. The authors showed
that the combined trained immunity with vaccine therapeutic efficacy appears to be dependent on a robust macrophage-NK
cell-cDC axis. While these results have clear clinical translational potential, there are some concerns in this study. 
1. The role of cDC1 is unclear in this study. While the authors showed that cDC1 were accumulated in the tumor-bearing
mice treated with trained immunity plus PC vaccine and its correlation with enhanced T cell activity (Figure 2), the direct
evidence to show that cDC1 accumulation is due to trained macrophages is lacking. In addition, cDC1 in mice normally do
not express CD11b yet Figure 4 scRNAseq data showed cDC1 cluster using sorted CD11b+ cells from tumors. 
2. The role of T cells. The authors depleted macrophages and NK cells to demonstrate that trained immunity+PC therapeutic
efficacy was dependent on macrophages and NK cells (Figure 6). The authors should also deplete CD4/CD8 T cells to
determine the relative contribution of innate antitumor immunity and T cell antitumor immunity in animal tumor models. 
3. Figure 1A-E. These publicly available datasets are from mouse pancreas but not from spleen. The author should use
these data in caution as some of myeloid cell subsets may be tissue specific. 
4. Figure 2. The authors analyzed DCs and T cells from draining lymph nodes. The authors should also provide these data
from tumors. In addition, it is unclear whether these myeloid cells have "trained immunity" phenotype. 
5. Figure 3. It is nice to show metabolomics data. The authors may want to validate some of these pathways. For example,
performing Seahorse assay to see whether indeed glycolysis pathway is enhanced in trained myeloid cells. 
6. NK cells. The authors need to examine the functionality of NK cells, such as granzyme and perforin secretion. 
7. Figure 8. The authors may use trained monocytes cultured with PDOs to see whether trained myeloid cells also have
direct cancer killing activity. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study found that β-glucan-induced "trained immunity" enhances CRC vaccine efficacy by epigenetically and
metabolically reprogramming macrophages. Mechanistically, trained immunity shifts CD11b+ myeloid cells toward aerobic
glycolysis, while creatine metabolism provides the additional ATP required for sustained cytokine and chemokine
production. This reprogramming converts immunoregulatory macrophages into anti-tumor effectors that recruit NK cells via
CXCL9 and CXCL10, thereby enhancing cDC1 function. Finally, using human PBMC and patient-derived organoid co-
culture models, they found that trained immunity enhance antigen-specific responses and tumor cell killing. This study
presents a promising approach to overcoming the limitations of conventional immunotherapies in CRC by leveraging β-
glucan-induced trained immunity to enhance vaccine efficacy. The mechanistic explanation is interesting but many
statements/hypotheses are short of experimental evidence support. Specifically, several issues remain unresolved: 
1. Figure 2B showed slightly enhanced anti-tumor efficacy of vaccination after β-glucan training, while Figure 2M is the result
of a combination therapy approach. But for the same “Mock” group, the tumor growth is faster in therapeutic setting than the
prophylactic setting. Did the mice receive the same amount of CT26 cells in both settings?

2. In Extended Figure 4, the authors showed tumor growth curves for 8 mice, but for the statistical analysis of dendritic cell
phenotype in draining lymph nodes, they selected only 3 mice per group (Figure 2C). It's unclear whether these results come
from three independent experiments. The same issue appears in Figures 2M-N. The authors need to describe their
experimental methods in detail. 
3. Figure 2D, the authors need to provide statistical results. From the presented data, the cell numbers in each group appear
inconsistent. 
4. Figure 3, the authors show broad alterations in cellular metabolism caused by different treatments, but these data contain
speculative interpretations without solid evidence. For example: Lines 250-252: ECAR and OCR analysis would be desired
to support their claims. Lines 277-279: Could the authors use isotope-labeled glutamate to validate the glutamate pathway
as a metabolic hub? Although previous studies have shown glutamine is important for trained immunity, glucose metabolism
is more widely recognized as the metabolic foundation of trained immunity (PMID: 27866838, PMID: 25258083). The
authors need direct evidence to support their hypothesis. 
5. Line 347 appears to describe results from Figure 4H, not 3H. 
6. Similar to Figure 3, Figure 4 seems more descriptive and speculative without substantial experimental evidence. For
example, why didn't the authors use CXCL9/10 blocking antibodies to confirm that trained macrophages recruit NK cells via
CXCL9/10 (lines 347-349)? Is there direct evidence that NK cells recruit cDC1 cells by secreting CCL5/4 (lines 354-358)?
Although Figures 4A-C show increased NK and DC cell numbers in the Trained+PeptiCrad group, these results appear to
reflect overall changes in the immune cell landscape. Why did the authors consider NK and DC cells particularly important
in this process? Although Figure 6B shows that depletion of macrophages and NK cells significantly weakens the anti-tumor
effects of Trained+PC, would depletion of either macrophages or NK cells alone also promote tumor progression? 
7. The authors consider trained macrophages as key immune cells in the NK-DC axis for anti-tumor effects, why does
macrophage depletion alone show much weaker anti-tumor effects compared to simultaneous depletion of both
macrophages and NK cells (Figure 6B)? Extended Figure 10 shows effective macrophage depletion. The authors need to
clarify whether macrophages and NK cells have an upstream-downstream regulatory relationship or a parallel relationship.
Trained neutrophils also have anti-tumor functions (PMID: 33125892). Did the authors consider this in their study? 
8. Figure 4C, it's unclear whether this represents quantitative results from Figure 4B, but the proportion of cluster 20 in Figure
4C seems inconsistent with Figure 4B. The color for cluster “0” is almost same as cluster “20”. Please modify the color to
better differentiate them. 
9. In Figure 5, the authors propose potential mechanisms regulating trained immunity, but I think the experimental design is
confusing. The authors suggest creatine regulates trained immunity, but the author add rapamycin (mTOR/glycolysis
inhibitor), ascorbic acid (HIF-1α inhibitor), and 3-guanidinopropionic acid (GPA; creatine uptake inhibitor) during rest and
secondary adenovirus stimulation rather than during WPG training (Figure 5A). Classical trained immunity inhibition is
typically administered during training (Such as: PMID: 25258083, PMID: 29328908). The authors' experimental design can
only show that rapamycin, ascorbic acid and GPA inhibit effector functions during secondary stimulation, not the priming
stage when the immune memory undergoes induction during primary stimulation. The results in Figure 5I seem to confirm
my suspicion, since 3-GPA didn't inhibit H3K4me3 modification levels, indicating that 3-GPA likely doesn't inhibit trained
immunity in myeloid cells but rather inhibits effector immune responses during PC treatment. 
10. If the authors want to firmly prove their conclusion (lines 422-423), I suggest transplanting bone marrow cells or splenic
monocytes/macrophages from mice treated with WPG +3-GPA into recipient mice to observe whether they can enhance the
anti-tumor effects of PC. 
11. As the authors describe, "trained immunity is driven by long-lasting epigenetic modifications." Previous studies also
consider epigenetics as the core mechanism of innate immune memory (PMID: 25258085). In my opinion, the genome-wide
H3K4me3 modification analysis lacks precision, and the result complemented with ChIP-seq or ChIP-qPCR validation of
relevant gene H3K4me3 modifications would be more accurate. 
Minor issues: 
1. The manuscript contains several errors that need correction: For example: Line 85: "TAMS" should be "TAMs". Line 132:
“SINFEKL” should be “SIINFEKL”; Line 866: "PBMcs" should be "PBMCs". The authors should carefully proofread the
manuscript. 
2. It is suggested that the authors provide some technical background information or brief introduction about the “PeptiCrad
(PC)” vaccine platform. 
3.The Figure layout appears unpolished, with inconsistent magnification scales and font sizes among subpanels within the
same Figure. The authors should revise these elements to enhance readability.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The study by Hamdan et al. explores the potential of β-glucan-induced trained immunity to enhance colorectal cancer (CRC)
vaccination by modulating macrophage metabolism and promoting an NK-cDC1 axis. While the work addresses an
important gap in CRC immunotherapy, its impact is limited by concerns regarding novelty, mechanistic depth, and
experimental rigor. Below is a detailed critique organized by section. 
Major Critiques 
1. Novelty and Overlap with Prior Studies 
- Trained Immunity in Cancer: The use of trained immunity as a cancer vaccine adjuvant has been previously demonstrated,
notably by Wattenberg et al. (2023, Sci Immunol), who reported synergistic effects of β-glucan with CD40/Dectin-1 agonists
in pancreatic cancer. The current study does not sufficiently differentiate its findings from this prior work. 
- NK-cDC1 Axis: The mechanistic role of NK cells in recruiting cDC1s via CCL5/XCL1 is well-established (Böttcher et al.,
2018, Cell). The authors should clarify how their results extend beyond these known mechanisms. 
- Creatine Metabolism: Although the link between creatine metabolism and immune cell function is an emerging area, Peng
& Saito (2023, Front Immunol) recently reported similar findings in macrophages, raising questions about the originality of
this aspect. 
2. Mechanistic Gaps 
- Epigenetic vs. Metabolic Contributions: The assertion that trained immunity is driven by "epigenetic and metabolic
rewiring" lacks robust support. While Chil3+ Ly6Chi macrophages are implicated in the NK-cDC1 axis, the specific
molecular mechanisms (e.g., how H3K4me3 regulates CXCL9/10 or creatine metabolism) remain unclear. Functional
validation (e.g., ChIP-seq, CRISPR-edited models) is needed to strengthen these claims. 
- Causality in the NK-cDC1 Axis: Depletion experiments (Fig. 6) do not conclusively determine whether NK cells or
macrophages are the primary drivers of cDC1 recruitment. Transwell assays (Fig. 6I) demonstrate macrophage-NK
chemotaxis but fail to directly link this to cDC1 priming. 
- Metabolomic Data Interpretation: The observed upregulation of creatine metabolites (Fig. 3C) is correlative. Isotope tracing
or genetic knockout experiments are needed to establish a causal relationship between creatine metabolism and cytokine
production. Additionally, does creatine supplementation rescue the effects of β-GPA inhibition in vivo? 
3. Experimental Design and Rigor 
- Mouse Model Limitations: The use of immunogenic models (CT26, MC38) may not fully recapitulate the
immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) of human CRC. Including syngeneic models with low mutational burden
(e.g., APC-mutant) would improve translational relevance. Furthermore, subcutaneous tumors do not adequately model
metastatic CRC; orthotopic models would be more informative. 
- Inducer Specificity: Relying solely on β-glucan (WPG) as a trained immunity inducer limits generalizability. Comparisons
with other inducers (e.g., BCG, LPS) would strengthen the conclusions. 
- Human Data Limitations: The PDO experiments (n=3) are underpowered, and PBMC data (Fig. 8D-E) lack sufficient donor
diversity. Expanding to additional patient-derived samples (e.g., PDX models) and quantifying antigen-specific T-cell
responses would enhance translational relevance. 
Minor Critiques 
4. Single-Cell RNA Sequencing 
- The reanalysis of Geller et al. (2022, Nat Commun) (Fig. 1A-E) does not clearly advance beyond the original study. The
authors should clarify its novel contributions. 
- Cluster annotation (Fig. 4A-B) requires protein-level validation (e.g., Chil3+Ly6C-high macrophages).

5. Metabolomics 
- Pathway impact analysis (Fig. 3E-F) does not adjust for multiple testing, increasing the risk of false positives. 
6. Methods Concerns 
- Fig. 1F-I: The SIINFEKL model is not CRC-relevant; justification for using ovalbumin-derived peptides is lacking. 
- Fig. 2L-N: A comparison with checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1) is absent, despite their clinical relevance. 
- Fig. 7 (MC38 Model): Data on tumor-infiltrating T cells and checkpoint markers (e.g., PD-L1) are missing. 
- WPG Dosing: The β-glucan dose (1 mg/mouse) lacks human-equivalent translation, raising translational concerns. 
7. Figure Clarity 
- Tumor growth curves (Figs. 2B, 2M) should include individual data points to illustrate variability. 
- Immunofluorescence images (Figs. 5I, 8G) need scale bars and higher resolution. 
- The scRNA-seq methods (e.g., batch correction, cluster resolution) require more detail for reproducibility.
