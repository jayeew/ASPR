Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Li et al. report the elucidation of the biosynthesis of avenolide, a hormone that regulates avermectins production in
Streptomyces avermitilis. Through heterologous expression and in vitro assays, they showed that the trifunctional
flavoenzyme SavA catalyzes a unique desaturation and hydroxylation–lactonization cascade to form a homochiral 4-
alkylbutenolide, and SavB, a cytochrome P450, then installs three sequential, regio- and stereospecific hydroxylations, to
complete avenolide assembly. This work highlights a sustainable biocatalytic route for butenolide synthesis using dioxygen
as the sole stoichiometric reagent. The authors successfully synthesized the fatty acid acyl-phosphopantetheine (Ppant)
substrate for SavA using a chemo enzymatic method. Furthermore, by comparing the product with a synthesized standard
and employing 18O2 as a labeled substrate, they identified the reaction of SavA acting on acyl carrier protein. They also
identified a key residue E418 which abstracts a proton from the substrate, based on the docking model by using the
AlphaFold3 structure of SavA. Therefore, the quality of the biochemical analysis in this study can be considered high. The
sequential oxidations catalyzed by SavA and SavB are intriguing as a point view of enzymology. New insights into the
biosynthesis of butyrolactone will contribute to elucidating its diversity as a signaling molecule and secondary metabolite,
making this research highly relevant to the fields of biosynthesis, chemical biology, and microbiology. Thus, the reviewer
recommends this study for publication after the authors corrected minor points as described below. 
1. (Figure 4a) What is the peak eluted at 28.3 min? The authors should label the product by using the product number. 
2. (Line 314) The authors should cite the Figure S10-11 here. 
3. (Line 419) The calculated ms should be described here. 
4. The reviewer thinks that the additional discussion on the catalysis by SavA should help the readers to understand the
uniqueness of its catalysis. This reviewer recommends the authors to compare the catalysis of canonical ACAD/AOX
enzymes with those of SavA and EpnF. 
5. The preference of SavA on the Ppant tethered substrates sound unique. Are there any ACAD/AOX enzymes which accept
Ppant-tethered substrates? The authors should describe the structural basis which accommodate Ppant arm or carrier
protein based on the docking model. 
6. Do the avenolide intermediates obtained in this study possibly work as a signaling molecule? If possible, the author
should do electrophoretic mobility shift assay for AvaR by using them. 
7. The tables for the NMR assignments of 3, 11 and 12 should be presented. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Li, Zhou and colleagues reports the biosynthesis of the actinobacterial transcriptional regulator avenolide
containing a butanolide moiety. By combining heterologous expression, gene inactivation, chemical synthesis and in vitro
enzyme assays, the authors convincingly demonstrate the involvement of two oxidoreductases, i.e. flavoenzyme SavA that
produces the butenolide ring from a medium-chain fatty acyl thioester, followed by CypP40 SavB that completes avenolide
formation by several oxygenation reactions. 18O-isotope labelling is employed to support the incorporation of a total of 3
oxygen atoms from O2 into the substrate. Sustainable routes for biocatalytic asymmetric butanolide formation are of
industrial interest and this study identified two enzymes that could potentially be applied. Overall, the data supporting the
role of these two enzymes is convincing and verifies an enzymatic route independent of the canonical PBS. Currently, the
proposed SavA mechanism is plausible but not further supported with mechanistic or structural data and the enzymes are

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

not characterized in terms of their kinetic properties and overall substrate turnover, which would be relevant when
considered as potential (starting point for the development of) green catalysts. Here, especially the CYP SavB seemed to
show very low turnover and activity. Nonetheless, this is an interesting study that is very well presented and pleasant to
read. A few points should be considered prior to publication: 
In my opinion, the field of flavoenzyme biochemistry could be better introduced and/or discussed. For example, I suggest to
briefly mention the current classification of flavoprotein monooxygenases = FPMOs (e.g. PMID: 33588053) and the
distinction between “external” (requiring exogenous e-donors such as NADPH) and “internal” (using the substrate as e-
donor) FPMOs, SavA belonging to the latter. Group D FPMOs have an ACAD fold but are typically two-component external
FPMOs (i.e. the flavin is reduced by a separate flavin reductase and then taken up by the oxygenase component), so I guess
SavA is not a classical group D FPMO (same for EpnF and TdaE - an enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of bacterial
tropone natural products). These enzyme rather seem to represent some new type of internal FPMO which may evolutionary
derive from former ACADs and combine typical ACAD chemistry (dehydrogenation of a substrate) with FPMO chemistry (O2
reduction and substrate oxygenation). 
Enzyme mechanism: The proposed mechanism is plausible but it is not a given that C4a-peroxide species are used by
these ACADs. For TdaE, N5-oxygenated flavins (N5-peroxide, N5-oxide) appear more likely (PMID: 34196542), which have
also been reported before in other internal FPMOs from Streptomyces involved in natural product biosynthesis. It is even
possible that no covalent flavin-oxygen species are formed, similar to nitronate monooxygenase (PMID: 19577534). Without
further evidence (e.g. stopped-flow spectroscopy to verify a C4a-peroxide, quenching to confirm transient N5-oxide species
by MS etc.), I suggest to at least briefly point out alternative mechanisms in the text and adjust the mechanistic discussion.
Also in that regard, EpnF and TdaE function as epoxidases rather than hydroxylases, which may possibly also be the case
for SavA. 
It could also be interesting to construct a phylogenetic tree of typical ACADs as well as EpnF, TdaE and SavA (and possible
additional enzymes) and compare active site features of the three internal FPMOs, not sure though if that will give any
insights.... 
Currently, no turnover numbers, kinetics etc. are shown in the paper. The assays should allow to determine some basic
kinetics, at least for SavA (SavB seems to be very slow, maybe not surprisingly for a CYP lacking native e-donors), e.g. KM,
vmax, catalytic efficiency? 
Minor points: 
Fig. 3c: Why is there so much unlabeled product with m/z of 241? Under 18O2 atmosphere one would expect more than that,
cf. also to Fig. 5a, where >80% seem to be labelled? 
L176: Protein is not homogeneous after Ni2+ affinity column based on Fig. 5a. Lots of impurities. 
L222: “Exogenous FAD is not required for SavA to catalyze butenolide formation and no reductant capable of regenerating
FAD from FADH2 is included in the enzymatic reaction mixture, suggesting FAD plays a catalytic role”. Please rephrase
(FAD can also play a catalytic role when NADPH is required), e.g., to “SavA catalyzes butenolide formation without requiring
exogenous FAD or a separate reductant, indicating FAD's catalytic role in this reaction is independent of external cofactors
and that the enzyme functions as internal FPMO using its own substrate as electron donor.” 
Also the statement “…observation that SavA purifies with bound FAD, which is not consumed during the course of the
reaction” is somewhat imprecise. What do the authors mean exactly by “consumed”? FAD is normally never “consumed”
(used up) in flavoenzymes and typically restored to FAD after each reaction cycle. For SavA, FAD is first reduced to FADH2
by the substrate and then reoxidized with O2 to generate the putative oxygenating species for subsequent oxygen transfer to
the substrate (in contrast to oxidases, which would simply reduce O2 to H2O2). 
Fig S5: For spectra in panel c, please adjust y- and x-axes (absorption <300 not relevant) so that the distinct flavin spectral
properties are highlighted. Also in panel d: “authetic” typo and FMN should also be shown as standard for comparison (or
MS data verifying FAD). 
Fig. 4b: I suggest to directly indicate time in min in this figure instead of using (i), (ii) and (iii). 
“The formation of desaturated species in early stages of the SavA-catalyzed reaction was consistent with the proposal that
dehydrogenation is the first step in the process of butenolide formation.” This is to some extent a necessity because the
reaction order cannot be inverted when the electrons for flavin reduction and O2 activation come from the substrate, consider
adjusting to “The formation of desaturated species in early stages of the SavA-catalyzed reaction was consistent with the
proposal that dehydrogenation is the first step in the process of butenolide formation, which also produces the reduced flavin
required for subsequent oxygenation chemistry by SavA.” 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript presents a compelling study on the enzymatic biosynthesis of avenolide, a 4-alkylbutenolide hormone
involved in regulating avermectin production in Streptomyces avermitilis. The authors elegantly demonstrate that SavA, a

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

multifunctional flavoenzyme, catalyzes a remarkable desaturation-hydroxylation-lactonization cascade to form the
homochiral butenolide core, utilizing dioxygen as the sole stoichiometric reagent. The subsequent tailoring by SavB, a
cytochrome P450, introduces regio- and stereospecific hydroxylations to complete avenolide assembly. The experimental
design is robust, integrating in vitro enzymatic assays, isotopic labeling, and mutagenesis studies to substantiate the
proposed mechanism. The writing is clear and accessible, making the work readily digestible for a broad audience. The
discovery of SavA’s unique multifunctionality and its potential as a biocatalyst for sustainable asymmetric butenolide
synthesis represents a significant advance in the field. This work is highly novel and merits publication pending minor
revisions addressing the following points: 
Specific Comments: 
1) Identification of Intermediate E/Z Isomers and Hydroxylation Regiochemistry: 
In the SavA in vitro assay (Fig. 4a), two m/z peaks corresponding to compound 2 were detected. The authors assigned one
peak as the E-isomer based on comparison with a synthetic standard. The other peak may correspond to the Z-isomer, but
this remains speculative since the Z-isomer was not tested as a substrate for SavA. Alternatively, competitive hydroxylation
at C-3 vs. C-4 could occur: 
If C-3 hydroxylation occurs, dehydration would yield E-2, which may not proceed to form 3. 
If C-4 hydroxylation occurs, lactonization (possibly via nucleophilic attack on the thioester) followed by desaturation could
yield the α,β-unsaturated lactone. 
To clarify the mechanism, we recommend probing the regiochemistry and order of hydroxylation steps, e.g., by testing
possible intermediates and/or conducting additional isotopic labeling. 
2) Stereochemical Assignment and Catalytic Efficiency of SavB: 
The configuration of the C-4 hydroxy group in compound 3 (generated by SavA) was assigned by comparison with a
racemic synthetic standard. However, this does not confirm whether the stereochemistry matches that of the final product (1).
To unequivocally establish enantiopurity, the authors should use enantiomerically pure substrates or products for
comparison. 
In the SavB in vitro assay (Fig. 6a), the use of racemic 3 as substrate makes it difficult to conclude whether SavB is
stereoselective. It is essential to use enantiopure 3 to assess stereospecificity and catalytic efficiency. 
Fig. 6a shows that the synthetic standard of 1 contains a small amount of 3. Could this impurity affect the interpretation of the
assay? Additionally, SavB’s catalytic efficiency appears low. Have the authors explored alternative redox partners or
cofactors (e.g., different ferredoxins/ferredoxin reductases) to optimize activity? The low efficiency may be due to suboptimal
partner proteins or reaction conditions. 
3) Order of Oxidations Catalyzed by SavB: 
The proposed sequence of C-9 and C-10 oxidation (C-9 hydroxylation → dehydrogenation/dihydroxylation-dehydration →
C-10 hydroxylation) is intriguing but relies largely on inference. To rigorously establish the order, the authors could test
putative intermediates (e.g., compounds 11 and 12, as mentioned) in SavB in vitro assays. This would provide direct
evidence for the oxidative cascade and strengthen the mechanistic proposal. 
4
）
Substrate Scope and Versatility of SavA: 
The SavA-catalyzed cascade (desaturation, hydroxylation, lactonization) is highly intriguing. To enhance the general
applicability and impact of this study, it would be valuable to test whether SavA can accept alkyl substrates of varying chain
lengths to produce butenolides with different alkyl substituents. If such versatility is demonstrated, it would significantly
broaden the enzyme’s utility for biocatalytic applications. If not, please discuss the potential for enzyme engineering to alter
substrate specificity or analyze structural/mechanistic constraints that may limit substrate flexibility. 
Conclusion: 
This work is of high quality and represents an important contribution to the fields of enzymatic catalysis. The mechanistic
insights into SavA’s multifunctionality are particularly noteworthy. Addressing the above points will further elevate the impact
and rigor of the study. Thus, I recommend minor revisions.

Specific Comments: 
 
1) Identification of Intermediate E/Z Isomers and Hydroxylation Regiochemistry: 
In the SavA in vitro assay (Fig. 4a), two m/z peaks corresponding to compound 2 were 
detected. The authors assigned one peak as the E-isomer based on comparison with 
a synthetic standard. The other peak may correspond to the Z-isomer, but this remains 
speculative since the Z -isomer was not tested as a substrate for SavA. Alternatively, 
competitive hydroxylation at C-3 vs. C-4 could occur: 
If C-3 hydroxylation occurs, dehydration would yield E -2, which may not proceed to 
form 3. 
If C -4 hydroxylation occurs, lactonization (possibly via nucleophilic attack on the 
thioester) followed by desaturation could yield the α,β-unsaturated lactone. 
To clarify the mechanism, we recommend probing the regiochemistry and order of 
hydroxylation steps, e.g., by testing possible intermediates and/or conducting 
additional isotopic labeling. 
We agree there is still no direct evidence confirming that  the other desaturated 
intermediate observed in the SavA -catalyzed reaction  is (Z)-2. Despite extensive 
synthetic efforts, the isomerization of the Z-configured double bond to the more stable 
E-isomer proceeds rapidly, promoted by the thiol and base in the reaction mixture , 
making preparation of the Z-configured thioester unachievable. This is also in accord 
with the fact that E-isomer is present in the SavA -catalyzed reaction, which may be 
isomerized from Z-2 under the enzymatic reaction conditions.

However, the proposal of the dehydrogenation-hydroxylation-lactonization cascade for 
SavA is most consistent with the properties of such flavin -dependent enzymes. The 
absence of a requirement for exogenous electron donors (e.g., NADPH) makes it 
unlikely that hydroxylation is the first step in the SavA -catalyzed reaction.  Current 
evidence from in vitro enzymatic reaction supports that SavA is more like a n internal 
flavoprotein monooxygenase which uses the substrate as electron donor. 
We therefore propose that dehydrogenation is the initial step in butenolide formation, 
thereby generating the reduced flavin required for subsequent oxygenation by SavA . 
This was also agreed by reviewer #2 (This is to some extent a necessity because the 
reaction order cannot be inverted when the electrons for flavin reduction and O 2 
activation come from the substrate). 
2) Stereochemical Assignment and Catalytic Efficiency of SavB:

In the SavB in vitro assay (Fig. 6a), the use of racemic 3 as substrate makes it difficult
