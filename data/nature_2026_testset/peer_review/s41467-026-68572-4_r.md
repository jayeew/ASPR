Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors use first-principles molecular dynamics (FPMD) simulations to evaluate the equation of state and sound speed
of Fe-Mg alloys. The results are significant because the addition of Mg into liquid Fe causes the sound speed to decrease.
By comparison all other candidates for the light alloying element in the Earth’s core (e.g. Si, O, C, H, etc) cause the sound
speed to increase. To account for the low seismic velocities detected near the top of Earth’s core you need to lower the local
concentration of the “usual” light elements. Unfortunately, this change increases the fluid density near the boundary, and it
makes the layer convectively unstable. In other words the layer needed to explain the seismic velocities could not survive.
The computed properties of Mg-Fe alloys appear to resolve this difficulty. 
A natural question is why Mg behaves differently than other alloying elements. The authors do not offer much insight here.
They note that their calculations for pure Fe reproduce previous results. How should readers access the validity of the
calculations with increasing concentrations of Mg? Perhaps other reviewers with experience using FPMD simulations could
comment on this point. If the results are reliable then the implications are important. 
The authors use prior results on the partitioning of Mg between silicate melts and Fe metal to access the amount of Mg that
could be added to the core during the formation of the planet. Separation of silicate and metal under moderate temperature
and pressure would not transfer enough Mg to the core to account for the observed seismic velocities. Instead the authors

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

appeal to the moon-forming impact to generate high temperatures, enabling a sufficient transfer of Mg into the core. The
resulting metal should have low density (due to the high concentration of Mg), so it would pond at the top of the core. This
configuration motivates a two-layer model with a low Mg concentration in the lower region and a high concentration of Mg in
the upper layer. The authors show that this configuration can match the seismic observations across the entire core.
Subsequent cooling causes the transfer of O into the base of the mantle (see Fig 4h). While this final step is not essential to
the authors argument, it does raise questions about the thermodynamic state on the silicate side of the core-mantle
boundary. What is the mantle composition that would allow transfer to of SiO2 or FeO into the mantle? If the authors are
going to make the claim then they should include arguments to support this claim. 
The upper layer of the two-layer model is taken to have a thickness of 600 km. This choice is motivated by several of the
seismic velocity models cited in the manuscript. However, there is general consensus in the geomagnetic community that
such a thick layer would negatively alter the morphology and time dependence of the Earth’s magnetic field (e.g. Gastine et
al., GJI, vol. 222, 2020). This potential concern should be acknowledged in the manuscript. 
A few minor things 
Line 31: convention -> convection 
Line 307: the computed density and compressional velocities are calculated using ideal mixing. There is support for this
approximation in prior studies. However, the description included in the manuscript differs from the approach in cited
literature (ref. 14). In this study the adiabatic bulk modulus is computed by weighted average of the adiabatic bulk modulus of
the individual components. By comparison, ref. 14 averages the isothermal bulk modulus. A standard correction is then
applied to convert the isothermal bulk modulus to an adiabatic bulk modulus (which is the one needed for comparison with
seismic observation). Have the authors actually converted each component to an adiabatic bulk modulus before computing
the weight average? What is the justification for the authors approach? 
Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Liu and Jing reports results from first-principles molecular dynamics (FPMP) simulations concerning the
equation of state and sound velocity of Fe-Mg alloy liquids under conditions in the Earth's outer core. The computed
properties of the Fe-Mg alloys are employed to elucidate the characteristics of the low-velocity layer observed in the
uppermost section of the outer core, referred to as the E' layer. It is hypothesized that primordial magnesium could have
been incorporated into the outermost outer core via the Moon-forming giant impact, occurring under extreme high-pressure
and high-temperature conditions. I have outlined specific areas below that need clarification and revision to meet publication
standards. 
(1) The manuscript's focus on first-principles molecular dynamics (FPMD) calculations falls outside my area of expertise. A
review by another expert familiar with this methodology would be useful to confirm if these calculations have been executed
correctly. The manuscript mentions (Lines 84-90 and Figures S2-S3) that the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)
utilized in the FPMD simulations requires pressure correction. It raises a question whether this correction is also
temperature-dependent, potentially increasing with higher temperatures. Additionally, it would be beneficial for the authors to
provide a detailed explanation of how the pressure correction was determined under the conditions present at CMB and ICB
from 4000 K to 7000 K. 
(2) Fig. 2: The representation of literature data on pure iron from Bargain et al. (2018), Anderson and Ahrens (1994), and
Kuwayama et al. (2020) could benefit from clearer plotting. The manuscript extensively discusses the impact of primordial
magnesium on the Vp in the Earth's outermost core. More comprehensive statistical and sensitivity analyses are necessary
to robustly validate the findings. 
While the decrease in density with the incorporation of magnesium in iron liquids is evident,
it is less apparent that this will also reduce Vp. This uncertainty stems in part from the complexities associated with fitting the
equation of state, highlighting the need for more precise analysis to clarify these effects. 
(3) Mg content in the Earth’s outer core: Because of the concerns raised in (2), how would the errors in determining Vp from
EOS fittings affect the results on Mg content in the Earth’s outer core. For reproducibility and open science, it would be
beneficial to include the codes for the modeling along with the manuscript as datasets. The determination of magnesium
content in the Earth’s outer core is critically dependent on the accurate determination of Vp, which is derived from the
equation of state (EOS) fittings. The errors inherent in these fittings and calculations based on bulk modulus and density, as
outlined in the concerns raised in (2), could significantly impact the inferred content of Mg in Earth’s outer core. This
introduces a potential source of uncertainty in our understanding of the outer core's composition. To enhance the reliability
and reproducibility of the findings, and in line with principles of open science, it is advisable for the authors to make the
computational codes used for modeling available along with the manuscript. Providing these codes as supplementary
datasets would allow other researchers to verify the results, potentially replicate the study, and extend the analysis further. 
(4) Fig S4: What are the uncertainties of Vp determined for 4000 K, 5000 K, 6000 K, and 7000 K from low to high pressures?

Thank you for the insightful comment and the recognition of the significance of our 
work.  
 
(1) To understand the influence of Mg on VP relative to other light elements, we 
computed the radial distribution functions (RDFs; Supplementary Fig. S9) and 
performed electron localization function (ELF) analyses (Savin et al., 1997) 
(Supplementary Fig. S10) for Fe156X16 liquids (X = Mg, Si, S, O, C, and H) at 6000 K 
and V=1226.52 Å3. The RDFs reveal that the positions and amplitudes of the first 
peaks for Fe–Fe, Fe–Mg, and Mg–Mg pairs in liquid Fe–Mg are nearly identical, in 
contrast to the distinct peak separations between Fe-Fe and Fe-X peaks observed in 
other Fe–light element alloying liquids. This structural similarity indicates that Mg 
forms metallic bonds with Fe that are nearly identical to Fe-Fe bonds. This is 
consistent with the absence of pronounced Fe–Mg orbital hybridization in ELF 
calculations (Supplementary Fig. S10c), with ELF values of ~0.5. However, unlike 
the delocalization of 3d electrons in Fe contributes to the cohesion of metallic bonds, 
Mg lacks d electrons and contributes only s electrons to the conduction band, leading 
to a lower electronic density of states near the Fermi level and weaker metallic 
bonding. Thus, replacing Fe with Mg atoms effectively decreases the strength of 
metallic bonds and in turn decreases the bulk modulus of the liquid. Although the 
smaller atomic mass of Mg than Fe decreases the density, the overall effect is that the 
sound velocity decreases slightly with Mg content. On the other hand, other light 
elements including Si, S, C, O, and H exhibit partial covalent interactions with Fe, as 
evidenced by shorter Fe-X distances in RDFs and their distinct hybridization features 
in ELF calculations (with ELF values close to 1.0) (Supplementary Fig. S10). The 
covalent nature and shorter length of Fe–X bonds enhance local stiffness and 
therefore increase the bulk modulus and sound velocity of Fe-alloying liquids, in 
contrast to the effect of Mg. 
 
(2) Regarding the reliability of our FPMD results at elevated Mg concentrations, we 
note that our simulations for pure Fe liquid reproduce published data with excellent 
agreement, establishing a solid methodological benchmark. The computational 
protocol for Fe-Mg alloys is identical to that used for pure Fe, ensuring consistency. 
To further assess convergence, we conducted additional FPMD simulations using 
supercells twice (Fe156Mg16) the original size and extended simulation time duration 
up to 50 ps––five times longer than in the original calculations, as recommended by 
Reviewer 1. These tests revealed very minor differences in the calculated pressures 
(less than 0.25 GP) and internal energies (less than 9.00 meV/atom) (see our 
responses to the comments of Reviewer 1 and Supplementary Figs. S2-3). For direct 
comparison, two representative thermodynamic states corresponding to the CMB and 
ICB were examined. The calculated VP values for Fe156Mg16 at these conditions are

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

7.79±0.08 km/s and 9.91±0.05 km/s, respectively, in excellent agreement with the 
values reported for Fe78Mg8 in the original manuscript (7.77±0.11 at the CMB and 
9.90±0.08 at the ICB), with deviations of less than 0.3%. This confirms the robustness 
of our computations.

Thank you for raising this point. The core-forming metallic liquids were in partial 
equilibrium with silicate melts in the early magma ocean produced by the Moon-
forming giant impact. However, once the metallic liquids penetrated the solid lower 
mantle through diapirism and formed the liquid core, there is no longer large-scale 
chemical equilibrium between the solid lower mantle and liquid outer core due to the 
slow diffusion rates in solids (Karato, 2023). The exsolution of components such as 
SiO2, FeO, and MgO from the liquid outer core is mainly due to the temperature 
dependence of solubility of these components in liquid Fe, but not the equilibrium 
partitioning of these elements between metal and silicate. Thus, the thermodynamic 
state of the silicate mantle does not have a strong control on the exsolution processes. 
As demonstrated in previous studies, the secular cooling of the core could lead to the 
exsolution of components such as SiO2 (Helffrich et al., 2020; Hirose et al., 2017; 
O’Rourke and Stevenson, 2016), MgO (Du et al., 2019, 2017; Helffrich et al., 2020; 
O’Rourke and Stevenson, 2016), and FeO (Deng, 2024; O’Rourke and Stevenson, 
2016), which may form sedimentary layers at the core-mantle boundary or be 
entrained to the mantle through convection.

Regarding the temperature dependence of the GGA pressure correction, we agree this 
is potentially an important issue for the accuracy of our FPMD simulations under core 
conditions. In fact, the temperature dependence of pressure correction remains a hotly 
debated topic in the literature. While most studies express it as a function of pressure 
or density (Alfè et al., 2002; Badro et al., 2014; Li et al., 2022), some have suggested 
a combined pressure and temperature dependence (Umemoto and Hirose, 2020). In 
our study, we follow the outer-core adiabatic temperature profile and effectively 
consider the pressure and temperature effects on pressure correction simultaneously. 
We applied the pressure correction by comparing the pressures obtained from our 
FPMD simulations for liquid Fe along the outer-core adiabat with those from shock-
wave experimental data (Anderson and Ahrens, 1994). The resulting correction 
pressures are ~10 GPa under CMB conditions (135 GPa, ~4000 K) and ~8 GPa at 
ICB conditions (330 GPa, ~6000 K), with values at intermediate conditions obtained 
by linear interpolation. These corrections are in close agreement with those reported 
in earlier studies (Alfè et al., 2002; Badro et al., 2014), and ensure that the calculated 
VP values along the outer-core adiabat are robust and inherently accounts for the 
temperature effect on the pressure correction.

Response to Reviewer #2 
The authors have done a good job responding to the points raised in my review. Brief 
statements have been included in the manuscript and additional details are presented 
in the supplement. I think the authors have made their case. 
 
I would add one final comment on the authors' statement about the solubility of Mg in 
liquid iron (see line 216-217). The view expressed here is perfectly reasonable. 
Exsolution describes the transfer of dissolved Mg to new phase (say solid MgO). In 
other words the solubility represents the concentration of dissolved Mg in equilibrium 
with solid MgO. Of course, the core liquid is also in contact with other solid 
components in the lower mantle. It is not entirely obvious that the equilibrium 
between the core liquid and these other solid components are unimportant, 
particularly if the exsolution occurs close to the core-mantle boundary. 
 
Reply: Thank you for your supportive comments. We agree with these comments and 
have revised the text in the manuscript as “Such exsolution processes are likely 
determined by the temperature-dependent solubilities of various components in the 
liquid core, which reflect chemical equilibrium between the core and the exsolved 
phases, although other solid components in the lower mantle, if in contact with the 
liquid outer core, may also affect the exsolution processes.”
