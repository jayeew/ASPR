Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors used a new method to conduct STM research on diamond and identified more than 40 defects as NV centers
based on dI/dV conductance spectra. The density-of-states mapping of two-lobed wavefunctions aligned along the [111]
crystallographic direction supports an anisotropic defect in diamond. However, in view of the following issues, we believe
that the identification of NV is not definitive. The following issues need to be properly addressed before publication. 
1. According to the “Sample preparation” part in methods, the NV depth is expected to be ~ 5 nm, while the STM
topographies of NV- in fig.2, 3 and 4 all show a size smaller than 0.5 nm. This counterintuitively indicates an atomic spatial
resolution with a tip-sample distance at the scale of 5 nm. Besides, the tunneling probability from the tip to the NV also
needs an explanation, since it decays exponentially with an increasing distance and should be neglectable under such a
distance. 
2. In fig.2 f, the authors show a histogram from 45+ defects, counts from -1 eV to 0 eV are all classified into the ground state
of NV-. Could the authors explain why the measured energy levels are so widely dispersed? P.S. the refs. of NV- and P1
energy levels (ref. 24, 25 and 26, 27) seem mislabeled. 
3. In fig.3, the directionality of NV centers should be checked with more reliable methods. For example, polarized excitation
laser can be used to identify the NV axis direction (refer to section IV of supplementary information of Nature Photonics 18,
230–235 (2024)). 
4. In fig.4, the authors claim that NV centers’ charge state is manipulated. The straightest way to verify is to measure the
photon counts before and after the manipulation (refer to NATURE COMMUNICATIONS 12, 2457 (2021)). It would really
help to enhance the reliability of the identification of NV if such data are displayed. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The submitted article by Raghavan et al. presents a compelling study demonstrating atomic-scale imaging and charge-state
manipulation of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) with a conductive
graphene capping layer. By imaging the graphene–diamond heterostructure, the authors observe the characteristic
honeycomb lattice of graphene and defect signatures attributed to sub-surface NV and P1 centers. These observations are
supported by in-situ optical photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. Using local differential conductance (dI/dV)
spectroscopy, the authors could observe defects that exhibit signatures which can be attributed to NV
⁻
, as well as P1
centers. The authors also demonstrate the capability to image the two-lobed spatial structure of NV
⁻
 wavefunctions along
the [111] crystallographic direction, and demonstrate tip-controlled charge-state switching from NV
⁻
 to NV⁰ using a positive
sample bias. 
The article is well written, and the method and results can be of high value to the scientific community. However, before
recommending the article for publication, the following points should be addressed: 
1. Tunneling Mechanism 
The nature of the electronic coupling between the buried defects and the STM tip, mediated through the graphene layer,

remains insufficiently explained. Specifically, the manuscript should clarify the nature of the tunnelling current observed in
this study. Additionally, any role of band bending, interface dipoles, or charge transfer effects at the graphene–diamond
interface should be discussed. 
2. Depth Sensitivity and Lateral Resolution 
A critical aspect of the study is the ability to detect sub-surface defects via STM. The authors should provide an estimate of
the depth sensitivity of their method, i.e., the depth of the defects from which they expect a detectable dI/dV signal.
Furthermore, the relationship between defect depth and lateral resolution should be addressed. Are there measurable trends
in the apparent size or symmetry of the observed NV features (e.g., contrast, sharpness, lobe spacing) that correlate with
defect depth? Such analysis could support the sub-surface origin of the features. 
3. Role of Laser Illumination and the Laser-STM Setup 
The authors mention, that the STM measurements were carried out in a newly developed laser-STM system. However, the
importance of this system is not clearly stated. According to the sample preparation the utilized samples should have a
homogeneous distribution of NV centers, so the in-situ PL measurements would not give any additional information on the
presence of NV centers, compared with PL measurements outside of the STM. Furthermore, it is not stated in the
manuscript, if laser-excitation of NV centers during the STM measurements is performed, and/or required for the described
measurements. This should be clarified in the text. If laser excitation is used during spectroscopy, any effect of the
illumination on the dI/dV spectra (e.g., peak shifts or intensity modulation) should be reported. Control experiments with and
without laser illumination could provide important context for interpreting the tunnelling signals. 
4. Defect Density and Statistical Analysis 
The manuscript reports imaging of approximately 45 defects across two type-Ib diamond substrates, but lacks quantitative
analysis of defect densities. The authors should provide the average areal densities for NV and P1 centers detected in STM.
This should be compared with expected densities based on the utilized samples. Additionally, it would be informative to
discuss the spatial distribution of defects, e.g., whether clustering is observed or whether the defects seem uniformly
distributed. 
5. Sample Set and Preparation Parameters 
The authors state that three diamond samples were used, two nitrogen-rich type-Ib diamonds and one electronic-grade
sample, but do not fully explain why two similar samples were required for the main experiments. More detail should be
provided regarding the estimated NV and P1 concentrations in each sample, as derived from irradiation and annealing
parameters, as well as supporting EPR or optical measurements. 
Furthermore, the statements about the implantation energy, and depth of the electronic-grade crystal(s) are not very detailed.
The authors should clarify the sample preparation method of these samples, as well as the motivation for choosing these
parameters. Was the NV fluorescence of the electronic-grade sample also verified in-situ in the STM? If NV
⁻
 centers (even
at lower density) were present optically but not detected in STM, this discrepancy deserves a more detailed discussion. 
6. Tip-Induced Charge-State Control 
The demonstration of bias-induced conversion between NV
⁻
 and NV⁰ should be better contextualized with respect to a
similar previous study (Bian, K., et al., Nature Communications, 12(1), 1–9. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-22709-9)
which demonstrated reversable charge-state control of single NV centers using AFM tip gating. The authors should describe
the unique aspects of their approach, and discuss how their observed charge-state dynamics compare to those prior results.
Furthermore, they should elaborate on the choice of +5
 
V sample bias: was this empirically determined? Can a switching
threshold voltage be defined? Has laser excitation been attempted to aid reverse the NV charge transition? 
7. Comparison Between NV and P1 Defects 
The manuscript would benefit from a direct comparison between the STM features attributed to NV centers and those
assigned to P1 centers. What spatial topographies do the P1 centers exhibit, and how do they compare to the NVs two-
lobed features? 
8. Literature Context and Citations 
The authors should be aware of a similar graphene-diamond heterostructure system, which has recently been demonstrated
to show NV coherence enhancement, and should be referenced in their work (Hao, Y., et al., National Science Review,
12(5). https://doi.org/10.1093/NSR/NWAF076) 
Conclusion and Recommendation 
This manuscript presents important advances in atomic-scale imaging and charge-state control of quantum defects in
diamond. However, to fully support its conclusions and improve clarity for a broad readership, the authors must address the
above points through additional discussion, clarification, and where possible, supporting data. With these revisions, the
study will be a valuable contribution to the fields of quantum sensing and scanning probe microscopy.

This manuscript presents a compelling study on the atomic-scale imaging and charge-state manipulation of nitrogen-
vacancy (NV) centers in diamond using STM. The technique and the results, particularly the wavefunction mapping and
local charge-state control, are of significant potential interest to the community. While I have one major concern regarding
the evidence chain, I believe it can be adequately addressed through clarification in the text. 
The most significant issue is that the spectroscopic identification of NV centers and their STM characterization are not
performed on the same diamond sample. 
Electronic-Grade Sample: As shown in Fig. 1, NV centers were created in this sample via ion implantation (~5 nm depth)
and identified optically via their Zero-Phonon Line (ZPL). However, subsequent STM measurements on this sample did not
detect any defect signals, which is consistent with the acknowledged STM probing depth limit of ~2.5 nm for defects in
insulators. 
Type-Ib Sample: The core STM data in Fig. 2-4 are acquired on high-nitrogen Type-Ib samples. The authors argue that the
observed defects are NV centers based on their characteristic dI/dV spectra and two-lobed topography aligned with the [111]
direction. 
While this is not a loop-hole free validation, the authors provide a strong statistical argument. The combination of the STM
data from over 40 defects with the ensemble photoluminescence from the Type-Ib samples makes a persuasive, population-
level case for the source of the STM signals. 
To prevent any potential misunderstanding and to accurately represent the nature of the evidence, the authors should
explicitly state that the identification of the STM features as NV centers is based on a statistical correlation across a large
defect population, rather than on a one-to-one, simultaneous optical and STM confirmation on the same defect. This
clarification should be prominently placed in the Abstract and/or the main results summary to ensure readers have the
correct context from the outset. 
With the above clarification added, the manuscript will be significantly strengthened and suitable for publication. The
methodological advance and the high-quality data presented are valuable contributions to the field. 
Recommendation: Accept after Minor Revisions

Regarding the comment on the lateral size of the NVs in the topographies, it is 
important to note that the lateral resolution of STM is not depth dependent for defects 
embedded in insulators. As there is almost no screening electron cloud near defects, 
how a defect below the surface appears in STM depends on the details of the wave 
function in the z-direction. Hence, one can often observe atomically resolved small 
lateral structure of sub-surface defects, as seen from the case of Ni defect at the CuO2 
plane below the insulating BiO and SrO2 planes in Bi2Sr2CaCu2O88 and Te vacancies in 
a topological insulator Bi2Te3 at a few-layers depth below the surface9. Also, another 
study shows that the sub-surface defects in hBN from different layers only show 
difference in their spectral weight, not in their lateral size6. 
In summary, the main type of samples used in our measurements were type-1b 
diamonds which are expected to have homogeneously distributed defects, including in 
the region from the surface to ~2.5 nm below the surface, which has been shown in the 
literature to be accessible by STM. Moreover, as is shown in the literature, the lateral 
resolution for the sub-surface defects in STM is not determined by distance from the 
surface for the case of insulators.

The submitted article by Raghavan et al. presents a compelling study demonstrating 
atomic-scale imaging and charge-state manipulation of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers 
in diamond using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) with a conductive graphene 
capping layer. By imaging the graphene–diamond heterostructure, the authors observe 
the characteristic honeycomb lattice of graphene and defect signatures attributed to 
sub-surface NV and P1 centers. These observations are supported by in-situ optical 
photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. Using local differential conductance (dI/dV) 
spectroscopy, the authors could observe defects that exhibit signatures which can be 
attributed to NV⁻, as well as P1 centers. The authors also demonstrate the capability to 
image the two-lobed spatial structure of NV⁻ wavefunctions along the [111] 
crystallographic direction, and demonstrate tip-controlled charge-state switching from 
NV⁻ to NV⁰ using a positive sample bias. 
 
The article is well written, and the method and results can be of high value to the 
scientific community.

important points for consideration. We respond to each point in detail below. 
However, before recommending the article for publication, the following points should be 
addressed: 
 
1. Tunneling Mechanism 
The nature of the electronic coupling between the buried defects and the STM tip, 
mediated through the graphene layer, remains insufficiently explained. Specifically, the 
manuscript should clarify the nature of the tunnelling current observed in this study. 
Additionally, any role of band bending, interface dipoles, or charge transfer effects at the 
graphene–diamond interface should be discussed. 
Given that the use of a monolayer graphene layer is an important component of 
our technique, we agree that further discussion would be useful, and we have now 
added the discussion and references below to the revised manuscript as 
Supplementary Note 1. There are several examples in the literature which discuss STM 
tunneling through graphene, including: 
 Qiu, et al. (2021)20, study the van der Waals magnetic insulator CrI3 
through a monolayer of graphene  
 Wong, et al. (2015)6 and Velsaco, et al. (2016)21 study defects in 
hexagonal BN through a monolayer of graphene

 Gonzalez-Herrero, et al. (2016)22, Tesch, et al. (2016)23, and Leicht, et al.

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This manuscript presents a compelling study on the atomic-scale imaging and charge-
state manipulation of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond using STM. The 
technique and the results, particularly the wavefunction mapping and local charge-state 
control, are of significant potential interest to the community. While I have one major 
concern regarding the evidence chain, I believe it can be adequately addressed through 
clarification in the text.  
 
The most significant issue is that the spectroscopic identification of NV centers and their 
STM characterization are not performed on the same diamond sample.  
 
Electronic-Grade Sample: As shown in Fig. 1, NV centers were created in this sample 
via ion implantation (~5 nm depth) and identified optically via their Zero-Phonon Line 
(ZPL). However, subsequent STM measurements on this sample did not detect any 
defect signals, which is consistent with the acknowledged STM probing depth limit of 
~2.5 nm for defects in insulators.  
 
Type-Ib Sample: The core STM data in Fig. 2-4 are acquired on high-nitrogen Type-Ib 
samples. The authors argue that the observed defects are NV centers based on their 
characteristic dI/dV spectra and two-lobed topography aligned with the [111] direction.  
 
While this is not a loop-hole free validation, the authors provide a strong statistical 
argument. The combination of the STM data from over 40 defects with the ensemble 
photoluminescence from the Type-Ib samples makes a persuasive, population-level 
case for the source of the STM signals.  
 
To prevent any potential misunderstanding and to accurately represent the nature of the 
evidence, the authors should explicitly state that the identification of the STM features 
as NV centers is based on a statistical correlation across a large defect population, 
rather than on a one-to-one, simultaneous optical and STM confirmation on the same 
defect. This clarification should be prominently placed in the Abstract and/or the main 
results summary to ensure readers have the correct context from the outset.  
 
With the above clarification added, the manuscript will be significantly strengthened and 
suitable for publication. The methodological advance and the high-quality data 
presented are valuable contributions to the field.
