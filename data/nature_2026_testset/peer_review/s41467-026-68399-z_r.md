(Remarks to the Author) 
In this paper the authors present a paper focuses on a very interesting area, namely the treatment of osteoarthritis. There is
currently a shortage of therapies to slow the progression of this disease. Likewise, the study of the connection between the
skin and the joint is also very innovative and opens the door to other types of treatments. 
Authors suggest that IL-36 agonists are age-related systemic inflammatory factors released from skin to joints and contribute
to OA development. IL-36Ra shows potential clinical translational value for OA treatment, and targeting IL-36R signaling in
aged skin with microneedles represents a promising disease-modifying approach. 
The manuscript is well written, clear and easy to read. The authors have performed a wide variety of in vitro and in vivo
models to prove their hypothesis. This methodology is well described and is easy to understand.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The article, "Aged skin exacerbates experimental osteoarthritis via enhanced IL-36R signaling," provides valuable insights
into the relationship between aging skin and the IL-36 signaling pathway in the context of osteoarthritis (OA). The results

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 2 -->

presented are both novel and compelling, particularly the discovery that aged skin contributes to IL-36 agonist signaling,
which in turn exacerbates OA progression. The study effectively supports this hypothesis through a series of well-designed
experiments, providing strong evidence for the potential therapeutic benefits of blocking IL-36 signaling to improve OA
outcomes. The overall flow of the study is coherent, and the experimental design aligns well with the research objectives.
However, there are a few areas that could be improved to further strengthen the manuscript. Addressing these points would
enhance the clarity and impact of the findings, ultimately resulting in an even more robust contribution to the field. 
1. The scale bars in the images for articular cartilage, subchondral bone, synovium, and osteophytes appear to be
inconsistent. Specifically, if the scale bar in the subchondral bone plate (SBP) image represents 50 µm, then the bar graphs
measuring SBP thickness may be inaccurate. This issue seems to persist across multiple figures. It would be beneficial to
double-check the magnification levels and scale bars for all images to ensure proper calibration and accuracy. 
2. It is important to clearly indicate which colors correspond to specific markers in the fluorescent staining images. For
example, as noted in Figure 5, mentioning that green and red represent different markers would help readers follow the
content more easily. This should be consistent across all relevant figures. 
3. The explanations in the manuscript would benefit from more detailed descriptions of the markers and cells studied. For
example, it would be helpful to clarify what the specific markers represent and the significance of their increase or decrease
in the context of the study. 
4. The timelines, depicted with arrows to represent weekly or biweekly intervals, appear to be drawn in an unclear or
inconsistent manner. 
5. There is an inconsistency between the text and figure legend regarding the administration of AAV2-Cre or D-gal. While
the text mentions topical application, the figure indicates intradermal injection, and the mouse scheme seems to suggest
liquid application on top of the skin. It is important to match these details to avoid confusion. 
6. In Figure 4c, while SBP thickness appears to be increased in the IL-36Ra fl/fl mouse, this was not statistically significant.
However, the text suggests that the knockout (KO) mouse showed significant improvement in all measured factors, which is
not consistent with the data. 
7. Certain abbreviations, such as SPE and SNL, are used without prior explanation. 
8. In Supplementary Figure 8c, the IL-36R-KD SNL group did not show recovery, but the main text could be misinterpreted to
suggest that recovery occurred. 
9. The concluding statement in Figure 5, “Thus, these data suggest that loss of IL-36Ra in keratinocytes leads to increased
IL-36 agonist secretion and induces chondrocyte catabolism and synovial inflammation by activating pro-inflammatory
signaling, including NF-κB/JAK-STAT/MAPK pathways,” could benefit from a more detailed summary of the results. The
explanation currently feels somewhat weak and should more clearly highlight how the results lead to this conclusion. 
10. In Figures 6 and 7, the comparison of OA recovery through microneedles (MN) should include both control groups such
as sham-treated mice and intra-articular (i.a.) injection treatment groups for a more comprehensive comparison.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Dear authors, 
Thank you very much for submitting this new version of the manuscript and for the significant effort you have made to
improve it and respond to all the questions and comments raised by this reviewer. You have addressed many of the
questions appropriately. However, my main criticism of the manuscript remains unresolved. 
My primary concern is that the connection between what happens in the skin and what happens in the joint is not
convincingly demonstrated, nor is it clearly shown that what occurs in the skin is the cause of knee OA. 
The authors suggest that "their results reveal that IL-36 agonists are age-related systemic inflammatory factors released from
skin to joints and contribute to OA development." This statement is not consistent with the levels of IL-36 agonists in serum
and synovial fluid of the patients. There is a higher concentration of IL-36 agonists in synovial fluid than in serum, which is
generally explained by local synthesis of IL-36 agonists in the joint tissues. To support the authors' hypothesis, one would
expect a higher concentration of IL-36 in the skin and serum than in the synovial fluid. Based on the data presented, what
can be concluded is that IL-36 agonists participate in the OA process—something that has already been previously
demonstrated. 
Authors also generated microneedles loaded with mouse recombinant IL-36Ra protein or spesolimab, inserted them directly
into skin to sustainably inhibit IL-36R signaling in epidermal keratinocytes, which both, with some dudes, attenuated OA
progression in male mice. How do the authors know that this is an effect exclusively occurring in the skin? This effect could
be because the mouse recombinant IL-36Ra protein or spesolimab reaches the joint and acts on chondrocytes and
synoviocytes. 
Regarding the histologic differences between groups, these are often very weak, mainly OARSI score, subchondral bone,
osteophytes and even synovial. Authors recognize that the evaluation of subchondral bone sclerosis and osteophyte
formation often does not show statistically significant differences, and they suggest that a possible explanation is the animal
model used in this manuscript. 
Overall, histological results (pictures) of the OA animal model are unclear and unconvincing. Differences between groups
are often very weak, mainly OARSI score, subchondral bone, osteophytes and even synovial. To clearly support their
hypothesis, the authors must convincingly demonstrate that the effects of IL-36 agonists in the joint are due to those derived
from the skin and not from IL-36 synthesized locally in the joint. 
References 24 and 42 are duplicated.

progression, typically including increased bone density, thickening of the subchondral bone

we assessed it as follows: we selected three sagittal slices spaced approximately 30μm apart 
from the load-bearing area of the mouse knee joint. Using Safranin O -fast green staining, we

surface and the top of the trabecular bone, calculated the average as the representative value for 
each slice, and used the mean of the three slices as the final representative value for subchondral 
bone thickness. This method reflects the overall changes in subchondral bone and provides an 
objective basis for evaluating subchondral bone sclerosis.

accelerate the pathological process of OA.

the Control group, suggesting that the serum from young mice contains relatively low levels of

ageing or OA. While the serum contains some anti-inflammatory and repair factors, these may 
not be sufficient to significantly influence the progression of OA. Furthermore, the 
inflammatory factors in young serum may be inadequate to counteract the pathological response
