Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
General 
In the manuscript by Møiniche et al., the authors present an allergy immunotherapy for treatment of pollen-food allergy
syndrome. The authors approach’ involved designing a consensus allergen that represents a broad spectrum of non-specific
lipid transfer protein (cnsLTP-1) allergens. Naïve mice were subsequently immunized with cnsLTP-1 using a traditional
protein formulation (with poly(I:C) as adjuvant) or using lipid nanoparticle technology containing mRNA (LNP-mRNA)
encoding the consensus allergen. Immunization with either formulation induced cnsLTP-1-specific IgG production, with
LNP-mRNA avoiding allergen-specific IgE production. The induced antibodies were shown to bind various nsLTPs, and
blocked binding of allergens by serum IgEs derived from allergic patients using an ELISA setup. In my opinion, this is a well-
written article that would be of interest broad for a broad readership.

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript by Moiniche et al. describes preliminary evauation of the potential of a designed consensus sequence
mRNA for nsLTPs for application in AIT. Two aspects are potentially novel: mRNA in nanoparticles and the consensus
sequence approach. Having said that I have some concerns and doubts. 
1) The authors designate LTP allergy as a PFAS. In some instances this may be true, but the consensus is that LTP allergy
is mainly driven by peach LPT (Pru p 3) as primary sensitizer. Primary sensitization by pollen LTPs is more exception than
rule. So, LTP allergy is not dominantly a PFAS. Since it is very cross-reactive between foods, the approach would still
potentially be relevant. 
2) As is illustrated in table S1, the ten patients are having their strongest IgE responses against Pru p 3 and Mal d 3,
confirming my point above: Pru p 3 (and possibly Mal d 3) are primary sensitizers, the rest is cross-reactivity. When this is
true, the whole consensus approach is unnecessary: just take mRNA for Pru p 3. That will do the trick. A comparison
between mRNA for Pru p 3 and for cnsLTP should have been included if added value is claimed (which the authors do). 
3) The approach for designing the cnsLTP sequence is not well described. This should be spelled out in more detail. 
4) The human serum samples should also have been tested for IgE against pollen LTPs (Art v / Par j / Ole e). 
5)How do the authors explain that the Mal d 3-specific antibody reacts to reduced but not to unreduced LTP? There is good
evidence that reduction and alkylation makes LTPs unreactive with IgE. 
6) How was IgE measured in mouse serum? It is well established that IgG in mice immunized with allergens blocks
recognition of IgE, so it is better to measure IgE in a capturing ELISA format coating plates with anti-IgE, and using a
biotimylated allergen. 
7) The diagnostic potential claimed in line 414-417 is not really appropriate. There are no data to support such a claim. A
real comparison should then be made between individual LTPs from different foods and the cnsLTP. 
8) Fig 3E in line 428 shows IgG2a not IgE. 
9) Line 437: do we really want proinflammatory antibody responses in AIT. Yes if accompanied by Treg but perhaps not if no
Tregs? 
10) Also for the other potenial applications, such as for PR-10 and profilins, just using mRNA for the primary sensitizer would
probably be sufficient. The 2S albumins are also mentioned but they are not very cross-reactive, so that will definitely not
work. 
11) The background on which the authors claim that AIT for PFAS with normal extracts does not work is based on birch
pollen AIT, not on LTP allergies. It has never really been tested whether Pru p 3 is effective for cross-reactive foods as well. 
12) The claim that mRNA approaches would require less immunizations (than the 3-5 years for conventional AIT) is not
substatiated by any proof.

(Remarks to the Author) 
Comments on “mRNA-delivered consensus allergens induce a neutralizing IgG response against food and pollen allergens”
by Rivera-de-Torre et al. 
General comments: 
This manuscript, by Rivera-de-Torre et al., is an experimental mouse study aiming to test a new allergen immunotherapy
(AIT) approach with a consensus allergen and furthermore to test mRNA vaccine technology or traditional method with
protein formula injection for immunization in naïve mice. They found good recognition of the consensus allergen and
induction of IgG and in the mRNA vaccine treated mice also avoidance of specific IgE production. 
The novel technique used is interesting, although we hesitate to call it AIT since the experimental mice were naïve, but there
are some question marks about some of the methods used and about conclusions drawn by the authors. 
Introduction: 
General comment: 
After reading the introduction section, it is still unclear to us what is known and what is not known in this research field. Is the
new AIT approach completely unique or are there other publications in the field? Can you be more precise about the
knowledge gap? 
Specific comments: 
Lines 67-76: About description of PFAS. First of all, authors must disentangle between LTP-related PFAS which can lead to
systemic reactions and PR-10-related PFAS known only to cause mild oral symptoms, except from rare case reports of
systemic reaction following intake of large amount of soy protein Gly m 4. Treatment with corticosteroids or adrenaline for
PFAS is generally not used, especially not for PR-10 related reactions. 
A better description of PFAS is needed since it constitutes both LTPS and PR-10 proteins but are highly different in their
clinical presentation and structure and might not be equally suitable for this kind of treatment. SLIT for the treatment of nsLTP
allergy with Pru p 3 as the allergen have shown positive results to both peach and cross-reactive allergens (1). There is a
need to focus more on LTPS (lipid transfer protein syndrome) rather than the much wider definition PFAS. 
Lines 97-101: The claim that 90% of discontinuation of AIT needs to be explained. Is it really true for all forms of AIT
including SCIT? We doubt that. And that patient with PFAS do not consider AIT can be because PFAS is not an indication

First of all, we are primarily clinical researchers and not experts on laboratory methods. We leave to other reviewers more

Further, the sequence of the pre-ordered

Specific comment:

or similar. 
Lines 229-238: How many mice were included in the immunization experiment?

Specific comments:

Figure 4. The effect on the adjuvant used in the protein immunization could also affect the IgG response with increased IgG1

Discussion: 
General comments: 
The authors needs to acknowledge the difference in LTP and PR-10 proteins before proposing that this kind of treatment
could be suitable for birch-apple allergy. The authors should focus on LTPS throughout the manuscript. 
To draw the conclusion of alleviation of symptoms using a handful of mRNA-LNP doses, doesn’t long-term studies need to
be conducted first? 
Specific comment: 
Lines 415-417: See first comment under results. Why is the consensus LTP more useful than Pru p 3? 
Conclusion: 
General comments: 
Motivate why the next step is clinical trials as proposed. The next step could also be further studies with the use of more
naïve mice, then immunized for example using an adjuvant triggering an allergic response like alum, analyse the T-cell
response to these different methods and check for the presence of autoantibodies after treatment. 
Also, the authors conclude that their mRNA-LNP formulation is superior over protein immunization; this statement cannot be
proven with the experiments done. The variability is too big, given the small number of mice, and the adjuvant used might
itself explain the better results of the mRNA-LNP formulation in these experiments.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The overall goal is to develop a novel form of alleren-specific immunotherapy for the cross-reactive LTP food and pollen
allergens. Accordingly, the authors present an interesting approach by developing a consensus LTP allergen sequence,
then formulating it into mRNA-LNP for subcutaneous administration. 
The work clearly shows induction of IgG1 and IgG2a
antibodies against LTPs in naive mice, which is a starting point in the development of a novel allergy immunotherapy
approach. 
However, I have some concerns with the manuscript in its current form: 
Major concerns: 
1. The work is designed towards developing a novel molecule, cnsLTP-1, for allergy immunotherapy. However, the authors
do not assess the molecule in allergic mice. 
Rather, all in vivo studies were conducted in naive animals. 
The authors should
strongly consider sensitizing mice to LTPs, then using the cnsLTP-1 mRNA or protein formulations as immunotherapy. 
This
will provide much needed efficacy data in an allergic mouse model. 
The data currently shown are not convincing that the
approach provides any therapeutic benefit to allergic animals. 
2. Similarly, the authors must assess safety of the cnsLTP-1. Of course it is "safe" in naive animals, but what happens when
a mouse is allergic and primed to have reactions due to existing LTP-specific IgE? 
The authors should sensitize mice to
LTPs and then inject the mRNA and protein formulations to look for symptoms of allergy. 
This safety data is mandatory
before considering human clinical trials. 
3. The IgE inhibition studies are nice, but this doesn't exactly predict the efficacy of IgG in a therapeutic context. 
The authors
should use cell based assays (human basophil activation tests, or RBL cell lines with human IgE) to better define whether
the cns-LTP IgG can interfere with IgE function. 
4. I would strongly recommend including T cell readouts. 
Does the cnsLTP alter T cell cytokine responses in an allergic (or
even naive) mouse. 
5. The sample sizes for the mouse studies are very low (n=3 per treatment group). 
More mice should be included for
reproducibility and rigor. Similarly, for Figures 3 and 4, there appears to be trends in differences between mRNA and
Protein-treated mice, however, the sample sizes are too small to detect these differences. 
Minor Concerns: 
Line 83 - Xolair (Omalizumab) is now also approved for treating food allergies. 
Line 367 - Figure 4C is referenced but did not appear in the submitted version of the manuscript. 
Figure 1A - why is the cnsLTP-1 a mixture of proteins? 
Shouldn't it be a single band like the other LTPs shown in the SDS-
PAGE gels?
