Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This study written by Baken et al. explores the influence of various additives on the non-classical nucleation pathways of
portlandite and gypsum, identifying distinct nucleation mechanisms for each mineral. It highlights the multifaceted roles of
additives, including their potential to delay or accelerate specific stages of nucleation. While the manuscript is well-written,
there are some questions that should be addressed before it can be accepted. 
On page 10, the authors state, "It is important to note that the observed decrease in transmittance depends on the
wavelength of the turbidity probe, suggesting that nucleation may, in fact, start earlier." It is recommended that the author
provide further explanation on how the observed decrease in transmittance is influenced by the wavelength of the turbidity
probe and why this suggests that nucleation may occur earlier. 
The same page: the authors state “No significant change in the free calcium concentration slope accompanied this drop,
implying nucleation proceeded via the consumption of bound species rather than free calcium ions”. The authors might
explain this sentence more. For example, what are bound species? 
Also, the authors state that a decrease in the free calcium concentration corresponds to the emergence of crystallinity in the
system. I am confused about his statement because crystallinity reflects phase change of the mineral but does not directly
consume free ions. 
On page 11, the authors mention that "stage (iii) is characterized by a decrease in the ion-selective signal (and a decrease in
the conductivity signal)." Based on this description, in Figure 3, stage (iii) should begin at the peak of the detected cation
concentration, where the decrease starts. However, in the figure, stage (iii) starts in the middle of the decline. The authors
should provide an explanation for this discrepancy. 
The same page: The authors state that STMP significantly delayed the drop in transmittance and free calcium concentration
for portlandite. However, I don’t see the data in Figure 2 support this statement. In the presence of STMP, the decrease of
transmittance seems to decrease even earlier. 
On page 14, the authors state that “these species should thus consist of at least the additive and hydroxide ions, with
calcium ions potentially also playing a role.” Did the authors perform experiments for titration of a NaOH solution in the
additive solutions to prove such statement? 
It is interesting to note the two-step decrease in transmittance in the portlandite-STMP system. On page 15, the authors
attribute the initial drop to prenucleation species. However, in the description of Figure 3, the authors state that “The ion
association and the formation of prenucleation species are characterized by the output signal of an ion-selective probe
compared to the amount of the dosed ions.” Yet, as shown in Figure 2(a), the slopes of these signals remain unchanged.
Could the authors provide more explanation on how the formation of prenucleation species contributes to the decline in
transmittance?

Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Mineral scaling is an interesting process both fundamentally and practically, and it has not been fully explored. This work
investigates the impact of additives on the nucleation pathways of portlandite and gypsum by combining potentiometric
titrations, molecular dynamics simulations, and in situ high-energy X-ray scattering. It extends the authors’ previous studies
(e.g., 1. Madeja, Benjamin, et al. New insights into the nucleation of portlandite and the effects of polymeric additives.
Cement and Concrete Research 173 (2023): 107258; 2. Kellermeier, Matthias, et al. From Ions to Crystals: A
Comprehensive View of the Non
‐
Classical Nucleation of Calcium Sulfate. Angewandte Chemie International Edition 63.51
(2024): e202408429). 
In this work, the authors claim that while both minerals exhibit multistep nucleation, portlandite undergoes a gradual
transition from a disordered to an ordered phase, whereas gypsum demonstrates an abrupt phase transition. The study
highlights the dual roles of additives, which simultaneously delay and accelerate different stages of nucleation, with specific
effects during the prenucleation stage. However, the combination of methods used is insufficient to fully support the paper's
conclusions. Also, using transmittance signal changes to back-interpret the nucleation process is questionable as it primarily
indicates solids formation. 
1. Lack of Direct Evidence: The manuscript provides detailed descriptions of the effects of additives on nucleation pathways
but relies heavily on indirect observations, such as changes in transmittance and free calcium concentration. Direct
evidence to support the proposed mechanisms is notably absent, reducing the robustness of the conclusions. For example,
there is no direct evidence of the transition from a disordered to an ordered phase during portlandite nucleation.
Explanations based on pH or chemical environment changes lack sufficient experimental evidence, such as additive-cation
coordination data. The proposed “sponge” role of PAA for calcium ions during the prenucleation stage is not substantiated
with direct proof. 
2. Two-Step Drop in Portlandite System: Why does the portlandite system experience a two-step drop? What causes this
phenomenon? The authors claim that an increased concentration of STMP accelerates the association of prenucleation
species (evidenced by the earlier transmittance drop) and promotes the formation of larger and/or more prenucleation
species (shown by the more significant transmittance drop). They should provide more direct evidence to support these
claims. 
3. Dual Role of Additives: The manuscript highlights the dual role of additives in modulating nucleation but does not fully
address why specific additives exhibit contrasting behaviors in portlandite and gypsum systems. Are there other effects of
additives besides pH change that influence nucleation mechanisms? 
4. Gypsum System Transition: Based on the continuous increase in diffuse scattering and a sudden decrease in variance
evolution, the authors speculate that gypsum experiences an abrupt transition from disordered species to a crystalline state.
However, no direct evidence, such as imaging or size distribution analysis, is provided to show whether the transition
involves sudden aggregation or crystallization events. Additionally, the attribution of diffuse scattering solely to disordered
species is not explicitly validated. Other phases, such as amorphous intermediates, might also contribute. The center of
mass for portlandite decreases at about 50 mM calcium added and then increases slowly. How is this change explained? If
calcium addition continues for gypsum, how would the center of mass change? The earlier decrease in the center of mass
for phytic acid and STMP compared to the pure portlandite system requires interpretation. What does this suggest about
additive effects? 
5. Stages of Nucleation and Crystallization: The authors conclude that in the gypsum system, the tested additives extend the
prenucleation and nucleation stages but do not affect the crystallization stage, which coincides with the drop in free calcium
concentration. However, it is not confirmed that only the prenucleation and nucleation stages are present without any

crystallization occurring earlier. 
6. STMP and Nucleation: In Figure 6a, the time lag observed when STMP is added is shorter compared to that of portlandite
alone. This phenomenon is not explained in the current context. How does STMP promote nucleation?

Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This study written by Baken et al. explores the influence of various additives on the non-
classical nucleation pathways of portlandite and gypsum, identifying distinct nucleation 
mechanisms for each mineral. It highlights the multifaceted roles of additives, including their 
potential to delay or accelerate specific stages of nucleation. While the manuscript is well-
written, there are some questions that should be addressed before it can be accepted. 
 
On page 10, the authors state, "It is important to note that the observed decrease in 
transmittance depends on the wavelength of the turbidity probe, suggesting that nucleation 
may, in fact, start earlier." It is recommended that the author provide further explanation on 
how the observed decrease in transmittance is influenced by the wavelength of the turbidity 
probe and why this suggests that nucleation may occur earlier. 
 
Reply: The statement in the original manuscript regarding the wavelength dependence of 
transmittance is related to the detection limits of turbidity measurements. The wavelength of 
the turbidity probe influences the size of the smallest particles that can be reliably detected. 
While shorter wavelengths are theoretically more sensitive to smaller particles due to 
increased scattering efficiency (Rayleigh scattering), this is also contingent upon factors such 
as particle concentration, refractive index contrast between the particles and the solution, and 
the intrinsic properties of the medium. 
 
Using a shorter wavelength for turbidity measurements could, in principle, enable earlier 
detection of nucleation because it could detect smaller, early-stage nuclei. However, this 
advantage is counteracted by increased water absorption at shorter wavelengths (particularly 
below 400 nm), which reduces the overall intensity of the transmitted light and compromises 
the sensitivity of the measurement. This creates a trade-off: shorter wavelengths may detect 
smaller particles, but also reduce the signal-to-noise ratio due to increased absorption. 
 
To investigate the practical extent of this effect for our mineral system, we performed 
supplementary experiments with gypsum using a multicell UV-Vis spectrophotometer, 
varying the measurement wavelength from 300 to 1100 nm. These experiments indicated that 
a substantial wavelength difference (approximately 500 nm or more) was required to achieve 
a statistically significant shift in the detected nucleation point. Even with such a large 
wavelength shift, the change in the observed nucleation point was relatively small (less than 
5%). Moreover, at high supersaturation levels where nucleation events are more rapid and 
less sensitive to wavelength variations, no significant difference was observed in the tested 
wavelength range. 
 
The shortest wavelength available with our Optrode turbidity probe is 470 nm. Based on our 
supplementary experiments and the limitations discussed above, using a shorter wavelength 
within the available range (around 470 nm) would not result in a significantly earlier or more 
accurate detection of the nucleation point. Therefore, the wavelength of 610 nm used in our 
experiments does not compromise the validity of our findings.

It is interesting to note the two-step decrease in transmittance in the portlandite-STMP 
system. On page 15, the authors attribute the initial drop to prenucleation species. However, 
in the description of Figure 3, the authors state that “The ion association and the formation of 
prenucleation species are characterised by the output signal of an ion-selective probe 
compared to the amount of the dosed ions.” Yet, as shown in Figure 2(a), the slopes of these 
signals remain unchanged. Could the authors provide more explanation on how the formation 
of prenucleation species contributes to the decline in transmittance? 
 
Reply: We thank the reviewer for identifying this ambiguity in our manuscript stemming 
from our use of the term "prenucleation species" to describe two distinct entities:  
● Formation of primary prenucleation species (PNCs): This involves the association of 
free ions (e.g. Ca²⁺ and OH⁻). This process consumes free ions and is therefore 
tracked by changes in the Ca-ISE signal slope. However, these primary PNCs are too 
small to significantly scatter light. 
● Aggregation of PNCs into a larger species: This is the process responsible for the 
initial drop in transmittance. It involves the aggregation of the already-formed 
primary PNCs. Since this step primarily reorganises existing clusters rather than 
consuming additional free ions from the bulk solution, it does not necessarily alter the 
Ca-ISE slope. 
 
To eliminate this ambiguity, we have revised our terminology. We now reserve the term 
"prenucleation species" for the solute clusters formed by ion association in a one-phase 
system (Process 1). We refer to the larger, light-scattering aggregates as an "intermediate 
species" (Process 2). With this clarification, the observation in Figure 2(a) is now consistently 
explained: the initial, stable Ca-ISE slope reflects the equilibrium of primary PNC formation, 
while the transmittance drop signals the aggregation of these PNCs into a new, light-

updated the text on page 15 and the relevant figure captions accordingly.

suggests a smoother surface, with -4 indicating a perfectly smooth surface and sharp 
interface.

formation lies in the additional complexity introduced by the incorporation of structural water 
in gypsum. Although both systems follow a similar non-classical nucleation pathway, 
involving the aggregation of prenucleation clusters, the subsequent structural reorganisation 
required to form a crystalline nucleus is significantly more intricate for gypsum.

doi.org/10.1002/anie.202408429) showed that gypsum formation proceeds through a multi-
step ordering process. Initially, primary species align into one-dimensional chains, which 
then stack to form two-dimensional proto-lattices. This process requires not only the ordering 
of the Ca-SO₄ cores but also the structuring of the two layers of crystalline water included in 
the structure of gypsum. The need to simultaneously organise both ionic and water 
components introduces a significant kinetic barrier to crystallisation. This additional barrier is 
most likely responsible for the observed lag phase during which solution turbidity increases 
but bulk crystallisation, indicated by a decrease in free ion concentration and the emergence 
of WAXS signal, has not yet commenced (cf. Figure 6b of this work). SAXS data further 
support this interpretation, revealing structural rearrangement within aggregates prior to the 
onset of crystal growth (c.f. stages III-IV in Stawski et al., 2016; DOI: 
10.1038/ncomms11177 | www.nature.com/naturecommunications). Once this organisational 
barrier, particularly the structuring of the water layers, is overcome and a viable (proto-
)crystalline gypsum nucleus is formed, growth proceeds very rapidly, resulting in the near-
instantaneous appearance of micrometer-sized crystals (c.f. Figure 5g, Kelleremeier et al., 
2024; doi.org/10.1002/anie.202408429).  
 
In contrast, the formation of portlandite involves the aggregation and subsequent 
reorganisation of Ca-OH species into stacked hydroxide layers. This process is structurally 
simpler and does not require the coordinated incorporation of water molecules into the crystal 
structure. As a result, the kinetic barrier to nucleation is likely lower or less sharply defined 
than in the case of gypsum. Consequently, the transition from disordered aggregates to 
crystalline nuclei in portlandite appears more continuous, giving rise to a gradual 
development of crystallinity without the distinct lag phase and abrupt crystallisation observed 
in gypsum under similar supersaturation conditions.

3. Dual Role of Additives: The manuscript highlights the dual role of additives in modulating 
nucleation but does not fully address why specific additives exhibit contrasting behaviours in 
portlandite and gypsum systems. Are there other effects of additives besides pH change that 
influence nucleation mechanisms? 
 
Reply:  As the reviewer correctly implies pH change does not fully account for the 
complexity of the additive behaviour in both mineral systems.  
 
For phytic acid, its protonation state appears to be the key factor governing its contrasting 
behaviour in the portlandite and gypsum systems. At pH 12, phytic acid is fully deprotonated 
and forms an amorphous Ca–(OH)–phytate complex, as confirmed by in situ HEXS 
measurements. Theoretical calcium-binding capacities (~0.5 mM Ca2+ at 50 ppm and ~4 mM 
at 500 ppm) align with the initial drop in turbidity and the delayed rise in both Ca-ISE and 
OH⁻ signals (cf. Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure S2c), indicating direct binding of Ca²⁺ 
and OH⁻ until the available binding sites on phytic acid are saturated. The resulting Ca–OH–
phytate complexes appear to be kinetically stable, as evidenced by the persistent turbidity 
plateau, and off-pathway, as they sequester reactants without significantly affecting the 
induction time for portlandite nucleation. Conversely, at pH ~5, the partially deprotonated 
phytic acid (Crea et al., Coordination Chemistry Reviews 252 (2008) 1108–1120) exhibits a 
dual role. It first acts as an anionic template to promote CaSO₄ PNC formation, as indicated 
by the reduced Ca-ISE slope (Table 2 in the manuscript). However, its bulky structure 
sterically inhibits the aggregation of these clusters. This mechanism of templating followed 
by inhibition, ultimately prolonged the induction time for gypsum precipitation. 
  
In contrast, the behaviour of STMP cannot be explained by a single mechanism. Although its 
intrinsic 1:1 binding capacity for Ca²⁺ is identical at pH 6 and 12 (0.164 mM of Ca2+ for 50 
ppm of STMP), its role in crystallisation of portlandite and gypsum diverges significantly. 
The observed behaviour in the gypsum system—namely, the absence of a significant 
decrease in the Ca-ISE signal and minimal nucleation delay—is consistent with direct 
chelation of Ca²⁺ by STMP. Since the amount of calcium bound by STMP is relatively small, 
the effective supersaturation remains largely unaffected. In contrast, for portlandite, STMP

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 27 -->

induces the formation of large, amorphous particles, evidenced by a two-step turbidity drop 
and the early appearance of surface fractal structures detected by SAXS. This behaviour 
stems from a cooperative mechanism unique to this system. The solution contains three key 
charged components: the mineral's precursor complexes (RSC Adv., 2016, 6, 45231–45240; 
Madeja et al., Cem. Concr. Res., 2023, 173, 107258), the highly anionic TMP³⁻, and free Ca²⁺ 
ions. Together, these act as building blocks that rapidly co-aggregate via electrostatic 
interactions to form an extended amorphous network. This model is supported by control 
experiments showing that aggregation is suppressed when Ca²⁺ is replaced by Na⁺ 
(Supplementary Figure S4), and by the absence of similar behaviour in the gypsum system, 
where neutral PNCs do not serve as electrostatic targets for TMP³⁻ and therefore cannot 
initiate bridging. 
 
Also in the case of PAA, the contrasting effects on mineral formation are dictated by the  
nature of the inorganic precursor clusters. In the Ca(OH)₂ system at pH 12, the presence of 
charged species enables a cooperative aggregation mechanism. Fully deprotonated PAA 
chains template the formation of charged prenucleation clusters (RSC Adv., 2016, 6, 45231–
45240; Madeja et al., Cem. Concr. Res., 2023, 173, 107258), as evidenced by the decrease in 
the Ca-ISE slope. These clusters are subsequently bridged by free Ca²⁺ ions, leading to the 
formation of a liquid-like amorphous Ca–OH phase, as indicated by the early transmittance 
drop. This behaviour was also observed in our previous work using PAA of slightly higher 
molecular weight (Mw ~6100 Da) (Madeja et al., Cem. Concr. Res., 2023, 173, 107258), and 
is consistent with PAA’s well-established role in stabilizing polymer-induced liquid 
precursors (PILPs) in CaCO₃ systems (e.g. Shao et al., Chem. Commun., 2024, 60, 3950–
3953). In contrast, in the CaSO₄ system at pH ~6, PAA exhibits a different, dual-function 
behaviour. Here, PAA primarily acts as a template that promotes the formation of neutral 
CaSO₄⁰ clusters, as indicated by the significantly reduced Ca-ISE slope. However, since these 
neutral clusters lack charge-mediated bridging capabilities, PAA instead stabilises them 
through steric and electrostatic interactions. This stabilisation prevents further aggregation, 
prolongs the induction time for nucleation, and inhibits the transition to a macroscopic 
crystalline phase.

Page 10: The mention is made of particles with an interface but if amorphous solids are

Reply: The reviewer raises a valid point about the use of the term "particle" in the context of 
an amorphous phase. In this work we use the term to denote distinct, phase-separated entities 
that, while lacking long-range crystalline order, exhibit a defined boundary or interface with 
the surrounding solution. This definition differentiates them from the prenucleation species 
(ionic clusters), which we consider to be solutes without a distinct interface. 
To support our classification of the amorphous phase as particulate in nature, we have now 
included complementary small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) data. These SAXS 
measurements were carried out with the same co-titration setup and experimental conditions 
as those used for the pair distribution function (PDF) experiments, allowing for a direct 
comparison across techniques. 
 
Since SAXS measurements are performed at lower energies compared to PDF measurements, 
they are better suited for probing larger-sized species. However, because portlandite is a 
weak scatterer, using lower-energy X-rays results in even lower scattering intensity than what 
we obtained in the PDF measurements—where we were already working at the detection 
limit. As a result, our analysis of the SAXS data was limited to interpreting the slope of the 
intensity curve I(q)  as a function of the scattering vector (Figure H), a parameter well-suited 
for probing early nucleation stages in dilute solutions (e.g. Turhan et al., 2024, J. Am. Chem. 
Soc., 146, 37, 25614–25624). 
 
The slope of the intensity curve I(q) versus the scattering vector q on a log–log plot provides 
insights into the structure of the species:  
● A slope between -1 and -3 typically indicates a mass fractal, where the exponent 
corresponds to the fractal dimension and reflects how densely the structure fills space.  
● A slope between -3 and -4 suggests a surface fractal, meaning the object has a rough 
or irregular surface, with -4 indicating a smooth surface.

(0.004 Å−1 < 𝐼 < 0.015 Å−1) started to increase at 13.3 mM added Ca2+. Initially, the slope

associated with prenucleation species that do not show a clear interface. However, as titration

Reply: During data analysis of the intensity curves from the co-titration experiments, we 
subtracted the water signal as background to isolate the contribution from the ions. This 
subtraction resulted in a very weak residual intensity curve, differing by no more than 0.51% 
from the original water signal. Given this minimal difference, artifacts introduced by 
background subtraction—such as artificial ripples or waves—may significantly affect the 
resulting structure factor. These artifacts can then translate into non-physical frequencies in 
the pair distribution function (PDF) after Fourier transformation. Since such artifacts are 
more likely to appear for shorter wavelengths, the likelihood of observing artificial peaks 
increases with higher r values (i.e. higher frequencies). Therefore, any barely visible peak at r 
values slightly beyond the first interatomic distances of pure portlandite should be considered 
ambiguous.

Review of manuscript NCOMMS-24-80196 Additive-Specific Influences on Non-Classical Nucleation Pathways in Industrially Relevant Minerals   This is a very well written, interesting paper on the role of additives on the early stages of mineral formation, using portlandite and gypsum as studied systems. It provides evidence of the complex influence of additives on the crystallization process and, in this sense, the results of this study provide valuable insights into the field of mineral formation, offering guidance for designing specialized additives to enhance industrial crystallization processes, but are also of potential interest for a wide audience, as the observations described and conclusions obtained can be extrapolated to fields different from industrial processes where mineral formation is modulated by additives/organics, such as biomineralization. The study in general is firmly grounded in existing literature, citing relevant works that establish a strong foundation for the analysis; however, the authors have missed some recent works using similar methodology on the studied minerals, which are not cited in the manuscript (see for example https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.cgd.0c00241 or https://doi.org/10.1002/smll.202402581).  Also, this research complements and builds on previous work, presenting data that reinforces the current understanding of the subject. However, the authors should make an effort to clearly stress which is the main novelty of their findings, beyond contributing to the existing body of knowledge on the impact of additives on the prenucleation and early post- nucleation mineral formation stages. The conclusions are well-supported by the experimental data provided (titration experiments and X-ray scattering analysis), with a sound interpretation of the main outcomes of their experiments. The methodology used and the rigorous data analysis adheres to the highest standards of quality expected in this area of research. As far as I can detect, there are no significant flaws in the data analysis, interpretation, or conclusions, and the evidence presented supports the claims made. However, I have a main issue here, as I think that the description made of the different stages during precipitation (see for example Figure 3) is not sufficiently clear. The authors describe stage 2 as the nucleation stage – the formation of an interface in the system-; but they do not indicate whether the emergent phase could be a dense liquid phase, or it is a solid phase. In the second case: is this first phase appearing necessarily amorphous? Does this rule out the possibility of the direct formation of a crystalline phase as the first (solid) phase in the system? And, if this were the case, would nucleation and crystallization coincide? I think this should be clarified in the text to facilitate the interpretation of the results. A minor issue is that, similarly to the role of additive protonation determined by pH, the authors should comment on the potential effect of the surface charge of the developing phases when explaining the differential interactions of additives with portlandite and gypsum during the crystallization stage (i.e. once there is a

Could this play a role (or not) at determining the different effects of the additives on the two mineral phases? Given my general impression of the manuscript described above, I consider that this research makes a substantial contribution to the field, meeting the expected standards of quality, and as such I would recommend it for publication, if the authors address the clarifications described above.
