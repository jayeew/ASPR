Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
The authors present an efficient method for designing bulk active metamaterials that 
enable precise control over the amplitude and direction of Willis vectors. Two compelling 
examples, a nonreciprocal directional filter and a steerable beamforming shell, are used to 
demonstrate their potential in wavefront control, both numerically and experimentally. This 
study outlines a comprehensive research process, from the formulation of the conceptual 
model to simulation validation and experimental measurements, presenting convincing 
results that make it suitable for consideration for publication in Communication Physics. 
However, there are a few issues that still need to be addressed. 
We thank the reviewer for appreciating our work and for the constructive comments and 
suggestions. 
 
1. The 1D case of nonreciprocal directional filter is well presented, but it seems that for 
incident waves opposite to coupling vectors are spitted into two outgoing waves. Is there 
any theoretical basis to describe their scattering angles? The author can give further 
discussion on this point. 
The apparent splitting of the incident wave is the result of the oblique reflections from the 
side of the square geometry. Similar reflections occur for many acoustic property sets for 
this geometry. When 𝑆⃗ is aligned with the incident wave, the bulk modulus and Willis 
monopole components cancel, so no scattering is produced. When 𝑆⃗ is opposed to the 
incident wave, the monopole components are instead approximately added. The result is a 
total field that is nearly identical to that for a slab of increased bulk modulus. The figure 
below compares simulated total fields for three property sets and two square sizes, 
showing how the 𝑆⃗ can be set to zero and the bulk modulus increased for similar results. 
Thus, the basis for the scattering angles can be found in the conventional case.

2. For the 2D case of a beamforming shell, the paper currently only considers a specific 
incident angle and how does the change in the incident angle relative to the center affect 
the asymmetric transmission? 
Because the shell is axisymmetric, the incident angle can be changed through the vertical 
displacement of the exterior x-dipole source. FEM simulated total fields are shown below 
for 𝐷𝑒𝑓𝑓=0.5, transitioning from the deflected case to the transmitted case. The shells 
exhibit rotational bias for any wave that enters, as is apparent from the middle case where 
the beam from the centered external source is guided downwards. The exact behavior is 
dependent on the geometry and the Willis vector magnitude.  
 
 
3. Considering that 2D planar metasurface platform have showed their ability in effectively 
achieve directional control of complex sound vortex fields , such as asymmetric vortex 
transmission, e.g.,Sci. Adv. 6, eaba9876 (2020) and Phys. Rev. Lett. 128, 104501 (2022), 
whether the method proposed in this paper can be extended to the control of acoustic

Thank you for the suggestion, we have added this topic in the discussion of Fig. 3f. Applying

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
In this paper, the authors proposed an active unit consisting of four microphones and four 
speakers to mimic the effect of Willis coupling. The microphones probe the incident 
signals, then microcontroller will drive the speakers according to the predesigned time-
modulated Willis coupling vectors. Several impressive wave behaviors have been 
demonstrated experimentally. Below are some comments. 
We thank the reviewer for appreciating our work and for the constructive comments and 
suggestions. 
 
1\ I notice that the active unit should be triggered by the incident signal. Are the speakers 
muted without excitation? 
An electrical trigger can be used to turn the cells on/off. When the cells are on and there is 
no acoustic wave to excite them, they are nearly silent. This is because the speakers are 
programmed to only produce a response which is a linear superposition of the local 
pressure and particle velocity conditions (essentially zero when there is no external 
excitation). 
 
2\ When the mics probe the signals, how to cancel the sound generated by the nearby 
speakers. The signal processing running on the microcontroller should be discussed. 
The sound from the nearby speakers is not canceled and needs to be taken into account in 
the design of transfer functions between the cell microphones and the speakers.  Details 
on how these transfer functions are selected in order to give the desired effective properties 
in the presence of sounds from nearby cells are included in the previous work with a similar 
metamaterial architecture [D. A. Kovacevich and B.-I. Popa, Smart Materials and Structures 
10.1088/1361-665X/ad7550 (2024)] and in the revised supplemental material.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

3\ When it comes to active metamaterials, the consistency of devices is a challenge. For a 
system consisting of many units like this case, how to choose the active elements, or will 
the consistency influent the performance? Besides, how to avoid the self-oscillation in this 
case. 
The selection of the active elements is important for the determination of the frequency 
response, but the manufacturing consistency is not a significant concern. The cells can be 
individually calibrated to adjust the gains and also, for a metamaterial of many 
subwavelength cells, there is an averaging effect such that a small deviation in the 
response of any single cell has little consequence. In addition, we have created a method 
that allows us to choose the transfer functions between the cell speakers and microphones 
in bulk metamaterials [D. A. Kovacevich and B.-I. Popa, Smart Materials and Structures 
10.1088/1361-665X/ad7550 (2024)]. According to this method, with highly subwavelength 
cells, only small gains are needed, avoiding unstable self-oscillations. 
 
4\ It is claimed that it is an ultra-fast design. What’s the highest frequency this system can 
deal with? 
The sample rate for these particular cells is 96000 Hz, setting this value as the upper limit 
for the rate at which the gains can be modified. However, the upper cutoff frequencies of 
the speakers and digital filters (necessary to maintain stability) will constrain the actual 
modulation that can be achieved in the acoustic response. The filter implemented in the 
experiments had a cutoff frequency of 5000 Hz, but a different filter could be used to enable 
a higher modulation rate.   
   
5\ The total pressure field as time change should be provided as a supplementary video. 
Thank you for the suggestion, supplementary videos of the experimental demonstrations 
have been added.  
 
6\ Please give more discussion why more harmonic frequencies can be excited. 
 
In radio communications, the amplitude/phase modulation of a carrier wave results in 
sidebands at integer multiples of the modulation frequency, centered on the carrier 
frequency. Similarly, the response of each cell of the active metamaterial with time varying 
acoustic properties is a modulation of the incident acoustic wave. The resultant acoustic 
field will therefore feature sidebands at intervals of the modulation frequency of the gain,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 10 -->

with the additional complexity of many cells and variation in space compared to the radio 
communications example. 
 
Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This manuscript presents an impressive experimental and theoretical study combining 
Willis coupling with time modulation in active acoustic metamaterials. The authors 
successfully demonstrate programmable, ultra-fast time-modulated Willis vectors that 
enable dynamic nonreciprocity and beam steering within a bulk two-dimensional acoustic 
medium. This study is rigorous with both simulations and experimental data and the idea is 
of practical interest. Therefore, I would like to recommend publishing this Letter if the 
following issues can be explained or supplemented. 
 
We thank the reviewer for appreciating our work and for the constructive comments and 
suggestions. 
 
1. The authors mention rapid modulation. How is the rapid response ensured, and have any 
specific quantitative indicators been measured to verify it? 
Because of the simple open loop design strategy, the gains of an active unit cell, and 
consequently the acoustic properties of the metamaterial, can be adjusted at the digital 
sample rate of 96000 Hz. The presence of the sidebands at the proper intervals indicates 
that the response was modulated at the desired frequency. For future work, one approach 
of interest would be to examine the response of just a single cell at different modulation 
frequencies and use the magnitudes/phases of the sidebands to quantify the frequency 
response. 
 
2. The modulation frequency reaches up to 2𝑓₀. What factors limit this range? Is it 
determined by digital control latency, speaker dynamics, or acoustic feedback stability? 
The sample rate for these particular cells is 96000 Hz, setting this value as the upper limit 
for the rate at which the gains can be modified. However, the upper cutoff frequencies of 
the speakers and digital filters (necessary to maintain stability) will constrain the actual 
modulation that can be achieved in the acoustic response. The filter implemented in the 
experiments had a cutoff frequency of 5000 Hz, but a different filter could be used to enable 
a higher modulation rate.  The modulation frequencies used in this work were mostly lower 
than the frequency of the incident wave because for the demonstrated applications it was 
desirable to have total fields that vary in time. The 2𝑓₀ example was chosen to show that for

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 11 -->

modulation frequencies greater than the incident wave frequency, the acoustic properties 
will be homogenized in time. Others have shown that high modulation rates may give rise to 
interesting phenomena such as gain or loss. This can certainly be a topic for another study 
using the same active metamaterial design approach, especially with properties that are 
varied simultaneously in space and time. 
 
3. How were the microphone and speaker responses calibrated to ensure consistent 
effective parameters across the 16-cell array? Could small gain mismatches lead to spatial 
inhomogeneity in the Willis vector? 
The cells can be individually calibrated to adjust the gains, but for a metamaterial of many 
subwavelength cells, there is an averaging effect such that a small deviation in the 
response of any single cell has little consequence. The impact of such variations at the unit 
cell level on the total field can be easily simulated using the polarized source method. After 
each unit cell was fabricated, their speakers were driven with pulses and the on board 
microphone signals were recorded. The results showed roughly 10% variation, which was 
small enough that the gains of each cell did not need to be individually adjusted for the 
demonstrations of interest. 
 
4. Have the authors considered other temporal profiles? Could non-sinusoidal modulation 
yield richer spectral sidebands or new effective properties? 
Yes, with this approach, the acoustic properties can be modulated by an arbitrary time-
dependent function (not necessarily periodic) through the programmed gains. Sinusoidal 
modulation was chosen here for the simplicity of having a single modulation frequency, but 
other works on time modulated metamaterials have used square waves because on/off 
functionality is the easiest to implement. It should be expected that more complex 
sidebands emerge for different profiles as they do in conventional time modulated signals. 
We believe that by presenting active acoustic metamaterials as a viable strategy for 
experimental demonstration, there is greater motivation for theoretical developments that 
will suggest applications of more advanced modulation, both in time and space. 
 
Reviewer #4 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
This work introduced an active metamaterial of two-dimensional geometry that can be 
programmed for desired Willis vector magnitude and direction. Combining theoretical 
analysis, and experimental results, the authors demonstrate the unique role of rapid 
construction of Willis vector magnitude and direction. The topic of the paper is novel and

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 12 -->

the work is comprehensive. Overall, the manuscript has the potential for publication, 
although several issues still need to be clarified and addressed. 
We thank the reviewer for appreciating our work and for the constructive comments and 
suggestions. 
 
1. The author mentions " The unit cell size relative to the wavelength at this frequency is 
λ0/5.5 sufficiently subwavelength for homogenization. " . What are the upper and lower 
limits of its operating frequency for the designed active metamaterial? How does the 
structural performance change as the operating wavelength decreases? 
 
In our experience with many types of metamaterials (active and passive), a rule of thumb is 
that the operating wavelength inside and outside the metamaterial should be at least five 
times bigger than the cell size. When the operating frequency increases above 1200 Hz so 
that the cell size is no longer smaller than λ0/5, then the metamaterial no longer behaves 
like a continuous material. The derived relationships between the programmed gains and 
acoustic properties also lose their validity. The fields such active metamaterials will 
generate can still be simulated through the polarized source method and the error 
quantified for a particular case. A metamaterial with large cells relative to the wavelength is 
better characterized as a phononic crystal. 
 
2. In the experimental section, when describing the sound source and measurement 
methodology, it is suggested to include a brief statement regarding the sources of 
experimental error or the measurement accuracy to enhance the repeatability of the 
results. 
Thank you for the suggestion, we have added such a statement. In consideration of 
repeatability, the largest sources of error would likely be in the physical fabrication of the 
active metamaterial rather than the measurements. Variations in the speakers from the 
manufacturer and inconsistencies in their mounting on the PCBs can result in a distribution 
cell responses. With a metamaterial of many cells, these errors should mostly average out, 
but systemic errors may also be present. For example, we encountered a change in speaker 
design between orders without a change in the part number or datasheet. Some systemic 
errors were overcome through a calibration process. 
 
3. Was thermo-viscous loss considered in the numerical simulations? If not, a brief 
discussion on how the loss might affect the results.

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 13 -->

Thermo-viscous loss was not considered. In passive metamaterials, significant loss can 
occur due to resonances and/or propagation through porous structures (e.g., propagation 
through passive labirinthine structures), but this is not the case for the active 
metamaterials presented here. Additionally, the waveguide is not so narrow that loss from 
the boundary layer in the waveguide plates is significant. The largest source of loss is likely 
actually leakage from the mounting of the metamaterial, which results in small gaps in the 
bottom plate of the waveguide. The excitation (background) field used in the simulations is 
the same as the experimental, so it also includes this loss. 
 
4. Can this scheme be extended to the design of three-dimensional metamaterials? If not, 
what are the issues compared to the two-dimensional case? 
Yes, the design strategy can be extended to three dimensions with unit cells that have an 
additional pair of speakers in the third direction. This is primarily a challenge of fabrication, 
but it will also be necessary to account for the scattering from passive structures that the 
2D arrangement conveniently circumvents. It is expected that for sparse supporting 
structure, small adjustments to the gain would suffice. 
 
5. What is the adjustment range for Seff and Beff in this work? 
The achievable property range for an active metamaterial was the topic of our previous 
work on the stability of these systems: D. A. Kovacevich, K. Grosh, and B.-I. Popa, Physical 
Review Applied 21, L051002 (2024). Many aspects of the design, including the active 
element frequency responses, the programmed filters, and the metamaterial geometry 
influence this range. For example, an isolated cell will be stable with a larger range of 
properties than a metamaterial of many cells. It is worth noting that for Willis parameters, 
feedback within a cell is minimal due to the cross pairings of monopole-particle velocity 
and dipole-pressure, yielding a larger stable range. The parameter ranges demonstrated in 
this work, though not the maximum that can be achieved, are 𝜅𝑒𝑓𝑓=1-1.4, 𝑆𝑒𝑓𝑓=0-1, and 
𝐷𝑒𝑓𝑓=0-0.4.
