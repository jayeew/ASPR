Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present Coral, a basecaller for nanopore DRS reads. Coral uses an encoder-decoder architecture with auto-
regressive to learn the contextual dependencies at both signal and sequence levels, outperforming the encoder-CTC
architecture and the compared methods (Guppy and RODAN) in basecalling accuracy. The authors demonstrate that the
improved basecalling accuracy benefits several downstream analyses, including transcript quantification, ASE analysis and
haplotype phasing. I agree with the authors that this work provide a benchmark for accuracy in nanopore DRS applications.
However, I also have several serious concerns. 
1. I believe one of the main contributions of this work is the auto-regressive decoder which considers the context
dependencies at both signal and sequence levels. The authors have shown the next base prediction accuracy given a
correct 5-mer context (Fig. 2g). I wonder if a 5-mer was wrongly predicted, how would it influence the prediction of the next 5-
mers or bases? 
2. The datasets used in this study are either RNA001 or RNA002 DRS reads. However, both RNA001 and RNA002 are now
considered outdated, replaced by the RNA004 kit. There are already publicly available RNA004 data now. It is necessary to
test Coral using RNA004 data. 
- Following this, I suggest that the authors develop the Coral tool to support POD5 format. 
3. The authors compared Coral with Guppy and RODAN. However, ONT has already released another basecaller Dorado
for practical use. Also, a third-party basecaller GCRTcall was released and published recently
(https://doi.org/10.3389/fgene.2024.1443532). 
- The authors should compare Coral with Dorado and GCRTcall using the datasets used in this study. 
- Dorado, Guppy, and GCRTcall all support RNA004 reads. A comparison on RNA004 data should also be performed
between Coral and all other methods. 
4. 
As we all know, basecalling of nanopore reads is a very computationally intensive task, so computational efficiency is an
important consideration in the development of basecalling tools. However, I didn’t find any description about the
computational efficiency of Coral. 
- Does Coral run faster and need less memory than other methods? The author should compare the time and memory usage
of different tools. 
- The authors demonstrated that the auto-regressive decoder in Coral outperforms the CTC-decoder in decoding accuracy.
As CTC enables parallel decoding, the authors may also compare the two approaches in terms of computational efficiency
for further investigation. 
Minor 
1. Regarding the tool, I suggest the authors output some statistics in the log/stderr of Coral, such as the number of reads that
processed, successfully basecalled, and failed. 
2. In Figure 4, the font in panel (e) is inconsistent with other panels.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript from Xie S. et al described a new base-caller Coral for base-calling direct RNA sequencing data. 
They show
that Coral is more accurate than two other base callers, Guppy and RODAN, and showed that this increase in accuracy
enables the authors to identify downstream events such as splicing isoforms and allele-specific transcripts better. 
While
direct RNA sequencing has been useful in the RNA field, its usage has been hampered by its relatively poor accuracy. 
As
such, this work is timely and important. 
However, additional controls need to be done to truly demonstrate Coral’s
improvements over existing tools. 
Below are my main comments: 
1) The authors benched marked Coral against Guppy and RODAN, unfortunately due to the rapid speed of advancements in
the nanopore field, Nanopore now has a more recent base caller Dorado. 
It would be important for the authors to include
Dorado in their benchmarking to show that Coral is still the best. 
2) It is unclear whether the sequencing in this manuscript is done using the direct RNA sequencing 002 kit or the 004 kit.
Nanopore has shown improvements in base calling accuracy for direct RNA sequencing based on the 004 kit vs the 002 kit.
The authors would need to show compatibility with 004 kit and whether their base-calling for the reads from the 004 kit are
also more accurate. 
3) The authors used annotated isoforms from NA12878 as benchmark- while this is useful, it is difficult to know the absolute
truth in this dataset. 
It might be more useful to benchmark on a synthetic RNA pool (such as using SIRV synthetic RNA
pools) whereby we know the truth- to determine the ability to detect transcript isoforms and low abundance transcripts. 
Using
this synthetic RNA pool, the authors could also determine the abundance level cutoffs that enable effective detection by the
three methods. 
4) For the newly identified isoforms (for eg RPL14-201)- can the authors do some experimental validation, such as RT-PCR,
followed by sanger sequencing to determine whether this new isoform indeed exists? I have the same comment for fusion
genes.

RNA sequencing (DRS). The authors propose an encoder-decoder network architecture that captures dependencies at both

Remarks to the author: 
The authors make adjustments to the initially proposed architecture by replacing the CNN-RNN encoder with the CNN-
Transformer encoder and the LSTM-decoder with the Transformer decoder. These new architecture brings improvements to
the initially proposed architecture in both accuracy and decoding time. 
Coral supports RNA001/002 and RNA004 chemistry,
outperforming existing basecallers across various chemistries, datasets, and evaluation metrics. The authors responded to
the concerns previously raised, including reviewer 2’s comments 1 and 2 regarding the support for RNA004 and its
comparison with Dorado. The authors also benchmark on the SIRV benchmark, showing that the obtained results are
comparable with Dorado, with improvements compared with other basecallers. Comment 4 is partially addressed with a
complementary in-silico and orthogonal validation analysis due to limitations in data access. 
However, there are some concerns regarding the generalizability of the model and the transparency of the ablation and
model size. 
Major comments: 
The authors evaluate the proposed models on multiple RNA001/002 datasets and a human RNA004 dataset. While the
evaluation sets correspond to different cell lines, and the benefits of having a model trained on as much data as possible are
obvious, a comprehensive argumentation of the model’s generalization ability needs to include a leave-one-out approach.
The authors should evaluate the model on data that was not seen by the model during the training in any way. This would
correspond to removing one species from the RNA001/002 training dataset, and evaluating the model on that species, as
well as removing one chromosome from the RNA004 training dataset and evaluating the model on that chromosome. Such
an analysis would show that the model does not memorize reference-specific details and can be used on previously unseen
species without the risk of collapsing. 
The authors support the proposed encoder-decoder architecture by performing a set of ablation experiments. However, they
do not provide many details of the performed ablation experiments. How is the Transformer-decoder replaced by the CTC-
decoder? How does the architecture of the obtained model compare to Dorado, which incorporates Transformer architecture
and CTC loss? If Coral’s CTC decoder architecture corresponds closely to Dorado, what brings the improvement compared
with Dorado; is it the size of the model? How does the size of the ablated model compare to the original Coral model? Are
there differences in that aspect that would affect the accuracy difference between the original and ablated model? The
proposed encoder architecture includes a 12-fold down-sampling, does that impose issues to CTC decoding? 
Minor comments 
The authors do not comment on the overall size of the proposed Coral model compared with other architectures. This
information would benefit the understanding of all advancements proposed by Coral’s architecture. 
The authors propose the architecture where the input is 12-fold down-sampled before being passed to the Transformer
encoder, and up-sampled at the output of the Transformer. Justification for this approach, along with the details of the up-
sampling module, would be beneficial for understanding the architecture. 
The proposed regularization loss FRE 
aims to penalize non-monotonic alignments by comparing the consecutive expected
alignment positions ß. Can the authors comment on the cases where values obtained for ß seem to be monotonic but are
actually averaged values of distant and unrelated positions in the signal? 
It would be beneficial if the authors could comment and support the design choice for training the Coral-FAST model using
part of the weights trained with the original Coral approach. Have you tried training from zero? How do the pretrained
weights help the training, given the objective shift? 
It would be important to mention that experimental validation of new isoforms and fusion genes is necessary.

Thank you for your thoughtful and constructive feedback on our manuscript. We 
sincerely appreciate the opportunity to address your comments and further enhance our 
work. Following the  suggestions, we have carried out additional experiments to 
demonstrate that Coral is competitive or superior to  Guppy, RODAN, Dorado and 
GCRTcall on both RNA001/002 and RNA004 reads (Response Figs. 2-3) in terms of 
basecalling accuracy. We also expanded validation to SIRV spike -in controls, where 
Coral delivers the highest concordance among all basecallers tested (Response Figure 
7). To address the concerns regarding computational efficiency raised by two 
reviewers, we have improved the decoding efficiency of Coral by replac ing the 
original LSTM-based latent-alignment decoder with a Transformer decoder that 
integrates cross -attention alignment and beam -search inference.  This change 
enables highly parallel decoding while improving alignment precision. Accordingly, the 
CNN–RNN encoder is updat ed to a CNN–Transformer architecture, so that both 
encoding and decoding now benefit from multi-head self-attention, improving accuracy. 
These changes together lead to a reduction of decoding time by nearly four-fold and an 
improvement of basecalling accuracy (Response Fig. 4).  
 
With the new version of Coral, the median read identity on the NA12878 RNA sample 
increased from 95.56% to 97.69%  (Response Fig. 3), which also facilitated the  
transcript isoforms discovery, haplotype phasing, and allele-specific expression. These 
refinements are attributed to the better long-range signal and sequence dependencies 
modeling capability of Transformers than the recurrent networks.  
 
All requested analyses have been re -performed with the new version of Coral  and the 
results have been updated in this revision
. In summary, we have introduced the 
following major changes: 
 
1) To reinforce the  cross-species generalization, we have expanded the training 
corpus from four to eight species with RNA001/002 kits, then tested across six 
independent datasets. Coral achieved an average median accuracy of 
approximately 97%, outperforming other compared basecallers (Fig. 2a–f).   
2) Coral was trained on a human reference RNA 004 sample and evaluated on an 
independent HEK293T RNA004 dataset. Coral achieved a median accuracy of 
99.52% (Fig. 2g), which surpassed other basecallers, including Dorado (98.28%).  
3) As suggested, we have involved Dorado (v0.8.3) and GCRTcall (v1) in comparison

Response: We fully agree that computational efficiency is a critical aspect for nanopore 
basecalling tools. To improve the computational efficiency of Coral, we introduced 
architectural changes to Coral by replacing the original LSTM -based decoder with a 
Transformer decoder of efficient cross -attention for alignment and standard beam 
search for decoding, to reduce the decoding time . We also developed the Coral-FAST 
mode, which predicts k consecutive bases per auto-regressive step. We provided both a 
theoretical complexity analysis ( Response Table 1 ) and empirical benchmarks 
(Response Figs. 4 and 5) comparing Coral with  the existing basecallers in terms of 
decoding time, memory usage, and basecalling accuracy. These results have also been 
discussed in the “Discussion” section of the revised manuscript.  
 
Comparison with existing CTC-based basecallers 
All CTC -based models—Guppy, RODAN, GCRTcall, Dorado, and Coral -CTC (i.e., 
Coral with CTC decoder)—share the same theoretical decoding complexity of 𝑂𝑂(𝑇𝑇 ∗
𝑑𝑑 ∗ 𝑉𝑉), where 𝑇𝑇 is the length of signal features , 𝑑𝑑 is the dimension size, and 𝑉𝑉 is 
the alphabet size. This is reflected in our benchmarks (Response Figs. 4 and 5), where 
the decoding time of the basecallers fall within a comparable range. However, Coral-
CTC consistently outperforms the others in terms of  basecalling accuracy —1–5% 
higher (e.g., Coral-CTC: 94.49% vs Guppy: 89.52%, RODAN: 90.64%, Dorado: 93.46% 
on A549 RNA002 data) —at a similar time and memory cost. We attribute this to the 
encoder in Coral, which provides enhanced feature extraction compared to other models. 
 
Auto-regressive decoding vs. CTC 
Compared to Coral -CTC, the auto-regressive decoding variants —Coral-LSTM (i.e., 
Coral with LSTM-based decoder), Coral, and Coral-FAST—all leverage a dual-context 
decoder that integrates long- range signal- and sequence-level dependencies, resulting 
in substantial accuracy improvements. Notably, Coral achieved a basecalling accuracy 
of 97.29% on RNA002 and 99.44% on RNA004 datasets. However, as shown in 
Response Table 1 , the computational complexity of these models scales with the 
sequence length  𝐿𝐿 , leading to a marked increase in decoding time and memory 
consumption (e.g., ~ 4000 seconds for Coral, as shown in Response Fig . 5). This 
overhead primarily arises from the long length of ONT reads and the inherent sequential 
limitations of auto -regressive models. The complexity of Coral-FAST is theoretically 
reduced to 𝑂𝑂(
𝐿𝐿
𝑘𝑘 ∗ 𝑁𝑁 ∗ 𝑑𝑑 ∗ 𝑑𝑑 ). As a result, Coral -FAST achieves a nearly 4× speed-up

RNA002). Compared to Coral, Coral -FAST also demonstrates a 4× speed -up with a

Comment 3: The authors motivate the down- then up-sampling approach by the need 
to preserve sufficient temporal resolution for robust decoding, with the up- sampling 
done along the embedding dimension rather than the temporal dimension. It should be 
discussed why a smaller scale down -sampling would not be a good choice compared 
with this approach, and how exactly this up -sampling approach helps preserve 
sufficient temporal resolution. 
 
Response: We thank the reviewer for this insightful question. The encoder architecture 
of Coral is intentionally designed to balance the Transformer's computational cost with 
the decoder’s requirement for high temporal resolution. To validate this choice, we have 
trained an ablated Coral -L3 model by removing the up- sampling step and directly 
applying 6-fold down-sampling followed by Transformer layers in the encoder . The 
ablated Coral-L3 was compared to the standard Coral-L3 model on the RNA004 dataset. 
As shown in Response Table 5 , the standard Coral -L3 attain ed the same median 
accuracy while requiring substantially fewer computational resources in terms of 
GFLOPs and GPU memory usage. The results indicate that a 12-fold down-sampling 
is sufficient for Transformer -based contextual modeling and is considerably more 
efficient. 
 
The up- sampling applied after the Transformer encoder restores an effective 6 -fold 
down-sampling resolution. The design ensures that, during training, the signal feature 
sequence remains longer than the target nucleotide sequence —a necessary condition 
for stable alignment in both CTC and Transformer decoder. In addition, the 6 -fold 
resolution provides finer granularity for decoding. As shown in Response Figure 2 , 
this resolution adequately covers the dwell time of most signal events. 
 
All corresponding results and detailed explanations have been provided in the revised 
manuscript and supplementary materials (Section “Methods”, Table S13, and Fig S22).
