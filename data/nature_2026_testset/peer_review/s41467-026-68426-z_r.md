Response: Thank you for your constructive advice. First , we provide a detailed 
schematic illustration of the setup and sample growth process in Fig.R1a. The setup 
consists of two stainless steel plates (10 cm × 2 cm × 0.3 cm) with threaded holes for 
fastening. During growth, MnSb2Te4 crystal powder s are  first placed onto a mica 
substrate. A eutectic mixture of NaCl and KCl in a 1:1 molar ratio is then evenly 
distributed on mica substrate. Another piece of mica is placed on top to cover both the 
precursor and the mixed salts. The setup is fastened with screws, and the applied 
pressure is regulated by the number of screw  turns. The setup is then loaded into the 
tube furnace, and argon gas is introduced to purge atmospheric oxygen. The system is 
heated to 700 °C within 20 minutes, held at that temperature for 3 minutes, and then 
allowed to cool naturally to room temperature with a constant Ar flow rate of 50 sccm  
throughout the process. The use of Ar is crucial for removing oxygen from the system  
because MnSb2Te4 nanosheets are highly sensitive to air exposure. Noted that when the 
temperature rises to the melting point of mixed salts ~ 650 °C, MnSb2Te4 crystal powder 
will dissolve into the molten salts to form a uniform flux, then hold at the target 
temperature, the flux will flow around the mica and vaporize slowly, finally, the sample 
can be precipitated and grown. After cooling, we can get samples from both inner 
surfaces of mica. Fig.R1b illustrates the reaction states at different temperatures during 
the growth process. At low temperatures, the mixed salts do not melt and thus fail to 
form a flux, preventing sample formation. In the temperature range of 650-720 °C, the 
target samples precipitate from the flux. At temperatures above 7 60 °C, phase 
separation occurs dramatically, leading to the formation of MnTe as a secondary phase.

Response: Thank you very much for your insightful advice. We fully agree that the 
substrate plays a critical role in guiding the growth behavior of MB2T4 flakes. As shown 
in Fig.1f, the flakes grown on mica exhibit a high degree of in-plane alignment, which

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

6 
strongly suggests epitaxial or quasi-epitaxial growth. This is likely due to the atomically 
flat surface and surface symmetry of mica, which provides a favorable template for the 
oriented growth of MnSb2Te4. MnSb2Te4 crystallizes in the R-3m space group with a 
threefold rotational symmetry. And the surface of fluorophlogopite, due to slight lattice 
distortion, also exhibits a reduced threefold symmetry [ 3, 4 ]. This symmetry 
compatibility may allow MnSb2Te4 to adopt energetically favorable orientations during 
growth. In addition to symmetry matching, the in-plane lattice constant of MnSb2Te4 is 
4.2385 Å, while that of fluor ophlogopite is 9.2123 Å. A supercell comparison reveals 
that a 9 × 9 MnSb2Te4 domain (9 × 4.2385 = 38.1465 Å) closely matches with 
fluorophlogopite supercell (36.849, 37.217 , and 37.228 Å in different directions ), 
resulting in a lattice mismatch of only ~ 2.4-3.4%, as shown in Fig.R3a. This relatively 
small mismatch, combined with symmetry compatibility, facilitates aligned growth of 
MnSb2Te4 via epitaxy.  A similar symmetry -guided alignment mechanism was 
previously demonstrated in our group’s work on α-In2Se3 (R-3m space group) growth, 
where the lattice and symmetry match with fluor ophlogopite enabled the formation of 
parallel-oriented flakes with preferred orientations at 0° and 60° [3].  
 
Fig.R3. Schematic illustration of MnSb2Te4 alignment on fluorophlogopite mica. a. The 
crystal structure along the c-axis direction of fluorophlogopite, among which 3 different 
alignment types are shown to match with the 9 × 9 MnSb2Te4 domain. b. The view along 
the c-axis direction of MnSb2Te4.  
To further study the substrate effect, we performed experiments using other common

7 
substrates, including sapphire and SiO2/Si. As shown in Fig.R4a and R4b, respectively, 
on these substrates, the samples resulted in randomly oriented and isolated thick crystals, 
rather than the well -aligned ultrathin flakes observed on mica . For sapphire, the in -

MnSb2Te4. In addition, the sapphire surface is artificially cut, producing atomic terraces 
rather than being atomically flat, thereby preventing effective symmetry matching 
during growth [5]. For SiO2/Si, it is amorphous and lacks any obvious lattice symmetry, 
making epitaxial alignment impossible. Furthermore, under identical growth conditions, 
MnSb2Te4 samples grown on sapphire and  SiO2/Si substrates tend to exhibit larger 
thicknesses, but lower areal densities compared to those synthesized on 
fluorophlogopite mica. This difference is primarily attributed to surface topography 
among the substrates. The atomically flat surface of fluor ophlogopite offers minimal 
kinetic barriers for lateral precursor diffusion and flux spreading, thereby facilitating 
growth of thin nanosheets. In contrast, the sapphire substrate typically presents terrace 
structures, which act as diffusion barriers and prevent precursor migration. Meanwhile, 
for SiO2/Si, the amorphous and defect-rich surface suppresses mass transport efficiency. 
Moreover, the chemically reactive nature of the flux may induce partial surface etching 
on SiO2, shown as the yellow circled part in  Fig.R4a, which further hinders the fast 
mass transport. In Fig.R4c, we observed that the samples exhibit well-aligned domains 
in certain areas. However, misaligned domains are also present. This misalignment may 
be attributed to the rapid growth rate of the samples. In our in -situ observation 
experiments, the nucleation and growth process were completed within only seconds. 
Such a short growth time may lead to misorientation . Additionally, intrinsic defects 
within MnSb2Te4 may further promote local misorientation of the grown domains.

were added to the Supplementary Information as Supplementary Fig.11 and 12.

Response: Thank you for your thoughtful question. In our experiments, we observed 
that the minimum flake thickness of MnSb2Te4 increases with increasing growth 
temperature, as illustrated in Fig.R25. This trend reflects a shift in growth mode from 
lateral expansion to vertical accumulation, which can be attributed to both 
thermodynamic and kinetic factors that govern crystal growth in van der Waals layered 
systems. At lower temperatures, the growth process is primarily kinetically dominated. 
Adatoms have limited surface mobility and insufficient energy to overcome the 
nucleation barrier in the vertical direction. As a result, growth is constrained to lateral 
directions, producing atomically thin, well-dispersed 2D flakes, as shown in Fig.R25a 
and R25c  (e.g., at 680 °C)  [25, 29 ]. As the temperature increases, the growth 
environment becomes thermodynamically dominated. Enhanced surface diffusion 
allows adatoms to more efficiently reach energetically favorable binding sites, and the 
thermal energy becomes sufficient to overcome the vertical nucleation barrier. 
Consequently, vertical growth becomes preferred, leading to the formation of thicker 
flakes (at 720 °C)  [26, 27 , 29]. At even higher temperature  such as  780 °C, it may 
destabilize the desired phase and promote the formation of secondary phases, such as 
Sb2Te3 or MnTe.

Response: We sincerely appreciate the reviewer’s insightful comments. We have 
carefully analysed the evolution of coercive field Hc as a function of both temperature 
and sample thickness. As shown in Fig.R26a, Hc decreases monotonically with 
increasing temperature for all thicknesses and vanishes near the Curie temperature. This 
trend is consistent with other two -dimensional ferromagnets and can be attributed to 
thermal activation: as temperature rises, magnetic a nisotropy becomes insufficient to 
counteract thermal fluctuations, facilitating magnetization reversal and reducing Hc. 
Fig.R26b reveals a non -monotonic trend in the Hc as a function of thickness at 2 K, 
characterized by a peak at intermediate thicknesses ( ~6-8 nm). This behavior suggests 
the presence of two competing effects that jointly determine the magnetic hardness of 
MnSb2Te4. In conventional 2D ferromagnets such as Fe3GeTe2 and Cr2Ge2Te6[34, 36], 
a monotonic increase in Hc with decreasing thickness is typically observed in Fig.R26c, 
which is attributed to the enhancement of magnetic anisotropy and domain stabilization 
in thinner flakes due to reduced dimensionality. This trend originates from intrinsic 
magnetism and strong spin-orbit coupling effects that become increasingly dominant in 
the 2D limit  [15-17, 35, 36 ]. The reason is that MnSb2Te4 exhibits a fundamentally 
different ferromagnetic origin. Its ferromagnetism arises from Mn -Sb antisite defects 
that disrupt the native A -type antiferromagnetic interlayer coupling and induce 
ferromagnetic exchange. As the sample thickness decreases, the density of these antisite 
defects also decreases. This reduction leads to weaker overall FM exchange interactions 
and lower magnetic moment density in ultrathin flakes. At the same time, thinner 
MnSb2Te4 flakes suffer from increased surface and edge disorde r, enhanced exposure 
to ambient degradation, and finite-size effects , all of which destabilize magnetic

domains and suppress magnetic anisotropy  [12, 24, 37-39]. As for the  thicker flakes, 
although the antisite density is higher, magnetic anisotropy is lower due to the reduced 
influence of surfaces , moderate magnetic anisotropy  and confinement, resulting in 
moderate coercivity. The combination of these two opposing effects leads to a 
maximum coercive field at intermediate thickness, where the balance between 
sufficient antisite-induced ferromagnetism and enhanced dimensionality anisotropy is 
optimized. Besides, similar non -monotonic behavior has been observed in 
mechanically exfoliated MnSb2Te4 samples, where the coercive field increases wi th 
decreasing thickness down to an intermediate regime and subsequently drops for 
ultrathin flakes in Fig.R26c [24].

anisotropy in thin flakes and the suppression of FM ordering due to reduced antisite 
density and low stability . Besides, we have added the figure into the Supplementary

4. In the 1st paragraph of the 9th page, the reason why MnSb 2Te4 shows FM behavior 
instead of A-type AFM behavior is explained as the change in magnetic properties due 
to the substitution of Mn and Sb. Can you explain why Mn and Sb are better substituted 
than Sb and Te, which are more similar in size? And does  MnSb2Te4 powder 
synthesized by CVT show A-type AFM characteristics? 
Response: Thank you for your valuable comments.  To explain the origin of the 
ferromagnetic behavior  observed in our MnSb2Te4 samples, we conducted first -
principles calculations. As shown in Fig.R27, DFT  calculations reveal that Mn -Sb 
antisite substitution, where Mn 2+ replaces Sb 3+ in the lattice, exhibits a negative 
formation energy of -0.1492 eV , indicating it is thermodynamically favorable. In 
contrast, Sb -Te antisite substitution ( both top Te and down Te) shows a positive 
formation energy of 0.9080 and 1.3335 eV , respectively, making both processes highly 
unfavorable. The preference for Mn-Sb antisite formation can be rationalized by ionic 
size compatibility and charge balance considerations. As  Fig.R27c shows, the ionic 
radius of Mn2+ is approximately 0.83 Å, while that of Sb3+ is 0.76 Å, a relatively small 
mismatch, which supports facile cation -cation substitution with minimal lattice 
distortion. In contrast, the ionic radius of Te 2- is 2.21 Å, which is significantly larger 
than either Mn2+ or Sb3+, making cation-anion exchange unfavorable. Furthermore, Te2- 
typically occupies chalcogenide positions with distinct bonding environments and 
valence states, further disfavoring substitution with trivalent or divalent cations.  This 
theoretical insight is well supported by other reports  [20, 37 -39]. These antisites  
introduce excess Mn magnetic moments and enhance ferromagnetic exchange 
interactions, thus destabilizing the ideal A -type antiferromagnetic interlayer coupling 
and resulting in a net FM ground state.
