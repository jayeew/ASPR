REVIEWER COMMENTS 
First of all, we would like to thank the reviewers for their valuable comments and suggestions. Their 
critical mindsets have significantly improved the quality of the manuscript. Please find below our 
point-by-point answers. Changes in the manuscript are highlighted in yellow. 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
The manuscript by Held et al identifies a disulfide bond in the TRPM3 extracellular pore 
loop demonstrates that this feature can modulate TRPM3 function as a redox switch. 
Overall, the technical quality of the paper is high , and the conclusions are well 
substantiated, but the scope of the findings is narrow. The physiological context in 
which such redox regulation of TRPM3 plays a salient is not clear – we have a situation 
where an interesting mechanism of TRPM3 regulation is in search of a physiological 
function. Additionally, there are some key unanswered questions about the role of this 
redox switch on TRPM3 selectivity.  
 
I have two concrete suggestions to improve the manuscript: 
1. The key question is whether such extracellular redox regulation is ever at play in 
physiological conditions. We need to be sure that this mechanism is not an oddity 
without any physiological significance. Ideally, this question is best answered by 
generating and phenotyping gene -edited mice wherein the key cysteine residues are 
mutated, but that would be an unreasonable demand for this study and I do favor timely 
publication of these findings. However, some intermediate cellular system is necessary 
to move forward with publication. Authors have shown nicely that PS -mediated 
activation of TRPM3 is highly sensitive to this redox switch. This needs to be studied 
further in the control of insulin release in pancreatic beta cells. This can be explored in 
a cell line such as INS -1 cells which are amenable to genetic manipulation. One clear 
prediction (based on the results of this manuscript) is that insulin secretion by INS -1 
cells is sensitive to the TRPM3 redox switch.  
We appreciate the reviewer's suggestion to further elaborate on the physiological impact of 
extracellular redox regulation of TR PM3. Consequently, we conducted a series of additional 
experiments to explore the ROS regulation of endogenous TRPM3 in pancreatic cells, 
specifically MIN-6 and INS-1 cells. 
Initially, we demonstrated that MIN-6 and INS-1 cells functionally express the TRPM3 channel. 
Microfluorimetric calcium imaging revealed that 90% of the cells exhibited a robust increase in 
fluorescence (mean amplitude representing intracellular calcium concentration) following 
stimulation by PS. Notably, after DTT treatment, both the p ercentage of PS-responsive cells 
and the amplitude were significantly reduced (SupplFig . 7 of the revised manuscript). These 
findings indicate that MIN -6 cells endogenously express TRPM3, which is sensitive to the 
redox status of the environment. 
Subsequently, we developed a novel technique to record calcium signals in 3D pancreatic β-
cell spheroids. These spheroids were derived from MIN -6 cells and cultured in 3D in glass-
bottom 96-well plates containing 19 micro-pillar structures per well. Using these pancreatic β-
cell spheroids, we demonstrated that glucose stimulation (8 mM) induced calcium oscillatory 
behavior, which was significantly potentiated by co -application of PS (50 µM) (Fig. 4A,  B). 
Interestingly, in the presence of DTT, the PS -induced oscillat ory behavior was significantly 
reduced to normal glucose-associated frequencies (Fig. 4B, C).

Previous results have shown a strong association between the area under the curve and insulin 
release. However, insulin levels could not be detected under TCEP -treated conditions, as 
insulin consists of two cysteine bridges that are degraded by ROS. Overall, these experiments 
illustrate that TRPM3 modulation by ROS has effects on the regulation of calcium oscillations 
in pancreatic cells, which is an important regulatory signal for insulin release. 
The reviewer's suggestion to perform genetic manipulations in MIN6 cells would unfortunately 
not provide much extra evidence due to the limited selectivity of the available pharmacology. 
1. Clotrimazole causes TRPM3 -independent changes in MIN6 and INS -1 cells  1. 
Additional experiments in MIN6 and INS -1 were conducted usin g clotrimazole as a 
stimulus to evoke calcium influxes after DTT treatment. Unfortunately, these 
experiments were unsuccessful because clotrimazole affects other ion channels in 
pancreatic cells  (like voltage -dependent calcium channels, calcium -dependent 
potassium channels (IKCa and KCNN4) 2, leading to effects unrelated to TRPM3 in non-
treated and DTT-treated conditions. 
2. DTT treatment causes the degradation of released insulin. Additional experiments of 
our lab with pancreatic β-cell spheroids show a strong correlation between the 
frequency of calcium oscillations and insulin release. In spheroids treated with DTT or 
TCEP, insulin release was never observed. Most likely because ROS degrade the 
cysteine bridges of insulin, making the peptide unstable and/or undetectable by ELISA. 
Therefore, genetically modifying INS1 cells would not provide convincing experiments  to 
illustrate the physiological impact of TRPM3 ROS modulation.  
2. There is evidence that the TRPM3a2 variant can conduct Zn2+, but not the a1 variant 
(Wagner et al., Pflugers EJP 2010). This could be of special relevance to pancreatic beta 
cells. Authors need to investigate if the TRPM3 redox switch has any influence on its 
ionic selectivity – especially Zn2+ influx. 
We appreciate the reviewer's constructive and insightful comments. Following the reviewer's 
suggestion, we conducted additional experiments to investigate the potential effect of the 
TRPM3 redox switch on the channel's permeability  properties. Since it is not feasible to 
evaluate the Zn 2+ conductance of TRPM3 under oxidized and reduced conditions using an 
identical stimulus, we examined the Zn2+ conductance under basal conditions. 
These experiments demonstrated a significant basal Z n2+ influx following the application of 1 
mM Zn2+ to the extracellular bath solution in untreated TRPM3-HEK cells. This influx could be 
blocked by the TRPM3 inhibitor isosakuranetin. Notably, the basal Zn2+ influx was significantly 
lower in DTT-treated HEK-TRPM3 cells. Non-transfected HEK cells exhibited no intracellular 
Zn2+ influx under both untreated and DTT -treated conditions, indicating that the Zn 2+ 
conductance is TRPM3-dependent (SupplFig. 1D). 
In conclusion, these experiments suggest that the basal permeability of Zn 2+ via TRPM3 is 
strongly influenced by the redox status of the channel. These new findings are included in the 
revised version of the manuscript (SupplFig; 1 and SupplFig. 9).

Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
 
In this manuscript, the authors investigate the effect of redox reagents on TRPM3 cation 
channels. As the authors point out, these channels are strongly expressed in 
somatosensory neurons (and elsewhere, e.g. in pancreatic beta cells), environments 
that can be exposed to oxidative stress. Interestingly, the effect of reductive reagents 
(such as DTT) depend on the TRPM3 isoform studied. The isoforms that have a shorter 
amino acid sequence in the protein region constituting the ion conducting central pore 
lose the ability to be activated by pregnenolone sulfate (the only known physiological 
ligand for TRPM3 channels) when exposed to DTT. However, under these conditions, 
they gain the ability to be directly activated by the antifunga l compound clotrimazole, 
and therefore effectively change their pharmacological profile to resemble the 
properties of long-pore TRPM3 isoforms (such as TRPM3alpha1). The authors then go 
on to show that the DTT acts on an intramolecular disulfide bridge for med by two 
conserved cysteines in close proximity to the pore region. The authors conclude that 
the length of the pore region determines whether a disulfide bridge can be formed 
(which then can be opened by reducing agents such as DTT).  
 
The experiments re ported (using electrophysiology or Ca2+ imaging) are technically 
sound and their graphical representation is fine. Also, for the most part, appropriate 
controls have been performed, and I am therefore convinced that most of the 
conclusions concerning the mechanism of action of DTT (and H2O2) are valid. However, 
other aspects of the paper are more speculative and preliminary. Moreover, the 
physiological relevance of the findings is, contrary to the assertions of the authors, 
unproven and perhaps non-existent. I will elaborate on these points below. 
In conclusion, I believe that this paper in its present form is of mechanistic interest to 
people working on TRPM3 channels or closely related proteins (such as TRPM1 or 
TRPM7), but it can be safely ignored by othe rs whose main focus are other areas of 
research. In particular, I do not believe that the reported findings will have an impact in 
the field of pain research. 
We appreciate the reviewer's opinion, although we are convinced that the modulation of 
TRPM3 by reactive oxygen species (ROS) can have a significant physiological impact. In the 
revised version of the manuscript, we demonstrate that calcium oscillations in pancreatic 
spheroids, which are  associated with insulin release , can be strongly influenced by TRPM3 
activity and its redox status. 
For instance, we show that pancreatic spheroids, which endogenously express TRPM3, exhibit 
glucose-induced calcium oscillations  correlated with insulin release  
(https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.05.25.655695v1) 3. The frequency of these 
glucose-induced oscillations significantly i ncreases in the presence of the TRPM3 agonist 
pregnenolone sulfate (PS). In the revised manuscript, we included data showing that pre-
treatment with TCEP eliminates the effect of  PS on calcium oscillations (Fig. 4). These new 
results indicate that ROS modu lation of TRPM3 can have a relevant physiological impact on 
insulin release. 
Furthermore, a dditional experiments were performed in which it was demonstrated that 
oxidative stress, induced by CFA -mediated inflammation, modulates pain sensitivity through 
TRPM3 channel activity. In TRPA1 ⁻/⁻ x TRPV1 ⁻/⁻ double ko mice, spontaneous pain was 
observed during the initial days following CFA injection but subsided by days 4 –5. However,

oxidative stress remained elevated in the affected paw on day 4, indicating a pe rsistent ROS

Upon administration of the TRPM3 agonist pregnenolone sulphate (PS) into CFA -pretreated

As an important side note, we would like to point out the limitations of the currently available 
tools to illustrate the modulation of TRPM3 in physiological conditions: 
1. To reduce the redox status of the environment, cells/tissues/animals are pre -treated 
with DTT, TCEP , or H2O2, all of which are very non -specific tools targeting and 
modulating many different proteins. 
2. The only available stimulus to activate TRPM3 under reduced redox conditions is 
clotrimazole. This antifungal drug modulates many different ion channels such as 
TRPV1, TRPA1, TRPM8, TRPM2, several K + channels (e.g., KCNN4, IK/KCa3.1)  1, 2, 
key enzymes (e.g., CYP51), and most probably other unknown targets. 
In the following I will supply a list of the points that appear to me to be especially 
problematic: 
 
1. The authors claim t hat the length of pore -region is an important determinant for 
disulfide bridge formation. Considering their earlier findings on TRPM3alpha1 (Held et 
al., 2020, Br J Pharmacol) this is a reasonable hypothesis, but it is not proven in this 
manuscript. The au thors use structure predictions of Alphafold to lend credibility to 
their claim, but do not experimentally verify them. It may be sufficient here to draw the 
attention to the recent publication of Terwilliger et al. in Nature Methods, that states that 
AI-based structure prediction are a great tool for hypothesis generation, but that these 
hypotheses should be tested. However, I would like to add that in the recently published 
CryoEM structure of TRPM3alpha2 (Zhao et al., 2023, Neuron), the corresponding pore 
region is not resolved, hinting on the possibility that this region is highly flexible (a 
structure of TRPM3alpha1 is not yet available). Therefore, a static structure might not 
be a good representation of the protein region. 
Direct evidence for the formation of the disulfide bridge was provided by the recently published 
cryo-EM structure of TRPM3 by Yin et al. (2025) in Nature Structural & Molecular Biology 5. In 
contrast to the earlier structural study (Zhao et al., 2023,  Neuron) 6, where the protein was 
prepared in the presence of DTT, this newer study did not use reducing conditions to prepare 
TRPM3 protein. Importantly, like we proposed in our original manuscript, this study shows the 
formation of the proposed cysteine bridge  in the pore loop  of the channel  5. The authors 
describe th at the pore loop, situated bet ween the pore helix and S6 from each subunit, is 
elongated and can be divided into pore loop a (P La) and PLb. Interestingly, the authors also 
emphasize the unique sequence pattern of the pore loop, with two critical cysteine residues 
forming a disulfide bo nd at the base of PLb, which stabilizes the cap domain. This additional 
information from the cryo-EM structure of TRPM3 strongly supports our hypothesis regarding 
the crucial role of cysteine residues in forming disulfide interactions and regulating the 
pharmacological properties of TRPM3.

However, contrary to the findings presented by Yin et al. (2025), we propose that the length of 
PLa is key to maintaining the orientation of the two cysteine residues. To further support our 
hypothesis, we gathered additi onal evidence for the importance of the pore region length in 
disulfide bridge formation. Using mutagenesis, we inserted additional alanines into the short 
pore loop variant (TRPM3α2) to increase the length of PLb. Interestingly, the insertion of one 
additional alanine (TRPM3 α2+1) resulted in a mutant channel that exhibited robust calcium 
influxes after stimulation by both PS and Clt under non -treated conditions. Remarkably, DTT 
treatment of the TRPM3α2+1 variant significantly reduced the responses to PS, w hereas the 
Clt-induced calcium influx was significantly increased (SupplFig.13). 
Furthermore, introducing two extra alanine residues (TRPM3 α2+2) resulted in a channel 
variant that was no longer sensitive to PS stimulation but only responded to Clt  stimulation, 
similar to what is observed in DTT/TCEP treated wild type TRPM3. Importantly, pre-incubation 
with DTT had no effect on the response pattern of the TRPM3 α2+2 variant (no response to 
PS, robust Clt response). The properties of this TRPM3 α2+2 variant are identical to the long 
pore variant TRPM3 α1 7. Altogether, these new data indicate that the insertion of one extra 
residue in PLb allows the disulfide bridge to remain intact and be disrupted by DTT incubation. 
In contrast, the TRPM3 α2+2 variant was no longer responsive to PS stimulation and was 
unaffected by DTT pretreatment. These findings further underscore the importance of the PLb 
length as a determinant for disulfide bridge formation.

corresponding section of “Materials + Methods”, they state: “The crystal structure data 
for TRPM3 was sourced from the RCSB Protein Data Bank ”. This cannot be true, as 
there is no crystal structure data for full-length TRPM3 (the above mentioned model was 
determined using CryoEM). Which pdb -number was used (there are several structures 
reported)? Did the authors work based on 8DDQ (as stated in a figure legend)? How was 
SWISS-Model used? Please give a full list of parameters and procedures. The resulting 
structures must be made available to the readers (and the reviewers).

2. The importance of the double cysteines in TRPM channels (located close to the pore) 
is not a new concept. It is long known that these cysteines are highly conserved (as 
acknowledged out by the authors) and functional studies have previously established 
their importance agonist sensitivity and rectification of I/V curves in other TRPM 
channels. As early as 2006 it was shown that the two cysteines in the pore region of 
TRPM8 proteins are important for menthol and icilin sensitivity of these channels 
(Dragoni et al., JBC, 2006 – this seminal publication was ignored by the authors). More 
recently, it was shown that the biophysical properties of TRPM7 channels depend on 
these cysteines (Duan et al., 2019). While the authors cite this work, they fail to mention 
that mutating these cysteines in TRPM7 alters the rectification of the I/V curve, very 
similar to what happens to TRPM3alpha2 channels in reducing conditions. The authors 
might want to more carefully discuss the previous literature. Rather then stressing that 
the “uniqueness” of TRPM3 channels, I feel that it might be a better idea to work out 
and emphasize the similarities of the effect that cysteine bridge formation has on the 
members of the whole TRPM family.

channels by ROS. The revised manuscript states:” In addition, the redox-modulation of TRPM3 
appears to be solely dependent on two cysteines located in the extracellular pore -loop, which 
is not aligning with the higher number of redox -sensitive cysteine residues that were reported

3. The authors claim that TRPM3 channels work in two “modes” and that switching 
between them might be an important way to modulate TRPM3 -dependent pain 
responses. I take issue with this claim. Not only is it not supported by the evidence 
presented, but the pr esented data actually make it unlikely that redox reactions under 
physiological or pathophysiological conditions affect TRPM3 channel function by the 
mechanism described in this manuscript. 
The authors point out, correctly, that under a variety of pathophy siological conditions, 
the level of “oxidative stress ” raises. However, TRPM3 channels overexpressed in 
HEK293 cells, or natively expressed in DRG neurons or pancreatic beta cells are already 
in the fully oxidized state, and this is shown by the authors’ own data: Clotrimazole does 
not induce a Ca2+ entry (or a current) in “non-treated” cells, but it does after treatment 
with DTT. The authors should point out more clearly that this means that without DTT 
(which actually is a very harsh reducing agent), TRPM3 channels are actually oxidized. 
Consequently, even under conditions of increased oxidative stress, the redox state of 
TRPM3 channels will not change.  
 
We appreciate the reviewer's critical remark. It is important to note that all experiments were 
conducted in in vitro settings, in an environment directly exposed to oxygen. This situation 
differs significantly from the in vivo context, where the channel is embedded in a less oxidized 
environment. Physiological redox potentials at pH 7 range from -400 mV to +400 mV. However, 
the exact redox potential at nerve terminals or pancreatic beta cells under normal or 
pathological conditions (e.g., inflammation) remains unknown.  
 
For the reduced state of TRPM3alpha2 to be pathophysiologically relevant, the authors 
must show that realistically, there are conditions under which TRPM3 channels exist in 
the reduced state. Furthermore, I would like to hear the authors ideas, how the redox 
transformation of TRPM3 channels is supposed to impact nociception. After all, the pro-
inflammatory and pro -nociceptive nature of TRPM3alpha2 channels rests, for all what 
is known, on their functional integrity.

Based on the reviewer's comments, we have conducted additional experiments to characterize 
the current after DTT treatment.  First, we evaluated the zinc (Zn 2+) permeability of TRPM3 
under non-treated and DTT -treated conditions. Since it was not feasible to assess the Zn 2+ 
conductance of TRPM3 in oxidized and reduced conditions using the same stimulus, we 
investigated the Zn2+ conductance under basal conditions in both non-treated and DTT-treated 
cells. These experiments demonstrated a robust basal Zn 2+ influx following the application of 
1 mM Zn 2+ to the extracellular solution in non -treated TRPM3 -HEK cells, which could be 
blocked by the TRPM3 inhibitor isosakuranetin. Interestingly, the basal Zn2+ influx was 
significantly lower in DTT-treated HEK-TRPM3 cells. Non-transfected HEK cells exhibited no 
intracellular Zn2+ influx under both non-treated and DTT-treated conditions, indicating that the 
Zn2+ conductance is TRPM3 -dependent (SupplFig. 9). These results may be explained by 
conformational changes of the pore loop induced by the degradation of the cysteine bridge , 
which also affects the selectivity filter of the channel. The selectivity filter of TRPM3 is located 
at the extracellular end of the pore, specifically within the pore domains formed by the S5 and 
S6 segments 15. This region is crucial for determining the ion selectivity of the channel, allowing 
it to selectively permeate certain ions while excluding others 15. Conformational changes in the 
selectivity filter of the channel could explain the difference in Zn2+ permeability in basal 
conditions.  
These new experiments clearly show that DTT treatment also affects the ion permeability of 
TRPM3 (SupplFig. 1 and SupplFig. 9). 
Does DTT affect the currents through the non-classical (omega) pore? 
The effect of DTT treatment on the alternative pore was further investigated using the D988R 
variant, which was previously described as a channel lacking the 'omega -current'. In the  
D988R mutant, the alternative pore is closed by the insertion of an additional positive charge 
in S4 (Held et al.,  J Physiol  2018) 7. Interestingly, we observed that pretreatment with DTT 
rendered this mutation insensitive to PS stimulation , and stimulation with Clt induced a large 
linear current , resulting in a channel phenotype similar to DTT/T CEP treated wild type 
TRPM3α2 and non-treated TRPM3α1, while the alternative pore remained closed. Altogether, 
these new data suggest that DTT treatment primarily affects the central pore of TRPM3 rather 
than the alternative pore (SupplFig. 9). 
Does DTT tr eatment affect the temperature sensitivity of the channels? Since the 
authors previously made very strong claims as to the nature of TRPM3 channels as 
temperature sensors, this question is surely of interest? 
To determine the effect of redox treatment on the temperature-dependent Ca 2+ response of 
TRPM3, non-treated and DTT-treated HEK-TRPM3 cells were tested in a 96-well plate assay 
in the presence and absence of PS. Consistent with previous findings, we observed that PS at 
10 µM shifted the thermal respon se profile of HEK -TRPM3 to lower temperatures in non -
treated cells. Interestingly, the Ca 2+ responses to increasing temperature were completely 
absent in DTT-treated HEK-TRPM3 cells, even in the presence of PS, indicating that the heat 
sensitivity of TRPM3 strongly depends on the redox status (Fig. 5A revised manuscript). 
Additionally, we further explored the TRPM3 sensitivity to the synthetic TRPM3 agonist 
CIM0216 under different redox conditions. Notably, DTT -treated HEK-TRPM3 cells exhibited 
a significan t shift in EC50 values for CIM0216 (EC50 ~ 22.4 µM) towards higher doses
