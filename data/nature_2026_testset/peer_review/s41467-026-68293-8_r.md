Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors of this paper have reported on the first observation of the quantum boomerang of light. 
Although this is an important and beautiful experiment that could open new perspectives for the study of this phenomenon in
more complex situations and for possible applications in the field of photonics (perhaps), I do not think that this article meets
the standards to be published in Nature Communications. 
The reasons for my statement are as follows: 
(1) As mentionned in the paper, this work is not the first observation of the quantum boomerang effect (it is the first
observation of the quantum boomerang effect of light). 
Indeed the quantum boomerang effect was observed for the first time three years ago (PRX 12, 011035 (2022), Ref. [77]) in
the context of ultracold gases in momentum space. 
(2) From a conceptual point of view, this work does not bring any new contribution. More in details: 
(2a) There is a long discussion about the fact that the boomerang effect is maximum when k0d=pi/2, but this is an evidence,
the group velocity being maximum at this point. Indeed it was the situation analyzed in Ref. [77] and also the one used in the
study of the coherent forward scattering effect, that happens in the conjugate space with respect the quantum boomerang
effect [arXiv:2503.01777]. 
(2b) The section of the supplemental material devoted to the theoretical explanation of the quantum boomerang effect is
identical to that already given in Ref. [77]. 
(2c) There is no discussion around the turning point and the mean-free path, neither on the time (z) needed to come back
with respect the theoretical model elaborated by Prat et al. (Ref[70]). 
The only discussion concerns the role of the losses, but there is not the analysis of a physical model that explains the role of
the losses in the quantum boomerang effect. All the analysis are numerical. 
In summary, this work concerns a beautiful experiment but (i) it is not the first evidence of the quantum boomerang effect in
the absolute, (ii) there are no new physical insights with respects the previous works on the subject, and (iii) a deep analysis
of the experimental data with respect the quantum boomerang effect theory is missing (there is the comparison with the
numerical simulations, but not with the analytical theoretical curves). 
For all these reasons even if, of course, I'm fully convinced that the results of this experiment deserve to be published, I think
that this draft does not meet the standards of Nature Communications.

Authors Response:
We fully agree with the reviewer that, in a clean tight-binding lattice, k0d = π/2 corresponds
to the maximum group velocity, and that this condition was indeed employed in Ref. [PRA 99,
023629 (2019), PRX 12, 011035 (2022)] and in the coherent forward scattering (CFS) studies
[arXiv:2503.01777].
We respectfully note that our work was not meant to re-establish this elementary fact [boomerang
effect is maximum when k0d = π/2], but rather to demonstrate — both theoretically and experi-
mentally, that this condition remains the optimum for observing the quantum boomerang effect
in a real-space disordered photonic lattice, where finite disorder strength, loss, and system size
could in principle shift or suppress this optimum value.
From the theoretical side, rigorous analysis of the 1D Anderson model shows that the peak
COM displacement scales with the scattering mean free path l (k0), which is maximized at the
band center (k0d = π/2) due to the combined effect of maximal vg and longest localization length.
Our numerical momentum scans [see Fig. 3a in the main text] confirm this prediction: the COM
excursion follows a sin( k0d)-like dependence, dropping to zero at k0 = 0 , π. Experimentally,
we directly mapped the boomerang signal at k0d = π/2 and found a clear maximum of the drift
COM ∼ 1.8d, which agrees well with theoretical prediction. This constitutes the first direct real-
space observation of the momentum dependence of quantum boomerang effect in any platform,
complementing the momentum-space cold-atom realization of Ref. [77].
In summary, while the group-velocity argument explains why k0d = π/2 is expected to be
optimal in the ideal case, our contribution is to verify and quantify this optimum under realistic
experimental conditions in a different physical setting.

3) Why is the low IPR (Fig.1c) a signature of localization? Furthermore, isn ’t a (slow) spreading

Authors Response:
We thank the reviewer for raising this important point. The Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR)
is widely used to distinguish between extended and localized states. In our case, the IPR starts at
unity (single-site excitation), decreases during the initial transient as the wave explores neighboring
sites, and then stabilizes at a finite, nonzero plateau (IPR ∼0.12). This saturation reflects that the
wavefunction has relaxed into a superposition of exponentially localized eigenmodes. In contrast,
in a diffusive or ballistic system, the IPR would continue to decay toward zero ( ∼ 1/L), reflecting
unbounded spreading.
Regarding the possibility of ’slow spreading’: indeed, at early propagation, the packet tran-
siently broadens, but Anderson localization implies that this broadening halts at long distances.
The exponentially localized eigenstates always enforce a finite participation ratio, and hence no sus-
tained diffusion (even sub-diffusion) is present in the long-time limit. The observed finite plateau
of the IPR is thus a direct hallmark of localization, rather than continued spreading.
To make this explicit and avoid any possible ambiguity, we have moved the IPR results to the
Supplementary Materials VIII-D.
