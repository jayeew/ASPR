Reviewers' comments:
 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
I read the manuscript by Sterling with interest. The authors report on neutron scattering and complementary DFT
investigations of small magnetoresistance compounds La1-xSrxMnO3 (x = 0.2, 0.3). In their results it is found that Jahn-
Teller-active optical oxygen vibrations collapse entirely above the materials Curie temperature and attribute this highly
anomalous behavior to exceptionally strong electron-phonon coupling. The magnitude of the magnetoresistance, they
conclude, correlates with the rate of diffusive motion of carrier-trapping oxygen sublattice distortions rather than with the
strength of Jahn-Teller electron-phonon coupling itself. The manuscript is well organized, and the figures are appropriate.
The results are important, topical, and of interest to the colossal magnetoresistance perovskites community, and hence I
recommend publication in Communications Materials as is. 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
Sterling et al. present a thorough inelastic neutron scattering study of the lattice and magnetic dynamics of perovskite
manganites (La,Sr)MnO3, known for their colossal magnetoresistance (CMR). The key experimental finding is that above the
ferromagnetic ordering temperature, a characteristic phonon mode “freezes out” into short-ranged quasistatic lattice
distortions, as known from other manganites with much stronger CMR. This suggests that the strength of electron-phonon
coupling (EPC) and the resulting mode-softening is not proportional to the magnitude of the CMR in these materials. 
The data are of high quality, and the authors provide a thorough and very careful analysis. The findings and analysis are
discussed in commendable detail in the manuscript and in the Supplementary Information file. The immediate conclusions
(i.e. the identification and the softening of a specific phonon mode and the absence of significant phonon-magnon coupling)
are fully convincing. 
Crucially, I found the paper poorly structured and indeed felt it was difficult to follow its reasoning on a first read. To give one
example, the Results section begins not by describing basic experimental findings (spread over Figs. 1 and 3), but with the
density functional model, which is, however, illustrated in between (Fig. 2). In several instances, important arguments and
information are contained only in figure captions, and these insights are referred to from the main text without in-depth
discussion. While individual panels provide useful illustrations, the organization of the manuscript frequently requires
comical cross-references (e.g. to compare a specific panel in Fig. 1 with Fig. 8 and Fig. 3). 
The results clearly merit publication in some form, although I would recommend a major reorganization and shortening; see
my (non-exhaustive) comments below. I felt that the paper sequentially presents a large amount of experimental evidence,
rather than using it selectively to support a linear story accessible to a broad audience. The main claim of impact hinges on
what can be considered “weak or strong” EPC and CMR. However, this comparison discussion remains qualitative. In
essence, the study highlights a significant shortcoming in common perceptions of the CMR mechanism in manganites. This
will be a caveat for researchers in the field and may motivate theoretical work. On the other hand, I am not convinced that
this insight alone moves the field forward to an extent that warrants a wide audience. 
*** Comments / questions 
• The mechanisms of colossal magnetoresistance are the main interest of this study - but CMR is not mentioned in the
abstract! 
• Organization: Both “2.2” and “3.1” are labelled “Density functional theory calculations”. I understand that both discuss DFT,
but it would be more helpful to label the “results” subsections by results, not by techniques. 
• Organization: Figures should appear in the order they are referenced in the manuscript. As far as reasonably possible, they
should be self-contained, each with the aim to support one of the observations discussed in the main text. 
• Organization: Captions should only contain the information necessary to understand the figure, not a discussion. 
• Introduction (l. 76 – 86): This paragraph should explain why these two samples were characterized. Are they equivalent, or
are they being contrasted? What is “weak” and “strong” (better to give quantitative information)? Consider, for example, l.
667-669: “ (…) much stronger CMR in La0.8Sr0.2MnO3 [than in La0.7Sr0.3MnO3]” vs. l. 701/702 : “Since La0.7Sr0.3MnO3
and La0.8Sr0.2MnO3 both have weak CMR” … this is of course a central point of the paper, and I think in the present form of
the manuscript it is more confusing than necessary. 
• L.91: “diffusive motion”: If intended for a wider audience, it would be helpful to introduce this concept as quasi-static (short-
ranged) lattice distortions.

Reviewer #3 (Remarks to the Author): 
The authors present neutron diffraction measurements and density functional theory calculations of La$_{1-
x}$Sr$_x$MnO$_3$ for x=0.2 and 0.3. The study is detailed overall and we believe worth publishing, but there are a few
points below that we believe need to be addressed. 
Major issues 
1. Many of the conclusions of the DFT calculations are based on removing some charge from the computational cell. The
authors state that this leads to a ``completely polarized Mn t2g core and eg conduction electrons." First, this description
should be improved. The authors must mean that they specify the total population of electronic states and add a uniform
compensating background charge to maintain charge neutrality of the cell - this description should be clarified in the text. In
addition, the effect on the electronic structure should be explicitly shown somewhere through either the projected electronic
density of states or the band structure. 
2. Related to comment 1, the removal of charge from the cell is a way to model the system of interest, La$_{1-
x}$Sr$_x$MnO$_3$, without actually adding Sr to the computational cell. This is understandable, but it is still an
approximation to the true system. It is not clear to what degree the results and findings here would actually translate to the
real physical system. For example, the authors claim that ``DFT rules out the orthorhombic-rhombohedral phase transition
and twinning, both known to occur in these compounds, as the origin of the phonon collapse pointing instead at giant EPC of
JT type." However there is not much discussion of the limitations of the DFT modeling approach when no Sr is present. For
example, how might the neglect of accounting for different ionic radii of Sr and La effect the results? Such issues should be
discussed in some detail in the text. 
Additional issues 
1. Line 114. Fig 1. caption ``Inelastic neutron scattering (c-d) and DFT calculations (e-f) identify the X-point (in the cubic
basis) half-breathing mode at $\approx$ 45 meV at Q = (4.5, 0, 0) and the quadrupolar mode at Q =(4.5, 0,-1.5) at 10 K.'
could include ``M-point' before ``quadrupolar mode' for clarity. 
2. Line 121. Fig 1. Is the ``quasielastic magnetic scattering near TC' the sub-20 meV signal in Plot b? Otherwise, Plot b is not
discussed. 
3. Line 189. ``This has no effect on the relevant bond-stretching phonons.' Is it more accurate to say ``minimal effect', rather
than ``no effect'? What supports this claim?

4. Line 247. Fig 2. Why are the X and M points a single point and not the entire vertical slice of Plot b? The specific point of
the dispersion curve is relating to the modes illustrated in Plot e, but the caption of ``The primed labels are q-points in the
orthorhombic basis, while the unprimed X and M points labeled by arrows in (b) correspond to the cubic basis.' should
specify the arrows point at a specific mode at the X and M points, and not that the arrows points to the X and M point. 
5. Line 256. ``The eigenvectors in the rhombohedral phase have been before [30].' is an incomplete sentence.

9. L. 134/135: “high-CMR compounds (. . . ), whereas low-CMR compounds (. . . )” this ap-

In the context of neutron scattering, diffusion rate (or drift speed, etc.) is characterized by
the width of the quasielastic peak ( E = 0scattering that arises from diffusive as opposed
to vibrational motion). In short, quasielastic scattering results from neutrons “bouncing
off” diffusive particles. The change in the neutron’s speed is directly related to its change
in energy. Since the quasiparticles have vanishing center of mass velocity (no net current),
their average speed is zero and the scattered intensity is centered on E = 0. The width is
related to the RMS speed: a broader peak means higher diffusion rate and vice versa.
The claim that CMR scales with diffusion rate is a hypothesis that is the subject of future
work; we do not provide proof in this manuscript. Previous studies of the quasielastic
scattering by some of us do indeed support this. Unfortunately we do not have sufficient
data to make definitive statements yet (more measurements are needed). We have modified
the text to de-emphasize this claim and have reframed it as a hypothesis.

Indeed our argument indicates that EPC is necessary but not sufficient for CMR. While
we don’t believe the reviewer’s first statement is wrong, we also don’t believe our result
is trivial. Our interpretation of the conventional understanding can be summarized as
“manganites with strong EPC can have large CMR” . “can have” ranges from vanishingly
small to infinitely large CMR. Our primary result is to establish a counter-example proving
that strong EPC is not sufficient for CMR. In other words, we show that “ can have” does
indeed include the small CMR end of the spectrum. The Sr doped compounds have CMR
magnitudes that are orders of magnitude smaller than e.g. Ca doped compounds, while
still hosting strong EPC as evidenced by total collapse of a mode as compared to simple
broadening/energy shifts. Such non-perturbative renormalization has been found in high-
CMR Ca doped compounds where it is expected; we are surprised to find it in the Sr doped
compounds. We believe this is of great practical importance for all of the usual reasons:
e.g. a theorist that desires to accurately model CMR needs the minimal set of necessary
ingredients. Indeed EPC is necessary, as is widely known. What we show is that EPC alone
is not the minimal set. We believe that this is information that everyone in the field, from
theorists to device engineers, ought to know.

Charge doping shifts the Fermi level down without changing the symmetry of the bands
as shown in Fig. 1. The projected spin-up and spin-down band structures in the cubic
unitcell show that only the spin-up Mn- d bands cross the Fermi level and the Mn- d elec-
trons are completely polarized (the total magnetic moment per unitcell is 3.8 µB); the
spin-down sector is gapped and La 0.8Sr0.2MnO3 is correctly described as a half-metal in this
approximation. The electronic structures between the cubic, orthorhombic, and rhombohe-
dral unitcells are essentially the same: they are half-metals with completely polarized Md- d
shells (the magnetic moment is 3.8 µB per formula unit for all unitcells). The primary effect
of the structural distortions is to reduce the symmetry of the crystal, raising degeneracies
of the bands. This can be seen in the DoS in Fig. 1 (e-g), where the smooth region around
the Fermi level in the high symmetry cubic cell corresponds to degenerate bands without
avoided crossings, while the distorted rhombohedral and octahedral have split bands which
appear as a spiky, comb-like structure in the DoS.
The effect of explicit doping (i.e substituting Sr for La) over charge doping in the simu-
lation, as described above, ought to be similar to the effect of structural distortions: the
qualitative shape of the band structure would remain the same, while the defects lift the de-
generacies and further split the bands. In reality, the impurity affects the local bond-angles,
which affects the competition between double-exchange and hopping, impacting the Curie
temperature, magnitude of CMR, etc. On the one hand, explicit substitution is computa-
tionally intractable. On the other, we do not expect single-particle DFT to describe these
interactions accurately in the first place, so it is unlikely that explicit doping would be a
significant improvement in any case. We assume that charge doping, as used in this work,
is competitively accurate at the level of single-particle DFT.
A similar argument can be made for the effect of explicit doping on phonons. The
change in bond-angles will change the local force constants, changing the dispersions
and so on. Moreover, the defect lifts degeneracies creating avoided crossings. Usually
the change in bond angle is relatively small and either does not induce a long ranged
structural transition or induces one between similar structures (cf. the rhombohedral vs.
orthorhombic comparison). Besides some “inhomogeneity broadening” of spectral lines
(https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.107.L020301 ), the quantitative affect on the
dispersions is likely quite small. This can further be argued based on the differences between
phonons in LaMnO 3 compounds doped with Sr, Ca, Ba, etc. For the relevant dopings, they
all maintain a perovskite-like structure with breathing phonons at similar energies.
