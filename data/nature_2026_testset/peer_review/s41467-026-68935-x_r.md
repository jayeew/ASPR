Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
In this manuscript, Chen and coworkers demonstrate the synthesis of a single-crystal WS2/MoS2 vertical heterostructure by
an S vacancy-mediated chemical vapor deposition method. The single crystallinity of the samples has been well
established. Moreover, the ferroelectric phenomena associated with the bilayer vertical heterostructure have been
experimentally observed. Given the significant importance of the production of single-crystal vertical heterostructures in the
field of two-dimensional materials, this work is expected to provide substantial impetus toward advancing this research
direction. Consequently, I suggest the work be published in Nature Communications after the authors have revised the
manuscript according to the following minor comments. 
1. In Fig. 2d, the authors showed that “…on MoS2 with S vacancies at step edges, the Gibbs free energy of WS2 islands
decreases by 0.35 eV and 0.17 eV compared to the terrace and vacancy-free step edges, respectively. This significant
energy advantage ensures the preferential nucleation of WS2 at step edges…”. The authors should provide experimental
evidence to demonstrate the preferential nucleation of WS2 at step edges. 
2. The authors claimed that the formation of S vacancies plays a key role in achieving the consistent orientation of the upper
WS2 layer and provides corresponding experimental evidence. Given this, whether the density of S vacancies influence the
growth behavior? A more in-depth analysis of this factor would strengthen the proposed mechanism of S vacancy-driven
unidirectional growth. 
3. Could Mo vacancies play a similar role in the unidirectional alignment of WS2? 
4. In supplementary Fig. 1a, two circles are displayed in distinct colors. The authors are required to provide corresponding
explanations, either in the figure caption or within the main text. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
This manuscript reports the controlled growth of large-scale, single-crystal, rhombohedral (R-stacked) WS2/MoS2
heterostructures via CVD. The authors propose a mechanism in which S vacancies at step edges serve as preferential
nucleation sites, which is suggested to break the symmetry between rhombohedral and hexagonal stacking. Ferroelectric
behavior in these heterostructures is also reported. However, several key issues remain unresolved and require further
clarification: 
1
、
The origin and concentration control of S vacancies during growth are not quantitatively addressed. How do process
parameters (e.g., temperature, sulfur partial pressure) influence vacancy formation and subsequent nucleation? 
2
、
The entire growth mechanism hinges on the formation of S vacancies at the step edges of the underlying MoS2 and their
role as nucleation sites. However, the direct characterization of these specific S vacancies is currently lacking. 
3
、
While symmetry breaking is attributed to S vacancies, the atomic-scale mechanism enabling predominant rhombohedral
stacking remains unclear. Are there competing factors (e.g., interfacial strain, edge energetics) that also influence stacking
order? 
4
、
The manuscript emphasizes the successful synthesis of "large-scale, single-crystal" films but omits critical data to
substantiate key claims regarding thickness and uniformity. To fully support these assertions, it is essential to provide
statistical thickness analysis—for example, by employing techniques such as atomic force microscopy across multiple,

widely separated locations to quantify layer thickness and confirm its consistency with the purported number of layers.
Furthermore, to verify the large-scale and uniform nature of the heterostructure, complementary spatial characterization
should be included, such as Raman and PL mapping over hundreds of micrometers. These maps are necessary to
demonstrate spatial consistency in layer thickness, stacking order, and crystal quality across the entire grown area 
5
、
The demonstration of ferroelectric properties relies solely on PFM. While PFM is a powerful tool, it is not sufficient on its
own to conclusively prove ferroelectricity, as it can be influenced by non-ferroelectric effects. To unequivocally establish
switchable ferroelectric polarization, complementary electrical measurements on fabricated devices are imperative. 
6
、
The authors focus on the growth of the WS2/MoS2 heterostructure. Can this mechanism also be applied to other TMD
heterostructures?

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors experimentally demonstrated a novel synthesis approach utilizing CVD to directly achieve large area growth of
single crystal WS2/MoS2 heterostructures. Furthermore, the authors meticulously validated the film quality using various
advanced characterization techniques, including SHG measurement. However, to merit publication in Nature
Communications, this manuscript requires more comprehensive validation from multiple perspectives. 
1. The manuscript frequently refers to the growth as “large-area” or “continuous.” However, the presented characterization
appears limited to cm-scale regions. Could the authors clarify the maximum wafer size that has been experimentally
demonstrated, and provide evidence of scalability beyond laboratory-scale samples? 
2. The orientation selectivity is attributed to S vacancies at step edges, but only indirect evidence is presented. Could the
authors provide direct quantification of vacancy density and spatial distribution (e.g., via STEM-EELS, STM, or other
techniques)? 
3. The claim that S vacancies form selectively at step edges requires stronger validation. What experimental data directly
supports this selective formation, as opposed to vacancy generation throughout the MoS2 surface? 
4. The manuscript does not specify the critical vacancy concentration required for unidirectional stacking. Is there an optimal
density of S vacancies that must be achieved, and how reproducible is this across different growth runs? 
5. To enable readers to easily reproduce the synthesis experiments and improve reproducibility, it would be highly beneficial
to include detailed photographs of the CVD setup in the Supplementary Information. 
6. Is there attempted fabrication of FET (field-effect transistor) devices or other practical electronic applications using single
crystal WS2/MoS2 heterostructures? The current study seems to be focused primarily on material characterization without
extending to electronic device performance, which may limit its relevance to device-oriented applications. To enhance the
impact and applicability, it would be netter to include experimental validation through device fabrications such as FET
measurements and providing long-term reliability (robustness) testing data for the proposed material and devices.

functional theory (DFT) calculations, which confirm that the formation  energy of a molybdenum 
(Mo) vacancy (8.19 eV) is significantly higher than that of a S vacancy (3.65 eV) [Applied Physics

we will elaborate on below, while also addressing the roles of other potential factors like strain. 
The fundamental reason for the energetic prefere nce of the 0° orientation is that the presence 
of S vacancies at the MoS 2 step edge creates highly reactive binding sites (exposed Mo atoms). 
These sites can interact far more strongly with one specific edge of a 0° -oriented WS 2 cluster 
compared to any edge of a 60°-oriented cluster. 
Specifically, when a triangular WS 2 cluster approaches the step edge, its orientation dictates 
how its boundary atoms interact with the defective MoS₂ edge. In the 0° orientation, one of the three 
edges of the WS₂ cluster can perfectly align with the MoS2 step, allowing the S atoms at the cluster’s 
edge to form strong chemical bonds with the exposed Mo atoms at the vacancy sites. This creates a 
powerful, stable interface. In the 60° orientation, geometric constraints prevent suc h an effective 
alignment. The cluster's edges are mismatched with the reactive sites on the MoS2 step, leading to a 
much weaker interaction. 
Our first-principles calculations quantify this difference precisely. As shown in Fig. R1c, the 
binding interaction between the defective MoS₂ step and the 0°-oriented WS2 cluster is stronger by 
a remarkable 0.84 eV/nm2. This substantial energy difference serves as a powerful thermodynamic 
driving force, making the nucleation and growth of the 0° orientation overwhelmingly favorable and 
leading to its selective growth. 
To further isolate the role of vacancies from other potential factors, we also considered the 
scenarios proposed by the referee: 
Interfacial Strain: To test the influence of strain alone, we modeled a scenario where the MoS2 
step edge is strained but possesses no S-vacancy defects (as shown in Fig. 1d-e in the main text). In 
this case, the calculated interaction energy difference between the step and the 0° and 60° oriented 
clusters was negligible, only 0.3 eV/nm2. This minor energy difference is insufficient to overcome 
thermal fluctuations during growth and cannot induce the selective growth of a single orientation. 
This calculation explicitly demonstrates that interfacial strain, in the absence of vacancies, is not the 
driving mechanism for the observed alignment.  This is also be demonstrated by the experimental

Our reply: 
We thank the reviewer for this insightful question regarding the practical requirements for our 
proposed mechanism. The question of a “critical” or “optimal” vacancy concentration is indeed 
central to understanding the process window and reproducibility of unidirectional growth. 
Our theoretical analysis shows that the vacancy -driven orientation control does not rely on a 
specific critical or optimal vacancy concentration. The bellowing is the detailed explanation based

vacancies are present at the step edge to provide reactive Mo bonding sites, the strong energetic 
preference for th e 0° orientation is immediately established. There isn ’t a lower -bound “critical” 
concentration threshold where the effect suddenly vanishes; rather, the presence of even a moderate
