Reviewer comments: 
Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors present a well-executed study investigating how locus coeruleus noradrenaline (LC-NA) shapes granule cell
computations in the dentate gyrus. Using a combination of slice electrophysiology, pharmacology, interneuron-specific
approaches, modeling, and calcium imaging, they show that NA enhances feedforward inhibition via CCK+ interneurons,
markedly narrows the granule cell integration window, sparsifies network activity, and decorrelates output patterns. The work
is timely, addresses an important gap in understanding neuromodulation of dentate computations, and is generally of high
quality. 
Nevertheless, I believe the manuscript would benefit from additional clarifications, some textual corrections, and in a few
cases further analyses or discussion. My detailed comments are below. 
Major Comments: 
1- While the electrophysiological and modeling data are convincing, the study lacks direct behavioral confirmation. A simple
contextual memory task—such as contextual fear conditioning—could provide a functional link between the observed NA-
dependent mechanism and context specificity in memory encoding. Even if experiments are not currently feasible, the
authors should at least discuss this point explicitly, as it would substantially strengthen the translational relevance of their
conclusions. 
2- The ex vivo decorrelation results are compelling. A brief addition in the Discussion highlighting predictions for in vivo LC
activation (e.g., during exploration or arousal) would help position the findings within the broader context of hippocampal
computation. 
3- The manuscript primarily attributes NA’s effects to enhanced inhibition. It would be helpful to clarify whether Medial
perforant path (MPP)-evoked EPSCs onto granule cells are unchanged by NA. If data are available, please report them; if
not, acknowledge this as a limitation. 
Minor comments: 
1- Ensure negative signs are consistently included for resting membrane potentials (e.g., −79.6 mV). 
2- Replace “unitary line” with “unity line” in correlation analyses. 
3- Correct “stain 010802” to “strain 010802.” 
4- Replace “sparsen/sparsening” with “sparsify/sparsification.” 
5- In Figure 7, clarify that decorrelation is measured as deviation from the unity line. 
6- For calcium imaging figures, explicitly link bin widths (e.g., 5 ms vs 275 ms) to the panels they correspond to. 
7- In describing ACEA sensitivity, consider phrasing as “ACEA-sensitive IPSCs” rather than “isolated with ACEA” for clarity.

(Remarks to the Author) 
In their study, the authors investigate how granule cells of the dentate gyrus perform input signal sparsification and
decorelation. They claim that this process is underlied by noradrenaline release onto local CCK inhibitory neurons, that in

separation is not novel at all, experimental evidence contributing to its understanding is welcome and this study provides a
clear, easily reproducible set of experiments proposing a local circuit mechanism for such computational function. The
manuscript introduction is very clear, the scope and claims are well established and the experiments strongly support the
claims presented here. This is a strong and focused paper where the result presentation is logical and the message very
clear. There are some statistical issues throughout the text, which by looking at the figures seem to play against the
message the authors are trying to convey. These issues should be easy to fix and help communicate an otherwise well
compiled study.

Reviewer #4 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Glovaci and her colleagues investigated the noradrenergic modulation of granule cells in the dentate gyrus. By employing a
multitude of techniques, they demonstrated that noradrenaline increases sparsity of and decorrelates granule cell activity via
feed forward inhibition by CCK-positive interneurons. They claim that by this mechanism, noradrenergic modulation
facilitates pattern separation by the dentate circuit. The data support the Authors’ claims and provide a mechanistic
explanation of noradrenaline’s action on the gate of the hippocampal memory system. However, there are contradictions
between the results of the current manuscript and those reported previously. First of all, noradrenergic action is shown to
support decorrelation of input patters i.e. pattern separation by narrowing the integration time window of granule cells and as
such, filtering out asynchronous inputs. However, in a recent paper, Seo et al. (2021, Neuron) arrive exactly to the opposite
conclusion despite presenting a similar mechanism, namely noradrenergic activation of interneurons and the resulting
suppression of granule cell activity. According to their findings, stimulation of the noradrenergic input of the DG impairs
context discrimination by preventing the emergence of context-specific population patterns and this effect is caused by
increased excitability of hilar interneurons. The Authors of the current manuscript cite this paper but only as a preceding
behavioral study (“Behavioral studies have found that activation of the noradrenergic system modulates memory encoding in
the dentate gyrus.”) not mentioning the fundamentally contrasting results. Therefore, the Authors ought to explain the
possible reasons of the discrepancies between their and Seo et al.’s findings (e.g. isn’t it possible that differences between

We strongly agree with the reviewer that our in vitro electrophysiological and modeling 
data should be supported by future behavioral experiments. The reviewer proposed 
contextual fear conditioning, which is widely used to assess the performance of pattern 
separation in the hippocampus.  Our electrophysiological experiments and modeling 
data suggest that activation of the locus coeruleus during reinforcement learning can 
enhance pattern separation performance.  However, th e locus coeruleus  is also 
engaged in behaviors that are associated with a negative valence. Hyperactivation of 
the locus coeruleus noradrenergic system during  stressful conditions, for example, 
fear responses evoked by electrical shocks during contextual fear conditioning, may 
lead to a n excessive recruitment of synaptic inhibition in the  dentate gyrus that 
potentially can compromise pattern separation by reducing the capacity of the network 
to generate distinct activity patterns1, 2. 
Testing these predictions will require manipulating  noradrenergic modulation  
locally in the dentate gyrus in mice performing a range of different behavior tasks, from 
novel object recognition  to context fear conditioning . In addition, manipulation of 
noradrenergic activity comes with three technical challenges. First, chemogenetic 
manipulation of noradrenaline release or pharmacological manipulation of adrenergic 
receptor functions at the systems level can produce undesired effects by changing the 
global state of the brain because the locus coeruleus regulates the arousal state3. 
Furthermore, local infusion of these chemical tools into the dentate gyrus lacks spatial 
and temporal specificity. Second, optogenetic activation of noradrenergic axons in the 
hippocampus potentially can generate antidromic spikes that can propagate to other 
brain areas along the same axon4, 5, because of the widespread projection pattern of 
each noradrenergic axon6. Consequently, t his may change the input to the 
hippocampus. Third, current methods of optogenetic silencing of axons have their 
limitations in terms of the ir efficacy and specificity  in inhibiting action potential 
propagation in long-range axons7, 8. 
Because of these reasons, we chose to discuss the functional implication of our 
electrophysiological results  as proposed by the reviewer (last paragraph of the 
Discussion section, on pages 15–16 of the current submission).

First, p revious studies  have demonstrated heterogeneous effects of
