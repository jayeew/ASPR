REVIEWER COMMENTS: 
Reviewer #1 (Remarks to the Author): 
Review of "Surface Roughness and Aperture Control on Carbon Mineralization in Dead-End Olivine Fractures: Implications
of Iron Oxide Passivation" by Yang et al. 
The paper reports on the results of experiments that react olivine with a CO2 rich fluid under conditions relevant for
subsurface carbon storage, specifically examining the role played by Fe oxides in passivating the surface of the reacting
mineral. They find that the precipitation of maghemite, particularly in fractures with wide apertures, is prevalent and that this
is likely to significantly reduce reaction rates. The paper is well written and on the whole clear, and the topic is certainly
timely. However, I have a number of comments that need to be addressed before it can be considered for publication. 
1. Line 92: "smooth and rough surfaces". The actual values for roughness are only given in the supplementary data, as far as
I could see. 12-16 micrometers versus 6-8 micrometers hardly seems like a huge difference. Were these values arbitrary?
What factors (other than the polishing and subsequent roughening) affect this value? Also, roughness is a scale dependent
measurement. What can the authors say about this dependence in their samples? 
2. Line 96: Reaction a. One of the products is silica which is typically thought to precipitate as amorphous silica during
olivine dissolution. This possibility is not mentioned at all at any point in the manuscript. Is there any reason to think that
silica will not precipitate? This is an important issue because of the authors' focus on magnetite as a driver for passivation in
their experiments. Can they rule out silica precipitation as a factor? 
3. Line 109: The authors' mention of self-diffusivity is very confusing here. First, I think the authors should clarify what exactly
they mean by the mechanism. Second, the effects of roughness on self-diffusion are typically only important at the nanoscale
(in fact, that is the focus of the paper they cite by Malek and Coppens) and specifically in nanopores in which the pore size is
approximately the same as the roughness length scale. In these experiments, the size of the smallest aperture is
approximately 500 micrometers while roughness is approximately 10 micrometers. These length scales seem to be
inconsistent with the mechanism the authors cite and I am not convinced that this mechanism is relevant. 
4. Figure 2: No silica seems to be present in the Raman spectra but that doesn't mean that it hasn't precipitated on the
surface of the olivine. Is it possible that the broad band that is typical of amorphous silica was inadvertently filtered out when

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 3 -->

calculating the "normalized Raman intensity"? 
5. On a similar note, a stoichiometric analysis of the XRD values in Table S1 suggests that some silica is missing in the
samples after reaction. Could this have precipitated as silica? 
6. Line 164: "Even without quantitative analysis…". Why not perform and show the quantitative analysis? 
7. Line 169: What do the authors mean by "surface volume loss"? How is it even calculated?Calculating volume changes
from profilometer requires the use of a reference. 
8. Figure 4: What are the spatial scales here in the figure? There is no scale bar. 
9. Line 201: Other models, such as shrinking core models which contain a porous secondary mineral coating, have been
proposed to simulate the effects of passivation coupled dissolution-precipitation reactions. Why do the authors assume that
reduced surface area is the only mechanism responsible? 
Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Author): 
This study attempted to verify the effects of differences in fracture aperture width and roughness on CO2 mineralization in
peridotite, and is considered to be of considerable value not only as experimental data but also as scientific knowledge.
However, primarily from the perspective of CO2 storage, there appears to be a discrepancy between the results of this study
and the conclusions drawn, and some additional consideration or explanation is necessary. Therefore, I believe that the
paper can be published in a journal with minor revisions to the following items. 
First, the authors speculate that the reaction mechanism differs depending on the fracture aperture width and surface
roughness, but the validity of such a mechanism is unclear. While it is understandable that this is a qualitative inference, an
explanation is needed as to whether the fracture aperture width and degree of surface roughness, which affect the diffusion
rate, are at least consistent with the experimental design of this study. Regarding the mechanism, the authors mention that
the reason why maghemite does not precipitate on smooth surfaces at smaller fracture aperture is that equilibrium with the
bulk fluid is promoted compared to rough surfaces, reducing the pH gradient and increasing the availability of carbonate.
Nevertheless, the fact that maghemite precipitation is not inhibited on rough surfaces as well as smooth surfaces at larger
fracture aperture requires more detailed discussion. 
Although this study focused on the precipitation of carbonate minerals in fracutures, the amount of carbonate minerals
precipitated was not examined. In this regard, smooth surfaces in smaller fracture aperture suppressed iron oxide
precipitation, but at least from the Raman spectroscopy observations, this did not appear to affect carbonate mineral
precipitation. The authors should investigate the effects of fracture aperture width and surface roughness on the final amount
of mineral carbonation. 
Related to the above, it is unclear what the results of this study mean for CO2 storage in mafic rocks. For example, this study
focuses on small fracture aperture, and as the title suggests, dead-end fractures. The volume of such fractures is very small
compared to the overall rock mass, and it is thought that they contribute very little to the amount of CO2 sequestration as
carbonate minerals. Furthermore, the authors' conclusions suggest that smooth surfaces within small fracture aperture are
important for suppressing iron oxide precipitation and promoting carbonate mineral precipitation. The authors should also
address the implications of this conclusion for efficient CO2 sequestration.

Reviewer 1 
The paper reports on the results of experiments that react olivine with a CO 2 rich fluid under 
conditions relevant for subsurface carbon storage, specifically examining the role played by Fe 
oxides in passivating the surface of the reacting mineral. They find that the precipitation of 
maghemite, particularly in fractures with wide apertures, is prevalent and that this is likely to

Comments:

Response: Thanks for bringing up this important point.  Yes. We provided roughness 
value in the SI. Within our analysis bandwidth, the rough surface overall has a roughness 
value twice as rough as the smooth surface.  We intentionally chose these surface 
roughness values through trial and errors.  Prior to the ma in experiments, we conducted 
several scoping experiments  to standardize sample preparation  and to ensure that the 
engineered roughness contrast would cause a measurable difference in olivine reactivity.  
Regarding what affects surface roughness, the main factor is the sandpaper grit size. We 
followed the same sample preparation workflow  across all chips  (same sandpaper type  
and diamond suspension ) to reproducibly generate the same roughness contrast. The 
detailed procedure to prepare the sample is described as follows: each specimen was first

hand-polished with progressively finer abrasive papers and then polished on glass using 
a 0.1 µm diamond suspension to obtain the smoothest surface achievable in our lab. We 
then roughened only one half of the same specimen using a coarser sandpaper, creating 
a controlled smooth/rough contrast to investigate the effect of surface roughness on CO2 
mineralization. 
To evaluate the roughness of synthetic fractures, we used SurfCharJ(Fiji) to compute Ra 
from plane-detrended height maps using the same ROI and sampling for all  chips. Our 
window size was 24 mm by 17 mm  (4.1 cm2) and the lateral sampling size is Δx = Δy= 
5.517 µm/pixel. Because we aimed to measure roughness values only for surface exposed 
to the CO 2-saturated solution, we did not measure the roughness for the entire fracture 
surface and masked the area covered by Teflon tapes used to bind the chips.  We did not 
apply additional band -pass filtering for height maps. Thus, the  reported Ra values are 
specific to the measurement window defined by our sampling and ROI , and are directly 
comparable across chips.
