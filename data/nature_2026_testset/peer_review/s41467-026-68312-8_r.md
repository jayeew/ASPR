Reviewer #1 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors report self-hybridized exciton-polariton photodiodes based on multilayer WS2, in which transparent ITO
electrodes act both as electrical contacts and anti-reflection coatings. Leveraging strong light-matter coupling, the devices
exhibit broadband absorption and internal quantum efficiency (IQE) approaching unity near zero detuning. The EQE and
IQE both show clear oscillatory behavior as a function of WS2 thickness, consistent with the dispersion of polaritonic modes.
The photodiodes further demonstrate high responsivity, low noise-equivalent power, and fast response time (~217 ns),
outperforming conventional excitonic photodetectors. These results suggest a promising design strategy for future exciton-
based optoelectronics. The manuscript is technically sound and well-supported by experiments and simulations. 
However, the so-called “self-hybridized” concept has already appeared in many prior studies, which raises some concern
about the novelty of this work. In addition, several specific issues require clarification or improvement before the manuscript
can be considered for publication. 
1. The IQE is derived from EQE and simulated absorption in WS2 using the transfer matrix method. The authors should more
clearly state the assumptions behind isolating absorption in WS2, in particular whether parasitic absorption in ITO or Au has
been considered or neglected. 
2. In Figures 1b and S1, while the authors aim to demonstrate spatially uniform photocurrent response, the corresponding
experimental conditions such as excitation wavelength, laser power, and beam spot size are missing. Even for qualitative
measurements, all reported data should be scientifically valid and reproducible. 
3. In Figure 2c, the x-axis is given in energy while all other spectra are plotted in wavelength. To ensure consistency and
improve readability, the authors are strongly encouraged to present the data in wavelength units as well. 
4. Although the reported response time of 217 ns is relatively fast among excitonic photodiodes, the term “ultrafast” is
generally reserved for sub-nanosecond regimes. The authors should revise the manuscript to avoid overstatement of the
device performance. 
Reviewer #2 
(Remarks to the Author) 
Zhao et al. report their findings on exciton–polariton photodiodes constructed from ITO/multilayer WS₂/Au structures and
present measurements of several key optoelectronic parameters—including EQE, IQE, responsivity, and rise time—all of
which exhibit peak values at a thickness corresponding to the emergence of possible polariton modes. The characterization
of the devices is thorough, and the results demonstrate a high degree of internal consistency. However, attributing the
observed phenomena to polaritons does not appear to be fully convincing, as some of the reported properties are not
consistent with typical polariton behavior. In my view, the manuscript would benefit from further clarification and supporting
evidence before it can be considered suitable for publication. 
1. In Fig 2a and 2b, the authors present the simulated and measured polariton modes for different WS₂ thicknesses. They
report an increase in the vacuum Rabi splitting with increasing WS₂ thickness. However, this trend raises several concerns.
First, as the thickness of the WS₂ layer increases, the mode volume V of the cavity is expected to increase. Since the light–
matter coupling strength g scales inversely with the square root of the mode volume (g∝1/\sqrt{V}), a larger mode volume
should lead to a decrease in g, and thus a smaller Rabi splitting. Second, the exciton oscillator strength typically decreases
in thicker flakes due to enhanced dielectric screening and a weakening of the direct K–K transition in WS₂. Therefore, one
would also expect a reduction in g with increasing thickness. These considerations appear inconsistent with the observed
trend and should be clarified by the authors.

3. In several figures (e.g., Fig. 2b, Fig. S5, Fig. S8), the authors use dashed lines as guides to the eye to indicate faint

analysis of excitonic spectra. This approach could help reveal subtle features more clearly and provide stronger evidence for
the existence of weak or overlapping polariton branches. 
4. It would be helpful if the authors could provide the quality factor of their cavity. This parameter is essential for evaluating
the cavity's optical performance and assessing whether the system operates in the strong coupling regime. Including this
information would also facilitate comparison with previous studies. 
5. In Fig. 2e, the authors present reflectance spectra at two different temperatures. Do the authors have data from a

Reviewer #3 
(Remarks to the Author) 
The manuscript reports a polariton photodiode in a self-hybridized TMD system and clarifies that some device performances,
particularly the response time (up to hundreds of nanoseconds), could be enhanced in the strong coupling regime. The
experimental data primarily support the interpretation of cavity effect of enhancement rather than one based on polaritons.
Although there are some decent performance improvements, the main merit appears to lie in the fast response time, which is
significantly faster than reported devices. While the performance improvements are noted, the enhancement mechanism in
the so called “strong coupling regime “ remains unclear, and the demonstrated evidence is not convincing. Below are my
detailed comments: 
1) The novelty of polariton photodiode bring out some concerns. In the context of exciton polariton photodiodes, this idea has
been tried several years ago in organic excitonic materials [e.g., ACS Photonics 5, 2921-2927 (2018)]. Though it may be an
interesting attempt to realize this idea within TMDs, the experimental evidence in this manuscript is not yet sufficient to
support polariton effects. Further data and clarification are required to substantiate the role of polaritons in the observed
performance enhancement. 
2) Insufficient evidence for the strong coupling regime. To claim the strong coupling regime, the coupling strengths were
fitted under different detuning by varying the TMD thicknesses. This is problematic. The strong coupling can be verified by
satisfying the criterion that the coupling strengths should be larger than the averaged linewidths of cavity modes and
excitons. This means the g^2 should be greater than (gamma_c^2+gamma_x^2)/2. This criterion has not been tested in their
spectroscopic measurements (Figs. 2a-b). The criterion in the SI they cited can only infer there could be spectral splitting,
which does not necessarily indicate the strong coupling regime (please see many references discussing about the
differences between the intermediate coupling and the strong coupling). It is also important to note that the guidelines do not
exactly follow the reflectivity dips (Fig. 2b), which are misleading. 
3) The coupling strengths is proportional to the sample thickness, which has been varied by more than 8 times. For a rough
estimation without considering the detailed cavity mode distributions (which was also ignored in the manuscript), the
coupling strengths in Fig. 2b could be varied by about 3 times. In this way, the so-called anti-crossing features in Figs. 2a-b
is misleading, as they do not share similar cavity strengths. It should be appropriate to measure samples of the same
thickness at different angles (angle-resolved reflectivity) and then extract the coupling strength specifically for each
thickness. 
4) In Fig. 2d, the authors claim that the normalized EQE data support the consistency of polariton resonances and EQE
peaks. However, this is not the case in close observations. For example, some EQE peaks coincide with the exciton
resonance instead of so called “LP” or “UP” states. To substantiate the polariton effect, it is strongly suggested that they
should first clarify the well-separated polariton peaks by checking the criterion of the strong coupling regime, which are
expected to be distant from the exciton resonances with respect to their linewidths. For instance, some convincing
measurements should be at least performed in the “zero-detuning” samples to better determine the strong coupling regime. 
5) In Fig. 2f, some error bars are excessively large, which are greater than the data variations. This largely undermines the
reliability of the data and makes it difficult to draw convincing conclusions. The authors are strongly recommended to retake
the data, and thus effectively reduce these error margins and provide more precise data to support their claims.

6) To strengthen the argument for a polariton-based photodiode, the correlation between the enhanced performances and
the polariton states should be unambiguously established. In Figs 3, 4b, and 4d, the performance improvement in the device
seems more likely to be due to cavity resonance rather than polariton formation. At the zero-detuning, the peak device
performance coincides with the exact cavity resonance (in Fig. S9), indicating that cavity enhancement might be the
optimized. This does not necessarily lead to polariton effects. To establish the polariton enhancements, demonstrating the
optimized formation of polaritons and unambiguously identifying the peaks of performance parameters at the polariton states
would be critical. 
7) The comparisons with reported device performance seem to be suspicious. In their comparisons, the on/off ratio, dark
current density, especially response time seems to be much larger than reported TMD-based photodiodes/photodetectors.
However, there are some mistakes in SI that comparisons of device parameters with previously reported devices show
several discrepancies. For instance, in SI Reference 20 [Adv. Electron. Mater. 7, 2000964 (2021)], the responsivity is given
as 777 mA/W. But the manuscript lists it as 360 mA/W (SI Table S2). As another example, the responsivity in SI Reference 8
[Appl. Surf. Sci. 481, 1127-1132 (2019)] is reported to be about 5 orders of magnitude higher than this work. But the
manuscript totally ignored it in the comparisons of responsivity (SI Table S2). The similar inconsistencies must be addressed
and ensure all comparative data are correct and well-cited without such suspicious discrepancies.

(Remarks to the Author) 
The authors have adequately addressed most of my previous comments. 
Before recommending publication, I have one final suggestion regarding the framing of the work. The use of self-hybridized
polaritons in an open cavity is presented as a practical approach for device applications. However, the relatively high
refractive index of TMDs results in an intrinsically moderate quality factor for such open cavities. While this configuration
demonstrates polariton effects, applications generally require cavities with higher quality factors and smaller mode volumes
for optimal performance. If possible, I would like to suggest the authors tone down the claims of novelty regarding the open
cavity configuration in the final version and instead acknowledge that future performance could be significantly improved
through the careful design of an integrated external cavity.
