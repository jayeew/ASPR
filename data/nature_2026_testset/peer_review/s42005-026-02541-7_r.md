4. The approach allows nanoparticles and biomolecules to remain at room temperature during 
optical trapping, improv ing the accuracy of precise measurements and minimizing damage, 
which is crucial for applications in nanotechnology and life sciences. can the authors elaborate

Reply: Optical trapping usually relies on high laser powers, which inevitably leads to 
localized heating (often tens to hundreds of degrees Kelvin) due to light absorption by the trap-
ped object. This results in system instability and photothermal damage, which is highly 
detrimental (Sci. Adv. 7, eabh1101, 2021; Phys. Rev. A 97, 043803, 2018; Nat. Photonics 11,

<!-- GEAR_PAGE: 9 -->

634-638, 2017).  
Specifically, in the context of biological applications, while optical tweezers enable stable 
trapping of cells, the substantial localized heating can cause severe  cellular damage. Laser-
induced heating is a major source of error in precise biophysical measurements. It introduces 
artifacts such as increased Brownian motion, altered fluid viscosity, and unintended 
conformational changes in biomolecules, all of which reduce measurement fidelity. Conversely, 
cooling below room temperature also risks cold damage (e.g., ice formation), making active 
temperature maintenance at ambient conditions crucial.  By maintaining a stable, room -
temperature environment, our method eliminates these thermal artifacts. 
For vacuum -based optical tweezers, which serve as a critical platform for precision 
measurement, stable trapping at low pressure is essential. In such systems, the motion of a 
trapped nanoparticle within the optical potential is fundamentally driven by thermal fluctuations 
(Brownian motion). A rise in particle temperature intensifies this motion, leading to particle 
escape from the optical trap and preventing high -precision measurements. Therefore, flexible 
and active  cooling to room temperature is of significant importance for enhancing the 
performance and accuracy of optical trapping systems in metrology. 
Our co-doped nanoparticle-based laser cooling approach provides a potential solution to 
this long-standing problem. The capability of our method is clearly demonstrated by the data in 
Figure 3b of the manuscript. During optical trapping at 1030 nm, the temperature of a 
conventional nanoparticle rises to approximately 500 K at 7 mbar, a point where intensified 
Brownian motion typically causes the particle to escape the trap (Phys. Rev. A 97, 043803, 2018. 
In stark contrast, when we activate our cooling laser under the same conditions, the nanoparticle 
temperature stabilizes at only about 380 K. This active cooling directly suppresses the Brownian 
motion, thereby stabilizing the trap and allowing the particle to remain captured at even lower 
pressures. This capability is crucial, as stable trapping at lower pressures is a prerequisite for 
achieving ultra-high mechanical quality factors and performing more precise quantum -limited 
measurements.

fundamental principle of our thermometry based on the population of thermally coupled levels. 
Furthermore, the accuracy of our Er 3+-based thermometry will be comprehensively addressed 
in the following point.  
2. Saturation Concern: Power-dependent luminescence studies were conducted.  As shown in

from the 4S3/2 state (around 540 nm and 555 nm) do not exhibit saturation  under our primary

remaining at 1030 nm), which provides even more efficient excitation for the Yb3+/Er3+ system.

a critical step in our data processing was to exclude the ~55 5 nm emission peak to ensure 
thermometric accuracy. This peak stems from the 2H2/9 level to 4I13/2 transition and would 
introduce error if included in the intensity ratio (Nat. Commun. 9, 4907, 2018).  
3. Temperature Measurements:  We have carefully studied the recommended work and 
acknowledge that the widely accepted standard model may not be directly applicable to the 
specific case of Er 3+ and Yb3+ ions in a β‑phase NaYF4 matrix under upconversion excitation. 
We would like to explain why this does not affect the validity of our luminescence intensity 
ratio (LIR) thermometry results for the following reasons:  
(1). We performed a cross -validation with the well -established Yb3+-based temperature 
sensing method (Nat. Photonics 11, 634-638, 2017; Opt. Lett. 46, 3797-3800, 2021), which is 
widely used in the field of laser cooling of solids, under identical conditions. This method also 
observed a cooling e ffect, and the results, as shown in Figure R1 3, demonstrated excellent 
agreement.

(2) Timescale of thermal equilibrium: The LIR thermometry based on thermally coupled 
levels (such as 4S3/2 and 2H11/2 of Er3+) relies on the establishment of a Boltzmann population 
distribution via rapid thermalization between these levels.  This thermalization occurs on an 
ultrafast timescale (fs –ps) (Ultrafast Dyn. Quantum Syst. Phys. Process. Spectrosc. Tech., 
Springer US, Boston, MA, 569-582, 2002; Sensors and Actuators A 299, 111628, 2019), which 
is orders of magnitude faster than the spontaneous emission rate (typically ns –ms, as reported 
in Nat. Photon., 16, 651–657, 2022 and Nat. Nanotechnol., 16, 367–368, 2021). Therefore, 
thermal equilibrium is fully established well before the detected emission occurs, guaranteeing 
that the measured LIR accurately reflects the nanoparticles' instantaneous temperature, 
independent of the excitation pathway.

(2) Timescale of thermal equilibrium: The LIR thermometry based on thermally coupled
