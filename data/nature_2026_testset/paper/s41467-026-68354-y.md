Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has identiﬁed intermediate epithelial
states in pulmonaryﬁbrosis, including KRT5-/KRT17+ aberrant basaloid cells in
humans and Krt8+ alveolar differentiation intermediates (ADIs) in mice. Their
functional contributions toﬁbrogenesis, however, remain unclear. Here, we
introduce an RNA-sensing-dependentprotein translation technology that
enables selective targeting of Krt8+ ADI cells in vitro and in vivo. Tran-
scriptomic analysis revealedSmall Proline-Rich Protein 1 A(SPRR1A)m R N Aa sa
shared marker of murine Krt8+ ADIs and human KRT5-/KRT17+ basaloid cells,
distinguishing them from other lung cell populations. Using programmable
RNA sensors, we demonstrated selective EGFP-labeling of Krt8+ ADI cells
in vivo, which faithfully recapitulated their transcriptomic and phenotypic
features. To test function, we developed an RNA-sensing-driven diphtheria
toxin receptor (DTR) system for conditional ablation ofSprr1a+cells. Targeted

transitional epithelial cells as pathogenic drivers and highlighting their
potential as therapeutic targets in pulmonaryﬁbrosis.
Pulmonary ﬁbrosis is a debilitating condition characterized by the
progressive scarring of lung tissue. Central to the pathology of this
disease is the failed regeneration and improper repair of lung tissue,
processes that are not yet fully understood 1–3. Under normal condi-
tions, the lung has the capacity to regenerate and repair following
injury, primarily through the proliferation and differentiation of resi-
dent stem cells
4. This regenerative process is tightly regulated in
healthy individuals, efﬁciently restoring lung architecture and func-
tion. However, in pulmonary ﬁbrosis, this regenerative process is dis-
rupted, often leading to aberrant wound healing where excessive
connective tissue builds up, severely compromising respiratory
function
5. The failure of lung regeneration inﬁbrotic conditions can be
attributed to several interconnected factors. These include altered
stem cell functionality due to cellular senescence and/or aging, genetic
predisposition, changes in the local microenvironment/stem cell
niche, and an imbalance in cytokine and growth factor signaling, which
contribute to the persistence of transitional cell states
6,7. The persis-
tence of such transitional cells may lead to a vicious cycle of damage
and ineffective repair, culminating in non-resolvingﬁbrosis.
Recent advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)
technologies have greatly enhanced our understanding of cellular
behavior during lung injury and repair. scRNA-seq enables the detailed
analysis of gene expression proﬁles at the single-cell level, allowing for
the detection of rare cell populations, including intermediate cell

Surfactant-producing cuboidal alveolar type II (AT2) cells function
as a facultative progenitor for the alveolar epithelium. Following

models of pulmonary ﬁbrosis have uncovered multiple novel cell

injury. For instance, AT2 cells initially proliferate post-injury, then exit

AT1 cells. This transitional state is marked by cell cycle cessation,
reduced expression of AT2 markers, a cuboidal or semi-spread mor-
phology, and expression of distinct signature genes such as cytoker-
atin 8 (Krt8).

The RNA-sensing-dependent protein translation technology harnesses
the activity of adenosine deaminases acting on RNA (ADAR), ubiqui-

lungs, we designed two sensor RNAs (sesRNAs) that speci ﬁcally

inosine editing alters the UAG stop codon to a UIG tryptophan codon,
thus initiating translation of an effector RNA (efRNA) encoding EGFP.
The resulting translation produces a fusion protein with an N-terminal
peptide and C-terminal EGFP, subsequently separated by self-cleavage
at the T2A site, releasing the EGFP (Fig. 1A).

Discussion
Recent advancements in scRNA-seq technologies have considerably
enhanced our understanding of cellular dynamics during lung injury
and repair processes. However, beyond transcriptome proﬁling, it is
critical to actively monitor and manipulate speci ﬁc cell types asso-
ciated with disease to further clarify their roles in the pathogenesis of
pulmonary ﬁbrosis. In this study, we introduced an in vivo cell-
targeting strategy that utilizes distinct transcriptomic signatures
sourced from publicly available RNA-seq datasets along with
CellREADR technology. Our approach successfully labeled Krt8+ ADI
transitional AT2 cells, enabling their isolation during lung injury and
repair in mice. Notably, targeted depletion of Krt8+ ADI cells resulted
in attenuated pulmonary ﬁbrosis in mice. This provides direct evi-
dence of the critical role of transitional AT2 cells in lung ﬁbrogenesis
by speciﬁcally targeting this cellular population. Compared to tradi-
tional lineage-tracing methods in transgenic animal models, our in vivo
transcriptomic signature-guided cell targeting approach offers a rapid,
more cost-effective, and highly speci ﬁc method for studying the
function of distinct cell lineages, particularly in the context of disease
progression. A major ﬁnding of this study is the identi ﬁcation of
SPRR1A as a robust and speciﬁc transcriptomic marker for both mouse
Krt8+ ADI cells and human KRT5-/KRT17+ aberrant basaloid cells. The
identiﬁcation ofSPRR1A as a shared marker across species strengthens
the translational relevance of ourﬁndings and provides an avenue for
targeted intervention strategies in pulmonaryﬁbrosis.

In this study, Sprr1a-guided DTR expression and subsequent DT
administration successfully diminished transitional AT2 cell popula-
tions by approximately 38% at their peak in ﬁbrotic mouse lungs. The
targeted depletion of these cells attenuated bleomycin-induced pul-
monary ﬁbrosis, suggesting a“causal” role for transitional AT2 cells in
lung ﬁbrogenesis. To our knowledge, this is the ﬁr s tr e p o r tt od e m o n -

Intriguingly, we observed that around 11.8% of Sprr1a+ cells in the
control group (untreated with DT) also underwent apoptosis at day 15
post-bleomycin administration, coinciding with the peak abundance of
transitional AT2 cells. This observation suggests a natural turnover of
these cells even in the absence of DT-mediated cell death. Further
longitudinal analysis of apoptosis in Sprr1a+ cells reveals a dynamic
pattern: apoptosis rates gradually increased from day 12, peaked
between days 17 and 19, and then declined by day 29. These results
indicate that transitional AT2 ce lls are not a stable population but
instead undergo programmed cell death as part of the normal pro-
gression and resolution of lungﬁbrosis. This dynamic apoptotic beha-
vior highlights the transient nature of transitional AT2 cells within the
injured lung microenvironment and suggests that tightly regulated cell
turnover may be critical for successful epithelial regeneration and
restoration of alveolar homeostasis. The phenomenon of natural
clearance of excess transitional AT2 cells in the context of resolving
bleomycin-induced lungﬁbrosis aligns with our results demonstrating
that targeted depletion of these cells at their peak amelioratesﬁbrosis in
this model. Corroborating ourﬁndings, analysis of single cells to iden-
tify enrichment of gene programs demonstrated that Krt8+ ADI tran-
sitional cells exhibited elevated expression levels of genes that promote
cell death compared to other epithelial cells
8. Furthermore, a recent
study has localized transitional AT2 cells not only within the ﬁbrotic
lung parenchyma but also within the airspaces, indicative of cell death
and sloughing processes
34. Additional studies have shown that transi-
tional AT2 cells located atﬁbroblastic foci exhibit apoptotic features40,41.
Our study builds on recent advances in programmable RNA-
sensing platforms22–25. For our experiments, we primarily used the web-
based CellREADR design portal ( https://cellreadr.neuro.duke.edu)t o
generate candidate sesRNAs, supplemented in some cases by manual
design following the general guidelines established in the above stu-
dies (e.g., prioritizing 3 ′ UTRs, avoiding reverse-complement stop
codons, and considering split-sensor conﬁgurations). Notably, not all
sesRNAs generated by the online tool yielded ef ﬁcient output in our
hands. Based on this experience, we recommend a two-stage valida-
tion pipeline: ﬁrst, in vitro screening of multiple candidate sesRNAs
(e.g., in HEK293 cells) to identify designs that consistently induce
reporter expression in a sequence-speciﬁc manner; and second, in vivo
validation of optimized sensors using complementary approaches
such as RNA FISH/RNA scope, immuno ﬂuorescence, and lineage-
labeling transgenic models. Finally, as highlighted in prior work, sta-
bility and delivery remain key hurdles for translational application.
Therapeutic versions may require incorporation of modiﬁed nucleo-
tides or circular RNA scaffolds to enhance stability and reduce
immunogenicity. The integration of such features, combined with
rigorous validation of speci ﬁcity, will be critical to enable clinical
translation of sesRNA-guided targeting strategies.
While ourﬁndings support the speciﬁcity ofSprr1a-SesR-mediated
targeting, several caveats should be considered. The activity of sesRNA
ultimately depends on faithful engagement of the Sprr1a regulatory
element, and it remains possible that rare epithelial subtypes or tran-
siently activated cells could display low-level off-target activation.
Furthermore, sustained DTR expression within transitional cells may
perturb cell-cell interactions in ways that are not fully captured by
short-term depletion experiments. Our study was designed to examine
the acute impact ofSprr1a + (Krt8+ ADI) transitional AT2 cell depletion
during their peak accumulation in the single-dose bleomycin model,
based on the kinetics previously de ﬁned by Strunz et al.
8. However,
equivalent temporal information is not yet available for repetitive
bleomycin injury or aging models, limiting the rational design of
depletion timing and preventing a comprehensive assessment of long-
term consequences on regeneration and epithelial homeostasis.
Notably, prior work indicates that both excessive and insuf ﬁcient
transitional AT2 cell states can drive pathological outcomes resem-
bling ﬁbrosis or emphysema
20, underscoring the importance of
maintaining balanced transitional states for proper repair. While our
present study focused on depletion, we envision that Sprr1a sesRNA-
guided targeting could be adapted for regenerative purposes. For
example, coupling this strategy with the delivery of transcription fac-
tors or other modulators might enable the selective reprogramming of
transitional AT2 cells toward bene ﬁcial repair outcomes rather than
elimination. Ultimately, long-term studies in models of aging or repe-
ated injury will be essential to determine whether Sprr1a+ cell deple-
tion exerts unintended effects on regenerative capacity and epithelial
homeostasis. Together, our ﬁndings highlight the importance of
transitional AT2 cells in the ﬁbrotic process and underscore the ther-
apeutic potential of targeting these cells to modulateﬁbrosis. Our data
also suggest that apoptosis may serve as a natural regulatory
mechanism to prevent the prolonged presence of transitional AT2
cells, which, if left unchecked, could contribute to pathologicalﬁbrotic
remodeling. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the precise
molecular pathways involved in the regulation of transitional AT2 cells.
Such insights are crucial for designing interventions that could either
enhance the natural resolution mechanisms or prevent the excessive
accumulation of these cells, potentially offering new therapeutic ave-
nues for treating ﬁbrotic lung diseases.
Methods
sesRNA design
Sensor sequences were designed based on the criteria described by
Qian et al.22.B r i eﬂy, sesRNAs were constructed as antisense sequences
complementary to the mRNAs ofSftpc, Pdpn,a n dSprr1a mRNAs, with
an optimal length of 200–300 nucleotides. Each sesRNA was linked in-
frame to an effector RNA ( EGFP, ZsGreen1,o r DTR), forming a single
open reading frame. A TAG stop codon was introduced near the
midpoint of the sesRNA sequence (typically 80-220 nucleotides from
the 5′ end) to regulate translation. To create a strategic A–Cm i s m a t c h ,
5′-TGG-3′ sequences in the sesRNA that would pair with 5′-CCA-3′ in the
target mRNA were mutated to 5 ′-TAG-3′. Additionally, to avoid unin-
tended translation initiation, no ATG start codons were included
downstream of the central TAG stop codon. The sesRNA cDNAs were
synthesized as gBlocks (Integrated DNA Technologies) and subcloned
into an pAAV-CAG-mTagBFP2-P2A-EGFP vector backbone (Addgene,
Plasmid #184943). All sesRNA sequences used in this study are listed in
Supplementary Table 1.

pump at a rate of 1 ml/min. The column was washed sequentially with
50 ml PBS and 50 ml 200 mM NaCl in PBS. Virus was eluted in a step-
wise manner using 1.5 ml of 300 mM, 3 ml of 350 mM, and 1.5 ml of
450 mM NaCl in PBS. Eluted fractions were ﬁltered through a 0.2 µm
syringe ﬁlter and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugalﬁlters
(100 kDa MWCO; Millipore) by centrifugation at 4000 ×g until a ﬁnal

was resuspended in PBS to a ﬁnal volume of 500 µl, aliquoted, and
stored at –80 °C. Viral genome titers were quantiﬁed by RT-PCR.
In situ hybridization
In situ hybridization was performed using the miRCURY LNA miRNA
ISH Optimization Kit (QIAGEN), following the manufacturer’sO n e - D a y
miRNA ISH protocol optimized for formalin-ﬁxed parafﬁn-embedded
samples. Tissue sections (5 µm) were deparaf ﬁnized in xylene and
rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Sections were then trea-
ted with freshly prepared Proteinase K solution (diluted in Proteinase K
buffer) for 10 min at 37 °C to permeabilize the tissue. Hybridization
was carried out using double-digoxigenin-labeled LNA miRNA probes
at a ﬁnal concentration of 40 nM, diluted in formamide-free ISH buffer.
Probes were denatured at 90 °C for 4 min prior to application. Hybri-
dization was performed at 60 °C for 1 h in a humidi ﬁed hybridization
chamber. Following hybridization, slides were subjected to a series of
stringent washes with saline-sodium citrate buffers at the hybridiza-
tion temperature to remove nonspeciﬁcally bound probes.
Single-cell RNA-seq library preparation and analysis
Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed using the 10x Genomics
Fixed RNA Proﬁling (scRNA-seq Flex) chemistry. Viable lung cells were
isolated from mice infected with AAV- Sprr1a SesRNA-EGFP and pro-
cessed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (10x Genomics Inc.).
Brieﬂy, single-cell suspensions were treated with a proprietary cross-
linking reagent to preserve RNA integrity and stored at–80 °C until all
experimental groups were collected.
Target transcript capture and sample barcoding were performed
using the Chromium Mouse Transcriptome Probe Set v1.0.1 (10x Geno-
mics). Each sample was labeled with a unique sample-speci ﬁcp r o b e
barcode. To enable detection of EGFP expression, a custom probe tar-
geting EGFP was included during hybridization. After probe
hybridization, samples were pooled and washed to remove unbound
probes. Barcoded cells from pooled samples were encapsulated into Gel
Bead-in-Emulsions (GEMs) using the Chromium Chip Q. Inside each GEM,
nicked probe pairs were ligated, and unique molecular identiﬁers (UMIs),
10x GEM barcodes, and partial Read 1T sequences were incorporated
through polymerase extension. Ligated and barcoded probe pairs were
then ampliﬁed via sample index PCR to generate sequencing-ready
libraries containing Illumina P5/P7 adapters and Read 2S sequences.
Library quality was assessed using the Agilent High Sensitivity
DNA Kit on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and library concentrations
were measured with a Qubit 2.0 ﬂuorometer. Libraries were pooled
and sequenced at aﬁnal concentration of 750 pM using paired-end and
dual-index reads on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform. Raw
sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger v9.0.0 (10x Geno-
mics), and reads were aligned to the GRCm39-2020-A mouse reference
genome using the Chromium Mouse Transcriptome Probe Set v1.1.1.
Computational analysis of scRNA-seq datasets
Single-cell RNA sequencing data from bleomycin-injured mouse lungs
were processed using the Scanpy package (v1.9.0). Initial quality con-
trol involved ﬁltering out cells with low gene counts or high mito-
chondrial transcript content. Data were normalized to a total count of
10,000 reads per cell and subsequently log-transformed. EGFP+c e l l s
were identi ﬁed based on non-zero expression of the EGFP gene.
Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component
analysis (PCA), followed by construction of a neighborhood graph and
clustering using the Leiden algorithm (resolution = 0.8). Two-
dimensional embedding and visualization were conducted using Uni-
form Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).
To deﬁne Krt8+ ADI-like cells, we curated a panel of marker genes
based on Strunz et al.
8. This panel included structural cytokeratins
(Krt8, Krt18, Krt7, Krt19) and injury-response genes (Sprr1a, Areg, Hbegf,
Tnfrsf12a, Itgb6, Cldn18, Cldn4, Cryab, Cdkn1a). Cells were annotated as
Krt8+ ADI-like if they expressed the markers from this panel at a nor-
malized expression level >1.
Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the
rank-genes-groups function in Scanpy, applying the Wilcoxon rank-
sum test. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the
Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and genes with an adjusted p-
value < 0.05 were considered statistically signi ﬁcant. To evaluate
transcriptomic similarity between EGFP+ and Krt8+ ADI-like popula-
tions, the top 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from each
group were selected. Pairwise similarity metrics, including Pearson
correlation, Spearman correlation, and cosine similarity, were calcu-
lated using the SciPy library (v1.10). All plots, including violin plots,
heatmaps, and UMAP visualizations, were generated using Seaborn
(v0.12) and Matplotlib (v3.7).

Animals and experimental protocol
All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care
and Use Committees (IACUC) of Tulane University and the University
of Alabama at Birmingham. Pathogen-free, 12-week-old mice were used
for all experiments. To minimize biological variability and maintain
consistency with prior bleomycin ﬁbrosis studies, the experiments
shown in Fig. 6D–F were performed exclusively in female mice. All
other mouse experiments, including ﬂuorescence microscopy/histol-
ogy, RNA FISH/immunohistochemistry, scRNA-seq, and cell sorting/
FACS, used both female and male mice. Sex-based comparisons were
not performed; therefore, results should be interpreted within this
context. Future work will be required to directly assess whether the
observed effects are sex-dependent.
Two transgenic mouse models were employed in this study: (1)
Krt8-Cre;Rosa-tdTomatomice, generated by crossingTg(Krt8-cre/ERT2)
17Blpn/J mice with B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J
mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME); and (2) SFTPC-Cre;R-
osa-tdTomatomice, generated by crossingB6.129S-Sftpc^tm1(cre/ERT2)
Blh/J mice with the same Rosa-tdTomato reporter line (The Jackson
Laboratory). Mice were housed under speci ﬁc pathogen–free condi-
tions on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and
water. Animal rooms were maintained at 20–24 °C with 30–70% rela-
tive humidity. To induce tdTomato expression in Cre-expressing cells,
tamoxifen was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of
50 mg/kg per day for 5 consecutive days. Lung injury and ﬁbrotic
remodeling were induced by intratracheal administration of bleomy-
cin sulfate (Almirall) dissolved in 50μl sterile saline at a dose of 1.5U/kg
body weight. Control mice received an equal volume of saline.
For selective depletion ofSprr1a+ cells in vivo, mice underwent a
sequential treatment protocol. On day 0, mice were anesthetized and
administered intratracheal bleomycin. On day 8, an AAV vector
encoding a sesRNA-guided DTR construct was delivered intra-
tracheally for cell-speciﬁct a r g e t i n go fSprr1a+c e l l s .O nd a y1 4 ,D Tw a s
administered intratracheally to ablate DTR-expressing Sprr1a+ cells.
Lung tissues were harvested on day 28 for ﬁbrosis analyses.
At harvest, mice were euthanized, and lungs were processed for
immunoﬂuorescence, histology, or biochemical analyses. For
immunoﬂuorescence, lungs were excised and in ﬂated with OCT
compound before embedding. For histological analysis, lungs were
ﬁxed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and paraf ﬁn-embedded. For
biochemical analysis, fresh lung tissues were placed into pre-chilled
tubes containing T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo
Fisher Scienti ﬁc) supplemented with Complete Mini Protease Inhi-
bitor Cocktail (Roche) at a ratio of 1 tablet per 10 ml of T-PER. Tis-
sues were homogenized on ice and centrifuged at 9000 × g for
10 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, and total protein con-
centration was quanti ﬁed using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo
Fisher).
Statistics and reproducibility
Statistical differences among treatment conditions were determined
by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Newman–Keuls method for
multiple comparisons) or two-tailed Student’s t test using GraphPad
Prism 8 software. Values of p < 0.05 were considered signiﬁcant. For
reproducibility, all key ﬁndings were con ﬁrmed in at least two inde-
pendent experiments using independently prepared samples, with
similar results. Representative images (e.g., immunoﬂuorescence and
histology images) are shown from independently repeated experi-
ments that yielded consistent qualitative patterns. Independent
repeats refer to biologically independent samples (e.g., tissues/cells
derived from different animals or donors and processed on different
days) rather than technical replicates. The number of biological
replicates (n) for each analysis is indicated in the corresponding plots
and/or described in the ﬁgure legends where applicable.
