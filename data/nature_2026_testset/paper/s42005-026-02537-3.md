Our identities are composed of multiple categories— such as ethnicity, gender, and socioeconomic status
— that shape our social networks through group-based connection preferences. As most research
focuses on one-dimensional interactions, a fundamental question remains: How do individuals integrate
multidimensional identity information when forming social ties? Addressing this question is crucial for
understanding social dynamics, mitigating segregation, and designing integration-promoting
interventions. Here, we develop a principled theoretical framework to model multidimensional social
interactions. Using Bayesian model selection,we compare competing preference aggregation
mechanisms in two empirical systems (high-school friendship networks and marriages) andﬁnd that a
simple latent preference model outperforms more complex alternatives: people evaluate each identity
dimension independently and form ties mainly whenall evaluations are favorable. The model yields
interpretable preference estimates and principledmeasures of dimension salience, providing a practical
tool for analyzing social choices and identifying which aspects of identity matter most for tie formation. Our
work reveals how social structures emerge from intersecting identities, with broad implications for
understanding social cohesion and addressing intersectional inequalities in networks.
Social categories like ethnicity, gender, and socioeconomic status are fun-
damental architects of our social co nnections. They forge our identities,
inﬂuencing how we interact and with whom we choose to connect1,2.W h i l e
our identities are inherently multid imensional, much of the research in
social network analysis has predominantly adopted a one-dimensional
perspective. Existing studies either examine interactions through single
attributes
3–5 or treat multiple attributes independently6,7.
Early research in social psychology recognized the inherent multi-
dimensionality of our identity, but postulated the existence of a salient
dimension that would dominate social behavior in each context
5,8,9.S u b -
sequent research has raised more nuanced questions: How do individuals
interact when they simultaneously occupy multiple identities? Can different
identity dimensions becomesimultaneously salient?
9,10.
In recent years, multidimensional id entities have b een increasingly
incorporated into the study of social networks11–15. However, these studies
aim to describe observed interaction patterns through statistical models
rather than uncover the mechanisms that translate identities into pre-
ferences and, ultimately, into the tie formation decisions that build social
networks. We still lack a principled theory of multidimensional interactions.
The primary challenge is understan ding how individuals aggregate
their connection preferences across multiple identity dimensions. DiMaggio
and Garip
16 vividly illustrate this challenge with an example of a female
Hispanic college graduate who prefers to associate with people like herself in
gender, nationality, and educatio nal attainment. Would she desire to
maintain friendships with people identical to herself on all three dimensions
so that, in effect,“female Hispanic college graduate”acts as a single category?
Would she connect with others based on an average of those three attri-
butes? Or would she integrate her preferences in any other way?
Closing this research gap is critical because understanding how indi-
viduals aggregate their preferences across identity dimensions has profound
implications for predicting and explaining patterns of social segregation,
cohesion, and inequality. A principled theory of multidimensional inter-
actions would not only illuminate the mechanisms driving social network
formation, but would also provide essential tools for designing interventions
to promote integration and mitigate systemic biases in diverse communities.
To address these questions, we develop aﬂexible modeling framework
that can incorporate different preference aggregation mechanisms-rules
describing how people combine preferences across identity dimensions

Early sociophysics approaches to multidimensional interactions
represented nodes in latent social sp aces, building connections based on
distances between them26,27. These models primarily aimed to reproduce
aggregate network statistics rather thanﬁt observed data directly. Related
computational approaches also used abstract latent spaces, inferring node
positions through Bayesian optimization 28. However, these abstract
dimensions are difﬁcult to interpret or directly relate to real-world identity
categories, as they do not correspond to observable attributes such as gender
or ethnicity. Consequently, they provide limited insight into how indivi-
duals aggregate information across known identity dimensions. Moreover,
because each node is assigned its own position in latent space, the number of
parameters scales with the network size, which can complicate inference.
A natural next step is to move away from real-valued latent spaces
toward categorical latent features that might better capture known social
attributes, an approach explored in the Nonparametric Latent Feature
model by Miller et al.
29. This model inferentially assigns an arbitrary number
of binary latent features to nodes and uses aﬂexible weight matrix to model
the probability of a link. Although this avoids the need for geometric
assumptions about the latent space, the model is aimed at link prediction
rather than understanding identity -driven structure, and the authors
themselves note that the learned features are generally not interpretable.
Moreover, performance gains over conventional blockmodels are relatively
modest despite including substantially more parameters. In terms of pre-
diction performance, these models have since been largely superseded by
deep learning techniques
30,31 and model ensembles32.
More closely aligned with our goals is the work by Kim and
Leskovec33,34, which also tackles the question of how do we combine node
features in a natural way to obtain probabilities of individual links?To this
end, they introduced the Multiplicative Attribute Graph model. While this
approach takes an important step to wards modeling multidimensional
interactions, the authors limit their analysis to a narrow class of model
variants with binary attribute vectors. More importantly, they postulate a
speciﬁc aggregation mechanism for combining preferences, rather than
evaluating alternative hypotheses. This limits the model’s ability to address
the central problem of how individuals integrate identity dimensions when
forming social ties. In contrast, our framework is explicitly designed to
compare competing aggregation mechanisms and assess their empirical
validity.

Application to real-world social networks
Building on the insights from the previous section, we now apply MAPS to
real-world social networks with two p rimary objectives. First, we aim to
identify the aggregation function that best explains how individuals interact
across multiple identity dimensions. To do this, weﬁt MAPS to empirical
data and assess which aggregation mechanisms best capture observed social
dynamics. Second, we demonstrate the practical utility of our framework by
analyzing the latent preferences uncovered by the model. To this end, in
subsection “Inference of model parameters ” of Methods we develop a
maximum-likelihood method for inf erring the model parameters from
empirical data.
Data description. To validate our model on real-world interactions, we
analyze two systems: high-school friendship networks, where ties are
mostly homophilic, and marriages, which are predominantly hetero-
philic in gender and homophilic in other dimensions.
We use the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health
(AddHealth) dataset
18, which contains information about friendship rela-
tionships among high school students in the United States and about the
students’sociodemographic attributes. We use a processed version of the
dataset that includes directed friendship networks and the grade (between
7th and 12th), ethnicity (Black, Hispanic, Asian, White, and mixed/other),
and gender (boy or girl) of the students
17. Therefore, the models have three
dimensions (grade, ethnicity, and gender), with six,ﬁve, and two categories,
respectively. In practice, some of these categories may be empty in some
schools (e.g., if there are no Asian students), effectively reducing the number
of categories in that dimension.
The AddHealth dataset includes friendship networks from 84 com-
munities. Some communities include one school and others two (a middle
school and a high school). We selected schools with more than 100 students
remaining in the network after processing the data and with more than one
gender and ethnicity category. As a result, 41,880 students from 70 com-
munities were included in our study. We have analyzed each of the 70
communities independently. We provide all the details about the data
processing andﬁltering in Supplementary Note 1.

Discussion
Our identities are composed of intersecting categories— ethnicity, gender,
socioeconomic status, etc.— that drive our interactions. Yet how we combine
this multi-level information to formsocial connections has long remained
unresolved. Understanding this process is crucial, both from a theoretical
perspective— to accurately model multidimensional social dynamics— and
for addressing practical challenges in promoting social integration, reducing
segregation, and designing interventions that account for the complexity of
social barriers. In this work, we address this question by introducing MAPS,
a conceptual and mathematical frame work to model social interactions
between individuals with multidimensional identities. We use MAPS as a
testbed to systematically investigate the core principles governing multi-
dimensional interactions by ﬁtting the model to two contrasting social
systems: high-school friendship n etworks and marriages. Through this
framework, we uncover the mental representations that structure connec-
tion preferences and reveal the aggregation mechanism we use to integrate
multidimensional identity information when choosing social ties.

Beyond these classical framework s, modeling approaches to social
networks span a broad spectrum. At one end lies the mechanistic tradition,
rooted in sociophysics, which builds in terpretable models that explicitly
encode hypothesized social processes and test them against real data23,55.A t

follows the sociophysics tradition, providing an interpretable and empiri-
cally testable alternative to black- box models that supports hypothesis-

In this work, we have considered the simplest variant of the MEAN
aggregation function, where all dimensions are weighted equally. Allowing
the weights to be free parameters could in principle improve its ﬁtb y
learning the relative importance of dimensions directly from the data. A
key feature of the AND aggregation that MEAN seems to struggle to
reproduce is its ability to generate highly modular networks (see Fig. 2),
which are pervasive in real-world social systems. TheAND rule multiplies
dimension-wise connection probabilities, so a single low probability in any
dimension drives the overall link probability down and naturally produces
sharp modular blocks. By contrast, an arithmetic mean smooths extreme
(low) values and assigns intermediate probabilities to “half-matches”
(nodes that match in one dimension but not another), which blurs block-
like separation.
However, suitably chosen weights and/or more extreme preference
values could also induce modularity: related models with MEAN-like
interactions have been shown to generate community structure under
appropriate parameter settings
26. Learning weights in a weighted MEAN
would be straightforward, but it would addD − 1 global parameters forD
dimensions. Although explicit weights would provide a direct measure of
salience, the AND function already encodes a form of differential relevance:
if a dimension’s probabilities are close to neutrality (similar across target
groups), its inﬂuence on the aggregated preference is minimal. Overall, the
weighted MEAN is a plausible extension, but its increased complexity would
need to be justi ﬁed by clear empirical gains, and we leave a systematic
exploration of this richer parameter space for future work.
Nevertheless, the limitation remains that we have explored only a
limited set of aggregation mechanisms (selected because they capture simple
and extreme forms of aggregation, from fully tolerant to fully selective).
Real-world social interactions may follow richer rules, for instance com-
bining tolerance on some dimensions with strict thresholds on others.
Future works should explore alternative possibilities, either by heuristically
proposing plausible mechanisms motivated by sociological theory and
experimentalﬁndings, or by adopting agnostic nonparametric approaches
that directly infer the aggregation f unction from data. Recent Bayesian
learning methods that discover clo sed-form mathematical models are a
promising direction
57. As demonstrated in our model comparisons, the
MAPS framework is inherently modular: the aggregation step can be
reformulated without altering the rest of the model, enabling systematic
evaluation of alternative mechanisms.

qualitative validation, as it demonstrates that the model provides mean-

preference between Black and White students. In marriages, weﬁnd strong

tional component in the economic dimension, where individuals in lower
terciles display a mild but consistent preference for partners from higher
terciles.
Our framework also contributes t o longstanding debates in social
psychology by offering a quantitative approach to identity salience; that is,
the relevance of identity dimensions for social behavior. While hierarchies of
salience have been widely theorized
9,50, there is little agreement on how to
measure them, especially when multiple dimensions may be simultaneously
important. By introducing two interpretable measures— one based on the
strength of individual preferences and another on the explanatory power of
each dimension in model selection— we provide a principled framework
that bridges abstract theory and observed behavior. This enables empirical
testing of when and where certain identity dimensions become more salient,
depending on context.
A natural challenge for multidimensional models is deciding which
and how many dimensions to consider. While adding further dimensions
can in principle provide a more nuanced representation of social differ-
entiation, in practice it risks generating an ever-growing number ofﬁnely
segmented subgroups, with diminishing empirical support and an
increased danger of over ﬁtting. This echoes a well-known critique in
intersectionality theory, the so-called inﬁnite regress problem
58,59. Within
our modeling framework, a pragmatic resolution is to sequentially intro-
duce dimensions based on their empirical salience and to rely on standard
model selection criteria (e.g., AIC/BIC) to evaluate whether additional
segmentation improves explanatory power relative to increased model
complexity. In this way, our approach remains open to richer identity
representations while maintaining parsimony and avoiding unjusti ﬁed
over-segmentation. In AddHealth, for instance, focusing on ethnicity and
grade might be enough, since the improvement (and information) pro-
vided by including gender is minimal. In the marriages dataset, however,
the four dimensions seem necessary to get a full understanding of identity
preferences.
