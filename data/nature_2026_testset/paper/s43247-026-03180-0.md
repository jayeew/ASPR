Regional climate variability manifests through distinct atmospheric regimes in ﬂuencing weather,
climate, and predictability. Yet, their response to global warming remains unresolved. Using a hundred
realisations from the Community Earth System Model Large Ensemble, we examine shifts in
wintertime North Atlantic atmospheric regimes and the North Atlantic Oscillation before and after
1995, highlighting the detectable inﬂuence of anthropogenic warming on atmospheric circulation. The
large-ensemble framework isolates internal variability by removing the ensemble mean. Under
anthropogenic warming, the number of regime states associated with the forced response remains
constant, although their spatial circulation patterns undergo substantial reorganisation. In contrast,
internal variability alone exhibits a reduction in the number of regime states. Future projections indicate
a shift toward more frequent positive phases of the North Atlantic Oscillation, accompanied by low-
amplitude negative phases late in the century, alongside a marked decline in its variability and altered
mid-tropospheric westerlies.
Understanding the potential for anthropogenic climate change to alter
internal climate variability is a major research question. Long-term climate
variability combines the forced response, i.e., the response to external for-
cings (e.g., volcanoes, anthropogenic emissions of greenhouse gases and
aerosols) and the internal variability ofthe climate system, i.e., the variability
inherent to the system, independent of external forcings. The subtle inter-
action of the internal modes of climate variability with the forced response
plays a crucial role in determining how the climate evolves and adds
complexity to accurateclimate predictions
1– 3.M a n ys t u d i e s4– 9 have pointed
to the growing evidence that both the frequency of occurrence and the
variability of the internal climate modes are being inﬂuenced by the global
anthropogenic climate change signal, with impacts on global and regional
communities and ecosystems
10– 15.

Atmospheric regimes are distinct quasi-stationary recurrent states of
the atmospheric circulation
25. These regimes are typically characterised by
recurring spatial and temporal variability. Several studies have investigated
the existence of different regime states over the Northern Hemisphere
(NH)25– 29. An early study by Mo and Ghil27 identiﬁed four different regime
states over the NH. Similarly, Smyth et al.28 also observed four regime states
over the NH with two separate regimes in the Paciﬁc and Atlantic sectors.
The Paciﬁc-North American (PNA) and the reverse PNA patterns were
identiﬁed over the Paciﬁc28, while zonal and blocked phases of the North
Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) were observed over the Atlantic sector28.
A majority of studies30,31 use various types of clustering techniques to
identify and classify atmospheric regime states. The goal of clustering
analysis is to identify and differentiate regime states from the atmospheric
background noise
32. Most studies25– 29 on identifying regimes are focused on
synoptic time scales, and less literature is available to study the evolution and
changes in the regime states over longer periods.

The NAO substantially inﬂuences the climate dynamics in the North
Atlantic
23,41– 43. A typical NAO-positive (negative) phase is characterised by
high-pressure (low-pressure) anomalies over the subtropical Atlantic and
low-pressure (high-pressure) anomalies over the Icelandic region. There has
been a lot of evidence regarding the emergence of the NAO from the internal
dynamics of the extratropical atmosphere
44– 46. In particular, the NAO can be
interpreted as a manifestation of large-scale, quasi-stationary Rossby wave
patterns43, which govern the preferred con ﬁgurations of the midlatitude
atmosphericﬂow. The NAO is inherently a part of the regime structure over
the North Atlantic20,43, representing one of the dominant modes through
which Rossby wave dynamics project onto the regional circulation.
Further, several studies have also identiﬁed the NAO as part of coupled
ocean, atmosphere and sea-ice dynamic processes 47,48. The atmospheric
variability associated with the NAO phases governs the overall regional
ﬂuctuations over the North Atlantic basin. Moreover, it has wide implica-
t i o n si ni nﬂuencing climate variability over Europe and the Sahel49,a sw e l la s
Arctic sea-ice variability50. Therefore, it becomes important to address the
changes in the NAO in a warmer climate.
Our study is driven by the need to understand how anthropogenic
warming inﬂuences the structure and frequency of North Atlantic atmo-
spheric regimes and NAO variability. Corti et al. 34 hypothesised that
anthropogenic global warming leads to changes in the frequency of internal
modes of variability or regime states. Here, we assess whether the externally
forced climate change signal alters internally generated regime behaviour.
The evolution of the atmospheric regime states over the North Atlantic
remains poorly understood, particularly regarding how external forcing
reorganises the circulation patterns and modulates the NAO phase
behaviour.
The North Atlantic basin is an important region for a wide range of
ecosystems. Understanding how anthropogenic forcing alters its climate
patterns remains crucial. Using one hundred ensemble member simulations
from the Community Earth System Model version 2 (CESM2-LE)
36 under
the SSP3-7.0 scenario51 enables us to systematically and robustly investigate
how forced response and internal variability inﬂuence the North Atlantic
climate variability in a global warming scenario.
The SSP3-7.0 scenario51, representing a high-emission pathway and
strong regional disparities with limited climate mitigation, provides a
valuable framework to assess how ant hropogenic warming can reshape
large-scale atmospheric circulation over the North Atlantic region. By
leveraging the large ensemble simulations, we deﬁne the forced response as
the ensemble mean
37,52,53, while departures of the individual realisations
from the ensemble mean characterise the modelled internal climate
variability
37,52,53. Using a large ensemble with a hundred members enables us
to detect changes in the phase space of the regimes and to attribute any
changes to anthropogenic global warming.
Results
Covariability of mid-tropospheric circulation to surface warming
Before presenting our results, we brieﬂy highlight our methodology. We use
the monthly mean streamfunction at 500 hPa during the winter season
(December, January, and February) from 1850 to 2100 for all one hundred
ensemble member realisations from CESM2-LE 36 to classify the atmo-
spheric circulation patterns over the North Atlantic region. After removing
the monthly climatology, we decompose the atmospheric patterns into two
categories:
(1) The full ﬂow ﬁeld (FF) represents patterns that combine both
internal variability and forced responses.
(2) The internal variability ﬂow ﬁeld (IV) represents the patterns
without external forcing, obtained by removing the ensemble mean from
each realisation.
We then employ the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) instead of
Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF) analysis to better capture the cou-
pled variability between the mid-tropospheric circulation and surface
temperature (refer to “Data and Methods ”). While EOFs identify the
dominant modes of variability within a single ﬁeld, the SVD provides a
framework to examine the co-variability between twoﬁelds. This coupling
from the SVD framework enables isolating the forced response signal, which
might not emerge clearly in single-ﬁeld EOF analysis. However, previous
studies, like Corti et al.
34 use the EOF analysis to base their results on the
manifestation of the climate chang e signal on the Northern hemisphere
atmospheric circulation regimes. Here, we elucidate the advantage of the
coupled SVD analysis, which provides a clear illustration of the response of
surface warming to the mid-tropospheric circulation.
Accordingly, we apply SVD between the streamfunction at 500 hPa
and surface temperature across the one hundred ensemble member simu-
lations to both theﬂow ﬁelds, i.e., FF and IV, for the period 1850 to 2100, to
identify the two most dominant modes for both streamfunction at 500 hPa
and surface temperature. The spatial patterns associated with theﬁrst two
SVD modes (left and right singular vectors) for FF and IV, along with their
corresponding principal components, are presented and discussed in the
Supplementary Information.
To further understand the role of anthropogenic forcing in modulating
the temporal evolution of these coupled modes, we focus on the ensemble
mean, i.e., averaging across the hundred realisations from the CESM2-LE, of
the ﬁrst two principal component time series, i.e., PC1 and PC2 from the FF
(Fig. 1c, d), which represents the forced response linked to the 500 hPa
streamfunction and surface temperature. To identify the anthropogenic
inﬂuence on the mid-tropospheric circulation patterns, we apply a chan-
gepoint detection analysis to the ﬁrst two principal components of the
streamfunction at 500 hPa over the North Atlantic in order to identify the
time when the slope changes (refer to “Data and Methods”). This change
point determines the start of the anthropogenic global warming imprint on
the atmospheric circulation in the North Atlantic (Fig.1c).
Notably, PC2 of the streamfuncti on at 500 hPa exhibits the earliest
statistically signiﬁcant change in slope around 1995 (p < 0.01), marking the
onset of anthropogenic warming in ﬂuence on atmospheric circulation
patterns in the North Atlantic (Fig. 1c). We therefore focus on the chan-

Summary and discussion
In this study, we demonstrate the role of external forcing in modulating
atmospheric ﬂow patterns over the North Atlantic region under global
warming. Ourﬁndings show the complex interplay between anthropogenic
warming and the internal variability of the climate system. Taking advan-
tage of the climate model large ensemble, we address two main points
regarding North Atlantic climate variability:
(1) Reorganisation of the North Atlantic mid-tropospheric circu-
lation under global warming:
Anthropogenic warming does not alter the number of atmospheric
regime states over the North Atlantic but reorganises their spatial char-
acteristics under external forcing. Thereorganisation arises from rearran-
gements in the centres of cyclonic and anticyclonic activity, reﬂecting the
inﬂuence of sustained greenhouse gas forcing. However, the internal
variability component shows a decline in the number of states after 1995.
This loss of a regime state in the Post-1995 period within the IV conﬁrms
that continued warming suppresses circulation patterns, thereby affecting
the natural variability of the climate system over the North Atlantic region.
Furthermore, we observe an increase in the frequency of extreme NAO-
negative events in the future, as also highlighted by Liu et al.
79.T r a c k i n g
these changes reveals clear shifts in both the frequency and spatial structure
of the regimes, offering new insights into how North Atlantic circulation
patterns evolve under global warming.
It is to be noted that we used the assumption of piecewise stationarity
for our clustering analysis through GMM. Weﬁnd that this assumption is
reasonably valid for the Pre-1995 period, where cluster proportions remain
stable over time (Fig. S12a). However,in the Post-1995 period, the clusters
exhibit changing proportions over ti me, highlighting the non-stationary
response of atmospheric circulationpatterns under anthropogenic warming
(Fig. S12b). Despite this non-stationary response, weﬁnd recurrent spatial
patterns over the two periods, even with the inclusion of external forcing,
indicating structural continuity. H ence, while we acknowledge that the
stationarity assumption is violated for the Post-1995 period, the spatial
patterns captured by the GMM are physi cally meaningful and consistent
with the Pre-1995 period. This supports the consistency of our analysis in
quantifying circulation changes across periods.
(2) Modulation of the NAO characteristics through frequency and
intensity changes in a future warming world:
With continued greenhouse gas emissions and projected warming, the

st

variability of the climate system due to external forcing

regime dynamics may have cascading impacts on agriculture, water man-

Data and methods
CESM2-large ensemble simulations
We use a hundred ensemble member simulations from CESM2-LE36 with a
nominal spatial resolution of 1° × 1° covering 1850 to 2100. All the hundred
ensemble members are fully ocean-atmosphere-land coupled and are forced
under CMIP6 historical and SSP3-7.0 future radiative forcing scenarios
conducted using the CESM2
85. In large ensemble frameworks, such as
CESM2-LE36, each ensemble member is subjected to identical external
forcing (e.g., volcanoes, anthropogenic emissions of greenhouse gases and
aerosols) but initialised with slightly perturbed initial conditions. These
perturbations lead to different realisations of internal variability, while the
externally forced signal remains common to all the ensemble members.
Averaging across the ensemble members effectively cancels out the internal
variability, allowing the ensemble mean to isolate the forced response
36,37,52,53.
Consequently, the deviations of individual ensemble members from the
ensemble mean represent the modelled internal climate variability
36,37,52,53.

Coupled singular value decomposition for full and internal
variability ﬂow ﬁelds
We perform a coupled SVD analysis86 between the monthly mean stream-
function at 500 hPa and surface temperature on the full (FF) and internal
variability (IV)ﬂow ﬁelds from 1850 to 2100 (months from December to
February only) over the North Atlantic region (0– 70°N and 98°W– 12°E).
We ﬁrst subtract the climatological mean for each month (December, Jan-
uary, and February) separately across the entire period, and the geophysical
variables are weighted according to latitude. We then deﬁne the IV as the
departure from the ensemble mean (forced response)
37, i.e., subtracting the
ensemble mean from the geophysical variable. In contrast, the FF is the raw
data, including the climate-driven trend, i.e., the combination of the internal
and forced responses. We perform the SVD analysis on the FF and IV by
combining the ensemble and time dimensions of the anomaly data. As such,
each ﬁeld is represented as a matrix with the number of rows given by the
number of spatial grid cells, and the number of columns by the number of
months times the number of ensemb le members. We then generate the
covariance matrix between streamfunction and surface temperature.

eigenvalue problem and generate eigenvectors for both the streamfunction
