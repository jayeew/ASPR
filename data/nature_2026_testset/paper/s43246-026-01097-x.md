Optical neuromorphic computing utilizes light-based neural networks for ef ﬁcient AI processing.
Conventional optoelectronic synapses are limited by single-dimensional perception and electrical
weight modulation. This study overcomes these constraints by developing all-photonic arti ﬁcial
synapses using water-mediated phase transitions in MAPbI ₃ perovskite. These synapses exhibit
reversible light-driven optical memory capabilities, achieving a broad transmittance modulation via
precisely controlled crystal deformation mechanisms. The high-stability synapses successfully
replicate neurobiological functions, including paired-pulse facilitation, short-term to long-term
memory transition, and humidity-dependent plasticity. Implemented within a recurrent neural network,
the synapses achieve 100% classi ﬁcation accuracy for multidimensional optical stimuli
encompassing power, duration, and environmental humidity parameters. Furthermore, integration
with a diffractive deep neural network enables recon ﬁgurable computing with 80 distinct
programmable transmittance states, achieving remarkable classi ﬁcation accuracies on the MNIST
handwritten digits and Fashion-MNIST datasets without hardware modi ﬁcations. This work
establishes a paradigm for developing intelligent systems that adapt to complex environmental
changes, demonstrating potential for applications in dynamic visual perception and multi-task
processing environments.
The rapid development of artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) technology has placed
higher demands on the energy efﬁciency, speed, and adaptability of computing
systems1–4. Conventional electronic computing architectures are constrained
by the von Neumann bottleneck—the physical separation between computing
units and memory, which leads to data transfer delays and high energy con-
sumption issues5,6.T h i sp o s e ss i g n iﬁcant challenges in handling complex real-
time tasks7–10. Optical neuromorphic computing, with its inherent advantages
of circuit-free, high-speed photon propagation, natural parallelism, and low
thermal dissipation, is considered a promising direction to overcome the
limitations of conventional electronic computing
11–13. Diffractive deep neural
networks (D2NNs) simulate the multi-layer structure of biological neural
systems and automatically extract complex features from data, demonstrating
performance advantages in image r ecognition, speech processing, and
decision-making14–16. Moreover, once the diffraction layers of a D 2NN are
designed, it becomes challenging to perform new tasks, signiﬁcantly impacting
the system’s adaptability andﬂexibility17.
As the core unit of neural networks, arti ﬁcial synapses mimic the
functions of biological synapses, en abling information transmission and
learning capabilities18–22.A r t iﬁcial optoelectronic synaptics have made
progress in simulating biological synaptic characteristics, such as paired-
pulse facilitation (PPF), short-term memory (STM), long-term memory
(LTM)
23–27, and learning experience28–31. However, the electrical nature of
synaptic weights, rather than the o ptical nature, restricts the recon ﬁgur-
ability of optical neuromorphic computing. Moreover, light inherently
possesses the ability to convey multi dimensional information from our
physical surroundings. Artiﬁcial optoelectronic synapses are limited by
single-dimensional signal perception18–22. These issues severely impede
applications in dynamic scenario p rocessing with high speeds and low
energy consumption. All-photonic synapses, which generate characteristic
and synaptic optical responses in response to light stimuli, eliminate elec-
trical crosstalk, enabling circuit-free AI tasks to be conducted with high
speed and low power consumption. The properties make them a key
research direction in multidimensional and reconﬁgurable optical neuro-
morphic computing
32.

Perovskite-based all-photonic synapses
In contrast to optoelectronic synapses, which encode synaptic weight via
photocurrent, all-photonic synapses di rectly utilize light-induced trans-
mittance modulation as the weight37. 365 nm illumination (291 mW, 30 s,
50% RH) on the all-photonic synapse results in a 17% increase in trans-
mittance, followed by a gradual decayupon pulse removal (Supplementary
Fig. 4). This non-contact optical modulation mimics the characteristics of
biological synaptic memory, offering advantages for neuromorphic vision
systems. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, the synaptic photoresponses are
wavelength-dependent under optical stimulation (380 mW, 60 s, 30% RH).
The transmittance changes are 66% at 600 nm and 40% at 450 nm. The
particularly pronounced modulation at 600 nm indicates an exceptionally
wide range of synaptic weight modul ation, a crucial feature for imple-
menting advanced neuromorphic optoelectronic systems. Following UV
illumination termination, the all-photonic synapses maintain their modiﬁed
transmittance values for extended du rations under low-humidity condi-
tions (30% RH). This persistent memory effect is attributed to signiﬁcantly
retarded hydration due to limited incorporation of water molecules,
representing an effective strategy for achieving non-volatile memory func-
tionality in optical synaptic devices.

Synaptic plasticity, the activity-dependent modiﬁcation of connection
strength among neurons, constitutes the fundamental mechanism under-

environmental humidity as a regulator of synaptic plasticity in all-photonic

recovery rate increases with hig her humidity levels. At 60% RH, the

thereby accelerating the recovery of tr ansmittance. Conversely, reduced

prolonged retention of the modi ﬁed state

observed in the recovery process canﬁrmly establish the analog character of
the all-photonic synapses (Supplementary Fig. 7). This tunability is essential
for emulating biological synaptic plasticity32.
The humidity-modulated plasticity of the all-photonic synapses is
systematically investigated through real-time grayscale image processing of
an “IPC” letter pattern (an abbreviation for the Institute of Photonic Chips)

extends retention signiﬁcantly longer. The results emulate biological visual
memory by integrating time-dependent image degradation and envir-

adaptive memory functions within a single unit.

Multidimensional optical neuromorphic computing
In the brain, neural networks connected by multiple synapses can perform a
variety of parallel tasks, in which synaptic weight updating plays a crucial
role in executing these tasks
42. A RNN is capable of identifying sequential
features within data and assists in predicting the next potential data point in
a given sequence
43,44. Leveraging the time-dependent characteristics of all-
photonic synapses, we employ a RNN to classify dynamic optical responses
under varying UV powers, illuminationdurations, and humidity values. A
schematic diagram illustrating the processing of multidimensional optical
neuromorphic computing is shown in Fig.4a.
The synaptic transmittance changes at 600 nm are measured under
different conditions: (1) UV power (214, 320, 383 mW, 30 s illumination
and 50% RH), (2) illumination duration (20, 40, and 60 s, 383 mW and 50%
RH), and (3) humidity (30%, 50%, and 60% RH, 383 mW and 30 s illu-
mination). For each parameter, 40 groups of transmittance change values
are used as the input to the RNN model. The RNN achieved 100% classi-
ﬁcation accuracy within 10 training epochs for the power recognition
(Fig. 4b), signiﬁcantly outperforming an artiﬁcial neural network (ANN)
that plateaued at 79% accuracy after 50 epochs. The ANN’s failure to achieve
perfect accuracy, despite the limited number of classes, underscores its
inherent limitations in modeling temporal dependencies within the synaptic
response, which are effectively captured by the RNN architecture. This
performance advantage stems from the RNN’s inherent ability to capture
temporal dependencies in events. The power recognition confusion
matrices of RNN and ANN are shown in Fig. 4c, d.
High accuracies are also observed for illumination time classiﬁcation,
in which the RNN demonstrates 100% recognition instantly (Fig. 4e),
outperforming the ANN. The power recognition confusion matrices of
RNN and ANN are shown in Fig. 4f, g. For humidity discrimination
(Fig. 4h–j), the RNN achieves 100% accuracy after just 19 epochs,
demonstrating its exceptional capabi lity in processing environmentally
modulated synaptic responses. Supplementary Figs. 8a–c demonstrate the
output intensity distribution using an RNN. The results establish that all-
photonic synapses effectively extract and classify multidimensional
dynamic features (power, duration, and humidity), overcoming the one-
dimensional limitation of conventional optical neuromorphic computing.
We have systematically evaluated the impact of additive noise on RNN
classiﬁcation performance across all three recognition tasks (Supplementary
Fig. 9). The network demonstrate exce ptional robustness, maintaining
classiﬁcation accuracy above 95% even under noise conditions ( σ =0 . 5 ) .
This performance can be attributed to the RNN ’s inherent capability to
extract temporal features from the entire response trajectory rather than
relying on speciﬁc time-point measurements.
The ultimate performances of optoelectronic synapses are governed by
the recognition resolution. To characterize high-resolution multi-
dimensional neuromorphic computing, an ultrashort optical pulse (0.2 s
duration) is applied. It induces a marginal change in transmittance of 0.36%
(Supplementary Fig. 10), con ﬁrming its exceptional photosensitivity.
Classiﬁcation of these time-resolved transmittance responses reveals that
the RNN performance is superior. It achieved perfect classiﬁcation accuracy
(100%) within 2 epochs, outperforming ANN’s 94% accuracy after 4 epochs
(Supplementary Fig. 11). Corresponding confusion matrices and output
intensity distributions are shown in Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13,
respectively.
Notably, the synaptic maintains fu nctionality at ultralow operating
power of 46 mW, exhibiting 0.21% tra nsmittance modulation (Supple-
mentary Fig. 14). The RNN also demonstrates superior recognition per-
formance over the ANN (Supplementary Fig. 15). The 50th-epoch
confusion matrices for 46 mW recognition are presented in Supplemen-
tary Fig. 16, and Supplementary Fig. 17 shows the RNN-processed low-
power output intensity distribution . The multidimensional recognition,
involving ultralow-power operation and ultrafast processing, establishes the
technology’sv i a b i l i t yf o re n e r g y - e fﬁcient and high-speed optical neuro-
morphic computing. It effectively circumvents the fundamental limitations
of conventional electronic architectures.

Fig. 21) further characterize the system’s recognition behavior under various
weight states.

Conclusions
All-photonic artiﬁcial synapses are prepared based on water molecule-
mediated phase transitions in dihyd rated MAPbI perovskite, achieving
reversible optical transmittance memory. A 66% transmittance modulation
is realized through crystal structure d eformation. Typical synaptic beha-
viors, including PPF and STM/LTM transition, are successfully mimicked
via non-contact optical transmittance measurement. Leveraging a RNN
architecture, we achieve multidimensional optical recognition of light
power, duration, and humidity with 100% classiﬁcation accuracy. Integra-
tion with diffactive neurons in a D
2NN demonstrates reconﬁgurable optical
neuromorphic computing, supporting 80 programmable weight states of
transmittance values, and achieves recognition accuracies of 87% (MNIST)
and 76% (Fashion-MNIST) without requiring hardware modi ﬁcations.
These advances establish an innovati ve platform for adaptive photonic
intelligence, with applications in environmental sensing, edge computing,
and optical neural networks.

Characterization methods
XRD patterns of the samples were characterized using a D8-ADVANCE
(BrukerAXS, Germany). FTIR spectra were acquired using a Nicolet 6700
(Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc, USA). The surface morphology and shape of the
sample were photographed using a ﬁeld-emission scanning electron
microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Gemini 300) operating at 15 kV. The surface
roughness of theﬁlms was analyzed using a CSPM5500 AFM. Absorption
spectra were calculated using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer
(Shimazu UV-2600i/2700i) in the wavelength range of 300 nm to 800 nm.
Real-time transmittance measurement was conducted using a self-
constructed testing system. This system comprises a 365 nm LED light
source to trigger transmittance changes, and the transmittance was mea-
sured using a high-speed CMOS spectrometer (model: Avaspec-
ULS2048CL-EVO).
Neuromorphic computing
These time-dependent transmittance responses (120 sets of data for each
parameter) were employed as input fortraining in a three-layer RNN model.
The network architecture comprises an input layer with 20 neurons, two
recurrent layers containing 20 and 30 neurons, respectively, and aﬁnal fully
connected layer with three softmax-activated output neurons. Training was
performed by minimizing categorical cross-entropy loss using mini-batch
gradient descent with a batch size of 10, with Adam optimization at a
learning rate of 0.003. During each training run, the dataset was randomly
shufﬂed and split into training and test sets at an 80-20 ratio. For com-
parison, an ANN model was also constructed, featuring a 20-neuron input
layer, two hidden layers with 20 and 30 neurons, and a three-neuron output
layer. All other training parameters were kept consistent with those used in
the RNN model. To assess system resilience to physical variability and noise,
we retrained and tested the RNN using transmittance curves perturbed by
Gaussian noise (σ = 0.1, 0.5, and 0.8). The D
2NN was trained using a dataset
comprising 60,000 images from bo th the MNIST and Fashion-MNIST
datasets. Model performance was evaluated on a separate test set of 10,000
images, with classi ﬁcation accuracy measured across 10 categories of
handwritten digits and fashion items. All input images were set at a wave-
length of 633 nm. The network architecture, implemented as an amplitude-
type diffractive neural network based on Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction,
consists of an input layer, a single diffraction layer, and an output layer. The
output layer is connected to 10 photodetectors, each corresponding to one
class. During training, backpropagation was performed using the Adam
optimizer with a learningrate of 0.0001 and a batch size of 10. The D²NN’s
robustness to input noise and physicalvariability was assessed using input
images corrupted by Gaussian noise (0.1, 0.5, and 0.8).
