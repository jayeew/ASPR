The phosphorus (P) cycle links the co-evolution of the biosphere and geo-
sphere over geologic time. Modern P availability is primarily controlled by
mineral adsorption, but how such processes might have operated under early
Earth’s anoxic conditions remains unclear. Here, we combine experimental
and theoretical investigations of P adsorption onto common phyllosilicates to
evaluate their role in the early P cycle. We show that the P adsorption would
have been signiﬁcantly enhanced in early ferruginous waters, primarily
through dissolved Fe(II) bridging between orthophosphate and mineral sur-
faces. Such enhanced P adsorption onto phyllosilicates could have facilitated
the riverine transport of bioavailable P during Archean anoxic continental
weathering, yet also promoted its rapid burial in shallow marine settings.
Moreover, phyllosilicate adsorption would have limited dissolved P release
during seaﬂoor weathering in the Archean and Proterozoic oceans. These
processes collectively could have limited dissolved P availability for the origin
and evolution of early life.
Phosphorus (P) is critical for life, occurring in the cellular membranes
and nucleic acids of all known organisms 1. Moreover, P is widely
thought to be a bio-limiting nutrient for marine ecosystems, con-
trolling primary productivity on geological timescales 2. Deciphering
oceanic P bioavailability and associated cycling pathways across
Earth’s history is thus critical for understanding the co-evolution of
the biosphere and geosphere. On the modern Earth, continental
weathering releases P from rocks and supplies it to aquatic systems
3.
Although detrital P, predominantly as apatite, is the largest compo-
nent of total P in riverine transport
3,4, this proportion is not available
for direct biological uptake. Instead, mineral-bound P, including that
bound by Fe(III)/Al-(hydr)oxides and clay minerals, accounts for the
largest proportion of potentially reactive/bioavailable P during
modern riverine transport
4,5. After arrival in aquatic systems,
microbes can readily utilize the bioavailable P to build biomass. The
organic P could be ef ﬁciently recycled for further biological utiliza-

including authigenic apatite, Fe(III)-bound P, and REE/Al-
phosphates
4,6. Moreover, some loosely bound P or organic P
experiences transformation into more stable mineral phases during
diagenesis
6,7. Overall, the interaction of dissolved P with minerals

The Precambrian Earth (before ca. 541 Ma) had vastly different
surface environments, resulting in different P cycling behaviors and
availability in early ecosystems8–12. For example, beneath the anoxic
and acidic atmosphere of the Archean Earth, oxidative weathering of
Fe(II) would have been nearly absent, and consequently, Fe(III)-bound
P is proposed to have been only a minor component of riverine
transport
13. Instead, recent studies emphasize the important roles of
clay minerals in ancient P cycles. For example, Hao et al 14. proposed
that kaolinite could substantially adsorb dissolved orthophosphate
during acidic continental weathering, later releasing the P in cir-
cumneutral seawater and, therefore, shuttling P to late Archean marine
ecosystems. Similarly, Fru et al
15. viewed clay minerals as an important
carrier of bioavailable P from the land to the ocean margin during the
melting of Sturtian Snowball ice sheets. Moreover, although alteration
of seaﬂoor basalts is commonly held to be a major P sink in the oxi-
dized modern ocean16, it has been proposed to be a potential source
for dissolved P due to the lack of oxidation of Fe(II) in the early anoxic
ocean ﬂoor
17. That said, a more recent study identiﬁed Al-P as the major
burial phase in mid-Proterozoic sediments and proposed (co-) pre-
cipitation of P with phyllosilicates and later diagenetic transformation
into aluminous phosphates as a major sink in marginal areas of Pro-
terozoic oceans
18, similar to the modern 19. This large controversy
regarding the actual roles of phyllosilicates in the early P cycle may lie
in the still nascent understanding of P adsorption onto phyllosilicates
under distinct early Earth conditions.
In nature, the interaction of dissolved P and mineral surfaces is
sensitive to not only pH, which was primarily emphasized in previous
studies
14,15, but also other water chemistry parameters, particularly
salinity and cation/anion composition. Compared with modern sea-
water, the early ocean had vastly different salt compositions, including
higher levels of Ca
2+ relative to Mg 2+20,a sw e l la sm o d e r a t el e v e l so f
Fe2+21 and silica22, but little SO 4
23. Previous experiments have shown
that high levels of dissolved silica in the Precambrian seawater sig-
niﬁcantly weakened P adsorption onto iron oxides
24, while the pre-
sence of divalent metals (e.g., Ca2+ and Mg2+) could relieve its inhibiting
effect20. Early water chemistry might also have affected the adsorption
of P onto phyllosilicates, particularly the potentially large effect of high
dissolved Fe(II) on the adsorption of orthophosphate groups
25,t h u s
helping to solve the above-mentioned controversy.
With this in mind, we performed batch adsorption experiments
of dissolved orthophosphate (the predominantly dissolved phase in
seawater)
26 onto common phyllosilicate products during con-
tinental and sea ﬂoor weathering in simulated early river water and
seawater solutions under anoxic conditions (see “Methods”, Sup-
plementary Information Sections S1, 2, Tables S1 –3). Speciﬁcally, we
examined orthophosphate adsorption onto aluminous phyllosili-
cates represented by kaolinite and montmorillonite under our
simulated river water and seawater conditions to clarify the roles of
detrital clays in transporting P during continental weathering on the
early Earth. Moreover, we conducted adsorption experiments on
ferromagnesian phyllosilicates represented by lizardite and non-
tronite under the simulated Fe(II)-rich seawater conditions to
explore the behavior of P during early sea ﬂoor weathering. Apart
from pH and alkaline earth cations, we particularly examined the
effects of dissolved Fe(II) and dissolved silica on P adsorption onto
phyllosilicates. After the adsorption experiment, the minerals were
separated for microscopic mineralogical analyses to monitor any
possible precipitation of secondary phases (see “Methods” and
Section S3). Complemented by theoretical simulations and geolo-
gical observations (see “Methods” and Sections S4, 5), we have
systematically assessed the roles of phyllosilicates in the early P
cycle, enabling us to articulate its implications for the co-evolution
of biosphere and geosphere.

Effects of dissolved Fe(II) and silica on phosphate adsorption
onto phyllosilicates
Under the anoxic conditions of the Archean Earth, dissolved Fe(II)
could have accumulated to substantial levels in surface waters (e.g., ~
0.1 mM and ~ 0.1–1.6 mM in river water and seawater, respectively), and
the same would apply to Proterozoic deep seawater (see Table S2 for a
compilation of relevant reports). As shown above, the addition of Fe
2+

representative aluminous and ferromagnesian phyllosilicates, com-

reached a plateau (Figs. 3, S3). Despite its relatively lower concentra-
tion, the enhancement effect of dissolved Fe(II) is overall more pro-
nounced than those of Ca

concentration (Fig. 3a). Even at 20 μMF e 2+, the enhancement effect

The control mechanism of dissolved Fe(II) and silica on phos-
phate adsorption onto phyllosilicates
The enhancement of P adsorption onto phyllosilicates by divalent
cations is mainly attributed to a previously proposed mechanism
called the cation bridging effect (e.g. 37, i.e., metal cations act as a
mediator between the mineral surface and adsorbent. In principle, 1:1
phyllosilicates (including kaolinite and serpentine minerals) possess a
near-zero permanent charge, but surﬁcial O atoms usually hydrolyze
into hydroxyl groups on the basal ande d g es u r f a c e si ns o l u t i o n .T h e s e
hydrolyzed surfaces hold net negative charges at pHs higher than their
point of zero charge (~ 3.5 for kaolinite and ~ 4.3 for lizardite,
respectively)
38,39. Unlike kaolinite and serpentine groups, the smectite
group (including montmorillonite and nontronite) has a negative
permanent charge per se on the basal surface due to isomorphic
substitutions
40. According to the principle of electrostatic interaction,
the negatively charged mineral surfaces should adsorb little, if any,
negatively charged orthophosphate, and adsorption should weaken at
elevated pHs. This deduction aligns well with the results of our NaCl-
only experiments (Figs. 3, S3). In this system, the addition of divalent
cations (e.g., Ca
2+,M g2+,a n dF e2+) might induce the formation of metal-
phosphate complexes (Table S7 for complexation constants) and
phosphate-bridged complexes on mineral surfaces, favoring the
overall adsorption.

lizardite and greenalite might represent the common mineral products

Delivery and burial of P facilitated by terrigenous clays during
early continental weathering
In the late Archean, the large-scale emergence of land initiated
unprecedented levels of continental weathering globally 46,47.U n d e r
such circumstances, global-scale formation and transport of clay
minerals, including kaolinite and montmorillonite, would be expected
(ref. 14. and references therein), consistent with the signi ﬁcant
increase in the preserved siliciclastic rocks in the late Archean (Fig.5d).
Meanwhile, high pCO
2,g and possibly temperate climate promoted
chemical weathering, even reaching modern ﬂuxes for some
elements13. Here, our results suggest that the presence of dissolved
Fe(II) in Archean river water would have promoted the adsorption of
dissolved P onto common detrital phyllosilicate minerals (Figs. 1, 3),
strengthening the earlier proposal that clay-bound P could have been
the major form of bioavailable/reactive P during riverine transport in
t h el a t eA r c h e a n
14. It is notable that detrital P, predominantly as apatite,
might still have been the largest component of total P in early riverine
transport
13, much as in the modern, but this proportion is not
bioavailable.
In the previous proposition, the clay-bound P, once transported to
the ocean, might be released back to the seawater triggered by the
elevation of pH in estuaries
14,15. On the contrary, our data indicate that
common dissolved divalent cations in early seawater, particularly Fe(II)
(Fig. 5a), can enhance P adsorption onto detrital clay minerals (Figs.1,
3a). Following the rapid deposition of most terrigenous clays in estu-
aries and on continental shelves (> 90%; ref. 48), this clay-bound P
would concurrently get buried directly without substantial release, but
with the potential to adsorb more dissolved P from coastal seawater or
porewater, facilitated by high dissolved Fe(II). This additional sinking
pathway may help explain the absence of a correlation between P and
Fe contents in Archean shales (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Dataset 1).
To assess the global importance of riverine clays in facilitating P
burial in the early ferruginous ocean, we conducted Monte Carlo
simulations by considering key environmental factors affecting clay
production and orthophosphate adsorption, including land fraction,
lack of vegetation, clay size, mineralogy mix, and adsorption capacity
(see “Methods”). Due to a lack of solid geological evidence, we com-
piled available constraints on these parameters and considered a wide
range of possibilities (Table S8). Our results suggest a rapid increase in
phyllosilicate-aided P burial ﬂux following the global emergence of
land in the late Archean (Fig. 6a). By the end of the Archean, up to
0.1 Tmol P/yr could be buried by clay adsorption in the coastal areas.
The estimated ﬂuxes are comparable with the previously reported
riverine transportﬂuxes of total reactive P (0.04–0.34 Tmol/yr) in the
late Archean
13, indicating that adsorption onto phyllosilicates was one
of the major P sinks in the late Archean P cycle. The result is reasonably
expected because clay-bound P should be the major P form during the
early riverine transport, and terrigenous clays could adsorb more P in
the Fe(II)-rich early seawater. However, there are still many unknowns
and large uncertainties about the late Archean weathering conditions,
particularly clay properties, which are required for a more accurate
estimation of the sinkingﬂux as clay-bound P as well as the steady-state
concentration of dissolved P in the coastal seawater. After the GOE

phyllosilicate properties, including surface area, adsorption capacity,

losilicates generated by early sea ﬂoor weathering should act as an

However, a previous study reported high P/Ca ratios in Archean
shallow marine carbonates, which was interpreted as the signal for
high dissolved P in Archean seawater
57. The discrepancy may lie in the
sampling bias, as another recent study showed different results 55.
Moreover, carbonate-associated phosphate (CAP) is experimentally
shown to be sensitive to multiple environmental factors, including but
not limited to dissolved orthophosphate, pH, alkalinity, and
mineralogy
58. During diagenesis, the carbonate record is susceptible to
dissolution and reprecipitation, neomorphism, or dolomitization
(cf
59). In this case, the CAP may largely reﬂect orthophosphate level in
porewater, which is usually much higher than dissolved inorganic P
levels in the water column60. Indeed, latest analyses of modern marine
sediments suggest CAP as a proxy for porewater alkalinity and phos-
phate level during carbonate diagenesis instead of seawater phosphate
level
61.
Apart from phyllosilicates, other sea ﬂoor weathering mineral
products, such as carbonate minerals, might limit P release during
seaﬂoor weathering via ef ﬁcient adsorption and possible co-
precipitation of dissolved orthophosphate 62. Moreover, the reverse
weathering was proposed as one major process in regulating the Pre-
cambrian biogeochemistry and could generate additional
phyllosilicates
63. A more quantitative investigation of P sink will need
to consider multiple poorly constrained sea ﬂoor settings in the
Archean, including the relative yields of P and phyllosilicates/carbo-
nates, the characteristics of the mineral products
52, and the chemical
environments of deep seawater and porewater. After the deposition,
this clay/carbonate-bound P would tend to undergo burial diagenesis
and transform into other more stable P-bearing minerals, such as
apatite
7 or Al-phosphate, as observed in the ancient marine sedimen-
tary rocks18,19.
Evolving P cycle and its roles in surface oxygenation on the
early Earth
Our results reveal previously unrecognized but potentially critical
roles of phyllosilicates in the early P cycle (Fig. 7). This has several
important implications for the availability of P for early life in the
oceans. For example, in the early Archean, the continents were largely
submerged
64–66 with minimal continental weathering and riverine
delivery of reactive P and other nutrients13,67. At the same time, seaﬂoor
weathering might have been substantial due to high rates of sea ﬂoor
spreading and CO 2 outgassing68. Consequently, atmospheric pCO 2,g
would have been high, and the seawater pH likely proposed to be
circumneutral or weakly acidic (Fig. 5b), potentially favoring the
release of reactive P from sea ﬂoor weathering
17. However, cir-
cumneutral pH would also favor the solubility of Fe(II) 53,g r e a t l y
enhancing the adsorptive loss of P onto phyllosilicates formed during
seaﬂoor weathering. Moreover, there were possibly other P sinks in the
ocean, such as the adsorption by Fe(III)-hydroxides
20,56 and
carbonates62 or the precipitation of vivianite 69,70.A m o n gt h e s es i n k -
ings, phyllosilicate adsorption might play a critical role, particularly in
early Archean oceans, where the deposition of Fe(III)-hydroxides was
likely limited (Fig.5e)70. Unlike Fe(III)-bound P, the clay-bound P is not
directly mobilized by redox shifts. Instead, it likely transforms into
more refractory authigenic P phases during early diagenesis (e.g
71),
thereby effectively limiting its immediate recycling and bioavailability.

It is notable that these sinks are not mutually exclusive, and the relative
importance would follow the principle of the minimum, i.e., the
strongest sink determines actual P availability. Overall, given the sup-
pressed sources but enhanced sinks, the early Archean ocean might
have had low availability of dissolved P for nascent life, consistent with
available geological records (Fig.5e, f).
Multiple lines of evidence jointly suggest the global-scale
emergence of continents and operation of continental weathering
during the middle to late Archean (~ 3.2 –2.5 Ga)
46,47,60,64,65,72, which
released increasing amounts of reactive P 13 and other bio-essential
nutrients (Fig. 5d). Dissolved Fe(II) could have enhanced orthopho-
sphate adsorption onto phyllosilicates in the late Archean river
water, therefore promoting the delivery of reactive P as clay-bound
form (Fig. 6a). In the ferruginous ocean, burial of dissolved ortho-
phosphate was facilitated by the adsorption onto phyllosilicate
minerals delivered by river or generated by sea ﬂoor weathering.
Indeed, geochemical analyses of Archean shales show generally
lower P but higher Fe contents than those of Phanerozoic samples
(Fig. 5c), which was proposed to re ﬂect enhanced phosphorus
scavenging in anoxic and iron-rich oceans
11.T h i se fﬁcient burial of P
by phyllosilicates, complemented by other P sinks discussed above,
might result in limited availability of dissolved orthophosphate,
which could explain the time delay between the origin of oxygenic
photosynthesis and the GOE (Fig. 5f)
9–12,26. However, recent studies
revealed the presence of phosphite and other reduced P species in
the late Archean ocean
73,74, which could serve as an additional P
source to facilitate the eventual oxygenation of the surface by the
end of the Archean.
After the GOE, oxidative weathering of Fe(II)-bearing minerals
initiated pervasively, and Fe(III)-(hydr)oxides started to dominate the
riverine transport of bioavailable P. However, Proterozoic oceans were
still generally ferruginous below the surﬁcial mixed layer, which would
favor either the direct deposition of dissolved orthophosphate as clay-
bound phases or later reductive transformation of Fe(III)-bound P into
redox-insensitive clay-bound phases. Later burial diagenesis and
metamorphism would be expected to transform this clay-bound P into
more stable Al-P minerals, recently observed as major phases of P in
Proterozoic sedimentary rocks deposited in shallow marine environ-
ments (ref. 18 and references therein). Phyllosilicate-facilitated burial
of P might, thus, contribute to the persistence of a low-O
2 atmosphere
through most of the Proterozoic Eon, until the Phanerozoic oxygena-
tion of the entire ocean established the modern biogeochemical
cycle of P.
In a totally oxic world like the modern, oxidative precipitation
of Fe could ef ﬁciently adsorb dissolved orthophosphate, acting as
one major sinking pathway in the ocean P cycle
6,16. Under these
circumstances, clay adsorption would play a minor role in sinking P
due to the absence of Fe(II)-bridging effect. However, adsorption by
phyllosilicates still plays an essential role in mediating surface P
cycling. For example, clay particles serve as one major carrier of
particulate P in terrestrial and freshwater environments such as
soils, rivers, and lakes
4, due to the high sorption af ﬁnity of surface
Al–OH sites towards dissolved phosphate. This remarkable capacity
is now leveraged in geo-engineering applications, where speci ﬁcally
modiﬁed clays are deployed to mitigate eutrophication in eutrophic
lakes, offering a redox-insensitive P-binding alternative that super-
sedes traditional metal salts like alum and ferric salts (e.g.)
75. The
subsequent ﬂuvial transport of this clay-bound P delivers a major
ﬂux to estuaries, where the increased salinity and pH trigger sig-
niﬁcant desorption 5,13, amplifying the riverine P ﬂux to the coastal
ocean5,14. The ultimate fate of clay-associated P in marine settings is
mainly governed by redox conditions. In oxic continental margin
sediments, the iron oxide or aluminum (hydr)oxide coatings on
clays continue to facilitate P retention. Upon the onset of sediment
anoxia, the reductive dissolution of reactive iron phases leads to the
release of P into porewaters, which can be later released back to the
water column
76 or transform into clay-bound phases 19. During burial
diagenesis or metamorphism, clay-bound P could be further trans-
formed into more stable and crystalline phosphate minerals (cf)
71.
Overall, this intricate interplay, from riverine transport to marine
burial via either adsorption or precipitation pathways, enables
phyllosilicates as an important control on P bioavailability and
geological sequestration in the Earth system throughout geological
history.
