Active avoidance responses (ARs) are instrumental behaviors that prevent
harm. Adaptive ARs may contribute to active coping, whereas maladaptive
avoidance habits are implicated in anxiety and obsessive-compulsive dis-
orders. The AR learning mechanism has remained elusive, as successful
avoidance trials produce no obvious reinforcer. We used a novel outcome-
devaluation procedure in rats to show that ARs are positively reinforced by
response-produced feedback cues thatdevelop into safety signals during
training. Males were sensitive to feedbackdevaluation after moderate training,
but not overtraining, consistent with a transition from goal-directed to habi-
tual avoidance. Using chemogeneticsand feedback devaluation, we also show
that goal-directed vs. habitual ARs depend on dorsomedial vs. dorsolateral
striatum, suggesting a signiﬁcant overlap between the mechanisms of
avoidance and rewarded instrumental behavior. Females were insensitive to
feedbackdevaluation due to a remarkable context-dependence of counter-
conditioning. However, degrading the contingency between avoidance and
feedback suggests that both sexes rely on safety signals to perform goal-
directed ARs.
Active avoidance is a form of response learning that allows subjects to
suppress fear and neutralize threats. Avoidance likely evolved to
expand the defensive behavior repertoire beyond a narrow set of
innate, in ﬂexible reactions (i.e., ﬁght, ﬂight, freezing). Avoidance
response (AR) learning may even underlie complex defensive beha-
viors that could not have evolved under predatory pressure. For
instance, commuters may buckle their seatbelt to protect against
future injury or slam their brakes to prevent imminent harm. Clinically,
active avoidance is important because adaptive ARs likely contribute
to active coping and resilience
1. Conversely, inappropriate or persis-
tent avoidance habits are thought to protect irrational fears from
extinction
2 and contribute to disorders of control like OCD3.
Active avoidance is typically studied by presenting subjects with a
warning stimulus (WS; e.g., sound) that predicts the delivery of an
aversive electric shock. This transforms the WS into a conditioned
threat that arouses fear and triggers defensive reactions like freezing.
Subjects eventually emit the AR (e.g., barpress) and gain exposure to
avoidance contingencies. As training continues, innate fear reactions
to the WS are progressively replaced by ARs.
Theoretical disputes about the reinforcement mechanism and
misconceptions about the mediating role of fear stalled progress in
understanding the associative structures and neurobiological
mechanisms of active avoidance
4,5. Prominent instrumental theories
focused on stimulus-response learning via negative reinforcement (i.e.,

on modern conceptions of instrumental behavior developed using
appetitive reinforcers
8,9. This work moved beyond the notion that
instrumental training produces only re ﬂexive stimulus-response
habits, demonstrating that subjects can also perform more cognitive,
goal-directed responses that depend on knowledge of action-outcome
associations and motivation to obtain valued outcomes. Goal-directed
responding is typically diagnosed with outcome-devaluation or con-
tingency degradation tests
8,9. Thus, barpressing for food is goal-
directed if reducing the food ’s value via satiation or counter-
conditioning reduces barpressing. The same can be said if food
delivered non-contingently (independent of behavior) reduces bar-
pressing. Habits are usually inferred by insensitivity to these tests.
However, the situation is more complicated for active avoidance, as
there is little agreement about what reinforces avoidance. If the
removal or absence of aversive stimuli contributes (i.e., warning sti-
mulus termination or shock omission), it is unclear how one could
veriﬁably devalue these outcomes or deliver more of them. Identifying
a precise reinforcement mechanism would establish that active
avoidance is instrumental and help differentiate between goal-
directed vs. habitual ARs.
A less studied hypothesis is that ARs are positively reinforced
by response-produced safety signals that are generated via their
inverse relationship with the warning stimulus and shock during
avoidance training
7,10– 12. Explicit feedback cues that immediately
follow ARs are known to develop conditioned inhibition (safety)
and reinforcing properties that promote avoidance 10,13– 16. Here,
“reinforcing ” is used in a general way, though many early studies
assumed strengthening of stimulus-response associations. It is
unknown, however, whether safety signals behave as valued out-
comes supporting goal-directed avoidance via action-outcome
associations, akin to food in a barpressing experiment. To our
knowledge, no active avoidance studies have attempted to devalue
response-produced safety signals. To examine this, we designed a
devaluation protocol where AR feedback cues are counter-
conditioned by pairing with shock. We also present data using a
contingency degradation test, where non-contingent safety signals
are presented during avoidance.
If ARs are positively reinforced by safety, this raises the exciting
possibility that active avoidance relies on instrumental brain circuits
already identiﬁed by appetitive studies. Mo derately trained instru-
mental responses are sensitive to outcome devaluation and sup-
pressed by manipulations impairing neural activity in posterior
dorsomedial striatum (pDMS)
17,18. In contrast, instrumental responses
overtrained or trained on interval schedules are insensitive to outcome
devaluation and suppressed by manipulations impairing dorsolateral
striatum (DLS)
18,19. Importantly, however, this latter effect is only evi-
dent in subjects that also received outcome devaluation or con-
tingency degradation. This is because goal-directed responding
mediated by a parallel pDMS circuit regains control when DLS-
mediated habits are impaired
19. Thus, if goal-directed and habitual
avoidance memories co-exist in different brain circuits, a tool like
outcome devaluation may be necessary to identify and study these
circuits. Impairing either circuit alone may produce no change in overt
avoidance behavior.
Here, we show that counterconditioning of response-produced
safety signals impairs moderately trained, but not overtrained, ARs in
male rats. We also demonstrate that moderately trained ARs depend
on pDMS, and overtrained ARs depend on DLS, as in appetitive studies.
Further, we found that ARs in female rats are insensitive to feedback
devaluation due to a remarkable context dependence of counter-
conditioning. Nonetheless, we show contingency degradation data
suggesting that both sexes rely on safety signals for goal-directed
avoidance.

Devaluation of response-produced feedback impairs moder-
ately trained avoidance in male rats
To test whether goal-directed avoidance depends on feedback (FB)
safety signals, male rats received moderate shuttlebox training fol-
lowed by paired or explicitly unpaired counterconditioning of the
feedback tone in separate chambers (Fig. 2a). The paired procedure
was used to reverse safety properties of the feedback tone in FB-
Devalued rats. The unpaired procedure controlled for stimulus expo-
sure during counterconditioning but preserved the safety value of the
tone in FB-Valued rats. A FB-Control group was treated identically to
the FB-Devalued group, except the feedback stimulus used during
avoidance training differed (5 s house light off). This allowed us to
assess retardation of acquisition
23 for FB-Devalued rats during coun-
terconditioning compared to FB-Control rats that also had avoidance
training, but the tone was novel. It also allowed us to assess whether
any avoidance impairment induced by counterconditioning was spe-
ciﬁc to the feedback stimulus used during avoidance training. After
counterconditioning, rats were returned to the shuttleboxes for two
avoidance tests (15 presentations of the WS). Shuttling did not affect
WS duration, and no feedback or shocks were presented.
Rats in all moderately trained groups displayed equally high levels
of avoidance responding (ANOVA, F

acquired more rapidly than habitual associations 18,w et e s t e dt h e

Although negative reinforcement theories dominated during the
heyday of active avoidance research, the idea that safety signals may
positively reinforce avoidance was introduced as early as 1936 by
Konorski and Miller. This was echoed by Soltysik, who called avoidance
feedback a “reward” that “stabilizes avoidance behavior and makes it
virtually independent of the noxious [unconditioned stimulus] ”
12.
Studies since then are consistent with the notion that response-
produced safety signals improve or“reinforce” avoidance but were not
designed to implicate goal-directed behavior10,15,35,51–53.O u rﬁnding that
devaluation impairs moderately trained avoidance during extinction
tests (no shock or feedback) is a direct demonstration that respon-
se→feedback associations can control avoidance. Initial work
attempting to distinguish between avoidance actions and habits
focused on devaluing aversive unconditioned stimuli (USs; e.g.,
shocks)
3,54–57. However, none show that avoidance decrements occur
from the beginning of extinction tests, when responding is most
clearly linked to a memory of the outcome value
24. Also, the amount of
training typically did not vary, and no studies have shown a loss of
devaluation effects with overtraining. Although these data suggest that
US value contributes to avoidance, it is possible that devaluing an
aversive US affects avoidance indirectly by reducing the value of safety
cues that predict its absence. Indeed, some have described the con-
ditioned inhibition that deﬁnes safety signals as a “slave process” to
aversive excitation
58. If true, US devaluation will necessarily devalue
safety signals produced by the avoidance response.
We directly tested the safety signal hypothesis of goal-directed
avoidance by creating a novel outcome-devaluation procedure. The
near-complete collapse of avoidance after devaluation suggests that
response-produced safety signals are the primary outcome supporting
avoidance after moderate training. These results help to resolve pro-
blems that slowed progress in understanding active avoidance. The
ﬁnding that safety signals reinforce avoidance may explain how ter-
mination of the WS and omission of shock contribute to avoidance
behavior
11. These events are inversely related to feedback cues and
likely both support Pavlovian safety learning . They also help explain
the extreme resistance to extinction sometimes observed after
avoidance training
59. Discontinuing shock does nothing to undermine
goal-directed (response → feedback) or habitual (WS → response)
associations that should maintain responding. Our results are also
consistent with many studies observing that measures of fear are
poorly correlated with avoidance
4,6. As avoidance is successfully
acquired, the meaning of the WS likely changes from a threat of
imminent harm to a contextualized threat that is easily evaded4,60.T h i s
should induce a shift from a central state of fear to one of anxiety,
where harm is less likely and volitional action is possible
61,62. Since fear
and anxiety are distinct defensive brain states63, innate fear reactions
like freezing are suppressed or remain untriggered during anxiety
states that support avoidance. However, if an effective AR is blocked or
otherwise impaired, the fear state returns along with in ﬂexible reac-
tions like freezing
64 (see also Figs. 2, 7,a n d8).
Although avoidance behavior was similar in males and females, we
found a signiﬁcant sex difference in our devaluation studies. Females
showed no avoidance decrement when the feedback tone was coun-
terconditioned after moderate training. This was not due to a failure of
safety conditioning or counterconditioning. Instead, females dis-
played a remarkable degree of context dependence in their expression
of counterconditioning that was not observed in males. We cannotﬁnd
any previous reports of this sex difference in the literature. However,
studies on a different form of coun terconditioning were conducted
primarily in females and the expression of counterconditioning was
context-dependent
65.A sap r a c t i c a lm a t t e r ,o u rr e s u l ts u g g e s t st h a t
feedback devaluation may not help distinguish goal-directed and
habitual ARs in female rats.
In our ﬁnal experiment, we used a contingency degradation pro-
tocol after unsignaled avoidance training to con ﬁrm our behavioral
ﬁndings in males and determine if females also rely on safety signals
for goal-directed avoidance. After moderate training, males adjusted
their avoidance rates downward when free (non-contingent) safety
signals were presented during additional training. This was replicated
in females by different experimenters at different institutions, sug-
gesting the effect is robust. Thus, contingency degradation may be
especially useful for studying neural circuits of goal-directed vs.
habitual avoidance in females. However, more work is needed to
determine if delivering free safety signals loses effectiveness with
overtraining, and if this form of contingency degradation can distin-
guish between brain circuits mediating goal-directed vs. habitual
avoidance.
Our results provide evidence for positive reinforcement in
avoidance, but we acknowledge several limitations. First, “positive
reinforcement” is an umbrella term that has been used to describe
response-produced outcomes that promote both goal-directed and
habitual instrumental behavior. We implicate safety signals in goal-
directed avoidance, but it remains unclear if these also provide the
teaching signal that stamps in stimulus-response associations under-
lying habitual avoidance. One recent study suggests they do
16.W ea l s o
cannot rule out additional roles for WS-termination and/or US-
omission in the negative reinforcement of ARs, especially early in
training before feedback cues develop inhibitory properties. However,
conditioned inhibition likely develops faster than is currently appre-
ciated (see Fig. S1 in ref. 66), and we suspect that ARs become safety-
directed within a few trials. Also, for safety signals to be considered
true positive reinforcers, they should support new response learning in
the absence of shocks. One study failed toﬁnd evidence for this
67,b u t
more work is needed to determine if this is generally true or if the task
needs optimization. Studies in rodents and humans show that
response-produced safety signals during avoidance induce dopami-
nergic prediction error signals and pleasant feelings like other positive
reinforcers
45,47,68,69. Second, our work implicates safety signals in
shuttlebox avoidance, but more work is needed to determine if feed-
back devaluation can differentiate between goal-directed and
habitual avoidance in other active avoidance tasks. We suspect that
devaluation of explicit feedback cues will be most effective in tasks
that otherwise induce little change in the pre- to post-AR stimulus set
70.
Though feedback safety signals likely contribute in all tasks, devalua-
tion of a speciﬁc feedback cue should only be effective to the degree
that it can overshadow other feedback stimuli to acquire safety
properties. Finally, the question of whether females also rely on par-
allel circuits in dorsal striatum for avoidance remains open. Our con-
tingency degradation data suggest that females also rely on safety
signals for goal-directed avoidance, but this paradigm used a different
avoidance task and method for manipulating safety. Thus, it cannot be

assumed that the neuralﬁndings from male devaluation studies apply

In summary, this work suggests that volitional action is possible
under threat, even intense threat71, provided that responses are avail-
able in the context to attain safety 61. Our safety signal devaluation
protocol provides a tool for differentiating between goal-directed and
habitual ARs in neurobiological studies and studies of aberrant
avoidance habits in humans. Recent work suggests that patients with
obsessive-compulsive disorder
3, or with a history of early life stress 56,
have stronger avoidance habits. It would be interesting to see if
evidence for more robust avoidance habits is obtained after safety
signal devaluation. Our data showing strong context-dependence of
counterconditioning in females may also have clinical implications if
replicated in humans. Experiences that devalue an avoidance reinfor-
cer may only reduce avoidance in the contexts where they happen,
possibly contributing to the higher rate of anxiety disorders char-
acterized by avoidance in women. Interestingly, a recent study found
that human ratings of “relief pleasantness” during response-produced
safety periods relate to reward prediction error signals that decline as
avoidance is acquired
68. However, those scoring low on distress tol-
erance showed no such decline, suggesting possible over-
reinforcement of avoidance habits. A safety signal devaluation test
may help resolve this question and potentially identify a new
mechanism contributing to anxiety. Lastly, our results provide
empirical support for reinforcement learning models that rely on
response-produced safety signals to explain active avoidance

Two-way signaled active avoidance training procedure
Each session began in illuminated shuttleboxes with a 5-min stimulus-
free acclimation. On the ﬁrst day of training only, after acclimation,
rats received a single Pavlovian trial consisting of the WS (15 s white
noise) paired with footshock (0.5 s; 1.0 mA males, 0.7 mA females),
followed by 30 active avoidance trials. The single Pavlovian trial is
necessary to ensure that subsequent shuttles during the WS are
responses under threat. The remaining days of training (day 2+) did
not include the initial Pavlovian trial. After the acclimation period on
subsequent training days (day 2+), rats received 30 active avoidance
trials with an average intertrial interval of 2 min (random assignment
without replacement, range 90 –150 s). Trials consisted of an 80 dB
white noise WS presentation that lasted a maximum of 15 s. Shuttling
during the WS led to immediate WS termination, prevented delivery of
the scheduled footshock (0.5 s; 1.0 mA males, 0.7 mA females), and
triggered the delivery of a pure tone feedback cue (5 s, 5 kHz, 80 dB),
or in the case of FB-controls (see below), a 5 s period of darkness
(houselights turned off). If no avoidance shuttle was performed during
the WS, the WS terminated, the inescapable shock was delivered, and
no feedback was presented. ARs were de ﬁned as shuttles during the
WS. After 30 avoidance trials were completed, animals were given a
60 s ﬁnal period in the shuttle box before removal. Training (one
session/day) continued for either 5 days (moderately trained) or
20 days (overtrained) with 5 sessions occurring per week. Rats failing
to reach 70% successful avoidance by session 5 (poor avoiders) were
removed from experiments because it is challenging to study the
mechanisms of avoidance in rats that avoid sporadically or not at all.
This is standard practice in studies of active avoidance memory/
performance
4,5,16,25,26,42, though the phenomenon of poor avoidance
presents an obstacle to studies of avoidance learning.
Counterconditioning procedure
During the last ﬁve days of avoidance training (days 1 –5f o r
moderately-trained rats, days 16–20 for overtrained rats), animals were
placed in the illuminated counterconditioning apparatus and given
30 min. stimulus-free habituation sessions. It is our impression that
this habituation phase is critical for obtaining effective counter-
conditioning of safety signals. 1 d after the ﬁnal active avoidance
training session, animals were placed in the illuminated counter-
conditioning boxes for a 3 min. acclimation period. FB-Devalued and
FB-control rats received 40 trials of the 5 s tone (5 kHz, 80 dB) that co-
terminated with a 0.5 s footshock (0.7 mA for females, 1.0 mA for
males; avg. inter-trial interval: 180 s, random assignment without
replacement, range 150–210 s). For the FB-control group, the tone was
novel since they were avoidance-trained with a visual feedback cue. FB-
Valued rats received 40 tones and shocks in an explicitly unpaired
fashion. Trial 1 began with a footshock, with tones and shocks alter-
nating thereafter. Shocks occurred on average every 3 min, with pla-
cement closer to the onset of the next tone than the termination of the
previous tone (to prevent trace conditioning). Shock-to-tone intervals
ranged from 28 to 88 s (mean: 58 s) and tone-to-shock intervals ranged
from 87 to 147 s (mean: 117 s). All intervals were selected from lists at
random without replacement. Total session times for all animals were
approximately 125 min. Two experiments with female rats conducted
counterconditioning in the shuttleboxes using only 20 tones & shocks
(Paired vs. Unpaired, Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). These sessions
were the same as the standard procedure except the total session
duration was shorter (~65 min).
Avoidance tests
All avoidance tests were conducted 10 min. after i.v. injections with
1 ml/kg injection volumes. For within-subject KORD studies, rats were
injected with Vehicle or SalB (counterbalanced). For other purely
behavioral experiments, rats were injected with Vehicle before both
tests. This was done to control for stress before testing in all studies,
and to allow team members to practice i.v. injections before the critical
KORD studies. After acclimation in the shuttleboxes (avoidance train-
ing context) for 5 min, rats received 15 presentations of the 80 dB, 15 s
WS (mean inter-trial interval: 120 s, random assignment without
replacement, range 90 –150 s). Shuttling was monitored throughout
the session but had no effect on WS duration (no WS-termination).
There were also no feedback (tones or houselight-off) or footshock
presentations. Avoidance tests occurred twice, with 1 d off between
tests. Note that feedback stimuli were omitted during theﬁnal tests to
ensure that avoidance performance re ﬂected a memory of the out-

phase
