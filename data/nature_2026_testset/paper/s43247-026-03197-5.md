Northern Eurasia tropospheric winter warming has been observed and modeled after major tropical
volcanic eruptions. Here we show that a high-latitude eruption with a persistent stratospheric volcanic
cloud from summer to early winter can also trigger winter warming. Our model simulations,
incorporating updated volcanic forcing for the 1783 Laki eruption, closely align with two recent
temperature reconstructions— whereas simulations of other eruptions lacking substantial cold-
season aerosol loadings fail to produce such warming. The aerosol-induced mid-latitude
stratospheric warming strengthens the meridional temperature gradient, enhances the polar vortex,
and shifts both horizontal and vertical energy redistribution in favor of Northern Eurasia winter
warming. Neutral or cold winters, nevertheless, remain possible in individual realizations due to
internal variability. These ﬁndings help resolve model-observation discrepancies and highlight the
crucial role of stratosphere-troposphere coupling in shaping large-scale circulation patterns in the
aftermath of volcanic eruptions.

Large explosive eruptions at mid-h igh latitudes have occurred less
frequently over the last millennium compared to tropical volcanic
eruptions
19,20, and traditionally have received less attention due to their
perceived limiting climatic impact 21. However, interest in extra-tropical
eruptions has recently surged, driven by the proposed disproportionately
large radiative forcing on the climate
22–24, and sparked discussions on their
potential to mitigate anthropogenic warming and serve as a reference for
polar geoengineering
25.
The 1783 Icelandic Laki eruption, for instance, is estimated to have
injected approximately 120 Tg of sulfur dioxide into the atmosphere over
an 8-month period26, which is about seven times the intensity of the well-
known 1991 Pinatubo eruption. This massive aerosol injection into the
westerly jet stream, which dominates the Northern Hemisphere ’su p p e r
troposphere and lower stratosphere, has been linked to a series of
extreme weather events: an unusual heatwave in northern and western
Europe, extreme cold in Russia and North America during the summer
of 1783, and a subsequent severe winter characterized by low tempera-
tures, frozen watercourses, and frequent snowfalls across western and
central Europe
10,27–30. Additionally, droughts, famines, and mortality were
also documented in various regions worldwide 27,31–33.
This study revisits the Ice-core Volcanic Index 2 (IVI2)-based
reconstruction19 of the Laki volcanic eruption by treating it as a multistage
event30, and validates the updated forci ng with that of the Whole Atmo-
sphere Chemistry Climate Model (WACCM), a high-top atmosphere
component of the Community Earth System Model (CESM), which inte-
grates interactive atmospheric chemistry, dynamics, and aerosol
microphysics
34,35. We then simulate the climate effects using CESM1.2 (see
“Methods”)a n di d e n t i f ys i g n iﬁcant WW during the ﬁrst winter following
the Laki eruption. Our model results are consistent with the WW recorded
in both the Ensemble Kalman Fitting Paleo-Reanalysis (EKF400)36,37 and the
WinTEDA reconstruction18,38, highlighting the distinctive dynamically-
altered climate response amidst the direct volcanic forcing. Theseﬁndings
have important implications for un derstanding the potential climate
impacts of interventions such as stratospheric aerosol injection.

Surface temperature response to the 1783 Laki eruption
In direct response to the 1783 Laki eruption, the average surface temperature
in the Northern Hemisphere experienced a sharp decline, reaching−0.5 K
by Sep0 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Despite the persistence of short-wave
radiation anomalies through the ﬁrst winter, the hemisphere-average
anomaly reduced to−0.3 K in midwinter before increasing to−0.4 K in the
subsequent spring. The spatial distribution of surface temperature
anomalies suggests that this suppression of hemisphere-average cooling was
primarily driven by signi ﬁcant warming in high-latitude regions during
winter (Supplementary Fig. 3). Speciﬁcally, Fig. 2a illustrates the surface
temperature anomaly averaged over northern Eurasia (55°N-70°N, 30°E-
150°E), where negative values persisted for only 5 months post-eruption,
reaching a peak of −1.8 K in Sep0 before transitioning to positive values,
peaking at 2.5 K in Dec0, throughout the boreal winter. The temperature
response in a single ensemble member re ﬂects the combined effect of
internal variability and external forcing. This is seen in the spread of the
ensemble members, with 13 warm members and 2 that are cool. All 15
members fall within the range of one standard deviation (Supplementary
Table 2). This indicates that while a Laki-like volcanic perturbation tends to
shift the probability toward WW, it does not prescribe a deterministic
outcome where cold winters are also dynamically viable (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 4).

Mechanism of post-Laki winter warming
Northern Eurasia WW has been suggested by previous studies to follow a
major tropical volcanic eruption
13, and attributed to the warming of the
tropical stratosphere that enhances the Arctic vortex via increasing the
stratospheric meridional temperature gradient
7. Building on these ﬁnd-
ings, our analysis delves into the coupling between the stratosphere and
troposphere.
During D0JF1, Laki volcanic aerosols predominantly accumulate in the
mid-to-high-latitude lower stratosphere (Fig. 4a), with a higher con-
centration of sulfate aerosols at higher latitudes compared to mid-latitudes.
However, due to the absence of sunlight in the north polar region during
boreal winter, mid-latitudes absorb more solar radiation, leading to an
anomalous warming of the mid-latitude lower stratosphere by more than
3 K (Fig. 4b). The strong spatial correlation between the warming and the
distribution of volcanic cloud undersc ores the critical role of the sulfate
aerosols. The pronounced warming at 100 hPa in mid-latitudes, coupled
with less warming in high latitudes, enhances the meridional temperature
gradient in the lower stratosphere. This, in turn, intensiﬁes the stratospheric
polar vortex (Fig.4c) through the thermal wind balance
43,45.
The climatological westerly jet stream exhibits a vertical inclination,
with its core positioned within the troposphere in the subtropical region and
shifting to the stratosphere in the 60 –70°N latitude band (Fig. 4c). The
Arctic polar vortex, characterized by circumpolar westerly winds, extends
from the mid-troposphere to the upper stratosphere. Temperature varia-
tions across different pressure levels in ﬂuence the jet stream ’s response,
producing contrasting effects in the troposphere and stratosphere. As a
consequence of the enhanced stratosp heric meridional temperature gra-
dient, westerly winds in the mid-latitude troposphere experience decelera-
tion, whereas those in the polar str atosphere are markedly intensi ﬁed,

Conclusions
This study reconstructs the spatiotemporal forcing of the 1783 Laki volcanic
eruption by accounting for its Icelandic and multi-episode nature, providing
ar eﬁned basis for further investigations into the climatic impacts of the Laki
eruption and similar events. Our large-ensemble simulations, incorporating
this updated reconstruction, reveal signiﬁcant WW over Northern Eurasia
in the post-eruption period, in strong agreement with reconstruction
datasets. This warming isattributed to stratospheric processes that inﬂuence
the polar vortex—a mechanism previously proposed mainly for tropical
volcanic eruptions but rarely reproduced in climate models.
Our ﬁndings suggest that successfully modeling WW critically depends
on the presence of substantial sulfat e aerosols in the stratosphere during
autumn and winter. This insight highlights the limitations of current climate
models in simulating WW. In our simu lations, Laki-derived volcanic
aerosols persist in the Northern Hemisphere stratosphere throughout the
boreal winter, absorbing solar radiation and warming the mid-latitude
stratosphere. This uneven heating strengthens the lower stratospheric
temperature gradient, which in turn perturbs the westerly jet stream and
intensiﬁes the Arctic polar vortex. These results emphasize the pivotal role of
stratosphere-troposphere dynamical coupling in driving WW. In most
studies, for eruptions larger than the1991 Pinatubo one, the volcanic forcing
is scaled with a two-thirds power wh en converting the injected aerosol
amount into AOD. Our Laki reconstruction does not use such two-thirds
power scaling, and therefore our 47 TgS injection actually results in larger
forcing than the 80 TgS injection with the two-thirds power scaling from
Dalla Santa and Polvani
43. Because we used CESM, our results could serve as
a multi-model con ﬁrmation of their results using the NASA Goddard
Institute for Space Studies model E2.2-AP, that a single 80 TgS (~160 Tg
SO₂) tropical injection can yield robust Eurasian warming.
Furthermore, our ﬁndings underscore the potential risks associated
with stratospheric aerosol geoengineering 51, which has been shown to
induce robust continental Eurasian WW as one of its many side effects.
These results highlight the import ance of considering latitudinal and
seasonal aerosol variations in geoengineering strategies and call for further
studies on the interactions among climate change, high-latitude volcanic
eruptions, and polar geoengineering.
Previous studies have shown that even after large volcanic eruptions,
internal variability can substantially alter or even dominate regional climate
patterns
18,43. Therefore, when assessing the climatic impacts of volcanic
forcing, it is essential to account for the inﬂuence of internal variability and
to evaluate the relative contributions of external forcing and internal
dynamics. Such consideration is key to improving our understanding of
both natural volcanic events and potential geoengineering applications
51.

Model setup
We use the CESM1.2 with the Community Atmosphere Model version 5
(CAM5). CAM5 shows considerable improvement in feedback strengths for
the globe56. The land component is version 4.0 of the Community Land
Model (CLM 4.0), and the active ocean component is based on an extension
of the Parallel Ocean Program Version 2 (POP2) from the Los Alamos
National Laboratory (LANL). The sea-ice component (CICE) is an exten-
sion of the LANL sea-ice model, which was developed through collaboration
within the CESM Polar Climate Working Group, and can run as a fully
prognostic component or in prescribed mode, where ice coverage is read in.
The model resolution is an approximately 2° ﬁnite volume grid for the
atmosphere and land components (1.9° latitude × 2.5° longitude), a gx1v6
Greenland pole 1-degree ocean and sea-ice grid, and a 0.5° river routing grid.

the reliability of simulations, which conﬁrm that there is a robust match
between observed or reconstructed and modeled properties

Reanalysis dataset EKF400v2
The monthly global paleo-reanalysis temperature data are version 2.0 of the
Ensemble Kalman Fitting Paleo-Reanalysis65 (EKF400v2). It employs Kal-
man ﬁlter technology to assimilate instrumental temperature and pressure
data, documentary evidence, and proxy records into 30 ensembles of
atmospheric general circulation model simulations. It is theﬁrst monthly-
resolution multivariable paleoclimate reanalysis dataset covering the period
from 1600 to 2005. This climate reconstruction dataset combines the
advantages of traditional reconstruction methods, i.e., being as close as
possible to observational results, with the strengths of climate models, i.e.,
providing global three-dimensional information of atmospheric variables at
a 4° × 4° spatial resolution.
Compared to the previous version, EKF400v2 has made major updates
based on a large amount of recorded data, including early temperature and
pressure instrument data, observed monthly precipitation data, historical
literature records, and proxy data such as tree rings and corals
36. Using
Kalman ﬁlter technology for data assimilation, the methodology was also
improved during the assimilation process. All the data are fused into a single
atmospheric circulation model, producing 30 ensembles, with the spatial
resolution of the data improved to 2°. This dataset is highly applicable for
analyzing past climate change, extreme events, and studying large-scale
climate dynamics.
Reconstruction dataset WinTEDA
The WinTEDA reconstruction is a sophisticated climate reconstruction
framework aimed at providing accurate and high-resolution estimates of
winter temperatures across Eurasia over the period from the pre-industrial
era to the present (from 1000 to 2000 CE)
11,38. It is built by integrating a wide
range of paleoclimate data in the data assimilation process, combining tree
rings, ice cores, and historical documents with outputs from climate model
simulations, CESM-LME, to improve the accuracy and reliability of the
temperature reconstruction. The reconstruction speciﬁcally targets winter
temperatures, especially selectin g proxies with high correlation with
instrumental observations in DJF. The paleoclimatic proxy records are more
concentrated in Eurasia and Greenland. The reconstruction contains 100
ensembles and is provided as a seasonal average near-surface temperature of
DJF on a 2° × 2° spatial grid. The temperature is presented as anomalies in
degrees Celsius with respect to 1000–1900 CE.
