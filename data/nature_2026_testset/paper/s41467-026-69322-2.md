Hydrogel coatings are a promising strategy to reduce friction and wear in
biomedical implants, yet replicating thedurability of natural cartilage remains
a key challenge due to the inherent trade-off between low friction and high
wear resistance. Here, we present a picot-ﬁber hydrogel coating (PFHC) that
mimics the hierarchical architecture ofcartilage by integrating a lubricious
surface layer with a tough,ﬁber-reinforced core layer. The picotﬁbers, formed
by folded peptide strands with hidden loops, endow the core layer with efﬁ-
cient load-bearing capacity, while the loosely packed, open-structured top
layer preserves hydration lubrication. The resulting PFHC achieves both
ultralow friction (~0.009) and high wear resistance under long-term sliding
over 100,000 cycles, comparable to natural cartilage. By decoupling lubrica-
tion and load-bearing functionalitiesacross distinct structural layers, PFHC
overcomes the conventional limitations of hydrogel coatings, providing a
generalizable strategy to integrate lubrication and mechanical durability for
reliable, long-lasting implant interfaces.
Wear of biomedical implants remains a critical challenge to their long-
term reliability1. Load-bearing devices like arti ﬁcial joints and soft
prostheses undergo millions of frictional cycles 2,3, generating wear
debris and interfacial damage that can trigger in ﬂammation and ulti-
mately lead to implant failure4,5. Conventional implant materials such
as ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UPE) are stiff and lack the
hierarchical structure of biological tissues to simultaneously achieve
great lubricity and load-bearing ability, exacerbating the risk of tribo-
logical failure
6– 8. To address this, hydrogel coatings have been
explored as lubricious surface layers that absorb water and form a soft,
elastic buffer at the implant – tissue interface, mitigating interfacial
damage relative to uncoated polymer surfaces
9– 11.
Numerous efforts have advanced hydrogels toward better tribolo-
gical performance12,13. For instance, hydrogels with lipid-rich boundary
layers exhibit self-renewing capabilities to maintain low friction, while
multilayered hydrogels with sacriﬁcial top layers offer sustained lubri-
cation under pressure14,15. Lubricant self-pumping hydrogels inspired by
the earthworm epidermis feature a dissociated lubrication layer,
enabling excellent lubricity under high contact pressures16.H o w e v e r ,i t
remains challenging to engineer ultralow-friction hydrogels into single-
layer coatings that simultaneously exhibit high toughness, strong
adhesion, and long-term wear resistance. Achieving ultralow friction in
hydrogels depends on the design of polymer networks and the resulting
hydration-lubricating layer, as demonstrated in the polyacrylamide-
based tribosystems
17,18. Thus, a key dif ﬁculty stems from the inherent
conﬂict between maintaining high water content— crucial for ultralow
friction— and ensuring robust mechanical integrity to minimize wear17,19,
highlighting the need for a robus t yet water-rich network within a

Design and structure of PFHC
In natural cartilage, a gel-like surface layer provides lubrication, while
an underlying collagen ﬁber-reinforced zone ensures long-term dur-
ability under load, working synergistically to deliver excellent tribo-
logical performance (left of Fig. 1a)
60. Inspired by this architectural
concept, the PFHC is designed as a continuous hydrogel coating with
two integrated layers, each fulﬁlling a distinct role (right of Fig.1a). The
top layer is an open-structured, loose polymer network that serves as a
lubricating surface. This lubricating layer possesses a high hydration
capacity, forming a thinﬂuid ﬁlm that minimizes friction at the sliding
interface
17,61. Beneath it, the load-bearing core layer is a dense, robust
hydrogel network engineered to sustain mechanical loads and dis-
sipate energy. This core layer provides the coating with high strength,
toughness, and fatigue resistance without sacri ﬁcing hydration cap-
ability so that lubrication performance is maintained even under
substantial pressure. At the bottom interface, the core layer anchors to
the solid implant surface through a combination of chemically coupled
surface modi ﬁcation and mechanical interlocking with the porous
substrate, thus enhancing adhesion and preventing delamination
during repeated loading.
Central to the performance of PFHC is its picot- ﬁber hydrogel
network
35, inspired by the ﬁbrous proteins found in natural
tissues62,63. The picot ﬁbers, formed by self-assembled peptides
interwoven with picot-like loops of hydrophilic polymers, sig-
niﬁcantly enhance the mechanical properties of resulting hydrogels
through synchronous energy dissipation and hidden length-release
during reversible disassembly-reassembly processes (right of Fig. 1a
and Supplementary Fig. 1)
54,64. Such reversible molecular self-
assembly allows the system to convert interfacial friction into tran-
sient molecular recon ﬁguration rather than permanent chain scis-
sion, a mechanism known to suppress wear of polymer
65. Here, we
developed an advanced system using a short peptide sequence
(acrylate– glycine– alanine– glycine– alanine– glycine– tyrosine, here-
after referred to as GY6; Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2), analogous
to silk ﬁbroin66,67. The hexapeptide GY 6 was selected to balance suf-
ﬁcient self-assembly for mechanical stability with a force-responsive
dynamic transition that enables effective energy dissipation, while
also providing opportunities for functional modiﬁcation through the
Tyr residue
50,68– 70. The acrylate peptides self-assembled into β-sheet-
rich nanoﬁbers71 and were subsequently copolymerized with acryla-
mide, forming polyacrylamide-based picot loops — termed “picot
ﬁbers” (Fig. 1b). The formation of these structures was con ﬁrmed by
atomic force microscopy (AFM) and circular dichroism (CD) spec-
troscopy (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 3). The presence of picot
loops increased the ﬁber height from 0.8 to 2.4 nm and reduced the
stiffness from 43.0 to 38.5 N m−1 (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 4).
AFM-based single-molecule force spectroscopy (SMFS) revealed the
sequential unfolding of the assembled peptides in ﬁbers, indicating
energy dissipation during the self-assembly rupture at the molecular
level (Supplementary Fig. 5). To construct a robust yet hydrated
network, we further crosslinked the picot ﬁbers using a multi-arm
covalent crosslinker, generating a densely entangled polymer matrix
with high water content (right of Fig. 1a).
To achieve the hierarchical structure of the hydrogel coating via
one-step synthesis, we performed in situ photopolymerization of the
hydrogel precursor on a porous substrate under the covering of an
oxygen-permeable membrane (Fig.1e). The oxygen diffusion from the
cover inhibited radical polymerization at the surface, intentionally
producing a loosely crosslinked an d porous hydrogel layer. Deeper
within the hydrogel— where oxygen was depleted through precursor
nitrogen-degassing and hydrophilized substrates — a densely cross-
linked network formed, and the enhanced hydrogel penetration into
the substrate micropores enabled strong mechanical interlocking
during polymerization. Simultaneously, photoinitiated grafting and
silane-mediated anchoring enabled robust integration of the hydrogel
into the porous substrate (Supplementary Fig. 6), as these reactions
generated covalent bonds at the interface and a mechanically inter-
locked network within the substrate pores.
As a result, the hydrogel coating exhibited a hierarchical archi-
tecture characterized by a continuous transition from a soft and
lubricious surface to a tough and integrated bulk and, ultimately, to a
strongly anchored interface through multiscale interpenetration.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging revealed three distinct
structural regions: a ∼50-μm porous top layer, a ~ 600 μmd e n s e l y
polymerized intermediate layer, and a deeply interpenetrating net-
work extending nearly 1 mm into the porous substrate (Fig. 1f). AFM
measurements on the three structural regions further clari ﬁed the
gradient in mechanical properties, revealing the reduced Young ’s
moduli near the top layer (Supplementary Fig. 7). This seamless gra-
dient structure was anticipated to optimize tribological performance
at the surface while providing mechanical robustness at the sublayer.

Mechanistic insight
We next explored the mechanistic insight of the outstanding tribolo-
gical performance of the PFHC from the strategic separation of lubri-
cation and load-bearing functions (Fig.4a). Since the lubrication state
of the hydrogel coating in this st udy corresponded to boundary
lubrication (Supplementary Fig. 23), the friction reduction of hydrogel
coatings was primarily governed by the hydrated andﬂexible polymer
chains at the surface
14. During sliding, the interfacial water and mobile
polymer segments from the open-structured surface of PFHC facili-
tated motion with minimal shear resistance, enabling ultralow friction.
The PFHC exhibited extreme hydrophilicity, as evidenced by a water
contact angle (WCA) of ~12°, in stark contrast to ~95° for bare UPE,
indicating the presence of a stable surface-bound waterﬁlm (Fig. 4b).
This highly lubricative surface arose from oxygen inhibition of free-
radical polymerization, which suppressed chain propagation near the
oxygen-permeable cover and consequently produced a loosely cross-
linked surface hydrogel layer. This mechanism is consistent with pre-
vious studies on the mold effect in hydrogels and oxygen-regulated
surface gelation
24,82,83. In comparison, the control sample fabricated
without oxygen inhibition regulation (Control) exhibited a denser
surface morphology with a higher WCA and CoF, due to the absence of
an open-structured surface and mobile polymer segments (Fig.4b, c).
The contribution of the picot ﬁbers to the mechanical stabiliza-
tion of the surface lubrication layer is likewise crucial (Fig. 4d). The
zoomed view of the PFHC surface revealed picot ﬁbers interwoven
within the loosely structured polymer matrix (bottom right of Fig.4d).
When sliding against AFM probes, the PFHC surface exhibited slightly
high friction in certain areas, whereas the PAMC sample showed a
more uniform friction distribution, despite both samples exhibiting
comparable surface roughness (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 24a).
Importantly, these spatial variations did not increase the average
friction (Supplementary Fig. 24b), where the average CoFs of PFHC and
PAMC remain comparable. The PFHC also showed distinct frictional
peaks during the trace scan, accompanied by reduced forces in the
corresponding retrace direction (Fig.4f). The asymmetry suggests that
the dynamic picot ﬁbers may deform to facilitate motion and dis-
assemble to dissipate energy, thereby protecting the hydrogel net-
work. This indicates that the presence of picot ﬁbers modi ﬁes the
friction distribution rather than compromising the lubricity of hydro-
gel (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 24b). Given the similar water
contents (Supplementary Fig. 25), the picot ﬁbers in PFHC helped
mitigate damage to the surface layer without sacriﬁcing hydration.
Wear resistance of well-lubricated hydrogel, on the other hand, is
governed by the fracture resistance and compression resilience of the
load-bearing network in the core layer
84. Under stress, the picot ﬁbers
dynamically disassemble to release hidden length and dissipate
energy, alleviating stress concentrations that would otherwise trigger
cracks (Fig. 4g). Upon unloading, the picot ﬁbers rapidly reassemble,

structures rather than increased polymer content, PFHC-Gel was
treated with elastase (ELA), which selectively cleaves peptide bonds
adjacent to glycine and alanine residues to disrupt the self-assembled
structures. Disruption of peptide assembly reduced both mechanical
strength and energy dissipation, conﬁrming the critical role of picot
ﬁbers in hydrogel network (Supplementary Fig. 27). Furthermore, the
interlocking between the PFHC and substrates effectively prevented
delamination, a bonding strategy that can be extended to various
substrates (Supplementary Fig. 28). Collectively, these synergistic
mechanisms enabled PFHC to maintain ultralow friction while resisting
wear, effectively coupling lubrication with mechanical durability.
Biocompatibility and in vivo stability
Finally, we assessed the biocompatibility and in vivo stability of the
PFHC to evaluate its suitability for long-term biomedical applications.
Co-culture with human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) revealed that
the cell morphology and viability were not affected by the PFHC,
indicating excellent cytocompatibility (Fig.5a and Supplementary Figs.
29– 31). Dorsal subcutaneous implantations in a rat model over 2, 5, and
7 weeks showed a mild systemic response and no pathological changes
as indicated by stable blood biochemistry, cellular indicators, and
histological examination of major organs (Fig. 5b, c and Supplemen-
tary Fig. 32). Throughout the 7 weeks, the PFHC exhibited favorable
biocompatibility with no instances of immune rejection or synovial
hyperplasia in surrounding tissues (Fig.5d and Supplementary Fig. 33).
Importantly, the PFHC maintained structural integrity and exhib-
ited minimal degradation, with solid content remaining stable after 2, 5,
and 7 weeks of implantation (Fig. 5e). Tribological tests on retrieved
PFHC samples demonstrated consistent anti-wear and lubricating per-
formance, comparable to that of pristine samples (Fig.5f, g). The pre-
served circular dichroism (CD) signal ofβ-sheet structures, along with
nearly unchanged mechanical strength, further conﬁrmed the long-term
structural stability in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 34). Consistently, PFHC-
Gel exhibited in vitro stability for 270 days in PBS and retained both
solid content and chemical integrity under simulatedﬂow and oxidative
conditions for 30 days (Supplementary Figs. 35 and 36). Theseﬁndings
underscore that the PFHC satisﬁed the stringent requirements for bio-
compatibility and mechanical durability in vivo, highlighting its poten-
tial as a robust and long-lasting implantable biomedical coating.

Discussion
In summary, the picot-ﬁber hydrogel coating introduced here achieves
a combination of superlubricity and mechanical resilience through a
bioinspired hierarchical design and a decoupled lubrication mechan-
ism (Supplementary Fig. 37). Through integrating a water-rich lubri-
cating surface and a tough energy-dissipating network, the PFHC
overcomes the traditional trade-off between low friction and high wear
resistance. It exhibits ultralow CoFs on the order of 10
−3 that remain
stable under extensive cyclic loading while maintaining toughness and
fatigue resistance comparable to many of the reported robust
hydrogels51,77,85. This combination effectively meets the critical demand
for durable and low-wear implant surfaces. Notably, the PFHC is con-
structed from biocompatible polymer and peptide components, and
preliminary tests conﬁrmed its non-cytotoxic and non-in ﬂammatory
properties in vivo. These characteristics underscore the potential of
PFHC as a translational solution for improving the longevity and safety
of biomedical implants.
Beyond the speci ﬁc application to joint prostheses or implant
liners, the design principles demonstrated by PFHC open avenues for
advanced material engineering. The concept of embedding picot
ﬁbers— self-assembling molecularﬁlaments with built-in hidden length
— within a continuous network could be adapted to create hydrogels
that are not only wear-resistant but also responsive or self-healing in
other contexts
86. More broadly, our approach highlights how
mimicking the hierarchical structures found in nature can yield syn-
thetic materials that defy conventional performance limits. By tailoring
the architecture from the nanoscale (polymer chain topologies) to the
microscale (porous layers and interlocks), one can decouple typically

Preparation of hydrogel precursor solutions

Preparation of hydrogel and hydrogel coatings
Different UPE substrates were immersed in a DMF solution containing
25% (w/w) BP for 12 h, followed by ethanol washing and air drying at
50 °C for 2 h. Plasma treatment was then performed for the substrates,
followed by the modi ﬁcation using a 20% (v/v) solution of 3-(tri-
methoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (TMSPMA) in ethanol. The samples
were subsequently air-dried at 50 °C for 2 h to obtain the treated UPE.
This combined treatment enabled both chemical grafting (TMSPMA-
mediated covalent bonding) and mechanical interlocking (hydrogel
penetration into the porous structure), ensuring robust interfacial
adhesion.
Before coating, the hydrogel precursor solution was degassed
with nitrogen to remove dissolved oxygen. The as-prepared UPE was
embedded into the grooves of a 25.5 mm × 25.5 mm × 3.4 mm PTFE
mold and covered with the precursor solution. The groove depth
exceeded the UPE thickness by ~0.4 μm, allowing complete ﬁlling of
the precursor solution. To create the hierarchical structure, oxygen-
mediated differential polymerization was introduced. After loading
the deperoxidized precursor, the mold was covered with a 1-mm-thick,
oxygen-permeable Teﬂon AF 1600 membrane cover. The system was
intentionally exposed to ambient air for ~30 min before UV initiation,
allowing oxygen to diffuse into the upper region of the precursor
solution. The polymerized coatings were washed with deionized water
and swollen in 1 M Tris buffer solution (pH 7.40) for 48 h before being
stored at 37 °C before measurements. Similarly, the PAMC was fabri-
cated using the same method, including oxygen inhibition and pre-
cursor solutions, except without the GY
6 peptide. The preparation of
close-structured coatings followed the same procedure as above,
except that the oxygen-permeable membrane was replaced with a 3-
mm-thick oxygen-impermeable glass plate. For visualization purposes,
all hydrogel coatings in digital photographs were dyed with a coloring
agent. Before biological testing, UPE substrates were sterilized by
immersion in 75% ethanol and subsequently air-dried. All precursor
solutions for hydrogel preparation were sterilized byﬁltration through
0.22 µm PVDF membranes (Millipore). Hydrogel and coating prepara-
tion and handling were performed in a laminar ﬂow hood under
aseptic conditions.
