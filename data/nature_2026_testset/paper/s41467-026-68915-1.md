The lytic-latent balanceis a major viral persistence strategy and obstacle to
curing viral diseases, yet its mechanisms remain poorly understood. Following
lytic infection in non-neuronal cells, herpes simplex virus (HSV) establishes
latency speciﬁcally in neurons and is reactivated by stresses. Here we identify
forkhead box (FOX) transcription factors that can strongly activate or repress
replication of HSV-1 and other alphaherpesviruses, and show that neurons
express activating Fox (e.g., Foxf) genes poorly but repressive Fox (Foxk)
genes abundantly while non-neuronal or stressed neuronal cells exhibit higher
expression of activating Fox genes. Remarkably, knockdown of Foxk1 or
overexpression of activating Fox genes induces reactivation from latency in
male mouse neuronal culture and in vivo. Of note, FOX proteins bind the viral
genome globally and nonsequence-speciﬁcally and interact with epigenetic
cofactors for gene regulation. FOXF1 interacts with CBP and P300 to acetylate
and open viral chromatin. FOXK1 works with SIN3A, a cofactor of histone
deacetylation, and MAX to suppress HSV-1 and antagonize activating FOX
proteins. Therefore, the viral lytic-latent balance is controlled by the relative
abundance of counteracting host transcription factors that recruit different
epigenetic regulators to the viral genome.
Switching between productive (lytic) and latent infections is a com-
mon strategy of many viruses to persist in hosts, and a major obstacle
for curing viral diseases. Herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) is an ubiqui-
tous pathogen that causes numerous human diseases. During lytic
infection, after entry of the viral DNA genome into the nucleus, viral
genes are expressed in the order of immediate-early (IE), early (E) and
late (L) genes leading to abundant production of viral genomes and
particles
1. In vivo, after lytic infection in non-neuronal cells of periph-
eral tissues, HSV establishes latent infection particularly in sensory
neurons where essentially all viral genes except the locus encoding the
latency-associated transcript (LAT) are silenced and production of new
viral genomes and particles ceases. Sporadically, certain stresses can
reactivate the latent virus to resume the lytic program. HSV has
evolved to strike a delicate balance between the two infection

However, the roles of FOX proteins in viral infection remain largely

In this work, we identiﬁed multiple FOX proteins that can strongly

their expression profoundly impacts the lytic-latent balance in neu-

associate with viral DNA and cooperate with epigenetic regulators to
modulate viral chromatin. These results lead to a new model about
how the viral lytic-latent balance is controlled.

Identiﬁcation of FOX proteins that can strongly activate or
repress HSV-1 neuronal replication
To identify FOX family proteins that can regulate HSV-1 infection, we
ﬁrst selected one member from each subfamily and transfected their
human genes before infecting mouse neuroblastoma Neuro-2a cells
(Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a). If a Fox gene from a subfamily had an
effect, other members of the subfamily were also tested. In case the
viral E3 ubiquitin ligase ICP0 interfered with these functions
40, both
wild-type (WT) strain KOS and its ICP0-null mutant (7134 virus)41 were
used. Proteins with signi ﬁcant impacts on both viruses at a low
multiplicity of infection (MOI) were selected. Thus, FOXA1, A2, A3, C1,
C2, E1, F1 and F2 that could substantially increase viral yields are
designated activating FOX proteins, and FOXK1 and K2 with opposite
effects are designated repressive FOX proteins (Fig. 1a). The effects
were generally greater on ICP0-null than WT HSV-1 suggesting that
ICP0 indeed interfered with FOX protein effects. To test whether this
could be due to degradation of FOX proteins induced by ICP0, we
examined FOXA1, C1, F1 and K1 expression in human embryonic kidney
293T cells infected with either WT or ICP0-null virus. Expression of
these FOX proteins was similar following infection by the two viruses,
with the possible exception of less FOXK1 expression in WT infected
cells (Supplementary Fig. 1b), implying that instead of targeting FOX
proteins for degradation, ICP0 might interfere with downstream steps
regulated by the FOX proteins. To avoid such confounding inter-
ference, some mechanistic studies described below used ICP0-
null virus.
For representative activating FOXF1 and repressive FOXK1, their
impacts were conﬁrmed by growth curves (Fig.1b) and by using Neuro-
2a cells stably transduced with these genes for doxycycline (DOX)-
inducible expression (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1a). Even at a high
MOI, FOXK1 could also suppress both viruses, although FOXF1 could
stimulate ICP0-null but not WT HSV-1 (Fig. 1d). Mouse FOXF1 and
FOXK1 had the same effects as their human counterparts (Supple-
mentary Fig. 1a, c), providing support for using mice or mouse cells to
study the human proteins. The natural sites of HSV-1 infection and
latency are human sensory neurons. Therefore, we employed human
induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)-derived sensory neurons
(iNeurons)
42, and found that lentivirus transduced FOXA1, C1, E1 and F1
all substantially increased WT HSV-1 yields while FOXK1 decreased
them (Fig.1e). However, in 293T cells, transfected FOXF1 had no effect
on either virus although FOXK1 decreased yields of both viruses
(Fig. 1f). In human foreskin ﬁbroblast (HFF) cells, transduced FOXF1
increased yields of ICP0-null but not WT virus while FOXK1 had no
signiﬁcant effect on either virus (Fig.1g). Therefore, the effects of FOX
proteins are cell-type speci ﬁc but conserved in human and mouse
neuronal cells.
FOX proteins can also regulate other alphaherpesviruses
To test whether similar neurotropic viruses are regulated by FOX
proteins, transfection-infection experiments were conducted for some
other alphaherpesviruses, including HSV-2, pseudorabies virus (PRV),
and bovine herpesvirus 1 (BoHV-1) (Supplementary Fig. 2a –c). HSV-2
and PRV, but not BoHV-1, ef ﬁciently infected Neuro-2a cells. TCID
50
assays of BoHV-1 stocks in Neuro-2a cells showed slow development of
cytopathic effects but TCID 50 titers similar to PFU titers in bovine
kidney MDBK cells indicating efﬁcient entry but inefﬁcient replication.
Regardless, sufﬁcient BoHV-1 yields were obtained in Neuro-2a cells by
using a high MOI (Supplementary Fig. 2c). For all three viruses, FOXF1
or K1 signi ﬁcantly increased or decreased viral yields, respectively
(Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). We found that varicella-zoster virus (VZV)
cannot infect Neuro-2a cells, but its gene expression could be detected
after the genome was transfected. Co-transfecting FOXF1 with the VZV
genome signiﬁcantly increased IE61 (homolog of HSV ICP0)a n d IE62
(homolog of HSV ICP4) mRNA levels, while FOXK1 decreased IE61 but
not IE62 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Thus, the role of FOX
proteins in regulating neuronal viral replication may be conserved in at
least some alphaherpesviruses.

FOXK1 suppresses lytic replication and is important for HSV-1
latency
Given their abundance, we examined the role of endogenous FOXK
proteins. Knockout (KO) of FOXK1 in Neuro-2a cells increased ICP0-
null virus yields, which were reduced again by transfected FOXK1
(Fig. 3a). In the FOXK1 KO cells, siRNAs that effectively knocked down
FOXK2 (Supplementary Table 1) increased viral yields further (Fig.3b).
We then employed a mouse primary TG neuron model of latency and
reactivation, which usually relies on chemical triggers to induce
reactivation
23. Considering possible functional redundancy between
FOXK1 and K2, we attempted dual knockdown. We selected one shRNA
for FOXK1 and one for FOXK2 that effectively knocked down the
respective proteins (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Remarkably, even without
chemical triggers, dual knockdown of FOXK1 and K2 caused
reactivation from latency in 50% of the wells, signiﬁcantly higher than
the 14% of control wells reactivated (Fig. 3c).

f/f mice
with LoxP sitesﬂanking theFoxk1 gene and crossed them with Advillin-
Cre (AvilCre+/−) mice which expressCre speciﬁcally in sensory neurons45.
The resulting FOXK1-conditional knockout (cKO) (Avil Cre+/−Foxk1f/f)
mice expressed less FOXK1 in TG than the control AvilCre−/−Foxk1f/f mice

with FOXK1 speci ﬁcally repressing HSV-1 replication in sensory neu-
rons. Accordingly, viral genome levels in FOXK1-cKO mice were also

asked whether these proteins associate with viral DNA. The following
studies focused mainly on FOXF1 and FOXK1 as examples of an acti-
vator and repressor, respectively, of viral replication and reactivation.
We ﬁrst performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR
using an anti-FOXF1 antibody to analyze DNA association in HSV-1-

viral DNA.
To investigate this in neuronal cells, where FOXF1 is poorly
expressed, we conducted ChIP-seq by pulling down transfected FOXF1
in Neuro-2a cells infected with ICP0-null virus. The positions of viral
and host peaks and their corresponding genes are listed in Supple-

the viral genome, the data reveal widespread occupancies of FOXF1 on

replicates (Fig.4b). The fraction of viral reads in total reads was 16-fold

(Fig. 4c). The viral peaks showed little or no enrichment around tran-

peaks at TSS (Supplementary Fig. 4b) indicating differential occupancy
patterns between viral and host DNA. Motif search identi ﬁed the
known DNA motif for FOXF1 as well as some de novo motifs that all

lacking this consensus sequence, and ChIP-qPCR results showed that
besides associating with the sites containing RTAAACA (motif

speciﬁc and nonsequence-speciﬁc( n o n - s p e c iﬁc) manners.

Activating FOX proteins generally associate with CBP/P300
To understand how FOX proteins modulate viral chromatin, we ﬁrst
considered the observation that FOXA1 binds core histones33. There-
fore, we pulled down transfected FOXA1, C1, E1, F1 and K1 after Ben-

FOXF1 (Supplementary Fig. 7c). The association with FOXF1 depended
on aa200–380 but not the DBD (Supplementary Fig. 7d) consistent
with the observation that FOXA1 uses a non-DBD domain to interact
with H3

associate with H3, this association cannot explain the activating or
repressive activities.
To explore binding partners involved in the regulatory activities,

FOXK1-TurboID, FOXF1 Δ200–380-TurboID, and unfused TurboID.

TurboID-FOX fusion proteins (Supplementary Fig. 8b). After transfec-
tion and infection of Neuro-2a cells, comparisons with the three con-
trols commonly identi ﬁed 53 potential interactors (Fig. 6b,
Supplementary Fig. 8c). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes
(KEGG) database analysis shows that these proteins are predominantly
related to ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling (Supplementary
Fig. 8d). Application of a more stringent threshold resulted in 11 pro-
teins (Fig. 6b), which form an interaction network according to the
STRING database (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Interestingly, homologous
histone acetyltransferases CBP and P300

induce reactivation indicating that HSV-1 latency requires optimal FOX
protein composition provided by neurons. Moreover, certain activating
Fox genes are quickly and substantially upregulated by certain
reactivation-related stresses suggesting that stress-induced activating
FOX proteins might be involved in the initial steps of reactivation. Thus,
these results introduce the notion that the viral lytic-latent balance is
controlled by counteracting host factors, whose optimal composition
provided by speciﬁc host cells is required for latency and perturbation
of the composition by stresses can induce reactivation.
What are the mechanisms by which these FOX proteins regulate
the lytic-latent balance? Our data converge into a model wherein these
proteins extensively bind the viral genome at least partly through
nonsequence-speciﬁc interactions and regulate viral chromatin and
gene transcription through cofactors (Fig.7i). The importance of non-
speciﬁc DNA interactions is not unprecedented since such binding of
FOXA proteins to the cellular chromosome has been reported to
contribute to gene reactivation post-mitosis
58,59. Some other pioneer
factors have also been observed to bind nucleosomes via non-speciﬁc
DNA interactions or partial DNA motifs60. Moreover, like FOXA133,61,w e
show that other FOX proteins can also associate with H3 independent
of DNA, which should strengthen the presumably weak non-speciﬁc
DNA interactions. Notably, interactions with core histones have been
shown to contribute to chromatin opening by FOXA proteins
61.
Although most of the other activating FOX proteins are not formally
known as pioneer factors, their ability to reactivate the latent virus, a
process involving opening of condensed viral chromatin
62,a sw e l la s
their extensive use of non-speciﬁc DNA interactions, is consistent with
pioneer transcription factor activities63.
Although they commonly associate with viral DNA, activating and
repressive FOX proteins function with distinct cofactors to regulate
HSV-1 replication. Activating FOX proteins generally interact with his-
tone acetyltransferases CBP and P300, and at least FOXF1 enhances
viral gene expression and histone acetylation through these enzymes.
Acetylation reduces the afﬁnity of histones for DNA by neutralizing the
positive charge of lysine, thereby increasing chromatin accessibility
64.
Accordingly, FOXF1 can globally enhance HSV-1 chromatin accessi-
bility. We note that the FOXF1 proximal proteins appear to form an
interaction network (Supplementary Fig. 8e), within which ARID1A,
besides CBP and P300, is required for ef ﬁcient HSV-1 activation
(Fig. 6f) raising the possibility that FOXF1 recruits a complex for gene
activation. On the other hand, FOXK1 is proximal to a different set of
proteins, among which at least SIN3A and MAX work with FOXK1 to
repress HSV-1 replication. It is intriguing that activating and repressive
FOX proteins associate with histone acetyltransferases (CBP/P300)
and a cofactor of deacetylases (SIN3A) respectively and antagonize
each other. MAX is primarily known as a MYC cofactor
65, but we did not
detect MYC in our proteomics data. The mechanism by which MAX
suppresses HSV-1 replication so strongly is unknown but highly worthy
of investigation in future.
Latency is a feature shared by many DNA viruses and retroviruses.
Given the non-speciﬁc DNA interactions, the activating or repressive
FOX proteins might also regulate the lytic-latent balance of other
viruses. Indeed, neuronal replication of multiple alphaherpesviruses
could be signi ﬁcantly affected by FOXF1 and FOXK1 (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 2). However, FOXK1 and K2 were reported to promote KSHV
replication
35,36 in contrast with their repressive role in HSV-1 neuronal
replication suggesting that FOX proteins regulate viral infection and/
or latency in a context-dependent manner. More work is needed to
further elucidate the role of FOX proteins in the viral lytic-latent
balance.

(protein N-term) were set as variable modiﬁcations. Mass tolerance of 7
ppm and 15 ppm were set for MS and MS2 spectra, respectively. Pro-
tein level 1% FDR was set to ﬁlter the result. Potential contaminant
proteins were identi ﬁed by DIA-NN build-in database and were
removed from subsequent analysis. For quantitative analysis, missing
values were ﬁlled with a MS intensity of 1000 (noise level), and all
intensity values were log2-transformed. Proteins enriched in the
TurboID-labeled samples were then selected using t tests ( P value <
0.05) in Excel and required a fold-change of at least 2 (corresponding
to an average intergroup difference of ≥ 1o nt h el o g 2s c a l e ) .
Statistical analyses
Statistical analyses were performed using Prism version 10.0 (Graph-
Pad Software). All measurements were taken from distinct samples. All
multiple comparisons have been corrected before adjusted P values
are presented. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-
tailed t tests were used. For comparisons among three or more groups,
one-way ANOVA with Dunnett ’s multiple comparisons tests were
employed. For multi-condition group comparisons, two-way ANOVA
with Sidak’s multiple comparisons tests were used. To compare reac-
tivation curves, log-rank (Mantel-Cox) tests were used. Data are
expressed as mean ± SD.
