Efﬁcient carbon dioxide mineralization in silicate-rich formations depends on reaction rates within
fracture networks, yet the role of fracture microstructure remains poorly constrained. We investigated
how fracture aperture and surface roughness in ﬂuence mineralization by reacting synthetic forsteritic
olivine fractures with controlled apertures (0.5–1.6 millimeters) and roughness (6–16 micrometers) with
supercritical carbon dioxide at 13.7 megapascals and 185 degrees Celsius. Optical microscopy and
Raman spectroscopy showed that rougher fracture surfaces promoted iron oxide precipitation, which
passivated olivine surfaces, limiting magnesium availability for carbonate formation while removing
iron as a reactant. These effects were more pronounced in smaller-aperture fractures. A geochemical
model incorporating surface topography and passivation reproduced the observed trends, conﬁrming
that increased roughness can reduce reactivity despite greater surface area. Our results demonstrate
that fracture microstructure strongly in ﬂuences iron oxide passivation and carbon dioxide
mineralization, with important implications for predicting long-term performance of engineered carbon
dioxide storage systems.
Geologic carbon storage (GCS) is a critical strategy for managing industrial
carbon dioxide (CO2)e m i s s i o n si nh a r d - t o - d e c a rbonize sectors. Capturing
CO2 at point sources and injecting it into deep geological formations offers a
potential solution formitigating the releaseof large volumes of CO2 gas that
would otherwise be vented to the atmosphere1. Storage reservoirs, including
deep saline aquifers, depleted oil and gasﬁelds, and maﬁc/ultramaﬁcr o c k
formations, can provide long-term containment through a combination of
physical and geochemical trapping mechanisms2. Despite increased interest
and investment in GCS, conventional storage in sedimentary basins faces
key challenges3. Supercritical CO2 injection depends on caprock integrity,
with long-term trapping governed by slow dissolution and mineralization
processes. Sedimentary formations oftenlack reactive divalent cations (e.g.,
Ca²⁺,M g ²⁺,F e ²⁺), resulting in mineralization that can take thousands of
years
4. Moreover, the buoyant nature of supercritical CO2 increases the risk
of leakage and storage uncertainty 5,6. Alternative storage reservoirs with
faster and more secure trapping mechanisms need to be identi ﬁed and
characterized.
In-situ carbon mineralization in reactive rock formations such as basalt
and peridotite has emerged as a promising alternative. Field projects like
CarbFix in Iceland
7,8, the Wallula site in the U.S.9, and the Samail Ophiolite
in Oman 10 have demonstrated rapid CO 2 mineralization potential7,8.
However, current deployments operate at kilotonne-per-year scales, far
below its full potential11–13. Scaling up requires adequate permeability and
porosity of the host rock formation, as pores and fractures serve as pathways
for ﬂuid migration, provide access to mineral surfaces that contribute
cations to the mineralization reaction, and create space for carbonate
precipitates
5. Due to the low permeability of many basalts and peridotite,
natural or arti ﬁcial fracture networks are essential to enable ef ﬁcient
mineralization of the rock mass14,15.
Experimental studies have shown that secondary mineral precipitation
in fractures can reduce pore volume, alter ﬂow paths, and affect reaction
kinetics depending on fracture geometry and mineral distribution 16–19.
Transport-limited zones such as de ad-end fractures often become pre-
ferential sites for carbonate precipitation 16,20. Within these diffusion-
dominated regions, fracture characteristics, including aperture and surface
roughness, critically affect local transport and reactivity. Kinetic Monte Carlo
simulations indicate that local dissolution rates can vary by over an order of
magnitude due to variations in roughness and reactive site densities
21,22.T h e s e
ﬁndings are highly relevant to naturalsystems, where rock weathering and
mineral reactions often generate roughsurfaces. Yet, no studies have directly
linked realistic fracture characteristics, such as surface roughness and aper-
ture, to mineralization kinetics in these zones. Existing knowledge derives
from analog systems, where rough surfaces tend to promote nucleation, while
smooth surfaces favor the growth of large calcite crystals
23.

In this study, we investigate CO2 mineralization in synthetic, sawcut
fractures of forsteritic olivine with controlled aperture and surface rough-
ness. Experiments are conducted under high-pressure, high-temperature
conditions using supercritical CO
2 in a batch reactor and secondary
alteration of the surface is evaluated.Additionally, a geochemical model is
developed in PHREEQC (v3.7.3) 24 using the Lawrence Livermore
National Laboratory thermodynamic database (llnl.dat) to simulate
CO
2 mineralization in olivine rocks with varying surface roughness.
We found that rougher olivine surfaces promoted iron oxide precipitation,
particularly in smaller aperture fractures. This work aims to elucidate the
role of fracture characteristics in governing mineralization efﬁciency, with
implications for the design and optimization of engineered carbon mineral
storage systems.

Effect of aperture on olivine fracture carbonation
For larger aperture fractures, the precipitation of maghemite was observed
on both smooth and rough sides of the post-reaction fracture surfaces
(Fig. 2b), consistent with optical micr oscopy observations of a uniform
reddish coating (Fig. 1d). Thus, the inﬂuence of surface roughness on the
carbonation of olivine samples diminished with increasing fracture aper-
ture, despite the observation that rougher surfaces promoted greater for-
mation of iron oxides. This phenomenon can be attributed to two primary
mechanisms: (1) interfacial ﬂuid composition and (2) surface passivation
effects. Previous studies

aperture length scale reduces the eff ect of surface roughness-controlled

Discussion
Previous studies have shown that reduced ﬂow zones, such as dead-end
fractures, are preferential sites for mineralization in reactive formations,
where reactions are transport-limited rather than kinetically limited
50.I n
these zones, a continuous supply of reactants through CO 2 injection is
critical for sustaining mineralization reactions, a process strongly dependent
on fracture properties. Our static batch experiments demonstrate how
fracture characteristics will impact precipitation within dead-end fractures
of forsterite-rich olivine, a target for ef ﬁcient, long-term CO
2 storage.
Experimental and simulation results conﬁrm that surface roughness can
directly control the type of secondary minerals precipitated on fracture
surfaces, with rough surfaces having more precipitation of iron oxides.
However, the impact of surface roughness on mineralization kinetics will
also depend on the size of the fracture aperture. Larger fractures showed less
impact of surface roughness and tended to promote the formation of iron
oxides, which can passivate reactive mineral surfaces. For this reason, sur-
face roughness will become increasingly important when considering car-
bon mineralization near fracture tips in natural fracture network or within
secondary branches of induced fractures that either cease to grow after
propagation or gradually close under stress.
While our work focuses on direct ob servations of surface-sensitive
geochemistry within dead-end olivine fractures, these ﬁndings are highly
relevant for ﬁeld-scale applications. Atﬁeld scale, the abundance of dead-
end fractures is heterogeneous and site-speci ﬁc, but the ultimate miner-
alization potential of a fracture network (i.e., the extent to which injected
CO₂ can be permanently converted to solid carbonate) depends primarily
on mineralization ef ﬁciency within dead-end fractures. This is because
dead-end fractures are diffusion-limited, with longer residence times and
higher effective surface-area-to- volume ratios. These fractures can
thus preferentially accumulate carbonates, whereas advective main channels
primarily support dissolution and solute transport
16,51,52. Through this study,
we demonstrate the dual role of surfaceroughness in controlling the mineral
trapping potential of divalent silicates in olivine rocks within supercritical
CO
2-dominatedﬂuids. Because geologic systems do not contain idealized,
perfectly smooth fractures, we used synthetic saw-cut fractures to simulate
precipitation within dead-end fractures and systematically varied surface
roughness and liquid-to-solid ratio. While rougher surfaces increase reac-
tive surface area, enhancing olivine dissolution and carbonation rates by
concentrating reactants at the interface, they also facilitate the formation of
passivating layers
34. These layers reduce the rate and extent of mineralization
by halting olivine dissolution and depleting the available iron, which could
otherwise form Fe-carbonates (e.g., siderite) 50,53. The extent and spatial
pattern of precipitation in dead-ends control sustained mineralization at
ﬁeld scale by determining whether these fractures clog or continue to
exchange with ﬂowing conduits. Accordingly, reactive-transport models
that simplify fractures to perfectly smooth surfaces are likely to overestimate
overall CO2 mineralization potential. In addition, reaction-driven cracking,
a critical positive feedback mechanism that can potentially sustain subsur-
face mineralization from secondary mineral precipitation, will most likely
occur in regions with stress concentrations (i.e., fracture tip)
10, where surface
roughness will impact both the initiation and propagation of fracture
networks54 and the reaction rate. Therefore, our study also highlights the
importance of incorporating surfaceroughness into coupled geochemical
and geomechanical modeling for in-situ mineralization.
Evaluating the effect of surface roughness on the overall mineralization
extent of olivine remains challenging due to the complex role of iron
oxides
55–57. Research has shown that iron oxides can react with CO2 to form
siderite (FeCO3)u n d e rH2O-saturated and anoxic conditions53,58.U n d e r
such conditions, surface roughness could enhance olivine mineralization
rates. However, in this study, sideritewas not detected in any Raman spectra
of carbonated olivine samples, which is consistent with previous batch
experiments performed by Gadikota et al. 59. If these conditions are not
achievable inﬁeld-scale injection53, rougher surfaces will negatively impact
long-term storage potential by passivating reactive surfaces and reducing the
concentration of reactive cations (e.g., Fe). Future work is necessary to
establish whether anoxic conditions naturally develop through oxidation
reactions to assess the rates and extent of iron oxide-to-siderite conversion.

Batch reactor experimental procedures
High-pressure, high-temperature batch reaction experiments were con-
ducted on the synthetic olivine fracture samples to evaluate the effects of
surface roughness and fracture aperture on mineral carbonation. Sawcut
fractures were placed inside a Series 4560, 300 mL Parr vessel (Parr
Instrument Company, Moline, IL, USA). Additionally, 0.08 g of pulverized
olivine (60–80 mesh) was included in the vessel for post-reaction mineral
composition analysis. Static batch experiments were conducted at optimized
reaction conditions for carbon mineralization of olivine (P
CO2 of 13.7 MPa
and 185 °C)62–64. High pressure enhances the solubility of CO2 while high
temperature accelerates the reaction, thus promoting mineralization within
observable timescales. Solution chemistry was also found to be important in
controlling reaction kinetics. Previous studies showed that adding NaHCO
3
as a pH buffer and source of carbonate ions can greatly enhance the car-
bonation rate. The optimal solutio n used in our experiments therefore
contained 0.64 M NaHCO3 and 1 M NaCl62,63.
The batch reactor, which contained 200 mL of the optimized solution,
was connected to a 500D Teledyne Isco syringe pump for CO 2 injection.
Prior to reaction, the reactor was ﬂushed three times with CO2 to remove
residual air from the headspace and tubing. The vessel was heated to 185 °C
using a heating jacket and CO
2 was injected until the pressure reached
13.7 MPa, yielding a starting pH of 4. 2. A 14-days reaction period was
selected based on preliminary experiments showing that the reaction period
was sufﬁcient to have appreciable carbonation.
Post-reaction sample characterization
After the reaction, the sawcut fractures were detached, rinsed with DI water,
and dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C prior to non-destructive character-
ization of reaction products on the fracture surfaces. The bulk liquid was
sampled at the end of each batch experiment, and its pH was measured. A
10 ml aliquot was withdrawn and immediately passed through a 0.45 μm
ﬁlter. Elemental concentrations in theﬁltrate were analyzed under ambient
conditions using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission
spectroscopy (ICP–AES). To prevent precipitation of silica prior to analysis,
aliquots were acidiﬁed with 2% nitric acid immediately after collection. Rock
samples underwent detailed characterization using a combination of tech-
niques, including optical microscopy, surface proﬁlometry, Raman spec-
troscopy, and QXRD. Optical microscopy was used to visually examine
surface textures and identify surface changes. Surface proﬁlometry showed
differences in topography across the reacted surface. Reaction products were
identiﬁed using Raman spectroscopy based on characteristic band positions.
Additionally, QXRD analysis was conducted on olivine powders to provide
quantitative mineralogical composition of the reacted olivine samples using
corundum as a standard. This multi-method approach provides a com-
prehensive assessment of surface ev olution, dissolution features, and
mineralization products, allowing for a deeper understanding of the
mineralization reaction process.
