and precision medicine, particularly in cancer research and neu-

and locating the genes precisely within tissues 1,2. This profound
understanding of spatial context is crucial for unveiling the
mechanisms of complex diseases, as many pathological processes,

speciﬁc cell populations at speciﬁc locations
3. With advancements in

immune responses or aiding tumor cell survival and spread, which
traditional transcriptomics often overlook. A detailed understanding

tumor diversity but also has the potential to uncover new therapeutic

These breakthrough technologies which offer near single-cell
resolution, have enabled whole-genome analysis of gene expression at
speciﬁcally captured locations, known as spots, achieving cellular or
even subcellular resolution. In early research, a suite of algorithms was
developed to process ST data at low resolution. Notable among these
are SEDR
11, STAGATE12, and GraphST13, each employs distinct strategies
to process spatial data for a comprehensive spatial representation.
SEDR learns spatial embeddings within a masked self-supervised
learning framework utilizing variational graph self-encoders, while
GraphST and STAGATE integrate gene expression proﬁles with spatial
information through graph attention mechanisms and graph con-
trastive learning. Although these methods provide effective solutions,
their accuracy is primarily observed in low-resolution and simple tis-
sues. In complex tissue environments, especially at higher resolution,
these methods often fail to identify anomalous clusters and struggle to
capture subtle cellular interactions and microenvironmental factors.
This limitation restricts their ability to delineate and identify small or
rare cell populations with high speciﬁcity, ultimately affecting a clear
understanding of tissue heterogeneity and potential biomarkers.
Meanwhile, recent niche-based methods such as CellCharter and
BANKSY
14,15 have been developed to better capture how local micro-
environments shape cellular states in high-resolution ST data. How-
ever, both approaches rely heavily on neighborhood averaging that
can dilute the transcriptional signatures of rare cells. Moreover, their
implicit assumption of spatial smoothness, while bene ﬁcial for
enhancing structural coherence, can obscure local transcriptional
heterogeneity and thereby compromise the detection of rare or low-
abundance cellular states.
The spatial clustering task in ST focuses on grouping spatial spots
into spatial domains, but rare cell populations or small spatial regions
require additional separation from the main clusters. Many methods
developed to identify rare cell populations from high-resolution
molecular data are primarily designed for single-cell transcriptomics,
such as FiRE
16, GapClust17, TooManyCells18, GiniClust19,a n dS C M E R20.
These tools frequently substantial challenges when analyzing cancer or
tumor tissues. For instance, they may identify nonexistent cell popu-
lations as rare metastatic or mutated cells. Moreover, they fail to
accurately distinguish certain highly speci ﬁc immune cells or pre-
metastatic cells that are critical to the TME, due to the high degree of
overlap in gene expression patterns
18. Additionally, these traditional
methods, primarily for single-cell sequencing data, do not adequately
consider spatial location information, which hinders their effective-
ness in ST. This limits the ability to uncover the structural details of
complex tissues and reduces the resolution of tissue microstructure
analysis
15.
On the other hand, other models leverage probabilistic frame-
works to achieve more reﬁned spatial domain deﬁnitions, enabling the
identiﬁcation of rare cell populations from multiple perspectives; for
instance, Giotto21 combines Hidden Markov Random Field models with
a spatial neighborhood prior to improve the accuracy of cell popula-
tion localization. BayesSpace
22 employs a fully Bayesian framework,
integrating prior knowledge into cell population delineation for more
reﬁned spatial domain de ﬁnitions. SPACEL
23 utilizes deconvolution
techniques to locate rare cells by integrating cell type composition
with spatial coordinates. However, these approaches depend on spe-
ciﬁc statistical models or assumptions, and their effectiveness is lar-
gely determined by the quality of the prior knowledge used, such as
cell type composition derived from deconvolution. Additionally,
methods such as CeLEry
24,T a n g r a m25, and SpaOTsc26 sought to align
scRNA-seq datasets with spatial transcriptomic data, aiming to recover
the spatial origins of cells by leveraging both gene expression and
spatial location. However, these methods may be limited by factors
such as a small number of training samples, the quality of single-cell
datasets, and the time required for training. As a result, identifying rare
cells or small spatial domains often fails to yield the desired results.
Recently, the dynamic graph attention network is seen to selectively
focus on relevant features, adapting the attention weights as the
relationship between data points evolves
27. This adaptation is critical
for detecting rare cell populations and small spatial domains, as it
allows models to isolate subtle and meaningful patterns in gene
expression. Moreover, focusing attention on speci ﬁc spatial regions
can reveal differences that are critical for identifying rare cells or local
expression patterns, potentially leading to developing targeted
therapies and providing insights into histopathology. Thisﬁne-grained

Here, we introduce the GARDEN framework, a graph attention
adversarial self-encoder that employs a dynamic graph attention
mechanism alongside a spatially aware graph fusion contrastive
strategy to uncover rare heterogeneity within spatial omics data.
GARDEN begins by integrating spatial expression proﬁles with spatial
information via a dynamic attention mechanism to create spatial
embedding representations. To enhance the discriminability of these
embeddings, we propose a spatially aware graph fusion contrastive
training strategy in GARDEN to improve the discrimination of ﬁne-
grained variations by differentiating between arti ﬁcially-generated
spurious variants and real microenvironmental variants, enhancing
the model’s ability to accurately characterize the microenvironment
at each spatial point. The spatially-aware strategy expands the sam-
pling range and introduces additional a priori information, sig-
niﬁcantly improving the model ’s capacity to resolve spatial
representation heterogeneity. Evaluations of spatial transcriptome
datasets across multiple dimensions, species, and resolutions (e.g.,
10x Visium, Xenium, and Visium HD) indicates that GARDEN not only
surpasses existing methods in localizing rare cell types but also
uncovers the biological structures linked to these rare cell types and
their unique spatial transcription patterns. GARDEN also incorpo-
rates a multi-section registration module, enabling precise alignment
of consecutive tissue sections in three-dimensional space, thereby
facilitating more complete reconstruction and localization of rare
cell spatial distributions. Moreover, GARDEN has proven effective in
accurately identifying cellular phenotypes associated with Alzhei-
mer’s disease and multiple types of cancer, which are often over-
looked by other methodologies yet are crucial for understanding
disease progression. We further integrated an interpretable module
into GARDEN, which enables the identi ﬁcation of key immune and
regulatory modules involved in complex diseases, thereby paving the
way for deeper mechanistic insights and potential therapeutic stra-
tegies. Lastly, when applied to spatial chromatin accessibility data,
GARDEN has better structural identi ﬁcation when applied to spatial
chromatin accessibility data, highlighting successful adaptation to
different modalities. In general, our results demonstrate the unique
ability of GARDEN to discern rare heterogeneity, promising approa-
ches to early clinical detection.

Overview of the GARDEN workﬂow
The primary objective of GARDEN is to provide a robust analytical
framework to identify rare pathogenic cell populations using deep
learning techniques, as depicted in Fig.1. By integrating dynamic graph
attention mechanism with spatially aware graph fusion contrastive
training strategy, GARDEN enables a range of in-depth analyses tai-
lored to the intricate features of spatial omics data, including: (1)
Precise identiﬁcation of rare cell types, with a particular emphasis on
disease-relevant causative cells, (2) Spatio-temporal dynamics analysis
of complex biological processes, offering insight into disease pro-
gression; and (3) Adaptability to other spatial modalities, supporting
the analysis and extension of emerging data types.

In addition, we incorporated a spatially aware graph fusion con-
trastive strategy into GARDEN to ca pture and amplify the authentic
microenvironmental variability of different cells, thereby improving
the model ’s ability to handle microenvironmental heterogeneity
(Fig. 1c). Speciﬁcally, weﬁrst generate pseudo-mutant gene expression
proﬁles to simulate arti ﬁcial cellular environmental variations and
obtain the corresponding expression embeddings through a dynamic
graph attention network. Subsequently, GARDEN uses a spatially-
aware strategy with two main objectives:ﬁrst, to combine predeﬁned
neighborhoods and second-order neighborhoods, thus expanding the
contextual scope for capturing spatial relationships; and second, to
preserve the original graph structure to ensure local smoothing while
fusing real and pseudo-mutant embeddings. For example, in cancer
tissues, this spatially-aware strategy effectively captures a broader
range of microenvironmental information, enabling the transmission
and integration of data from dispersed cancer effector cells, metastatic
cells, and other molecular mechanisms. This approach aids in identi-
fying cancer regions linked to speciﬁc pathological processes. Finally,
the contrastive training strategy is incorporated via a discriminative
module to differentiate between the real microenvironment and the
artiﬁcially generated pseudo-mutant environment. By optimizing the
adversarial loss, GARDEN strengthens its ability to capture micro-
environmental differences between embedded representations and
gene expression recovery, thereby facilitating the precise localization
of rare cell types. To capture diverse rare-cell scenarios, we present a
comprehensive benchmarking and scalability assessment of GARDEN
in Supplementary Note 1: “Comprehensive Benchmarking of GARDEN
for Rare-Cell Detection and Scalability ” (including Supplementary
Notes 2– 8 and Supplementary Figs. 1 – 18 for the corresponding ana-
lyses and results).
To accurately identify and localize rare cells within the three-
dimensional tissue architecture, we extended GARDEN to perform 3D
alignment across multiple consecutive ST slices, thus enabling more
robust detection and localization of rare cell types within a uniﬁed 3D
framework (Fig. 1d). Speciﬁcally, GARDEN constructs an initial low-
dimensional representation by concatenating restart random walk
embeddings with low-dimensional gene expression representations.
Then, a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) then jointly optimizes feature
reconstruction and triplet losses, thereby aligning the cell embedding
distributions across slices. Finally, optimal transport is computed in
the embedding space to derive cell matching matrices between adja-
cent slices, from which rotation and translation matrices are estimated
to achieve spatial registration of the tissue slices.
GARDEN provides a more precise characterization of ST samples.
The versatile mechanisms of GARDEN equip it with robust capabilities
to address diverse analytical tasks. First, its dynamic attention
mechanism effectively captures heterogeneous signals of spatial
regions, enabling the effective identi ﬁcation of rare regions and
uncovering hidden biological patterns in complex samples (Fig. 1e).
Second, by constructing a 3D graph structure from stacked slices,
GARDEN not only delineates spatial domains in the three-dimensional
space, but also denoises gene expression data, thus provides a more
precise characterization of ST samples (Fig. 1f). Furthermore, lever-
aging its spatially aware graph fusion contrastive training strategy,
GARDEN effectively identi ﬁes microenvironmental differences
between disease regions and associated pathological cells, such as
metastatic cellular environments in cancer and pathogenic micro-
environments in Alzheimer’s disease (Fig. 1c). This approach offers
valuable insights into disease mechanisms, demonstrating GARDEN’s
adaptability to various pathological conditions. Finally, the ﬂexible
framework of GARDEN allows seamless extension to other data mod-
alities, broadening its potential applications across multiple domains
(Fig. 1g). In summary, GARDEN represents a powerful and versatile tool
for spatial analysis, integrating dynamic attention mechanisms, 3D
graph structures, and spatially aware graph fusion contrastive training
strategies to address complex biological questions.

Cross-species validation and rare region discovery
To evaluate the robust capability of GARDEN in ﬁne-grained spatial
delineation across diverse species, we applied it to brain datasets from
mice and American salamanders. These species are evolutionarily
distant and exhibit distinct neuroanatomical characteristics, serve as
an ideal framework for evaluating GARDEN ’s precision in identifying
and delineating complex spatial domains within varied biological
structures. This cross-species application not only demonstrates
GARDEN’s effectiveness in diverse organizational contexts but also
conﬁrms its precision and dependability in navigating complex and
distinct biological systems.
Initially, we tested our method on a STARmap PLUS dataset
derived from slices of adult mouse central nervous system (CNS),
providing single-cell resolution
36. This comprehensive dataset
encompasses 25,948 spots and 1022 genes, spanning various struc-
tures from the olfactory bulb to the cerebellum, including the medulla
(Fig. 3a). We applied GARDEN and other benchmark models to identify
and annotate 16 distinct cell states within these sections, achieving
alignments consistent with established annotations. Results illustrated
in Fig. 3b demonstrate that while each method effectively annotated
major regions, distinguishing features such as the olfactory bulb from
the cerebellum, GARDEN, GraphST, SEDR and BANKSY notably pro-
vided accurate annotations of the ventral striatum (STR) region, situ-
ated in the lower left corner of the brain section. In contrast, STAGATE
and CellCharter failed to identify this speci ﬁc structure entirely. We
also observed that GARDEN identi ﬁed subdivisions within the
LSX_HY_MB_HB region. Further analysis revealed that these spatial
domains differed substantially in both cell-type composition and gene
expression proﬁles, suggesting that they represent genuine biological
heterogeneity rather than artifacts arising from technical noise or
algorithmic processing (Supplementary Note 11 and Supplementary
Fig. 23). To quantitatively assess the performance of different methods
in recognizing spatial domains, we utilized both the external metric
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and the internal metric Average
Silhouette Width (ASW) to evaluate their clustering effectiveness
(Fig. 3c). The results indicate that GARDEN outperformed all other
methods in both metrics, highlighting its superior ability to
accurately identify and delineate complex spatial domains within the
dataset.
In particular, we observed that GARDEN effectively distinguished
the dorsoposterior part of the medulla region (MYdp), located at the
lower right side of the slice. This result aligns closely with the original
annotation, con ﬁrming the reliability of GARDEN in ﬁne spatial
recognition. In contrast, the other baseline methods often overlooked
this small region or erroneously merged it with larger adjacent areas,
overlooking critical biological features potentially. Such omissions can

Robust 3D tissue registration and rare cell localization
Although ST has greatly enhanced our ability to map cellular organi-
zation, most datasets remain con ﬁned to two-dimensional (2D) sec-
tions, limiting the accurate reconstruction of cellular distributions in a
true 3D tissue context. This poses particular challenges for rare,
sparsely distributed cell types, where isolated signals in individual
sections may reﬂect technical noise rather than true biological struc-
ture, leading to fragmented interpretations, false-positive assign-
ments, or complete omission of biologically relevant populations.
Moreover, because such cells appear only in select slices at extremely
low abundance, analyzing them without integrating the tissue depth
dimension obscures their alignment with anatomical landmarks and
pathological architectures, hindering accurate 3D reconstruction and
functional inference. To address these limitations, we extended GAR-
DEN to perform 3D alignment across multiple consecutive ST sections,
thus enabling more robust detection and localization of rare cell types
within a uniﬁed 3D framework.
To comprehensively evaluate the performance of GARDEN in
spatial alignment tasks, we performed systematic comparisons with
state-of-the-art ST alignment methods, including SANTO, SLAT, and
PASTE
41– 43, under various experimental conditions. Speci ﬁcally, we
utilized tissue sections generated by Visium and STARmap PLUS 44,45,
representing multi-cell and single-cell resolution scales, respectively.
Our initial assessment focused on the Visium human brain dataset,
where we applied various artiﬁcial rotations to simulate spatial trans-
formations. As illustrated in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 27a,
GARDEN consistently achieved accurate, robust alignment across all
rotation angles, whereas the performance of other methods deterio-
rated under certain transformations, occasionally showing clear mis-
matches. To quantitatively gauge alignment quality, we employed the
Pearson correlation coefﬁcient (PCC) and a cell-type matching index
(CI), assessing both expression similarity and cell-type consistency.
Results indicated that GARDEN successfully aligned every section at
every rotation condition, attaining the highest performance metrics
and demonstrating strong robustness across transformations (Fig.4c).
In contrast, SLAT performed well only at speci ﬁc angles, exhibiting
considerable variability when alignment conditions became more
challenging.
Subsequently, we applied the same evaluation procedure to the
STARmap PLUS dataset of the mouse hippocampal region to assess
GARDEN’s adaptability across different sequencing technologies. Our
results indicated that GARDEN accurately aligned key anatomical
landmarks, including CA1-CA3 and the dentate gyrus (DG), achieving a
structure-level spatial consistency that other methods failed to repli-
cate (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 27a). Notably, PASTE exhibited
alignment failures under certain conditions (e.g., 90-degree rotation),
likely due to its reliance on optimal transport, which may be sensitive
to large-scale structural variations and prone to local minima. Quan-
titative assessments based on PCC and CI further con ﬁrmed that
GARDEN achieved the best performance on this dataset as well, high-
lighting its robustness and generalizability across spatial resolutions
and technological platforms (Fig.4d).
Finally, we evaluated the global registration capabilities of dif-
ferent methods on a complete mouse brain hemisphere dataset to
assess their performance on complex biological structures. In this
dataset, the spacing between adjacent sections is relatively large
(~200 μm), posing a greater challenge for accurate alignment
46.W e
applied GARDEN, SANTO, SLAT, and PASTE to align all sections and
visualized the resulting reconstructions from multiple perspectives.
While SANTO, SLAT, and PASTE could achieve relatively stable align-
ment over only a limited number of consecutive slices, each exhibited
notable misalignments in several regions. These local discrepancies
accumulated throughout the tissue volume, ultimately distorting or
fragmenting the reconstructed 3D architecture (Fig. 4f and Supple-
mentary Fig. 27b). In contrast, GARDEN maintained consistent, con-
tinuous alignment across the entire series of sections, accurately
preserving the brain’s original 3D morphology (Fig. 4e). This demon-
strates GARDEN’s superior robustness and global coherence in hand-
ling large-scale tissue registration.
To assess GARDEN ’s capability in reconstructing three-
dimensional distributions of rare cell types, we constructed a cell
mesh model from the aligned tissue data and performed a 3D recon-
struction of the hippocampal architecture
36. Notably, the pyramidal
layer (HIP CA) and subiculum (HIP SUB) are anatomically adjacent,
functionally synergistic regions implicated in cognitive processes.
However, the associated rare cell types are present in only a subset of
tissue sections and exhibit extremely low abundance in each section-
often with only a few dozen or even single-digit expression spots-
making their spatial patterns difﬁcult to detect through conventional
2D slice analysis (Supplementary Note 13 and Supplementary Fig. 28).
Leveraging the GARDEN-aligned coordinates, we successfully recon-
structed the CA and SUB layers in 3D, revealing the true distribution of
these rare cells along the tissue depth axis (Fig. 4g, h). These results
demonstrate that GARDEN substantially enhances the localization
accuracy of rare cell types in 3D space, offering high applicability and
spatial resolution for in-depth tissue analysis. Furthermore, the align-
ment framework facilitates accurate detection of rare cell populations
by clustering algorithms, as joint clustering on GARDEN-aligned sam-
ples recovers biologically plausible M1 macrophages with stronger
signature signals than single-slice analysis. It also corrects misannota-
tions, thereby enhancing consensus across replicates and improving
clustering consistency (Supplementary Note 13 and Supplementary
Figs. 28, 29).

Discussion
ST is an advanced technology that enables in situ measurement of
gene expression at speci ﬁc tissue locations, with ongoing advance-
ments toward greater transcriptomic depth, higher spatial resolution,
and larger sample scales. Despite numerous existing methods for
analyzing spatial transcriptomic data, accurately characterizing rare
spatial expression heterogeneity remains a formidable challenge. This
paper introduces GARDEN, a computational framework that marries
dynamic attention mechanism with spatially aware graph fusion con-
trastive training to robustly identify rare cell types within spatial
transcriptomic datasets. The framework employs a dynamic graph
attention encoder to effectively extract spatial embeddings from
preprocessed gene expression matrices. A contrastive training strat-
egy is then applied to re ﬁne these embeddings by distinguishing
between arti ﬁcial perturbations and genuine microenvironmental
variations. To enhance the precision in quantifying expression dis-
tribution and microenvironmental differences at each spatial point, we
propose a spatially-aware sampling approach that incorporates addi-
tional prior information, enriching the model ’s capability to capture
detailed microenvironmental features and thereby boosting its proﬁ-
ciency in identifying rare cell types.
To explore the advantages of GARDEN in capturing rare spatial
expression heterogeneity, we conducted extensive benchmarking
analyses across a variety of spatial transcriptome datasets, encom-
passing diverse species, dimensions, and resolutions (e.g., 10x Visium,
Xenium, and Visium HD). Our results consistently show that GARDEN
surpasses existing methods, notably enhancing the precision of spatial
regional localization and the accurate identiﬁcation of rare cell types.
These ﬁndings conﬁrm the robustness and effectiveness of GARDEN in
capturing spatial heterogeneity and distinguishing subtle micro-
environmental variations.Furthermore, the GARDEN framework was
applied to spatial chromatin accessibility data, highlighting the
potential applicability of its processing strategy to other spatial
modalities.
Our study demonstrates that GARDEN effectively identi ﬁes rare
and discretely distributed disease-related regions in disease samples,
contrasting with the coarser spatial domain distributions identiﬁed by
traditional ST methods. In the mouse Alzheimer ’s disease model,
GARDEN not only uncover normal tissue structures missed by other
methods, but also precise ly reveals highly co-localized disease-asso-
ciated cells in amyloid β plaques-biomarkers closely linked to disease
progression-and efﬁciently visualizes the interactions between these
cells. More critically, the rare pathogenic regions identi ﬁed by GAR-
DEN are often implicated in potential metastatic mechanisms. In
nasopharyngeal carcinoma samples, GARDEN accurately identi ﬁes
tiny, dispersed TLS and uncovers their distinct immune states. In
human breast cancer tissue data, GARDEN detects extremely rare type
1 macrophages near the DCIS region, highlighting their potential
involvement in multiple cancer metastatic pathways. This ﬁnding
deepens our understanding of breast cancer metastasis and under-
scores the broad applicability and potential of GARDEN to reveal
complex tissue structures.
Finally, GARDEN offers a range of functional modules designed to
deepen the analysis of rare cell types. Among these, the multi-section
registration module enables precise 3D reconstruction of rare cell
distributions, thereby retaining critical spatial signals and clarifying
cell-to-cell interactions within the intact tissue context. This capability
provides insights into the spatial organization of neural activity. In
addition, GARDEN’s interpretable module assists researchers in iden-
tifying key immune and regulatory modules within complex disease
tissues, laying a theoretical foundation for re ﬁned disease subtyping
and the development of individualized treatment strategies.
In summary, GARDEN has demonstrated excellent performance in
quantitatively characterizing rare spatial expression heterogeneity,
providing insights for disease diagnosis, studying mechanisms of dis-
ease progression, and identifying potential therapeutic targets.
Although GARDEN has made signi ﬁcant advances in ST, it is not
without limitations. A major challenge is the dependence on the
quality and resolution of the input data. Lower resolution or poor-
quality datasets can severely impact the model’s ability to accurately
capture and interpret subtle spatial and cellular interactions. In

Simulation dataset generation setup
We generated simulated datasets using the simulation framework
provided by scCube (version 2.0.1)80,108.S p e c iﬁcally, we systematically
varied two key factors: the proportion of rare cells and the degree of
spatial continuity. To create datasets with random spatial patterns, we
employed the scCube.generate_pattern_random()function. For
the proportion of rare cells, we applied the sc.pp.subsample()
function to downsample the anchored rare cell types in the single-cell
data using Scanpy (version 1.9.6)
96, setting their proportions to 0.5%,
1%, and 3%, respectively. For spatial continuity, we adjusted the para-
meter delta in scCube.generate_pattern_random()to 5, 15, 30,
50, and 200, thereby controlling different levels of spatial smoothness.
