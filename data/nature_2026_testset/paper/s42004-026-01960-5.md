The use of small molecules as ﬂuorescent DNA markers has been extensively explored since the mid-
1960s, revealing limitations in designing selective probes due to the largely missing relationship
between probe structure and DNA conformation. Here, we present a multistep computational protocol
applied to a recently proposed ﬂuorescent marker, QCy(MeBT) ₃, capable of simultaneously
recognizing both B-DNA and G-quadruplex DNA through distinct emission signatures. Our protocol
identiﬁes the conformations that enable selective probe–DNA binding and predicts a remarkably high
afﬁnity. Moreover, these same conformations are responsible for the different absorption and
ﬂuorescence shifts observed experimentally upon binding to B-DNA or G-quadruplex. Overall, we
establish a fundamental principle for predicting the performance of ﬂuorescent probes in complex
biological environments: the properties of speci ﬁc molecular conformations determine their
photobiophysical fate upon binding to a given DNA sequence, and thus their ability to recognize a
particular DNA topology.
DNA recognition and selectivity are fundamental for understanding the
structural diversity of nucleic acids and their role in regulating biological
function1. Precise molecular recognition enables the development of precise
probes and therapeutic agents that can distinguish between different DNA
conformations
2. This capability is critical not only for elucidating genome
organization and dynamics3 but also for advancing biomedical applications
including diagnostics, drug delivery, and cancer therapy4. Different DNA
conformations can be selectively tar geted depending on the biological
function of interest. In addition to double-stranded DNA—which includes
the B, A, and Z forms (with B-DNA being the most common) —non-
canonical structures are also relevant. Among these, G-quadruplex DNA
(G4), formed by stacked guanine tetrads and commonly found in telomeres
and gene promoters, has attracted signi ﬁcant attention due to its high
relevance in potential anticancer therapies5,6.
The use of small molecules for DNA recognition and selectivity has
been a strategy attempted for many years, dating back to the 1950s, when
actinomycin, mitomycin, and other intercalators, were introduced as che-
motherapeutic agents
7. Since then, the ﬁeld of DNA-binding small mole-
cules is still considerably active, with substantial progress over the decades8,9.
Usually, these molecules are categorized by their DNA-binding modes, such
as major and minor groove binders, intercalators, or operating by top (also
called end) stacking. Nevertheless, this poses the attention solely on the type
of binding mode, lacking information about the probe’s structure. Common
structural categories include planar molecules, tweezer-shaped molecules,
and star-shaped molecules. Structure-function relationships indicate that
groove binders have usually curved shapes matching the DNA groove
contour, intercalators include planar aromatic rings, tweezer-shaped
molecules often show induced-ﬁt binding, and star-shaped molecules can
allow multi-site DNA recognition
2,10.
Small molecules can enable various detection methods, with emissive
spectroscopic signatures—such as ﬂuorescence and phosphorescence—
being commonly used. This requires the molecular probe to be a chromo-
phore with an absorption wavelength ideally falling within the so-called
therapeutic window (650–950 nm) to ensure optimal tissue penetration.
This, in turn, would lead to emission at longer wavelengths in the near-
infrared region, which is typically required for in vivo detection, due to
reduced light absorption and autoﬂuorescence by tissues
11,12.R e g a r d i n gt h e
type of emission, ﬂuorescence is usually preferred over phosphorescence
because, when properly tuned, it provides a more intense signal, a parti-
cularly desirable feature when strong emission is needed to overcome low
signal-to-noise ratios in living tissue
13.

In this work, we aim to address this gap by applying a purposefully
designed multistep simulation approach that integrates multiple levels of
theory and computational techniques (Scheme1): at ﬁrst, we hypothesized
that the star-shaped architecture of QCy(MeBT)₃ does not merely provide
three conjugated branches but rather introduces conformationalﬂexibility
within each branch. This implies that, to properly simulate the distinct
binding behaviors with B-DNA and G4 —and the resulting emissive
properties—it is crucial to develop a force ﬁeld capable of accurately
representing the vast range of QCy(MeBT)₃ conformations, hence enabling
consistent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.
Given that the probe –DNA binding modes were initially proposed
considering only one possible QCy(MeBT)₃ conformation for the spectra
simulation, we performed separate molecular docking of each chemically
relevant probe’s conformation, to obtain plausible initial complex structures
for subsequent MD simulations on both ground and excited electronic
states. The structural insights derived from these simulations were then used
to evaluate binding energies and, by applying a hybrid quantum mechanics/
molecular mechanics (QM/MM) approach, to calculate the absorption and
ﬂuorescence spectra (see “Methods” section and Supporting Information
(SI), §1). These computed spectra can be directly compared to the available
experimental data
22, that is, the spectroscopic signatures of the QCy(-
MeBT)₃ probe.
Our results reveal a subtle interplay between the conformational
landscape of the QCy(MeBT) ₃ probe and its selective recognition of a
speciﬁc DNA form. Indeed, conformational interconversion of the parent
QCy–DT was shown to determine an induced- ﬁt mechanism, already
suggesting the relevance of the innerﬂexibility of this type of molecule, even
when including only two branches 23. Ultimately, our results indicate the
structural and electronic factors that lead to distinct absorption and emis-
sion spectra when the probe is free in solution or complexed with either
B-DNA or G4.

Conclusions
In this study, we have built a computational protocol through a multistep
approach to rationalize the mechanisms underlying the experimental
ﬁndings of the ﬁrst dual-color switch-onﬂuorescent probe able to bind at
t h es a m et i m et w od i f f e r e n tD N As t r u c t u r e s :G - q u a d r u p l e x e s( G 4 )a n d
double-helix B-DNA. As a guideline for designing small organic molecules
that act as selective ﬂuorescent bio-probes, we highlight the fundamental
importance of recognizing the possible binding modes associated with each
probe conformation. This understanding can signiﬁcantly aid in predicting
probe selectivity toward speciﬁc DNA structures, following a lock-and-key
recognition mechanism. In particular, we have identi ﬁed which QCy(-
MeBT)₃ conformations are responsible for binding to G4 and which to
B-DNA. In spite of the torsionalﬂexibility offered by the probe, we can safely
assign a distinct and stable binding mode pattern when forming a complex
with G4 or B-DNA. This makes it possible to reliably calculate the inter-
action energy of QCy(MeBT) ₃ toward both targets, ﬁnding out (after
comparison with available experimental data) a remarkably high afﬁnity,
comparable with some of the most known G4 stabilizers. This could be
taken into consideration for future theranostic applications, since the sta-
bilization of telomeric G4 sequences hindering the telomerase activity could
be coupled to its switch-onﬂuorescence properties.
Regarding solely the diagnostic application (i.e., its function as a probe),
we have elucidated the basis of the absorption and emission once the
ligand–DNA complexes are formed. We have shown how our protocol
quantitatively reproduces the absor ption spectra; moreover, underlying
how the initial experimental design concept of a probe with a donor core and
three acceptor branches cannot be accomplished by QCy(MeBT)₃, since a
limited charge transfer from the quinone to only two branches is observed.
Future investigations could there fore rely on a priori computational
approaches to clarify the nature of the absorption process, considering that a
red-shifted absorption usually results in a red-shifted emission, thus opti-
mizing the design of aﬂuorescent probe. Moreover, we have demonstrated
how the absorption intensity at a certa in excitation wavelength experi-
mentally selected can result in a differentﬂuorescence intensity, and that this
strongly depends on the speciﬁcp r o b e’s conformation. Even more inter-
estingly, we have shown that the ﬂuorescence wavelengths of the different

Methods
The proposed computational protocol is based on a multistep approach
composed of distinct stages and levels of theory:
Quantum chemistry: geometry optimization andﬂuorescence
spectra in water
All possible QCy(MeBT)₃ conformations, avoiding artiﬁcial steric clashes,
were manually generated rotating around the bonds depicted in Scheme1.
The ground state (S 0) geometry of each conformer was optimized using
Density Functional Theory (DFT) 44 at the PBE0/6-31 + G*45–49 level of
theory in an implicit water solvent using the Integral Equation Formalism
variant of the Polarizable Continuum Method (IEFPCM)50 as implemented
in Gaussian1651. The choice of the functional was based on previous
studies21,22 of DNA-interacting molecules of this type, including the speciﬁc
molecule studied here. The basis set was shown to produce results with
negligible difference compared to those obtained using the basis set from the
aforementioned works (6-311++G**) at a lower computational cost (see
SI, §1.8). The same functional and basis set were used to optimize the
geometry of each conformer in the ﬁrst excited state (S

Dependent DFT52 (TDDFT) formalism. All S0 and S1 optimized geometries
were conﬁrmed to be minima by frequency calculations, not resulting in any
imaginary frequency. The relative population of each QCy(MeBT)₃ con-

Boltzmann distribution analysis (see SI, §1.2). Starting from the S 1 opti-

Molecular dynamics and binding energy estimation
As resulted from molecular docking, the best QCy(MeBT) 3/B-DNA and
QCy(MeBT)3/G4 structures were considered as initial geometries for run-
ning 300 ns MD in S 0 for each QCy(MeBT)3 conformer. For comparison
purposes, MD simulations were also run starting from the S0 (DFT) opti-
mized geometry of each QCy(MeBT) 3 conformer in explicit water. All
calculations were performed using the GPU version of Amber22 68.I na l l
cases, the system was solvated with TIP3P 75 water molecules within a
truncated octahedral box, ensuring a solvent shell of at least 12 Å from any
solute molecule. Due to the charge of the system, K + ions were added to
neutralize the complexes, while Cl − ions were added to neutralize
QCy(MeBT)3 in explicit water. Both B-DNA and G4 were described using
the OL1576 force ﬁeld, whereas the ligand was described through the ab-
initio parametrized force ﬁeld for S 0 described above. First, each binding
pose was minimized for 2000 steps using the steepest-descent algorithm and
then a maximum of 18,000 steps using the conjugate gradient algorithm.
Then, it was progressively heated for 2 ns from 100 K to 298 K in the NVT
ensemble using a 1 fs timestep controlling the temperature by means of the
Langevin thermostat

hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm 78.A f t e r -
wards, the density was equilibrated w ith an isotropic position scaling
through a 2 ns (1 fs timestep) simulation in the NPT ensemble, using per-
iodic boundary conditions. The temperature was controlled as in the heating
process, and the pressure was kept at 1 bar by the Monte Carlo barostat79.

Hybrid quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics: absorption
and ﬂuorescence spectra when bound to DNA
The MD segments exhibiting the highest binding afﬁnity were selected and,
if a selected conformation–binding mode pair was described by a segment of
< 300 ns, the corresponding MD simulation was extended, ensuring that no
conformational change occurs for a total of 300 ns, thus consistently
representing all selected segments. Additionally, for each chosen segment, a
further 300 ns MD was carried out on S
1 (again checking that no change in
conformation occurs) using the forceﬁeld built for the excited state. From
each of these S0 and S1 extended trajectories, 100 equally spaced snapshots
were extracted for single-point Quantum Mechanics/Molecular Mechanics
(QM/MM) calculations, using Cobramm 2.087 as an interface.
QCy(MeBT)3 was treated at the QM TD-DFT level, while the sur-
rounding DNA and solvent environment were described using MM. For the
snapshots extracted from S0 simulations, the lowestﬁve excited states (Sn,
n =1 –5) were computed, and Gaussian convolution of the resulting vertical
transitions (S0→Sn) across all snapshots was used to generate the simulated
absorption spectra for each conformation–binding mode pair. An analo-
gous procedure was applied to the S 1 simulations, yielding the corre-
sponding S1 → S0 emission spectra.
To obtain an absorption spectrum d irectly comparable with experi-
mental results, a weighted sum of all the absorption spectra was performed,
using as weights the relative populations of each conformation –binding
mode pair. For the emission spectrum, anadditional weight was added, i.e.,
the absorption oscillator strength of each conformation–binding mode pair
corresponding to the excitation wavelengths used experimentally to obtain
the ﬂuorescence spectra (see SI, §1.7).
