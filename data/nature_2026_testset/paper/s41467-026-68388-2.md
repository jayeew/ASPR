increase in the proportion of TPRX1-EGEP+ cells within totipotent-like

expression. Moreover, our ﬁndings revealed that OTX2 regulated

To identify upstream regulators o ft h e8 C L Cp r o g r a m ,w ee m p l o y e d
TOBIAS, a footprinting framework that enables genome-wide analysis of
transcription factor (TF) binding dynamics

to analyze key TFs involved in ZGA using published ATAC-seq datasets
of human preimplantation embryos at various developmental stages

Our investigation focused on the cellular transitions initiated before and
during ZGA, particularly the transition from the 4-cell (4C) to the 8C
stage, as well as from the 8C stage to the inner cell mass (ICM) stage.
TF binding plots created by TOBIAS revealed a shift of TF activity
for PRD-like homeobox genes, including OTXs, CUXs, ONECUTs,
DPRX, and PITXs at the human 8C stage compared to 4C stage or ICM
(Fig. 1a–c), suggesting a role of homeobox genes in regulating ZGA at
8C stage. To further elucidate key regulators, we constructed a TF
regulatory network integrating gene expression data of 4C and 8C
embryos. Among these factors, OTX2 emerged as a central regulator
with high expression preceding and around the 8C stage, suggesting
its critical role in ZGA regulation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Further ana-
lysis of OTX2 gene expression at the mRNA level in early embryos
revealed thatOTX2 expression is high at the zygote, 2 C, and 4C stages
but decreases from the 8C to the blastocyst/ICM stage (Fig. 1d, e),
suggesting thatOTX2 may function as a maternal factor. Therefore, we
focused on the potential role of OTX2 in the next investigation.

OTX2 negatively regulates the transition from primed hPSCs
to 8CLCs
To monitor the dynamics of 8CLC generation, we ﬁrst constructed a
TPRX1-EGFP knock-in reporter in the primed hPSCs using CRISPR/Cas9
technology (Fig.2a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). We applied a modiﬁed
version of the established 4CL/e4CL step-wise protocol to induce 8CLCs
by replacing TSA with abexinostat, a pan-HDAC inhibitor (Fig.2b). This
modiﬁed e4CL condition (termed me4CL) achieved efﬁcient induction
of 16.64% (± 1.31%) of TPRX1-EGFP
+ cells with round domed morphology
(Fig.2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 2b). RT-qPCR analysis revealed an
upregulation of totipotency genes such as TPRX1, ARGFX, ZSCAN4,
LEUTX, ZNF280A,a n d DUXA, and downregulation of primed plur-
ipotency genes such asOCT4 and SOX2 in me4CL cells (Supplementary
Fig. 2c). Naive pluripotency genesKLF17and DPPA3were upregulated in
the intermediate 4CL cells, and further upregulated in me4CL cells
(Supplementary Fig. 2c), mirroring their in vivo expression patterns,
where both genes are highly expressed at the 8C stage
11. Since 8C are
characterized by the expression of TEs, particularly members of HERVL
families, we examined the expression levels ofMLT2A1 and HERVL-int,
which are LTR and intermediate elements associated with HERVL,
respectively. Notably, both ofMLT2A1 and HERVL-intwere upregulated
after me4CL treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Transcriptome analysis
of stage-speciﬁc genes revealed distinct expression pro ﬁles corre-
sponding to primed, naive (4CL condition), and 8CLC (me4CL condi-
tion) stages, supporting the robust induction of totipotent-like me4CL
cells from primed and naive hPSCs (Supplementary Fig. 2d).
Next, we sought to assess the role of OTX2 in 8CLC reprogram-
ming from primed hPSCs. We knocked out OTX2 using CRISPR/Cas9
based on TPRX1-EGFP reporter hPSCs (Fig. 2e and Supplementary
Fig. 2e, f). After the step-wise treatment, we observed an obvious
increase of the TPRX1-EGFP
+ population in OTX2-KO me4CL cells
compared to wild-type (WT) me4CL cells (Fig. 2d). Flow cytometry
analysis showed that ~28.05% TPRX1-EGFP + cells were generated in
OTX2-KO me4CL cells (Fig.2f, g). The proportion of 8CLCs was notably
higher in OTX2-KO me4CL cells, as evidenced by the upregulation of
key 8C-speciﬁc genes and TEs such as LEUTX, TPRX1, ZSCAN4, DUXA,
ZNF280A, HERVL-int and MLT2A1 along with the downregulation of
typical primed pluripotency markers such as SOX2 and NANOG
(Fig. 2h). Immunoﬂuorescence analysis further veriﬁed the upregula-
tion of 8C-speci ﬁc markers TPRX1, ZSCAN4, and SCL34A2, and the
downregulation of OCT4 and SOX2 at the protein level in OTX2-KO
me4CL cells (Fig. 2i). These results strongly suggest that OTX2 nega-
tively regulates the transition of 8CLCs from primed hPSCs.
To further validate the role of OTX2 in totipotency induction, we
overexpressedOTX2 in both WT and OTX2-KO hPSCs, followed by step-
wise treatment. Overexpression of OTX2 (OTX2-OE) led to a reduced
proportion of TPRX1-EGFP
+ cells in WT me4CL cells (Fig.2d, j and Sup-
plementary Fig. 2g, h), along with a downregulation of 8C-speciﬁcg e n e s
(Fig. 2k). Importantly, overexpression ofOTX2 in OTX2-KO me4CL cells
restored the repressive effect of OTX2 in 8CLC induction, as evidenced
by the decreased expression of 8C-speciﬁc genes and TEs (Fig.2l). Con-
sistently, immunoﬂuorescence staining demonstrated that OTX2 and
TPRX1 are expressed mutually exclusively in distinct populations of
me4CL cells (Supplementary Fig. 2i). To test whether OTX2 acts as a
universal repressor of totipotency, we performed additional experiments
using the spliceosomal repression protocol
12. We observed a slight
repressive trend of ZGA-like and pre-ZGA genes by OTX2 in both short-
term PlaB treatment (72 h) and hTBLC- passage 3 (P3) conditions (Sup-
plementary Fig. 2j). However, the effect was much less pronounced
compared to that observed in our 8CLC induction protocol, likely
because our hPSC line could tolerate only a low PlaB concentration
(1 nM), which may be insufﬁcient to fully induce a totipotent-like state
under these conditions. Theseﬁndings collectively suggest that OTX2
acts as a negative regulator of 8CLC reprogramming from primed hPSCs.

To further characterize these cells at single-cell level, we per-
formed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). We integrated scRNA-
seq datasets from previously published totipotent-like induction
studies-Mazidet al. (4CL and e4CL cells) and Li et al. (hTBLCs), as well
as in vivo human embryo datasets. UMAP visualization showed that all
data generally aligned well with previously reported developmental
stages (Fig. 3j). Notably, OTX2-KO and WT me4CL cells distributed
along the continuum from E3 to E5, with a small subset clustering near
E3 and 8C blastomeres (Fig.3j and Supplementary Fig. 3a), supporting
the acquisition of 8CLC identity at the single-cell level. Further analysis
of our own 4CL and me4CL populations revealed two major clusters
(Fig. 3k). One cluster included OTX2-KO and WT 4CL cells as well as a
subset of WT me4CL cells, which predominantly expressed naive and
primed pluripotency markers (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c).
The other cluster consisted of OTX2-KO me4CL cells and the remain-
ing WT me4CL cells, enriched for a panel of 8CLC-speci ﬁc genes
(Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). A dot plot showed that OTX2-
KO me4CL cells had a higher proportion and expression level of 8CLC/
morula markers, with marked downregulation of primed pluripotency
genes (Fig. 3l). Interestingly, we consistently observed the down-
regulation of epithelial genes under OTX2-KO compared to WT con-
dition in me4CL, but not in 4CL cells (Supplementary Fig. 3d),
suggesting that OTX2’s regulation on epithelial cell fate may facilitate
8CLC induction.
We further focused on me4CL cells under both WT and OTX2-
KO conditions. Unsupervised clustering identi ﬁed seven clusters,
with cluster 2 (C2) exhibiting a strong transcriptional resemblance to
E3-stage embryos (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The C2 population was
signiﬁcantly larger in OTX2-KO cultures (25.48%) than in WT
(16.76%), consistent with TPRX1-GFP proportion analyzed by ﬂow
cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). This 8C-like identity of C2 cells
was supported by their high correlation with E3 cells (Supplementary
Fig. 4c) and signiﬁcant enrichment of 8C-speciﬁc gene sets via GSVA
(Supplementary Fig. 4d). Together, these data support that OTX2
deletion increases the proportion of 8C-like cells at the single-
cell level.
To assign developmental identities to these subpopulations, we
employed a recently developed human embryo reference mapping
tool

clustered primarily with epiblast cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). WT

The ability of early blastomeres like 8C to contribute to both
embryonic and extraembryonic tissues characterizes them as toti-

totipotent-like developmental potential, we assessed their differ-
entiation potential into embryonic (ICM) and extra-embryonic
(trophoblast, TE) lineages in chimeric blastocysts. We injected
EGFP-labeled WT and OTX2-KO me4CL cells into mouse 8C
embryos, then cultured these embryos in vitro until the blastocyst
stage (Fig. 5a). We found that both WT and OTX2-KO me4CL cells
could contribute to ICM and TE, while OTX2-KO me4CL cells
showed higher overall chimerism ef ﬁciency (78.7% vs. 61.3%) and
contributed more to TE (16.4% vs. 4.5%) and ICM&TE (8.2% vs. 2.3%)
(Fig. 5b, c). By co-staining ICM and TE markers in injected blas-
tocyst, we con ﬁrmed that injected EGFP

CDX2+ TE and SOX2 + ICM (Fig. 5d).
Following transplantation of chimeric embryos into pseudo-
pregnant mice, we detected human mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) in
both E6.5 and E12.5 mouse conceptuses (Supplementary Fig. 7a –d),
indicating successful integration and persistence of human cells
during development. Further analysis of E12.5 chimeric conceptuses
revealed clear EGFP
+ cell contribution to fetal, yolk sac, and placenta
tissues, particularly in chimeric embryos injected with OTX2-KO
me4CL cells, and to a lesser extent, WT me4CL cells, but not in
uninjected control embryos, consistent with the E12.5 human mtDNA
quantiﬁcations (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 7a –d). Immunos-

conﬁrming differentiation of injected me4CL cells into extra-

tion of EGFP intensity in placental sections revealed a higher
contribution of OTX2-KO me4CL cells compared with WT me4CL
cells (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Together, these results demonstrate
that OTX2-KO me4CL cells exhibit enhanced capacity to contribute
to both embryonic and extraembryonic lineages.
OTX2 primarily regulates the transition from naive to
totipotent-like state
Next, we sought to determine which stage of the transition from
primed hPSCs to 8CLCs is in ﬂuenced by OTX2. Principal component
analysis (PCA) of our samples with human embryonic samples and
hESCs revealed that the transition mirrors a trajectory from blas-
tocysts/hESCs to morula to 8C (Fig. 6a). PCA revealed that OTX2-KO
had minimal impact on the identity of primed hPSCs compared to
WT, a moderate effect on the naive-like identity of 4CL cells, and a
pronounced impact on the 8CLC identity of me4CL cells (Fig. 6a).
Chromatin accessibility dynamics using ATAC-seq data yielded
similar results, showing that OTX2-KO has the most signi ﬁcant
impact on me4CL cells, while minimal impact on primed hPSCs and
4CL cells (Fig. 6b).
To explore OTX2 ’s role in developmental progression, we pro-
jected these cells, alongside sorted TPRX1-GFP⁺ cells from OTX2-KO
condition and published bulk RNA-seq datasets, onto the human
embryo reference map
22. The resulting trajectory of cell populations
during the 8CLC transition mirrored a progression from epiblast to
ICM, then to a prelineage state andﬁnally to morula (Fig.6c). OTX2-KO
cells diverged most prominently from WT at the me4CL stage, with
minimal differences at the primed and 4CL stages (Fig. 6c). Sorted
TPRX1-GFP⁺ cells occupied the trajectory endpoint and clustered clo-
sely with morula-stage cells. Notably, me4CL cells under both WT and
OTX2-KO conditions approached this endpoint of trajectory more
closely than E4CL-D5 cells (Mazid et al.). Stage-speci ﬁcg e n ee x p r e s -
sion analysis con ﬁrmed stronger activation of 8CLC genes and TEs
alongside greater downregulation of primed pluripotency genes in
OTX2-KO compared to WT me4CL cells (Fig. 6d). Together, these
ﬁndings indicate that OTX2 primarily regulates the transition to the
totipotent-like state by modulating both transcriptomic proﬁles and
chromatin accessibility.
We further examined OTX2 ’s stage-speci ﬁc effects during the
transition. As a known marker of primed pluripotency, OTX2 showed
limited impact on primed hPSCs, with 807 DEGs identi ﬁed upon
knockout, but no signiﬁcant changes in core pluripotency genes like
OCT4, NANOG,a n d SOX2 (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). In
contrast, OTX2 deletion had a stronger effect on 4CL cells, with 2,414
DEGs compared to WT (Fig. 6e–g). Notably, key naive pluripotency
genes such as KLF17 and DPPA3 were downregulated in OTX2-KO 4CL
cells (Fig. 6f), suggesting that OTX2 plays a role in the transition to
the naive stem cell state. GO analysis of these DEGs revealed
enrichment in developmental and differentiation processes (Fig.6g).
ATAC-seq analysis showed that OTX2 deletion signiﬁcantly increased
chromatin accessibility at 8C-speci ﬁc genes such as TPRX1, DUXB ,
and LEUTX, with moderate effects on naive pluripotency genes like
DPPA5, and minimal impact on primed pluripotency genes (Supple-
mentary Fig. 8c).

OTX2 binds to 8C-speciﬁc sites as a repressive factor
To explore the regulatory role of OTX2, we employed the CUT&Tag
technique, which allows for precise mapping of protein-DNA interac-
tions at speciﬁc genomic loci. We ﬁrst analyzed the binding dynamics
of OTX2 during the transition from primed hPSCs to 8CLCs. The
binding sites were categorized into four distinct clusters: primed-
speciﬁc, naive-speciﬁc, 8CLC-speciﬁc, and shared sites (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 9a). Notably, primed-speci ﬁca n d8 C L C - s p e c iﬁc sites were
found to be distributed around primed- and 8C-speci ﬁc genes
respectively, suggesting a potential connection between OTX2 binding
and gene expression during the transition (Supplementary Fig. 9b).
To further elucidate how OTX2 binding inﬂuences 8CLC identity,
we compared the OTX2 binding sites in OTX2-OE versus WT me4CL
cells. We identi ﬁed unique binding sites that either increased or
decreased following OTX2 overexpression, corresponding to the
change of OTX2 motif enrichment (Fig. 7a). The OTX2-OE-speci ﬁc
binding sites were primarily distributed around 8C-speci ﬁcg e n e s ,
while WT-speciﬁc binding sites were inversely correlated with these
genes (Fig. 7b). GSEA revealed that the expression of these 8C-speciﬁc
genes was signiﬁcantly downregulated in OTX2-OE me4CL cells, sug-
gesting that OTX2 binding suppresses their gene expression (Fig.7c).
Speciﬁcally, OTX2 binding signals on 8C-speciﬁc genes, such asTPRX1,
ZSCAN4, DUXB,a n d DPPA3,w e r es i g n iﬁcantly increased in OTX2-OE
me4CL cells, displaying an inverse correlation with their gene
expression patterns (Fig. 7d). Interestingly, although OTX2 binding
signals at primed pluripotency genes includingOCT4 and NANOG were
elevated upon OTX2 overexpression, their gene expression levels were
also higher, indicating a distinct regulatory mechanism of OTX2 in
pluripotency and totipotency genes (Fig.7d). Furthermore, we found
that OTX2 showed increased binding signals at 8C-speciﬁcT El o c is u c h
as MLT2A1, MLT2A2, HERVL-int,a n d LTY7B in OTX2-OE compared to
WT me4CL cells (Fig. 7e). Collectively, these ﬁndings suggest that
OTX2 binds to 8C-speci ﬁc sites to repress their expression, con-
tributing to the regulation of 8CLC identity.
OTX2 functions independently of DUX4
DUX4 has been identiﬁed as a crucial regulator of human ZGA and a
key TF that drives ZGA gene expression in primed hPSCs in vitro3–6.T o
explore the relationship between OTX2 and DUX4 during the 8CLC
transition, we conducted a series of experiments. Initially, we observed
that neither OTX2-KO nor OTX2-OE me4CL cells express DUX4
(Fig. 8a), and CUT&Tag sequencing showed no signi ﬁcant OTX2
binding at the DUX4 locus (Fig.8b), suggesting the regulatory function
of OTX2 is not mediated through DUX4. Additionally, a previous study
indicated that DUX4 overexpression does not signi ﬁcantly alter the
expression of OTX2 (Fig.8c). These results indicate that the repressive
role of OTX2 in regulating 8CLC transition is independent of DUX4. We
further explored whether these two factors function in parallel to
regulate the transition to the 8CLC state. Comparing upregulated
genes in OTX2-KO me4CL cells with known DUX4 targets
23 revealed a
signiﬁcant overlap (Fig. 8d), suggesting that although OTX2 does not
directly regulate DUX4, it may affect DUX4 target gene expression
through alternative pathways.
To further explore the potential parallel functions of OTX2 and
DUX4, we overexpressedDUX4 in both OTX2-KO and WT hPSCs. After
step-wise induction, DUX4 overexpression led to an increase in TPRX1-
EGFP
+ proportion (Fig. 8e and Supplementary Fig. 10), as well as the
activation of key 8C-speci ﬁc genes and TEs such as TPRX1, ZSCAN4,
HERVL-int, DPPA3 and KLF17 in WT me4CL cells (Fig. 8f). Notably, in
OTX2-KO me4CL cells, DUX4 overexpression further enhanced TPRX1-
EGFP
+ proportion and the expression of 8C-speciﬁc marker genes and
TEs (Fig. 8e, f and Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting that DUX4 can
augment the effects of OTX2 de ﬁciency in the 8CLC transition from
primed hPSCs.
Transcriptomic analysis revealed that DUX4 overexpression
induced signiﬁcant gene expression changes in both WT and OTX2-
KO me4CL cells, including the upregulation of a panel of 8C-speci ﬁc
genes, with a more pronounced effect observed in OTX2-KO cells
(Fig. 8g, h). By comparing the DEGs between different groups, we
found that DUX4-OE and OTX2-KO resulted in a similar number of
DEGs, with a small subset of DEGs overlapped including key ZGA
genes such as Z SCAN5B, ZSCAN5A, DUXA, ZSCAN4, LEUTX, ARGFX,
PITX1,a n d PRAMEFs etc. (Fig. 8i). These ﬁndings suggest that OTX2
likely acts as a repressor that affects some of DUX4 target genes,
while DUX4 can independently drive the expression of ZGA-related
genes, enhancing the reprogramming potential in the absence
of OTX2.
Discussion
In this study, we investigate the role of OTX2 in the transition of
primed hPSCs to a totipotent 8C-like state, uncovering its crucial
function as a repressive barrier and emphasizing its signi ﬁcance in
early embryonic development and stem cell biology (Fig.8j).

Mouse chimeric assay
All mouse embryo microinjection and embryo transplantation
experiments were performed in the laboratory of Luzhou New Drug
Evaluation and Research Center. No statistical methods were used to
predetermine sample size. Animal studies involved randomization and
blinding of investigators during data collection and analysis. The
group allocation was revealed only after the complete dataset was
ﬁnalized. Each experimental condition was repeated in multiple inde-
pendent cohorts. Mice experiments were repeated multiple times.
EGFP-labeled me4CL cells were digested into single cells using
Accutase, then carefully layered on top of 10 mL of 0.1% BSA in
DMEM/F12 media in a horizontal rotor in a 15 mL conical centrifuge
tube, and centrifuged at 400 rpm for 10 min. After centrifugation,
the cell pellet was resuspended in 4CL medium at the proper density.
The cell suspension was incubated on ice within 30 min before
microinjection. Before microinjection, mouse oocytes and sperms
are subjected to in vitro fertilization in G-IVF PLUS medium (Vitrolife,
10136). They are then transferred into KSOM (Sigma-Aldrich, MR-121)
drops covered with mineral oil (Sigma-Aldrich, M5310) and cultured
under 5% CO
2 at 37 °C until they reach the 8-cell stage. To obtain
chimeric blastocysts, around 10 cells were injected into each 8C
mouse embryo. After microinjection, the embryos were incubated in
the KSOM drops for an additional 40 –48 h culture until the blas-
tocyst stage. Then, chimeric embryos were imaged, ﬁxed and
immunostained for microscope analysis.
For the post-implantation chimeric experiments, injected 8C
embryos were balanced in KSOM drops for an additional 24 h. Later,
10–15 chimeric embryos were transferred into the uterine horn of
pseudopregnant mice (E0.5). To observe and analyze chimeras, the
conceptuses were dissected at the E6.5 or E12.5 developmental stage.
Chimeric embryos were dissected on ice in PBS with 10% FBS. The
fetus, yolk sac and placenta were separated from conceptuses with
ﬁne-pointed forceps and imaged using a stereo ﬂuorescence micro-
scope (OLYMPUS, SZX16) to detect EGFP
+ cell localization.

For bulk RNA-seq data analysis, we implemented a comprehensive
quality control and processing pipeline. Initial quality assessment of

and read length <30) to remove low-quality sequences. The processed

data. Gene-level quanti ﬁcation was performed using featureCounts
(v2.0.1)40 to generate raw count matrices, followed by normalization of
expression values to Transcripts Per Million (TPM) to enable cross-
sample comparison and downstream analysis.

position weight matrices from the JASPAR CORE 2022 database49.

We performed differential peak analysis using the MAnorm2 50 to
compare peaks between experimental conditions. Differential peaks
were identiﬁed through statistical testing using the MAnorm2 frame-
work, with the following selection criteria: signi ﬁcant differential (p-
value < 0.05), the peak had high enrichment at this sample (normalized
RPKM > 2), and positive enrichment (normalized RPKM > 0).
Motif analyses
Motif localization analysis was conducted using annotatePeaks.pl to
identify speciﬁc genomic regions containing the enriched motifs. To
characterize the spatial distribution of motifs relative to peak centers,
we performed high-resolution motif density analysis using annotate-
Peaks.pl with the following parameters: annotatePeaks.pl hg38 -size
−2000, 2000 -hist 10.
CUT&Tag data processing
The raw FASTQ ﬁles were preprocessed using Cutadapt (v1.18) with
stringent parameters (-e 0.1 for maximum error rate and -m 5 for
minimum read length) to remove adapter sequences andﬁlter out low-
quality reads. Following adapter trimming, a comprehensive quality
control assessment was performed using FastQC (v0.11.9) to evaluate
read quality metrics. High-quality reads were then aligned to the hg38
human reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.2.2) with default para-
meters for optimal mapping efﬁciency. The resulting alignments were
processed using SAMtools (v.1.7) to ﬁlter out low-quality mappings
(Q < 30). The ﬁltered reads were converted to BAM format, and PCR
duplicates were removed using Picard Tools (v.2.8.0) with the REMO-
VE_DUPLICATES = true. For peak identiﬁcation, we employed MACS2
(v.2.2.7.1)
47 with speciﬁc parameters: --narrow for narrow peak calling,
--keep-dup all to retain all duplicates during model building, -B for
storing fragment pileup information, and --SPMR for signal per million
scaling. Promoters were deﬁned as ±2.5 kb around the TSS. Peaks at
least 2.5 kb away from TSSs were de ﬁned as distal peaks by bedtools
(v.2.29.0)
48. Finally, genome-wide visualization proﬁles were generated
using deepTools (v3.1.3) to facilitate comprehensive analysis of the
sequencing data.
