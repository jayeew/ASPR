Reconstructing networks of neurons in vitro is essential for advancing our understanding of functional
mechanisms and disease pathogenesis. However, neuronal culture methods including organoids are
limited in network structure complexity required for their functionality and dynamics. In this study, we
present modular organoid network tissues – loop connectoids – in which multiple cerebral organoids
are connected via axon bundles using micro ﬂuidic devices. We compared network activity of three-
and four-membered loop cerebral connectoids, two reciprocally connected organoids, and single
organoids. We observed a signi ﬁcant trend in larger organoid networks exhibiting more complex
activity, showing longer activity periods, more bursts, and richer temporal patterns. Additionally, the
activity in connectoids shifts closer to a critical state, a hallmark of ef ﬁcient information processing in
the brain, as more organoids are connected. Pharmacological perturbation reveals prominent
excitatory and inhibitory responses, supporting the physiological relevance of the observed dynamics.
Furthermore, optogenetic stimulation of organoids in a speci ﬁc sequence can in ﬂuence their
spontaneous activity propagation pattern within the network. This work represents a foundational step
toward constructing more complex and physiologically relevant neural networks in vitro, offering a
platform for studying neuronal network function and therapeutic intervention.
Brain (neural) organoids have emerged as a powerful tool in neuroscientiﬁc
research, enabling modeling of human brain development, the investigation
of neurodevelopmental disorders, and the exploration of disease mechan-
isms. This is because organoids can recapitulate many aspects of brain
development, including the formation of distinct layers, the emergence of
diverse neuronal cell types and complex cellular environment found in the
brain

Construction of multi-organoids connectoids using microﬂuidic
devices
Human iPS cells were aggregated and differentiated into cerebral organoids.
The resulting organoids showed robo ust expression of neural lineage
markers, including neural progenitor marker SOX2, forebrain marker
FOXG1, glial marker GFAP, and mature neuronal marker NeuN (Fig.
S1A–C). Cortical identity of cells was con ﬁrmed by the presence of
layer–speciﬁc populations expressing CT IP2 (deep-layer) and SATB2
(upper-layer) (Fig. S1D). Both excitatory and inhibitory neuronal popula-
tions were present (Fig. S1E), forming synaptic connections within the
organoid network (Fig. S1F). The cerebral organoids were plated into a
chamber of a Matrigel-coated microﬂuidic device after 4 weeks of differ-
entiation. The devices contain 2, 3, or 4 round chambers (2 mm diameter)
that are interconnected through 150μm-wide channels. Upon plating, the
organoids attached to the Matrigel-c oated surface and started extending
axons radially in all directions. The chip design introduces physical and
spatial restrictions for the axons, allowing them to only extend along the
channels that connect the chambers (Fig. 1A ) .I nt h i sw a y ,a x o n sr e a c h e d
other chambers, connecting with oth er organoids with a self-assembled
bundle structure of axons in 2 weeks of culture, which gradually increased in
thickness with time (Fig. S2). Axon bundle formation could be observed
when 2, 3 or 4 organoids were added into the device (Fig.1B, C), whereas 3
and 4 organoids formed loop structures.The structural integrity of the axon
bundles is maintained even when the microﬂuidic device is removed and the
connected organoids are extracted (Fig. S3).

Theoretical studies suggest that the brain operates at or near a state
known as criticality to achieve stable andﬂexible computation. The presence
of “neuronal avalanches” (cascades of propagating activity) is widely
reported in the spontaneous activity of in vivo cerebral networks. A hall-
mark of criticality is reported to be the power law distribution of the ava-
lanche sizes, which could be observed in all organoid conditions (Fig.5A, B).
Additionally, metrics such as the branching ratio or shape collapse error of
avalanches can be quantiﬁed to further evaluate criticality characteristics
17.
The branching ratio represents how activity propagates through a network.
If the branching ratio is 1.0, network activity would neither saturate nor
dampen over time indicating that the network is tuned to near critical state
(Fig. 5C). Connected organoids were signi ﬁcantly closer to the critical
branching ratio of 1.0 compared to the single organoids (Figs.5Ca n dS 7 B ) .
The shape collapse error can be deﬁned by scaling all avalanches in duration
and size, and calculate the error from the quadratic polynomial ﬁto ft h e
avalanche temporal proﬁle. When the network is tuned to a near critical
state, temporal pro ﬁles of avalanches should be identical across scales,
therefore, shape collapse error is min imized. Analysis on shape collapse
error in connected organoids showe d smaller error compared to single
organoids (Figs. 5D and S7C), suggesting that they function closer to a
critical state.
Modulation of neural activity through drug treatment and opto-
genetic entrainment
To investigate contribution of different types of synaptic receptors to the
activity of the organoids, organoids were treated with CNQX, AP5, and
bicuculline, antagonists of the major synaptic channels NMDA, AMPA, and
GABA, respectively. Consistently, CNQX and AP5 decreased burst activity
in all organoids, suggesting that excitatory synaptic transmission played a
critical role in driving the activity in the organoids (Figs.6Aa n dS 1 0 A–C).
Burst activity was signi ﬁcantly increased following bicuculline treatment
(Figs.6A and S10D). However, the interburst interval coefﬁcient of variation
decreased following bicuculline treatment, resulting in more periodic burst
activity, suggesting that inhibitory synapses were critical for generating
complex activity in organoids (Fig.6B). Furthermore, burst size coefﬁcient
of variation was decreased following all drug treatments in multi-organoid
connectoids, but not for every drug in single organoids where the base value
of the parameter was already at a lower level in control condition (Fig.6C).
To demonstrate functional modula bility and plasticity of the loop
connectoids, we employed an optoge netic approach using channelrho-
dopsin (ChR2). ChR2 was virally introduced into organoids, and infection
was con ﬁrmed through imaging the red ﬂuorescent protein tdTomato
tagged to ChR2 (Fig. 7A). To evaluate the tissues’functional capacity, we
conducted sequence speci ﬁc entrainment on the three-membered loop
connectoids after 4 months of culture (Fig. 7B). The 3 organoids were
sequentially stimulated, and their spontaneous activity propagation pattern
was evaluated for modulation by the stimulation patterns. The sequential
stimulation at 1 Hz frequency for 20 min evoked increased spiking activity
in the stimulated organoids (Fig. 7B). 20 min recordings were conducted
before, during, and after stimulation to compare the occurrence of the
stimulated sequence pattern. During the stimulation period, pattern
occurrence increased signiﬁcantly, suggesting successful response of sti-
mulated pattern (Fig. 7C). However, pattern occurrence of stimulated
pattern did not differ before and after the stimulation, suggesting that
20 min stimulation was not enough toinduce long-term modulation of the
network functionality. We next stimulated organoids in a sequential pattern
for longer periods (12 h) inside an incubator (Fig.7D). Interestingly, after
organoids were stimulated for 12 h, spontaneous activity more frequently
followed the stimulated sequence p attern than before the stimulation
(Fig. 7E). To probe the mechanisms of the pattern entrainment, organoids

Discussion
In this study, we have found that multiple organoids can be structurally and
functionally connected through axon bundles in a loop structure, which are
named “loop connectoids”. Previously, we reported that reciprocally con-
necting two cerebral organoids enha nces abundance and complexity of
activity of the organoids
16. In this study, we further extended the network
structure-function characterization to reciprocally connected three- and
four-membered loop networks.
One of the key observations is the emergence of different degrees of
synchronized activity in the connectedorganoid network. Within individual
organoids, we observed strong local synchronization, while inter-organoid
synchronization varied depending on the connectivity pattern. For
instance, in the four-membered loop connectoids, directly connected
organoids exhibited moderate synchronization, and indirectly connected
ones showed only weak coupling. Thi s graded synchronization pattern
might represent a simpliﬁed form of the large-scale network organization in
the brain, in which local ensembles are tightly coupled, and long-range
coordination is moreﬂexible. From the perspective of the network topology,
connecting multiple organoids also holds physiological relevance and has
been extensively studied using patterned neural cultures in 2D
18–22.A sw e

network size, connectivity, and modularity. The network exhibits a modular

We speculate that longer bursting periods observed in the connectoids
primarily arise from the additional re ciprocal connections introduced by
incorporating more organoids into the network. Each additional organoid
provides a source of external input and recurrent feedback and expanding
the number of pathways for activity propagation. As activity in one organoid
propagates through axonal connections, it can elicit responses from other
connected organoids, which propagates back, generating sustained network
activation. This recurrent activity across multiple reciprocal connections
may underlie the observed prolongation of burst duration and enhancement
of temporal complexity through a feedback-driven ampliﬁcation mechan-
ism (Supplementary Video 1). Similar effects were found in studies where
2D neural clusters connected through thin channels were compared with
random, unstructured 2D cultures. Structured networks were found to have
higher dynamical richness
21, functional complexity22, and increased net-
work metrics20. Furthermore, our previous work with organoids demon-
strated increased neural activity when organoids were connected via axon
bundles, whereas fusing organoids with same number of neurons did not
produce such effects, highlighting the critical role of long-range inter-
organoid connections

signiﬁcant differences were detected between linear and loop conﬁgurations

complexity in our multi-organoid networks are primarily driven by the
additional axon-mediated connections rather than by the total number of
neurons or the geometric loop structure itself.
This elevated baseline activity in connectoids provides a methodolo-
gical advantage, as higher dynamic complexity enhances the sensitivity for
detecting perturbation-induced changes. Increased variability and richness

Methods
Human induced pluripotent stem cell culture
The use of human iPS cells was approved by the Institute of Industrial
Science, The University of Tokyo. The human iPS cells were handled in
accordance with approved protocols. Two human induced pluripotent stem
cell (iPSC) lines, 409B2 (HPS0076) and 201B7 (HPS0063), were used in this
study. Both lines were obtained through the RIKEN Bioresource Center Cell
Bank and are derived from a healthy adult female donor. Human iPS cells
were cultured in mTeSR plus medium on Matrigel-coated tissue-cultured
12-well plates. Culture media was changed every other day and passaged
when 80% con ﬂuency was reached. Here, iPS cells were washed with
phosphate buffered saline (PBS) without calcium and magnesium, lifted off
the plate with ReLeSR reagent (Stemcell Technologies), and seeded into new
Matrigel coated wells at 1:15 ratio.
Cerebral organoid formation
Cerebral organoid culture was adapted from previously reported protocols16.
The iPS cells were dissociated at 80% conﬂuency through 3 min incubation in
TrypLE Express. Cells were resuspended in DMEM/F12 with HEPES and
centrifuged at 250 × g for 5 min. 10,000 cells per well were seeded into
U-bottom ultra-low attachment 96-well plate (Prime surface, Sumitomo
Bakelite) in mTeSR plus supplemented with 10μM of Y-23632. 24 h later,
media was replaced with neural induction media (NIM), consisting of
DMEM-F12 with HEPES, 15% (v/v) knockout serum replacement, 1% (v/v)
minimal essential media non-essential amino acids (MEM-NEAA), and 1%
(v/v) GlutaMAX, supplemented with 100 nM LDN-193189, 10μM SB431542,
and 5% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS. On day 2, NIM was replaced without the
supplement of FBS and changed every other day until day 10. From day 10 to
18, culture medium was replaced and changed every other day with neural
differentiation media 1 (NDM1), consisting of 1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 with
HEPES and Neurobasal medium, 0.5% (v/v) N2 supplement, 1% (v/v)
B27 supplement without vitamin A, 1% (v/v) GlutaMAX, 0.5% (v/v) MEM-
NEAA, 0.25 mg/ml human insulin solution, and 1% (v/v) Penicillin/Strepto-
mycin/Amphotericin (PSA) (Sigma, A5955). On day 18, culture medium was
replaced with neural differentiation media 2 (NDM2), consisting of Neuro-
basal medium, 0.5% (v/v) N2 supplement, 1% (v/v) B27 supplement with
vitamin A, 1% (v/v) GlutaMAX, 0.5% (v/v) MEM-NEAA, 0.25 mg/ml human
insulin solution, 200 mM ascorbic acid, and 1% (v/v) PSA, supplemented with
20 ng/ml brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). After day 28, culture
media was switched to neural maintenance medium (NMM) consisting of
Neurobasal medium supplemented with 2% (v/v) B27 supplement with
vitamin A, 1% (v/v) Glutamax, 20 ng/ml BDNF, and 1% (v/v) Penicillin/
Streptomycin. For organoids subjected toelectrophysiological recordings, with
each media change, culture media was stepwise switched (25% steps) to
Recording Media (RM) consisting of BrainPhys media, supplemented with 2%
(v/v) B27 supplement with vitamin A, 0.5% (v/v) N2 supplement, 1% (v/v)
Glutamax, 1% (v/v) PSA and 20 ng/ml BDNF. Cells were kept at 37 °C and 5%
CO
2, and half media change of NMM or RM was performed every 3–4d a y sf o r
the duration of organoid culture.
PDMS culture chip fabrication
PDMS culture chip was fabricated using previously reported protocol16,31.
Brieﬂy, a master mold was fabricated, a nd polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)
device was molded from using the mask. For the master mold, SU-8 (2100 or
2075) was poured onto a silicon wafer and spin coated at 1200–1500 rpm for
30 s. Next, the wafer was baked for 9 min at 65 °C and for 40 min at 95 °C on
a hotplate. Then, UV light (365 nm, 2.5–3.0 mW cm
2) was irradiated using a
photomask with desired pattern for 60–75 s. The wafer was baked for 7 min
at 65 °C and for 13 min at 95 °C on a hotplate. After cooling down the wafer,
SU-8 was developed by using SU-8 developer for 15 min, then washed with
isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for 3 times. The wafer was baked for 3 min at 150 °C
in the oven. Culture chip was made with a PDMS silicone elastomer kit,
Sylgard184 (Dow Corning). Silicone elastomer and a curing agent were
mixed at a weight ratio of 10:1, degassed, poured onto the master mold, and
cured in the oven at 80 °C for at least 2 h. PDMS structure was cut out using
scalpel, and holes for organoids were cut using 2 mm biopsy punches.
Connectoid generation
PDMS microﬂuidic devices were soaked in 70% ethanol to disinfect, dried,
and attached to the bottom of a well in a culture treated 12 well plate or to a
with 70% ethanol sterilized MEA probe (MED-R5004A-NR). Internal
channel of the chip was coated usingMatrigel in DMEM/F12 with HEPES
(1:50) for 1 h at RT. Coating solution was replaced with NMM, and cerebral
organoid at day 28 were placed into each of the holes of the culture device
using wide-bore pipette tip. Axons from the organoid start extending on the
surface. Given the physical restrictions of the PDMS micro ﬂuidic device,
axons can only elongate along the channel and spontaneously assemble into
a bundle after 2 weeks in culture.

Data analysis
The collected data was analysed using MATLAB 2020a. The recorded signal
was bandpassﬁltered with a frequency range of 300–3000 Hz, whereas any
positive and negative peak with a value of more than 4 times the standard
deviation was identiﬁed as spikes. For network analysis, such as burst fre-
quency, spiking activity from all electrodes was summed up for each
organoid and binned with a time window of 100 ms. Burst was identiﬁed
when binned network activity surpassed a certain threshold, and burst peak
was computed usingﬁndpeaks() function. Time difference of detected peaks
was used for interburst interval, and coefﬁcient of variation was calculated
from it. Inter organoid activity correlation coefﬁcient was calculated from
the summed spiking activity per organoid using Pearson correlation coef-
ﬁcient function corrcoef(). For intra organoid correlation, most active 3
electrodes per organoid were selected to calculate correlation between them.
Time frequency analysis was performed by employing Continuous Wavelet
Transform (CWT). Functional connectivity was analyzed using graph-
theoretical measures implemented in the Brain Connectivity Toolbox
(BCT)
32. Spiking activities were used to construct undirected weighted
graphs based on pairwise correlations of spontaneous activity across elec-
trodes using spike-time tiling coefﬁcient (STTC)33,34. Network parameters
were quantiﬁed across thresholds from 0.0 to 1.0 (Fig. S12). To balance false
positive inter-organoid connectionswith lower thresholds and false nega-
tives with higher thresholds (Fig. S12A), electrode pairs with STTC weights
higher than 0.3 were used to generate connectivity map and were binarized
to create the adjacency matrix. The number of Nodes were deﬁned as the
number of active electrodes participa ting in at least one edge (non-zero
entry in the adjacency matrix). Modularity was computed using the mod-
ularity_und() function, which identiﬁes community structure based on the
Louvain algorithm. A node was classiﬁed as a hub if its degree exceeded 1.5
times the standard deviation above the mean degree across the network.
Analysis on neural avalanches and criticality was conducted using functions
from the neural complexity and criticality (NCC) toolbox
35. Here,
calculations were performed from spiking activity in each electrode, binned
with a 10 ms time window.

Cells and tissues wereﬁxed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h and washed 3x
with PBS. Fixed organoids were incubated for at least 1 day at 4 °C in PBS
containing 30% w/v sucrose. Next, organoids were transferred into Optimal

tissues were sectioned into 20 µm thick sections and transferred onto a glass
slide. For staining, sectioned tissues were permeabilized for 30 min in PBS
containing 0.1% Triton-X100 and then after 3 washed with PBS. Next, cells
were blocked for 1 h in PBS with 2% Goat Serum at room temperature.
Following, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibodies in
PBS containing 2% Goat Serum. Prima ry antibodies used were rat anti-
CTIP2 (Abcam, ab18465), rabbit anti-FOXG1 (Abcam, ab18259), mouse

Statistics and reproducibility
Statistical tests used, and the number of technical and biological replicates
for all experiments are mentioned in ﬁgure legends. Statistical analyses
were completed from a minimum of 3 replicates. All statistical tests
and graphical representations were performed using MATLAB 2020a.
For comparisons between two sample groups student’s t-test using function
t-test() was used. Comparisons with more than 2 sample groups
were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using
anova1() function, followed by Tukey-Kramer post-hoc test for pairwise
comparisons using multcompare() function. Signiﬁcance was assigned for
p-values < 0.05.
