Human disturbances are modifying animal behavior in ecosystems worldwide,
with the potential to reshape species interactions. For instance, human-induced
shifts in diel activity may disrupt the alignment of daily activity patterns
between interacting species and destabilize temporal niche partitioning. To test
this hypothesis, we leverage a global meta-analysis on the effects of human
disturbance on diel activity and overlap of 480 mammalian predator–prey and
intraguild predator dyads from 57 studies. We demonstrate that human dis-
turbance has no overall effect on temporal overlap. Instead, the body mass
ratios between dominant species andsubordinate species shape the inﬂuence
of human disturbance. When subordinates are larger than dominant species,
humans compress the temporal niche (i.e., higher diel overlap), but when
dominant species are larger than subordinate species, humans expand the
temporal niche (i.e., lower diel overlap). These results suggest that larger bodied
mammals “lose” the temporal predator–prey response race under human dis-
turbance, with large predators experiencing less overlap with their prey, and
large prey facing more overlap with their predators. As the human footprint
expands globally, we can expect continued alterations to the animal temporal
niche, with consequences for species interactions, population persistence,
community structure, and evolutionary dynamics.
The rapid expansion of humanity’s global footprint is a critical threat
to biodiversity1–3. In addition to direct killing and habitat loss, humans
have powerful effects on animal behavior by reshaping risks and
opportunities available to wildlife
4–6. To navigate these novel trade-
offs, animals adjust their temporal activity patterns to avoid both
human infrastructure and direct encounters with humans, while still
taking advantage of resources in human-dominated areas7,8. While the
effects of humans on diel activity are well-documented, less is known
about how shifts in diel activity can alter species interactions, with
implications for populations, ecosystems, and human societies
9–11.

responses of predator–prey and intraguild dyads to disturbance and to
understand the generality of global patterns of temporal niche parti-
tioning under human disturbance.
We present a meta-analysis of 57 studies representing 116 species
on 6 continents that quanti ﬁes the change in dominant –subordinate
overlap of terrestrial mammals across areas or coarse time periods of
low and high human disturbance
6,15–66. To simplify analysis, we treat all
interactions as dyadic, while statistically accounting for pseudor-
eplication and acknowledging that interaction networks are far more
complex. We use a metric of overlap ranging from zero (complete
temporal separation between dominant and subordinate species) to
one (complete overlap in temporal niches). Our dataset includes 480
effect sizes for 306 predator–prey and 174 intraguild predator dyads
(deﬁned as an interaction where a species hunts another species), with
a total of 33 dominant predator species, 73 prey, and 33 subordinate
predators (Fig. 2A). For each study, we calculate the difference
between logit transformed (proportion) temporal overlap values from
low and high human disturbance as a measure of effect size. We then
test how the effect of disturbance on temporal overlap varied with
traits such as dominant and subordinate body mass, categorical pair-
wise diel activity pattern (e.g., nocturnal dyad), and interaction
intensity
67–69. Finally, we explore the percent change in temporal
activity of each species in response to human disturbance and whether
such temporal shifts tended in a particular direction (i.e., toward
nocturnality).

Human disturbance alters animal temporal activity patterns
To understand how each member of each dyad responded to human
disturbance, we evaluated a subset of our dataset that reported the
temporal overlap of activity patterns of a single species between low
and high-disturbance areas (256 individual data points from 31 stu-
dies). Overlap again ranged from zero (completely different activity
patterns between low and high-disturbance) to one (no effect of dis-
turbance on activity patterns). We used the logit of the inverse of
overlap as a measure of effect size for each species in each study. Both
dominant (β = −1.68 [95% CI = −1.948, −1.415], Fig. S2) and subordinate
(β = −1.76 [95% CI = −2.00, −1.52]) species responded strongly to
human disturbance. After back-transforming to the inverse of overlap
(i.e., the difference in activity between low- and high-disturbance
areas), our model estimates indicate that dominant species ’ activity
patterns differed by 14.7% (95% CI: 11.9 –17.9%) and subordinate
species’activity patterns by 15.7% (95% CI: 12.5 –19.5%) between high
and low disturbance.
We also tested how species traits (body mass and daily activity
timing) inﬂuenced how species temporally responded to humans with
subsequent meta-regressions. We found a marginal but relatively
strong relationship between dominant body mass and the change in
activity between low and high-disturbance areas, with the largest
bodied dominants exhibiting somewhat stronger responses (β =0 . 1 3
[95% CI = −0.014, 0.28], p =0 . 0 7 6 , c o n d i t i o n a lR
2 = 0.62; Fig. 5). Body
mass did not in ﬂuence how subordinates responded to humans
(Table S5). Daily activity timing did not in ﬂuence animal temporal
responses to humans (Table S5).
Finally, to better understand the direction of human-induced
differences in temporal activity, we ran phylogenetic multilevel meta-
analytic models to assess whether predators and prey exhibited more
nocturnality or diurnality in response to human disturbance. This
analysis was restricted to a further subset of our data (165 single spe-
cies responses from 19 studies) that reported the number of day and
night detections across low and high human disturbance treatments.
We calculated the log risk ratio (RR) as a measure of effect size, where a
positive RR indicates that species were more nocturnal in high-
disturbance conditions, and a negative RR indicates that species were
more diurnal. Surprisingly, in contrast to previous studies
9, we found
that neither dominant nor subordinate species were consistently more
nocturnal in conditions of higher human disturbance (Dominant:
β = 0.069, [95% CI = −0.076, 0.214], p = 0.35; Subordinate: β =0 . 0 2 0 ,
[95% CI = −0.093, 0.134], p = 0.72); Fig. 6; heterogeneity in methods).
No functional traits in ﬂuenced the effect of human disturbance on
nocturnality (Table S6). It is important to note that both the single-
species activity and nocturnality analyses are based on subsets of our
data, and therefore may not fully capture the patterns observed in the
complete dominant–subordinate dataset.
Discussion
Our meta-analysis revealed variable effects of human disturbance on
predator–prey and intraguild predator diel overlap, rather than a
globally consistent shift toward either temporal niche compression or
expansion, consistent with previous ﬁndings14. The effects of dis-
turbance on temporal partitioning were mediated by the body mass of
dominant and subordinate species: under human disturbance,
whichever species is larger tends to “lose” the temporal “response
race,” in which predators seek to maximize overlap with prey and prey
seek to minimize overlap with predators
70,71. When subordinates are
larger than dominants, disturbance compresses the temporal niche,
increasing diel overlap. When dominants are larger, disturbance tends
to reduce overlap, reﬂecting avoidance of high human activity periods
by large dominants. While the underlying ecological mechanisms of
these results are difﬁcult to pinpoint, they suggest that animals adjust
temporal strategies in context-speci ﬁc ways to balance risks and
resource opportunities under human disturbance, altering the tem-
poral niche partitioning that shapes lethal ecological interactions.
Subsequent analyses suggest that these size-mediated effects of
disturbance on overlap are, in part, driven by size-dependent
responses of dominant species to disturbance. Although only mar-
ginally supported, we found that larger-bodied dominants exhibited
greater shifts in temporal activity than their smaller counterparts,
consistent with previous reports of strong diel responses by large
carnivores to humans
31,48,49,72, and with evidence that larger-bodied
species show stronger shifts in diel activity in response to human
disturbance
9,73. Large predators ’ extensive spatial requirements and
the risks they pose to humans and livestock make them particularly
vulnerable to conﬂict and persecution, likely driving such behavioral
shifts. As large dominants alter their activity, subordinates may exploit
the resulting temporal human shield
74, shifting their activity toward
these safer periods, while large dominants become temporally

Where human disturbance alters dominant–subordinate overlap,
encounter rates between species may either increase (under niche
compression) or decrease (under niche expansion). These changes in
encounter rates may cascade through the predation sequence
(encounter, attack, kill) to alter patterns of predation, with potential
consequences for predator and prey population dynamics and
downstream trophic interactions
75,76. However, it remains unclear
whether changes in temporal overlap translate into changes in pre-
dation rates. While some studies have found that temporal overlap can
increase predation or encounter rates
77,78, others show no direct link79.
More research is needed to understand the relationships between
human disturbance, spatiotemporal overlap, predation, and down-
stream ecological consequences.
Our research adds to a growing body of work suggesting that
animals occupy novel temporal niches in the Anthropocene
9,13,72.O n
average, we found differences in animal diel patterns of approximately
15% between high and low disturbance conditions. Mammalian tem-
poral niches have evolved in response to factors other than just pre-
dation risk and access to prey, such as physiological thermal
requirements, social environments, competition, and access to other
resources
12,13. Any human-induced change in diel activity may increase
metabolic costs, reduce foraging opportunities, and compromise
sociality, all of which can in ﬂuence survival and reproduction, in
addition to changing patterns of temporal niche partitioning
13,80,81.T o
alleviate human pressures on wildlife, previous authors have sug-
gested “temporal zoning”9, the temporal exclusion of humans from
areas of high conservation value when animals are most active.
Our ﬁnding of no universal shift toward nocturnality (Fig.6)m a y
appear to contrast with Gaynor et al.9, who documented a consistent
nocturnal shift under human disturbance across a broad range of
species. Our systematic search was not designed to capture the full
breadth of single species responses to human disturbance and thus we
do not have the same coverage of the literature as Gaynor et al.
9.T h i s
earlier meta-analysis examined single species in isolation (across
76 studies), whereas our dataset is composed exclusively of
predator–prey and intraguild predator dyads (across 19 studies, for the
nocturnality analysis). Another factor we cannot address in our current
framework is crepuscularity — a key temporal niche to which both
dominant and subordinate species have been observed to shift, and an
important subject of future research
48. Our study contributes to a
growing body of work suggesting that the shift to nocturnality under
human disturbance may be more variable and context dependent than
previously thought
48,82,83.
Species interactions in the Anthropocene differ greatly from
those of the recent past, taking place between novel species (e.g.,
introduced species), with novel behaviors (e.g., temporal activity pat-
terns), on novel stages (e.g., cities)
84–86. As novel biosphere conditions
continue to emerge87,88, animal behavior may play an important role in
mediating the effects of disturbance on the persistence and strength of
species interactions
89,90. Continued systematic approaches to under-
standing the mechanisms underlying changing species interactions in
the Anthropocene are therefore crucial to conserving life on Earth.

could stop screening when we had reached 95% of the total estimated
number, and after 100 irrelevant articles in a row. To train the active
learning model, we pre-selected 10 relevant and irrelevant articles
from the second search. We also included data from one thesis that
was known to the authors but not identiﬁed in the ProQuest Thesis and
Dissertation search.
Inclusion and exclusion criteria
We screened titles and abstracts from bibliometric records to identify
empirical studies on how human disturbance shapes temporal overlap
between predator–prey or intraguild predator dyads. Studies were
retained for the full-text screening stage if they mentioned temporal
activity, overlap, or partitioning between predators or predator and
prey and any kind of human disturbance in the abstract, or if they
referred to any predator–prey or intraguild predator interaction in the
title. A study then had to meet three inclusion criteria to progress from
the full-text screening stage to the data extraction stage: (1) discuss a
potential predator –prey or lethal intraguild predator interaction
between two terrestrial mammal species; (2) present a metric of
overlap between dominant and subordinate species in time (e.g., Dhat1
or 4) or the data to calculate such a metric and; (3) document temporal
activity across a spatial or temporal human disturbance gradient. We
excluded studies that occurred entirely in disturbed or“pristine” areas
(i.e., a study of predator–prey interactions in an urban area only). We
also excluded livestock and domestic dogs, and did not include species
<0.5 kg, given that camera traps are not set up to systematically
monitor smaller-bodied species. Where relevant data (e.g., 95% CIs or
sample size) were not presented or insuf ﬁciently reported, we con-
tacted authors for clariﬁcation or provision of raw data, and excluded
studies where authors did not make such data available.
Quantifying dominant–subordinate pairings
Understanding which species interact is one of ecology ’s great chal-
lenges (the Eltonian shortfall)95,96, and reports of the strength of such
interactions can be unreliable or sparse in primary literature. To
account for uncertainty in the strength of the predator –prey and
intraguild predator interactions in our study, we sorted each of our
interactions into evidence categories spanning from“strong evidence”
to “some evidence”. The purpose of this classiﬁcation was to facilitate
sensitivity analysis using only dyads with strong evidence of interac-
tion, and to ensure we were not including any dyads that were highly
unlikely to interact.

Meta-analysis
To quantify the effects of human disturbance on
dominant–subordinate temporal overlap, we constructed phyloge-
netic random-effect meta-analytic models with the rma.mv function in
the metafor package (version 4.6)
108. These models weight each effect
size within the model by the inverse of the sampling variance; this
allows the models to account for data quality, with larger sample sizes
having higher weight within the models.
Our meta-analytic models featured six random effects, each an
important source of non-independence within our effect sizes. These
were (1) study ID (unique study identi ﬁer to account for study popu-
lation non-independence), (2) observation ID (a unique ID assigned to
each effect size, equivalent to residual term in a normal linear model),
(3) dominant phylogeny, (4) subordinate phylogeny, (5) dominant
identity (non-phylogenetic part of dominant species), (6) subordinate
