Alkali metals have been widely used as promoters in heterogeneous metal
catalysts to improve activity, product selectivity, and stability. Despite their
prevalence, the precise role remains not fully uncovered due to the complex
interplay between catalytic components and reactants involving dynamic
structural changes. Herein, we elucidate the promotional effect of alkali metal
in hydrogenation reactions from a uniﬁed perspective. Using Na-doped Rh/
TiO
2 as a representative system, we demonstrate a phenomenon, termed
“Strong Metal–Base Interaction (SMBI)”.T h eb a s i cN as p e c i e s( e . g .N a2O)
intimate to the Rh particles facilitates heterolytic H2 splitting, capturing H+
while leaving H− on Rh sites. Consequently, there are suppressed hydrogen
spillovers from Rh to the TiO2 surface and electron-rich Rh sites, fundamen-
tally altering the catalytic behavior during hydrogenation. SMBI enablesﬁne
control of both activity and selectivity: Na doping shifts product selectivity in
CO2 hydrogenation completely from CH4 to CO, enhances C=C hydrogenation
via weakened H adsorption and electron-enriched Rh, and suppresses C=O and
N=O hydrogenation due to restricted H spillover. These ﬁndings provide a
uniﬁed framework for understanding alkali metal promotion and offer a
rational approach for designing efﬁcient hydrogenation catalysts.
Transition metal (TM) catalysts are widely utilized in energy and
environmental applications due to their unique ability to adopt various
oxidation states and activate multiple chemical bonds
1–3. To enhance
catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability, alkali metals (AMs) such as
Li, Na, K, Rb, and Cs are frequently employed as promoters in het-
erogeneous catalysis
4–6, in particular, hydrogenation reactions. AM-
promoted catalysts have demonstrated remarkable improvements in
several industrially important reactions such as Fischer –Tropsch
synthesis (FTS
7–9), CO2 hydrogenation10–17,N H3 synthesis/cracking18–21,
and various other applications22–25.
The effects of AM promoters are generally classi ﬁed into two
primary perspectives: geometric and electronic effects. The geometric
effect generally refers to the structural alteration of the active metal
component, speciﬁcally the rearrangement of atoms on the catalyst
surface and the consequent creation of improved active sites
18,26–28.

While electron transfer from highly electropositive AM to TM is

between TM, AM, and support.
Given that AMs present as oxide form, the well-established
concept of Strong Metal –Support Interaction (SMSI) becomes a
useful framework to evaluate whether analogous interactions might
occur between metal nanoparticles and AM oxides

ifests through phenomena such as encapsulation of TM nano-

the consequences of SMSI are highly system-dependent: other stu-

support reducibility, and pretreatment conditions

metal and the support 46–51, typically induced by high-temperature
reductive pretreatment. However, unlike common reducible sup-
ports that induce SMSI, AM oxides are non-reducible. This raises
important questions: could AM oxides induce any dramatic interac-
tion with TM nanoparticles like SMSI? During hydrogenation, parti-
cularly, what are the catalytic results of such interactions? These
questions become more intriguing in ternary systems composed of
TM, AM, and reducible support, where multiple interfacial interac-
tions coexist and intertwine.

Effects of Na dopants in H2 activation
The effect of Na dopant on H2 activation was investigated through H2-
TPD, H2–D2 exchange, and D2 adsorption DRIFTS (D2-DRIFTS). In the
H2-TPD proﬁle, Rh/TiO2 shows a distinct peak at 430 K but RhNa 1.0/
TiO2 shows a peak at a much lower temperature (340 K) (Fig.3c). This
lower-temperature peak reﬂects that the surface H species on the Na-
doped catalyst are more labile and more easily desorbed/recombined,
indicating a higher reversibility rather than simply a weaker
metal–hydrogen bond. By microscopic reversibility, this enhanced
desorption/recombination also implies more facile H
2 adsorption and
activation. H 2–D2 exchange at various temperatures (303 –493 K)
offers the estimation of activation energy (Ea)f o rh y d r o g e na c t i v a t i o n
over the catalysts (Fig. 3d). RhNa1.0/TiO2 shows a markedly lower Ea
(57 kJ mol−1) compared to Rh/TiO2 (Ea = 103 kJ mol−1). Coupled with the
H2-TPD results, we conclude that Na doping leads to the facile H 2
activation; the H atoms on RhNa 1.0/TiO2 a r em o r er e v e r s i b l ef o r
adsorption and desorption compared to those on Rh/TiO 2.F u r t h e r -
more, these results suggest that heterolytic H 2 splitting on Rh-Na2O
may be kinetically superior to homolytic H 2 splitting on isolated Rh
particles.
D2-DRIFTS over time-on-stream quantitatively shows the degree
of hydrogen spillover in Rh/TiO 2 and RhNa 1.0/TiO2 catalysts. Both
catalysts exhibit a substantial amount of O –H vibration peak
(3000–3600 cm−1) at the initial state (Fig. 3e, f) due to the presence
of surface hydroxyl groups on TiO 2 surface. When subsequently
dosed with D 2, Rh/TiO 2 shows a remarkable increase in the O –D
vibration peak (2000–2800 cm−1) with a negligible change in the O–H
vibration peak (Fig. 3e). This indicates the surface coverage of the
hydrogen species (i.e., H + D) gradually increases under D 2 ﬂow, in
other words, a favorable deuterium (D) spillover through the TiO 2
surface. On the other hand, RhNa 1.0/TiO2 shows a simultaneous
increase in the O–D vibration peak and decrease in the O–H vibration
peak (Fig. 3f). As Prins highlighted66, this does not indicate hydrogen
spillover because the D 2 dosing does not increase the surface cov-
erage of hydrogen species (H + D) on the catalyst surface. Instead,
this observation represents the OH–OD exchange, taking place at the
intimate interface between Rh and Na
2O. In summary, Na doping on
Rh/TiO2 accelerates H 2 activation with promoted heterolytic H 2
splitting, prevents hydrogen spillover, and thus concentrates H
atoms at the Rh-Na
2O interface.

tronic characteristics of the proposed SMBI concept. For model Rh-

Discussion
Using RhNa/TiO2 as a model system, we present a concept of SMBI as a
governing interfacial phenomenon in alkali metal-promoted transition
metal catalysts for hydrogenation reactions. SMBI arises from the
strong interaction between non-reducible, strongly basic alkali metal
oxides (e.g., Na
2O) and transition metal nanoparticles. During H 2
activation, these basic oxides preferentially capture protons (H +),
leaving hydride species (H⁻) on the metal surface. This heterolytic H2
splitting enriches the metal with electrons while simultaneously sup-
pressing hydrogen spillover to the support, thus fundamentally
modiﬁes the catalytic environment. This understanding provides a
coherent explanation for the electronic promotion effects of alkali
metals, which cannot be fully rationalized by traditional direct charge
transfer mechanisms. Importantly, this interpretation distinguishes
the proton afﬁnity (basicity) of alkali metal oxides from their reducing
power; basicity governs H
+ stabilization rather than implying electron
donation to the metal.
Under typical hydrogenation conditions, SMBI can override con-
ventional SMSI, as demonstrated in the RhNa/TiO2 system, leading to
signiﬁcant changes in both activity and product selectivity. In CO 2
hydrogenation, the system switches selectivity from CH4 to CO, while
in broader hydrogenation reactions, reactivity varies with the nature of
the substrate (e.g., C=C, C=O, or N=O). Beyond the RhNa/TiO2 system,
the concept is extendable to other alkali metals (e.g., Li and K) and
transition metals (e.g., Ni) composites. These ﬁndings show the
potential value of SMBI for tailoring both reactivity and selectivity in
industrially important hydrogenation reactions.
