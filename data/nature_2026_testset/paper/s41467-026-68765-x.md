Filopodia formation by tumor endothelial cells (TECs) is critical for tip cell-
guided sprouting angiogenesis and tumor growth. However, the cytoskeletal
organization that underlies this process remains elusive. Here, we demon-
strates that TECs highly express actin-binding genes, with PDZ and LIM domain
5 (PDLIM5), a cytoskeletal protein, signiﬁcantly upregulated in TECs and cor-
related with poor patient survival. Endothelial-speciﬁc deletion of Pdlim5
inhibits sprouting angiogenesis by disruptingﬁlopodia formation through its
interaction with actinin-1/actinin-4 (ACTN1/ACTN4) via its S593/F596 residues,
promoting ﬁlamentous actin (F-actin) bundling. Pdlim5 knockout not only
reduces tumor growth but also normalizes tumor vasculature, alleviates
hypoxia, and enhances immunotherapy and chemotherapy responses. These
ﬁndings highlight the PDLIM5’s role in facilitating tumor angiogenesis via
ACTN1/ACTN4-mediated F-actin bundling and tip cell ﬁlopodia formation,
providing mechanistic insights that may inform future therapeutic strategies
targeting this pathway.

Results
Elevated actin-binding related genes promote tip cell formation
and tumor neovascularization
To investigate the cytoskeletal changes in TECs, we analyzed the
expression of genes encoding actin-binding proteins using publicly
available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of human lung
TECs and normal ECs (NECs) (E-MTAB-6308)
5. Compared with NECs,
70 actin-binding-related genes were upregulated, including MYO1B,
CXCR4,a n d SYNPO, while 11 genes were downregulated, including
SLC6A4 and S100A4 (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a). This ﬁnding sug-
gests abnormalities in the TEC cytoskeleton that promote cell migra-
tion. Among these genes, we found that the cytoskeleton-related
PDLIM family proteins, including PDLIM1, PDLIM7,a n d PDLIM5,w e r e
highly expressed in TECs, indicating an important role for PDLIM
family proteins in TECs. PDLIM proteins are known for their roles in the
regulation of cytoskeletal organization and signal transduction,
usually serving as molecular scaffolds that link signaling molecules to
the cytoskeleton. Further analysis revealed that the expression of
PDLIM5 in TECs was notably higher than that of other PDLIM family
proteins (Supplementary Fig. 1b). PDLIM5, in particular, has been
implicated in various cellular processes, including actin dynamics, cell
adhesion and migration, and signal transduction
14.P r e v i o u ss t u d y
found that high PDLIM5 expression in non-small cell lung cancer
(NSCLC) cells promoted cell migration and accelerated lung cancer
metastasis by regulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)
15,
suggesting that PDLIM5 is an important oncogenic gene. To gain a
more comprehensive understanding of PDLIM5 expression in stromal
cells within the tumor microenvironment, We compared PDLIM5 levels
in stromal cells from tumors versus normal tissues. Beyond tumor
cells, PDLIM5 expression was higher in TECs than in normal endothelial
cells NECs (Fig.1b). Similar results were observed in TECs isolated from
mammary tumor models and human COLO 205 subcutaneous graft
tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1c–f).
To further analyze which subtypes of TECs highly expressed
PDLIM5, we collected paired cancerous and adjacent normal tissues
from seven patients with lung cancer. TECs are subdivided into ﬁve
subtypes based on their unique gene transcription signatures: Arterial
ECs (AECs), which are responsible for the transport of oxygenated
blood away from the heart; Venous ECs (VECs), involved in returning
deoxygenated blood to the heart; Lymphatic ECs (LECs), which play a
role in immune surveillance andﬂuid balance; Capillary ECs (CapECs),
which facilitate the exchange of nutrients, waste, and gases between
blood and tissues; and TipECs, which are involved in angiogenesis,
guiding the formation of new blood vessels (Fig. 1c, Supplementary
Fig. 2a–e). Our results showed that, compared with those in NECs, the
proportion of TipECs in TECs increased, whereas the proportion of
CapECs and AECs decreased (Fig. 1d), which was consistent with pre-
vious studies
5. This shift in cell subtype proportions re ﬂected angio-
genic phenotype during tumorigenesis. Further analysis revealed that
PDLIM5 was upregulated in AECs, CapECs, LECs, and TipECs (Fig. 1e,
Supplementary Fig. 2f). Consistent with our data, we re-analyzed the
scRNA-seq dataset E-MTAB-6308 using their published methodology
5,
and PDLIM5 was signiﬁcantly upregulated in TECs versus NECs across
multiple activated subpopulations, including tip cells, artery ECs,
immature ECs, and activated postcapillary vein ECs (Supplementary
Fig. 2g). Additionally, in clinical lung cancer tissues, we observed
higher expression of PDLIM5 in TECs compared to adjacent normal
tissue ECs. Elevated PDLIM5 expression in ECs was correlated with
poorer survival outcomes (Fig. 1f–h). Beyond lung cancer, patients
with high PDLIM5 expression across several other cancer types also
exhibited worse survival outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 3). This sug-
gests that PDLIM5 in TECs might play a broader role in cancer
progression.
A previous study showed that patients with NSCLC expressing
high angiogenic TEC gene set signatures had shorter overall survival
5.
We hypothesized that the highly expressed cytoskeleton-associated
protein PDLIM5 in TECs causes abnormal tip cell function and pro-
motes angiogenesis. To test this hypothesis, we constructed a Lewis
lung carcinoma (LLC) subcutaneous graft tumor model in Pdlim5
global-overexpressing (CAG-Pdlim5) mice. Results showed that tumors
in CAG-Pdlim5 mice exhibited more rapid growth and more abundant
vessels (Fig. 1i–m). These ﬁndings suggest that PDLIM5 promotes
tumor angiogenesis in TECs.
Endothelial Pdlim5 deletion impedes tumor growth and
angiogenesis
To further investigate the effect of endothelial PDLIM5 on tumor
angiogenesis, we engineered a conditional knockout mouse model. We
crossed Pdlim5
ﬂox/ﬂox mice with Cdh5-CreERT2 mice to generate
Pdlim5ﬂox/ﬂox; Cdh5 CreERT2 mice (Pdlim5 cKO), allowing for speci ﬁc
deletion of Pdlim5 in ECs upon tamoxifen treatment (Supplementary
Fig. 4a, b). This strategy allowed us to systematically evaluate the
endothelial-speciﬁc role of PDLIM5 in tumor angiogenesis while
maintaining its physiological function in other cell types. Notably,
genetic ablation of endothelial Pdlim5 did not perturb normal tissue
homeostasis or vascular integrity, as demonstrated by unaltered vas-
cular density, and preserved endothelial permeability in Pdlim5
cKO
mice (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). These ﬁndings were consistent with
our previously published observations16.

PDLIM5 promotes ﬁlopodia formation and vessel sprouting
PDLIM5 is highly expressed in tip ECs, which are crucial for angio-
genesis, and deletion of PDLIM5 inhibits tumor angiogenesis. It is dif-
ﬁcult to directly observe the subtle morphological and functional
changes of tip cells by immuno ﬂuorescence in tumour sections. To
better elucidate PDLIM5 ’s role in tip cell function, we performed
whole-mount staining of postnatal day 7 (P7) retinas, which allows for
the visualization of tip cells andﬁlopodia formation. Pdlim5 knockout
signiﬁcantly delayed the expansion of the retinal vascular plexus to the
periphery (Fig. 3a), indicating its crucial role in angiogenesis. Further
analysis revealed that vessel coverage, branching points, and the
number of tip cells and ﬁlopodia were markedly reduced (Fig. 3b–e),
highlighting PDLIM5’s involvement in vascular sprouting and expan-
sion. However, retinal vascular networks in Pdlim5 knockout mice at
P14 and P17 showed no signiﬁcant defects compared with the control
group (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). These results suggest that PDLIM5
deﬁciency does not affect the ﬁnal maturation and function of retinal
vessels. Second, we utilized the aortic ring assay, a method that mimics
vessel sprouting in Matrigel, allowing the assessment of sprouting
angiogenesis in vitro. ECs with Pdlim5 deletion displayed defects in
this assay (Fig. 3f–i), reinforcing the ﬁndings from the retinal model
and emphasizing PDLIM5’s role in EC behavior. Lastly, to examine tip
cell ﬁlopodia formation in vitro, we employed an EC ﬁbrin bead assay
(FBA), which provides a simpli ﬁed and controlled setting to observe
cellular behavior. Silencing PDLIM5 in ECs with two lentiviral vectors
encoding different small hairpin RNA (shRNA) of PDLIM5 (shPDLIM5)
effectively reduced PDLIM5 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 5a).
This knockdown (KD) led to a signi ﬁcant decrease in the sprouting
area, number of tip cells, and ﬁlopodia (Fig. 3j–m, Supplementary
Fig. 5b), further con ﬁrming the importance of PDLIM5 in tip cell
function.
In addition, TECs isolated from Pdlim5 KO mice exhibited marked
down-regulation of tip cell marker genes, including CXCR4, ESM1, and
DLL4, when compared with those from control tumor-bearing mice
(Supplementary Fig. 5c). Conversely, TECs from Pdlim5-
overexpressing mice displayed an augmented sprouting area, accom-
panied by a larger quantity of tip cells andﬁlopodia, in comparison to
TECs from control tumor-bearing mice (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e).
Taken together, these results imply that PDLIM5 facilitates tip cell
ﬁlopodia formation and vessel sprouting.
To further investigate the role of endothelial PDLIM5 in angio-
genesis, we ﬁrst used a tube formation assay and found that PDLIM5
KD decreased tube length (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g). Conversely,
PDLIM5 overexpression using a lentiviral vector encoding PDLIM5
increased tube length (Supplementary Fig. 5h–j). We also performed
RNA-seq to assess the transcriptional landscape of control and PDLIM5
KD human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs). Gene Ontology pathway
analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed enrichment
in angiogenesis, ECM organization, cell adhesion, and response to
hypoxia pathways (Supplementary Fig. 5k). Genes enriched in these
pathways were downregulated in PDLIM5 KD HUVECs, including col-
lagen type IV alpha 1 ( COL4A1), collagen type IV alpha 2 ( COL4A2),
matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP2), and vasohibin-1 (VASH1) (Supple-
mentary Fig. 5l). RT-PCR further validated the downregulation of these
genes in PDLIM5 KD HUVECs (Supplementary Fig. 5m). Previous stu-
dies have reported that these cell migration-related genes are regu-
lated by integrin/focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling
17,a n do u r
results showed that the FAK inhibitor Y15 suppressed the expression of
VASH1, VEGFR3, and MMP2 (Supplementary Fig. 5n). We then investi-
gated whether decreased ﬁlopodia formation in PDLIM5 KD HUVECs
would also inhibit integrin/FAK signaling activation by reducing the
response to the ECM. We found that PDLIM5 KD inhibited FAK acti-
vation, whereas PDLIM5 overexpression promoted FAK activation
(Supplementary Fig. 5o). These results indicate that PDLIM5 deletion
impedes FAK activation and ﬁlopodia formation in tip cells.
PDLIM5 promotes F-actin bundling duringﬁlopodia formation
by interacting with ACTN1/ACTN4
To unravel the molecular mechanisms behind the defects in endo-
thelial ﬁlopodia formation in the absence of PDLIM5, we hypothe-
sized that PDLIM5 plays a crucial role through interactions with
cytoskeletal proteins. To test this, we overexpressed PDLIM5 with a
Flag tag in HUVECs, followed by Flag immunoprecipitation (IP) and
mass spectrometry (MS) analysis (Fig. 4a, b), which led to the iden-
tiﬁcation of 145 PDLIM5-interacting proteins, including actin. To
further validate PDLIM5 ’s involvement in cell migration and adhe-
sion, we analyzed adhesion datasets and found PDLIM5 at one of the
highest frequencies among cell migration-related genes (Supple-
mentary Fig. 6a), with 13 of these proteins also identiﬁed in the actin-
binding gene set and meta-adhesome dataset. Our MS analysis spe-
ciﬁcally revealed that PDLIM5 interacts with actinin-1 (ACTN1) and
actinin-4 (ACTN4), proteins critical for F-actin bundling andﬁlopodia
formation
18,19. These interactions were con ﬁrmed by immunoblot
analysis in both HEK293T cells and HUVECs (Fig. 4c, d), where
PDLIM5 showed high co-localization with ACTN1/ACTN4 in the ﬁlo-
podia of tip cells (Fig. 4e, f, Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). To further
validate the regulatory role of ACTN1/ACTN4 in FAK signaling and
ﬁlopodia formation, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of
ACTN1/ACTN4 in endothelial cells. Results showed that knocking
down either ACTN1 or ACTN4 effectively suppressed FAK activation
and tip cell ﬁlopodia formation (Supplementary Fig. 7a –e).

hypoxia, suppressed immune cell function, and impaired delivery of

led to increased coverage of smooth muscle actin (SMA)-positive
pericytes around blood vessels (Fig. 7b, Supplementary Fig. 10e –g),
which is indicative of enhanced vascular stability and maturation.
Tumor vessel perfusion was also improved in Pdlim5-deﬁcient mice, as
assessed by ﬂuorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-lectin staining (Fig.7b),
implying better blood ﬂow. Furthermore, we observed a reduction in
tumor hemorrhage with decreased presence of Ter119-positive blood
cells in the interstitial space (Supplementary Fig. 10h –k) and a
decrease in tumor vessel permeability (Supplementary Fig. 10l –o).
These combined effects indicate that the loss of Pdlim5 in TECs not
only alleviates hypoxia but also promotes the normalization of the
tumor vasculature, potentially enhancing immune cell function,
thereby providing a therapeutic advantage in chemosensitization and
immunotherapy.
Previous studies have shown that normalization of tumor vessels
can inhibit tumor metastasis
22–24. Therefore, to determine the effects of
Pdlim5 knockout on tumor metastasis, we established subcutaneous LLC
tumor xenografts, performed surgical resection of the primary tumors,
and analyzed lung metastasis after tumor regrowth. Results showed that
Pdlim5 deletion signiﬁcantly inhibited LLC cell lung metastasis (Sup-

induced tumor vessel normalization could enhance the efﬁcacy of low-
dose chemotherapy. Results showed that low-dose cisplatin (4 mg/kg)
combined with Pdlim5 deletion further reduced tumor growth and
compared to cisplatin alone (Supplementary Fig. 11d–j).

Discussion
Current anti-angiogenic therapies in clinical use primarily target the
VEGF-VEGFR pathway, but their efﬁcacy is inadequate. Recent research
on tip TECs has shown therapeutic beneﬁts, and remarkable progress
has been made in characterizing their key functions and molecular
targets5. However, the mechanism underlying cytoskeletal organiza-
tion duringﬁlopodia formation in tip TECs remains unclear. Our study
demonstrates that PDLIM5, an actin-binding protein, is signi ﬁcantly
upregulated in TECs, correlating with poor patient survival in lung
cancer. Mechanistically, PDLIM5 promotes tip cellﬁlopodia formation
and tumor angiogenesis by interacting with ACTN1/ACTN4 to facilitate
F-actin bundling. Intriguingly, endothelial-speciﬁc Pdlim5 deletion not
only reduces tumor angiogenesis but also ameliorates tumor hypoxia,
normalizes the tumor vasculature, and enhances the response to anti-
PD-L1 therapy, particularly in immunotherapy-insensitive tumors.
PDLIM family proteins, known for their scaffolding functions
37,38,
are involved in various cellular processes, including cell migration,
differentiation, and EMT
14. Recent studies have found that PDLIM5 is
upregulated in prostate carcinoma 39, thyroid papillary carcinoma 40,
and lung cancer 15,41, mainly promoting cell migration and EMT 42.
However, the speciﬁc role of PDLIM5 in vascular endothelium has been
relatively understudied. Our previous study found that PDLIM5 was
upregulated in injured mouse arteries and human atherosclerotic
plaques and that endothelium-speci ﬁc deletion of Pdlim5 reduces
carotid artery neointima formation induced by ligation or wire injury
16;
this study extends its role to TECs, where it promotes ﬁlopodia for-
mation and angiogenesis. These ﬁndings suggest that PDLIM5 is a
potential target for inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis, although
further research is needed to develop targeted therapies, such as using
proteolysis-targeting chimera or lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) to degrade
PDLIM5.
The interaction with α-actinin appears to be a conserved yet
functionally diversiﬁed feature across multiple PDLIM family mem-
bers, extending beyond the previously emphasized roles of PDLIM5/7
and PDLIM1. While PDLIM1 selectively binds α-actinin4 (ACTN4) at
Asn145 to suppress F-actin overgrowth and sustain Hippo signaling via
LATS1 stabilization
43, Pdlim2 interacts broadly with α-actinins to reg-
ulate cytoskeletal stability 44,45, suggesting isoform-speci ﬁc partner-
ships ﬁne-tuned for distinct cellular outputs. Similarly, PDLIM5/7 not
only recruit YAP via their PDZ domain but also rely on directα-actinin
binding to translocate to stressﬁbers under mechanical force, thereby
coupling cytoskeletal tension to YAP activation46. This recurring theme
—where LIM domains engage α-actinins to scaffold cytoskeletal orga-
nization, while PDZ domains either anchor to F-actin or recruit sig-
naling effectors (e.g., YAP) —implies that α-actinin interactions are
integral to the PDLIM family’s dual role as mechanosensitive adaptors.
Such functional conservation highlights α-actinin as a central node
enabling PDLIM proteins to spatially integrate cytoskeletal dynamics
with pathway-speciﬁc signaling, a mechanism likely critical for all
family members despite context-dependent variations in binding
partners or downstream targets.
Oriented cell migration depends on the formation of ﬁlopodia
and lamellipodia in the cell’s apical segment, which act as “antennae”
during cell attachment, migration, and development
47. As cell-attached
structures, ﬁlopodia recruit integrins that mediate the physical con-
nection between the cell and its external environment (e.g., ECM) to
perform a range of cellular activities 47,48. The formation of ﬁlopodia
relies on the bundling of actinﬁlaments, a process regulated by cross-
linking proteins such as scruin, fascin, and espin 18,49. α-Actinin, a
member of the spectrin/dystrophin family, has two isoforms in non-
muscle cells: ACTN1 and ACTN4
47. It consists of an N-terminal actin-
binding domain (ABD), a central rod domain, and a C-terminal cal-
modulin-like domain (CaMD), which is responsible for binding F-actin.
The central rod domain is responsible for forming antiparallel homo-
dimers, while the C-terminal CaMD is reported to sense cytosolic cal-
cium signals, thereby regulating ABD orientation in the dimer to
effectively cross-link F-actin
50. ACTN1 and ACTN4 promote ﬁlopodia
formation and cell migration51–53. In this study, we found that PDLIM5
binds to ACTN1 and ACTN4 through the S593 and F596 sites. Addi-
tionally, our results showed that the N-terminal PDZ domain of PDLIM5
can directly bind F-actin, consistent with the previous results reported
by Nakagawa et al.
54. Thus, PDLIM5 acts as a scaffolding protein to
promote the binding of actinin-1 and actinin-4 to F-actin, increasing
F-actin bundling. In tumor vascular tip cells, increased PDLIM5
expression promotes ﬁlopodia formation in tip TEC cells. Recently,
another study showed that the sar comeric protein Cypher/Z-band
alternatively spliced PDZ-motif protein, a member of the PDZ-LIM
protein family, also stabilized F-actin by binding to F-actin, thereby
promoting cardiomyocyte maturation
55.

function, exacerbates hypoxia and impairs immune cell function,
reducing immunotherapy efﬁcacy

Normal lung ECs and tumor ECs were isolated from mouse lung tissues

Proteomics analysis
HUVECs were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding PDLIM5
with a C-terminal Flag tag for 48 h, subsequently lysed in IP buffer
(50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium
deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 1 mM ethylenediaminete-
traacetic acid, and protease inhibitors) and incubated with magnetic
anti-FLAG beads overnight at 4 °C. After washing, proteins were eluted,
concentrated, and subjected to in-gel digestion. Gel bands or spots
were cut into approximately 1 mm³ pieces, destained, dehydrated, and
reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol at 37 °C for 1 hour, followed by
alkylation with 55 mM iodoacetamide in the dark for 30 minutes. After
dehydration, gel pieces were washed with deionized water, rehydrated
in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, and digested with trypsin overnight
at 37 °C. Peptides were extracted, combined, and desalted using C18
columns. Liquid chromatography –tandem mass spectrometry was
performed with a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher
Scientiﬁc Inc) using Nanospray Flex ™ ion source with a 1 µgs a m p l e .
The liquid chromatography conditions included aﬂow rate of 600 nL/
min and a gradient from 8% to 95% acetonitrile over 60 minutes. Pro-
tein identi ﬁcation was performed using the human database with
Proteome Discoverer2.4 software. The protein mass spectrometry
sequencing data were uploaded to the iProX database ( https://www.
iprox.cn/page/home.html). The Project ID is IPX0010745000, and the
ProteomeXchange ID is PXD059449.
RNA-sequencing analysis
Total RNA was isolated from HUVECs transfected with shPDLIM5 or
shScr lentivirus for 48 h using an RNA-Quick Puri ﬁcation Kit (ES-
RN001, Yishan Biotechnology). Subsequently, cDNA libraries were
constructed and sequenced by the Be ijing Genomics Institute using
the BGISEQ-500 platform. The bioinformatics work ﬂow was per-
formed according to procedures established by Dr. TOM (BGI). A false
discovery rate-corrected p-value of 0.05 was considered statistically
signiﬁcant. The corresponding raw data were uploaded to the Genome
Sequence Archive (GSA) (Project No.: HRA009823).

ormaldehyde (PFA) or stored at −80 °C until further analysis.
To elucidate the effect of PDLIM5 deletion on tumor vessel per-
fusion and leakage, we conducted assays for hypoxia in the tumor
microenvironment, pimonidazole staining, and vascular leakage and
perfusion, as previously described. Tumor hypoxia was quantiﬁed with
a Hypoxyprobe kit (HP1-100, Hypoxyprobe) in accordance with the
manufacturer’s instructions. To evaluate vascular permeability, 100μl
of FITC-conjugated dextran (25 μg / m L ,7 0 k D a ,F D 7 0 ,S i g m a - A l d r i c h )
was administered intravenously to the mice, followed by perfusion
with 1% PFA solution 30 min later. Subsequently, the tumors were
dissected and embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound for sub-
sequent analysis. To quantify vessel perfusion, we administered
intravenous injections of DyLight 488-conjugated Tomato lectin
(1 mg/mL, 100 μL, DL-1174-1, Vector Laboratories) 30 min before
sacriﬁcing the mice, after which the tumors were harvested.
To explore the synergistic effect of PDLIM5 deletion on cisplatin
treatment, tumor-bearing mice were intraperitoneally injected with
4 mg/kg cisplatin (MCE, HY-17394) dissolved in saline every 3 days
starting at tumor volume ≈60 mm³; control groups received equal-
volume saline injections ( n = 5/group), with tumor dimensions mon-
itored by caliper every 2 days (volume=0.5×L×W²).
In experiments utilizing immunotherapeutic checkpoint inhibi-
tors, 200 μg (10 mg/ kg) of anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (BioXcell,
BE0101) or isotype immunoglobulin G was administered intraper-
itoneally to each mouse at 3-day intervals. Mice were subsequently
sacriﬁced at the indicated times, and tumors were collected for sub-
sequent analysis.
Patients and samples
All human studies complied with institutional and national ethical
regulations and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the
Fourth Af ﬁliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine
(Approval No.: K2022179). Written informed consent was obtained
from all participants prior to sample collection. For single-cell RNA
sequencing, paired tumor and adjacent non-tumor lung tissues were
prospectively collected from seven patients with NSCLC between
November 2022 and January 2023 at the Fourth Af ﬁliated Hospital of
Zhejiang University (Yiwu, Zhejiang, China). Fresh specimens were
enzymatically dissociated immediately after surgical resection to
obtain single-cell suspensions for library construction and sequencing.
For immunoﬂuorescence (IF) analysis, paired tumor and adjacent non-
tumor tissues from an additional ten patients were used to compare
PDLIM5 expression levels in endothelial cells (CD31 ⁺). PDLIM5 ﬂuor-
escence intensity was quantiﬁed using ImageJ software and normalized
to DAPI to control for cell density. For survival analysis, paraf ﬁn-
embedded tumor samples from twenty NSCLC patients were sub-
jected to IF staining for PDLIM5 and CD31 to stratify cases into PDLIM5-
high and PDLIM5-low endothelial expression groups based on median
signal intensity. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from diagnosis to
death or last follow-up. Survival curves were analyzed by Kaplan–Meier
log-rank test and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression
using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.1), with statistical signiﬁcance deﬁned as
p < 0.05. Comprehensive clinicopathological information of all
patients is provided in Supplementary Table 6.
Single-cells sequencing and data analysis
The lung tissues were dissociated into single-cell suspensions to
facilitate further analysis. Single-cell suspensions were prepared at a
concentration of 1 × 10
5 cells/mL in PBS and loaded onto microﬂuidic
devices. scRNA-seq libraries were constructed using the GEXSCOPE®
Single-Cell RNA Library Kit (Singleron Biotechnologies, China). Next,
the individual libraries were diluted to 4 ng/ µL and subsequently
pooled for sequencing. The pooled libraries were subjected to
sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument with 150 bp
paired-end reads. Additionally, the raw data were subjected to a series
of processing steps to generate the gene expression proﬁles. This was
achieved using CeleScope v1.14.0, a software package developed by
Singleron Biotechnologies, with the default parameters retained. The
Scanpy package, version 1.8.1, was utilized for quality control, dimen-
sionality reduction, and cluster analysis in the Python 3.7 program-
ming language. We ﬁltered the expression matrix by the following
criteria: 1) cells with gene count less than 200 or with top 2% gene
count were excluded; 2) cells with the top 2% UMI count were exclu-
ded; 3) cells with mitochondrial content > 20% were excluded; 4) genes
expressed in less than 5 cells were excluded. The batch effect was
removed using the Harmony software. Furthermore, the DEGs were
identiﬁed by comparing the two groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum
test with the standard parameters applied. Genes were considered
differentially expressed if they were expressed in at least 10% of the
cells in either of the two groups and exhibited an adjusted p-value
below a threshold of 0.05. This was calculated through the Benjamini-
Hochberg correction for multiple hypothesis testing. The human
single-cell transcriptomics data are deposited under the accessions:
HRA012795.

independent experiments and analyzed using GraphPad Prism
8.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Statistical signiﬁcance
between individual groups was evaluated using two-tailed indepen-
dent Student’s t-tests. When examining multiple groups, a one-way or
two-way analysis of variance was performed. Survival differences were
assessed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and statistical signi ﬁcance
was determined using the log-rank test. Statistical signiﬁcance was set
at p-value < 0.05, and this is indicated in theﬁgures as follows:p <0 . 0 5
