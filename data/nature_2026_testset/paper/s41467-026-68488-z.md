Multistable systems, ideally, could stabilize at any desired, switchable state
within a continuous spectrum. However, conventionalsystems, constrained by
signal-mediated mutual activation or inhibition, are limited to aﬁnite set of
discrete steady states. Here, we propose a rational framework for achieving
continuously tunable multistability through reversible displacement reaction-
mediated competition between positiveautoregulatory DNA polymerization/
nicking modules. This framework harnesses the chemical energy of dNTP
hydrolysis to suppress spontaneous interconversion between modules for
stabilizing at any target state along a continuous compositional gradient. With
unparalleled tunability, the frameworkenables continuous, orthogonal state
transitions and concentration-adaptive molecular memory in response to
transient stimuli. Moreover, the single-stranded DNAs generated by poly-
merization/nicking reactionscan be customized with predeﬁned structures
and functions, enabling continuously multistable control over downstream
processes, e.g., biocatalysis and RNA transcription, while maintaining multi-
stability. This framework establishes a versatile and robust platform for
developing chemical and material systems with continuously tunable
multistability.
In cells, highly sophisticated molecular reaction networks 1–4 orches-
trate various biochemical activities to maintain cellular function and
homeostasis. These networks operate via different mechanisms,
including feedback
5, feedforward6, intercommunication7,a d a p t a t i o n8,
oscillation9, and multistability 10, to achieve precise modulation of
biological functions. Inspired by the complexity and ﬁne-tuned mod-
ulation observed in natural systems, alternative strategies 11–13 have
been developed for bottom-up assembly of synthetic networks in
systems chemistry
14,15 and synthetic biology16,17 over the past decade.
These efforts focus on replicating the properties of living systems18–20,
such as environmental adaptation 21–23 and complex information
processing24–27. For instance, synthetic networks have been designed
to govern temporal28–31,s p a t i a l32,33 or multistage34 self-assembly pro-
cesses for emerging functions 28,35, harmonize transitions between
material steady states36–38, generate chemical or material spatial pat-
terns through coupled reactions and diffusion 39,40, or orchestrate
spatiotemporal signaling and developmental patterning processes41–43.

Although largely unexplored in molecular programming, con-
tinuous multistability has precedents in other ﬁelds. For example, in
neuroscience, continuous attractor networks are well-established
models for processing analog information
68–70. The oculomotor inte-
grator, a neural circuit that maintains arbitrary eye positions in
response to transient inputs, provides a canonical example of persis-
tent analog memory. Similarly, in mechanical metamaterials, arrays of
bistable elements can adopt a continuum of structural conﬁgurations
in response to global inputs, illustrating how discrete components can
be organized to exhibit graded behavior
71.
In this study, we present a rational design for constructing scal-
able and controllable continuously tunable multistability networks
(CTMNs) through reversible displacement reaction-mediated compe-
tition among positive autoregulatory DNA polymerization/nicking
modules, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. By dissipating free energy from a
dNTP reservoir, the system operates under far-from-equilibrium con-
ditions where competition among these modules suppresses their
spontaneous transitions, thereby stabilizing the system at any desired
compositional state, a phenomenon we term continuously tunable
multistability. We validate this continuously tunable multistability by
experimental data and mathematical modeling. The tunable
interconversion between modules mediated by reversible displace-
ment reactions enables continuous, orthogonal state transitions and
concentration-adaptive molecular memory in response to transient
stimuli. Moreover, reversible displacement reactions simplify the
inherent complexity of conventional signaling pathways, offering a
signiﬁcant advantage in modular scalability. This approach allows for
the construction of larger-scaled, more complex CTMNs by seamlessly
incorporating additional DNA polymerization/nicking modules. A
unique feature of these networks is their ability to achieve precise,
state-speciﬁc DNA replication via polymerization/nicking reactions,
enabling the on-demand synthesis of single-stranded DNAs (ssDNAs)
that can encode diverse structural and functional information. These
ssDNAs further extend the system ’s versatility by regulating down-
stream processes, such as DNAzyme-catalyzed reactions
72 and RNA
transcription73, in a stepless and programmable manner, without
compromising the system’s multistability.

Design of a continuously tunable multistability network
We developed CTMNs based on reversible displacement reaction-
mediated far-from-equilibrium competition between positive auto-
regulatory DNA polymerization/nicking modules, such as CTMN-1,
illustrated in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1. The network consists of
four precisely engineered DNA duplexes: AA’,B B’,B A’,a n dA B’.E a c h
duplex incorporates an exchangeable double-stranded domain (ZZ’),
facilitating a reversible displacement reaction between competing
modules AA ’ +B B’ and BA ’ +A B’
74,75. This reversible displacement
reaction offers two critical advantages over traditional designs. First, in
contrast to traditional systems producing binary outcomes —one
module is fully activated while the other is entirely suppressed, a
reversible displacement reaction allows precise, proportional control
over modules’ fractions by modulating their transitions, enabling the
ﬁne-tuning of network states. Second, employing a reversible dis-
placement reaction to mediate competition between network modules
could simplify the architectural design relative to traditional systems,
which often rely on complex signaling pathways to establish mutual
inhibition or activation between modules. Therefore, this competitive
architecture could reduce assembly complexity and improve scal-
ability and robustness (vide infra).

two modules, we coupled this reversible displacement reaction with a

This design adheres to the fuel-driven, far-from-equilibrium
principles observed in other multistable biochemical systems, such
as genelet

hydrolysis fuels multistable behaviors. In these systems, RNA or DNA
strands produced via polymerization serve as regulatory species,
mediating mutual activation or inhibition to sculpt system dynamics.
The concentrations of these species are tightly regulated through
coordinated synthesis and degradation, allowing precise control of
network behavior. Meanwhile, inert degradation byproducts gradually

ensure well-de ﬁned initial conditions, each duplex component of
the network modules was pre-annealed prior to its introduction

changes associated with the network were recorded, and the measured
data were subsequently converted to concentrations of the corre-
sponding DNA duplexes using calibration curves that correlate
ﬂuorescence intensities with DNA duplex concentrations (Supple-
mentary Fig. 4).
First, we investigated the reversible displacement reaction
between modules AA’ +B B’ and BA’ +A B’ in the absence of DNA poly-
merase, nicking endonuclease, and dNTPs, thus excluding poly-
merization/nicking reactions. As depicted in Fig. 3a–ca n d

robust and functionally meaningful within a biological context. Guided
by this conceptual framework, we adopted a criterion for steady-state

Scaling the continuously tunable multistability network
In conventional multistable networks, integrating additional network
modules for scalability requires the establishment of complex reg-
ulatory interactions between the introduced modules and each of the
existing ones. As the number of introduced modules increases, the
signaling complexity increases quadratically, posing a signi ﬁcant
challenge to the scalability of these systems. In contrast, our design,
based on the reversible displacement reaction-mediated competitive
interaction between network modules, provides a more simpli ﬁed
approach to scalability. It enables the modular expansion of the CTMN
by directly integrating multiple pre-designed DNA duplexes, each
containing a shared exchangeable double-stranded domain and a
distinct template domain. These duplexes naturally compete via
reversible displacement reactions, thereby circumventing the need for
intricate regulatory interactions typically required in conventional
multistable systems.

Discussion
This study introduces the concept of continuously tunable multi-
stability and establishes a robust framework for designing such net-
works, marking a signiﬁcant advancement in multistability-controlled
molecular architectures. Traditionally, multistability in molecular sys-
tems has been achieved by modulating signaling molecules through
either activating or inhibiting network modules, resulting in discrete
steady states. Instead, we employ reversible displacement reactions to
mediate far-from-equilibrium competition between autoregulatory
DNA polymerization/nicking modules via dissipating free energy from
a dNTP reservoir. Both experimental data and theoretical simulations
demonstrate that this competitive framework effectively suppresses
spontaneous transitions between network modules, stabilizing the
system at any desired compositional state. This enables continuously
tunable multistability across a continuous spectrum of network com-
positions, transcending the conventional limitations of discrete-state
multistability in molecular systems. LSA shows that the CTMN archi-
tecture can, in principle, support a continuum of multistable steady
states with continuously tunable compositions. Experimentally, how-
ever, the number of practically resolvable states is constrained by
biochemical factors such as dNTP availability. To assess experimental
scalability while accounting for the combined effects of system-wide

Although this represents only a subset of the theoretically accessible

steady states. Continuous- ﬂow operation could alleviate substrate
depletion and extend steady-state duration, whereas engineered
feedback motifs may deepen attractor basins and further suppress
long-term drift.
The CTMN retains key features of traditional multistable systems
while introducing several unique properties: (i) Continuously tunable
multistability: Unlike conventional mechanisms restricted to discrete
steady states, the CTMN can stabilize at any target point along a con-
tinuous compositional space, for example, a one-dimensional con-
tinuum in CTMN-1 or a two-dimensional triangular plane in CTMN-2,
offering unprecedented accessibility of steady states; (ii) Hysteresis

continuously tunable steady states, the CTMN preserves the hallmark

incorporates a waste-to-signal conversion mechanism, in which
energy-dissipative reactions generate ssDNA outputs (P ₓ)t h a ta r e

programmable signals that can drive downstream modules (e.g.,
DNAzymes or transcription machinery) without perturbing core net-
work dynamics. This design enhances both system stability and
downstream functional versatility. Collectively, these features estab-
lish a generalizable framework for programming continuously tunable
multistability in both biochemical and material systems, unlocking
new functions such as graded signal processing, proportional memory,
and biomimetic analog computation.
From a broader chemical perspective, this work represents a
paradigm shift in the design and control of far-from-equilibrium sys-
tems. While the energy-fueled competitive mechanism employed here
offers a compelling alternative to conventional signaling inhibition
strategies, the principles of reversible displacement reactions remain
at an exploratory stage and have not yet been fully realized. Beyond
inter-module competition, our system also highlights the potential for
intra-module cooperation, where the dynamic changes in one com-
ponent (such as AA’ in module AA’ +B B’) drive synchronized response
in another component (BB ’). This agonistic relationship may be an
alternative to classical signal-based activation, providing an additional
mechanism for regulating far-from-equilibrium dynamics. The design
framework presented here offers a foundation for constructing far-
from-equilibrium networks with more intricate topologies and
dynamic behaviors, including oscillatory, chaotic, and other complex
systems. Moreover, future studies could extend the core principle of
far-from-equilibrium competition to molecular systems beyond
nucleic acids, such as small organic molecules, peptides, and proteins.
By applying reversible displacement reactions across a broader range
of molecular platforms, it is anticipated that the versatility and
applicability of these mechanisms in far-from-equilibrium networks
will be signi ﬁcantly enhanced, paving the way for applications in
complex biochemical systems.
