Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in proteins drive phase separation (PS)
to form biomolecular condensates, which organize cellular matter. While IDRs
are recognized as critical drivers of PS, the systematic identiﬁcation of
sequence motifs governing this phenomenon and their compositional deter-
minants remain a key challenge. Here we developPhaSeMotif,ad e e pl e a r n i n g
framework for interpretable and precise predictions of essentialphase-
separating motifs within IDRs. We experimentally validate PhaSeMotif,
demonstrating that mutations of predicted motifs signiﬁcantly reduce or
eliminate the PS capabilities of IDRs. The identiﬁed motifs possess diverse
amino acid compositional features thatare critical for determining PS pro-
pensities and condensate partitioning. Furthermore, PhaSeMotif integrates
generative models to create validation-ready motifs that preserve these critical
compositional features, empowering direct experimental veriﬁcation and
deeper mechanistic investigation of PS-driving IDR motifs. Overall, by com-
bining motifs prediction, generation,and validation, PhaSeMotif provides an
open-access toolkit to facilitate more efﬁcient IDR motifs investigation and
provides insights into the molecular determinants of PS.
Phase separation (PS) of proteins and nucleic acids commonly
underlies the formation of biomolecular condensates, supporting the
spatiotemporal regulation of cellular activities such as gene regulation
and signal transduction 1,2.B e s i d e st h ei d e n t iﬁcation of PS proteins,
there have been major efforts to delineate the molecular mechanisms
and functions under normal and pathological conditions. Recent stu-
dies have also illuminated the link between aberrant PS and diseases
including developmental de ﬁciency, cancers, and infectious
diseases
3– 6. However, investigating the molecular mechanisms and
functions of individual PS proteins often requires extensive manip-
ulation of protein sequences and numerous experiments

fundamental physicochemical principles driving PS by IDRs have been
extensively studied7,9,10, systematically identifying the full diversity of
these functional sequence motifs using computational tools remains a
key challenge. This gap is particularly challenging for understudied
novel proteins, where the absence of prior information makes it difﬁ-
cult to formulate feasible hypotheses and hinders systematic analysis.

Most IDRs are biased in amino acids composition and possess
sequence patterns such as short linear interaction motifs (SLiMs) or
repeated sequences
19. These IDR motifs are recognized for driving
interactions that promote PS and mo dulate PS properties, including
condensate material states20 and partitioning behavior21. For instance,
the N-terminal IDR of Ddx4 arranges charged residues into clustered
blocks of net positive and negative charge, driving PS through elec-
trostatic interactions
22. RNA-binding proteins such as FUS contain
multiple RG/RGG motifs, whose cation-pi interactions with aromatic
tyrosine residues govern PS properties
8. Additionally, replacing nega-
tively charged IDR motifs in HMGB1 with arginine-rich motifs enhances
mis-partitioning into the nucleolus and causes nucleolar dysfunction
3.
Compared to structural domains, investigating important motifs in
IDRs is more challenging due to the lack of sequence conservation and
clear deﬁning features. Typically, in-depth investigation of IDR motifs
requires extensive mutational studies, such as complete or partial IDR
truncation and/or multiple substitutions
8,23. These approaches are
labor-intensive and difﬁcult to conduct without guidance from effec-
tive computational tools.
Here, we introduce PhaSeMotif, an interpretable deep learning
framework designed to address these critical limitations by enabling
in-depth investigation of IDR motifs essential for PS and their mole-
cular principles. PhaSeMotif predicts PS propensity of IDRs and, cru-
cially, reverse infers sequence regions critical for positive predictions,
pinpointing IDR motifs through attention mechanisms and guided
backpropagation
24,25. PhaSeMotif integrates generative models to
design artiﬁcial motifs that preserve key features of identiﬁed motifs,
providing validation-ready motifs for direct experimental substitution
and veri ﬁcation. We experimentally validated PhaSeMotif, demon-
strating that mutation of ~83% (14/17) of predicted motifs impaired or
abolished PS. Furthermore, by clustering motifs based on composi-
tional features and using generative models to create artiﬁcial motifs,
we experimentally con ﬁrmed the importance of these features in
driving PS, with ~86% (18/21) of generated motifs restoring PS cap-
ability. Systematic analysis of human IDR motifs revealed motif com-
binations linked to condensate partitioning and biological functions.
Overall, PhaSeMotif provides a framework for ef ﬁcient and
mechanism-driven IDR motif investigation, integrating interpretable
prediction, validation-ready motif generation, and experimental vali-
dation to accelerate the discovery of molecular determinants of phase
separation.
Results
Framework for investigating IDR motifs essential for PS
To facilitate the investigation of PS IDRs and motifs within, we pro-
posed a framework that utilizes computational predictions for
hypothesis formulation and PS experiments for hypothesis validation
(Fig. 1a). Speciﬁcally, for an IDR of interest, the PS propensity is pre-
dicted based on its sequence. For high PS propensity IDR, reverse
inference provides the relative importance of each region and thus
predicts key regions or motifs. The motifs can be validated through
mutation experiments, in which the disruption of motifs would
attenuate or even eliminate PS of IDRs while the negative control
resulted in insigniﬁcant effects. The motifs are clustered according to
their compositional features, which represent the underlying princi-
ples. If the attenuation of PS is rescued by substituting mutated motifs
with generated motifs with similar features, the validity of motif fea-
tures would be con ﬁrmed. Through the prediction, generation and
validation framework, researchers would be able to capture the
molecular determinants of PS IDRs.
Establishment of sequence-based high PS propensity IDR deep
learning model
We organized the dataset by leveraging both IDRs prediction and PS
propensity predictions. Comprehensive proteome-wide IDRs were
identiﬁed using several predictors and the consecutive residues were
kept. We used PhaSePred, a comprehensive multimodal PS protein
predictor, to predict the PS propensities of these IDR-containing
proteins
11. Based on the predictions, we established our dataset: IDRs
from proteins with high predicted PS propensity were denoted as
positive set (“high propensity” set), while IDRs from proteins with low
predicted propensity were used as negative set ( “background pro-
pensity” set) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We selected an optimal thresh-
old for positive set IDRs in order to balance the true positive rate and
precision, minimizing potential noise from predicted labels (Supple-
mentary Fig. 1b) (Methods) .A sar e s u l t ,at o t a lo f3 , 1 3 1I D R sw i t hh i g hP S
propensity and 11,763 IDRs with background propensity in human
proteome were collected (Supplementary Data 1). This dataset was
randomly split into training, validation, and test subsets (3:1:1), which
were conﬁrmed to be balanced, diverse, and compositionally similar
(Supplementary Fig. 1c-f). A similar approach was conducted to collect
datasets in other species, which were used for model validation
afterwards (Supplementary Fig. 1g).
Explaining the internal behavior of deep learning models has been
extensively studied and attention mechanism are dominating the
explainability of deep models
26. We presumed that by predicting the
PS propensity of an IDR using attention-based models, the relative
importance of each region within the input sequence is determined.
The most important regions identi ﬁed in this way can represent
potential motifs essential for PS to some extent. Hence, we built Pha-
SeMotif, an interpretable attenti on-based deep learning model for
high PS propensity IDR prediction. Due to the variability in IDR lengths,
the model accepts IDR sequences of arbitrary length to enhance ease
of use. The input sequences are processed using one-hot encoding
based on the amino acids and the amino acid alphabet (Supplementary
Table 1, Methods). The model consists primarily of convolutional
layers
27, multi-head attention layer 24, pyramid pooling layers, fully
connected layers, and an output layer (Fig. 1b). The convolutional
layers are responsible for extracting local features (15 residues in this
context), while the attention mechanism layers capture both regional
and global relationships within the sequence. The pyramid pooling
layers convert inputs of arbitrary length into ﬁxed-length representa-
tions, facilitating subsequent fully connected layers for output
probability
28. The model was trained with training and validation set
and the performance was then evaluated with the independent test set
(Methods). The ﬁnal area under the receiver operating characteristic
curve (AUROC) of PhaSeMotif in test set achieved 0.86 (Fig. 1c, Sup-
plementary Fig. 1h) and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC)
achieved 0.69 (Supplementary Fig. 1i), demonstrating its strong per-
formance. PhaSeMotif also displayed similar performance in datasets
of other four species (Fig. 1c). The distribution of PhaSeMotif predic-
tion scores between validation and test datasets were similar and
around 30% of human IDRs were scored above 0.5 (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 1j).

PhaSeMotif extracted IDR motifs using guided backpropagation
and attention scores
As mentioned above, convolution and attention layers captured
regional and global features during training. We utilized guided
backpropagation (GBP), a gradient-based method to interpret deep
learning models
25, and attention scores24 to extract the attribution of
input IDR’sm o t i f st h a tl e a dt ot h eﬁnal positive prediction. Speciﬁcally,
during GBP, only activated neurons with positive forward and back-
ward gradients were retained (denoted as positive regions) (Supple-
mentary Fig. 2a). As each positive region was the weighted
combination of the input regions, larger attention scores reﬂect more
important original input regions. Subsequently, we determined a cut-
off to identify the lists of most important input regions and deﬁned the
attribution score of each input region as the relative frequency that it
had been included in the positive regions set (Fig. 2a). Notably, as
longer IDR sequence often harbors more IDR motifs, the cutoff setting
was relevant to the length of individual IDR (Methods). Consequently,
the regions with top attribution scores were retained and merged,
resulting in the ﬁnal determination of motifs. Overall, we identiﬁed a
total of 17594 motifs derived from 2418 PS IDRs through this approach
(Supplementary Data 2). More than 59% of motifs were shorter than 20
amino acids (Fig.2b), which represent a small fraction of IDRs (Fig.2b).

Analysis of IDR motifs composition features revealed diverse
clusters
After predicting motifs within high PS propensity IDRs, decoding the
sequence features would advance our understanding of underlying
principles. We constructed the IDR motifs dataset consisting of 17,594
predicted IDR motifs derived from 2418 IDRs (Supplementary Data 2,
Methods). Both IDRs and motifs displayed high portion of disordered
prompting residues such as proline, glutamic acid, serine and lysine
(Fig. 4a)
41. Furthermore, proline, serine and glycine were more sig-
niﬁcantly enriched in motifs, consistent with the previousﬁnding that
self-assembling PS proteins contain a higher portion of these
residues11.
We next sought to cluster motifs based on sequence composi-
tional features. The ratio of individual or two consecutive amino acids
and each amino acid alphabet were calculated as basic sequence fea-
tures (Supplementary Table 2). We also deﬁned ‘abundant’ and ‘poly’
features which describe whether a particular amino acid is highly
enriched and whether it appears consecutively multiple times. The
distribution of each feature was then compared between high PS
propensity IDRs, background IDRs, and IDR motifs, resulting in 207
signiﬁcant sequence features (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary
Data 5). Considering the possible redundancy and linear/nonlinear
relations between features, we applied autoencoder to perform
dimensionality reduction. UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation
and Projection)
42 and DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of
Applications with Noise)43 were utilized to visualize and cluster the
motifs (Fig. 4b, Methods). Consequently, nine clusters with diverse
representative sequence features were identi ﬁed. We clustered the
motif using the sequence features and named them after the most
representative residue types, namely Cluster 0, Polar, Positive, and
negative (Pos&Neg), Proline, Glycine, Positive, Aliphatic, Negative and
Glutamine (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 4a).
To further dissect compositional features, we analyzed the rela-
tive amino acid abundance across motif clusters to further dissect their
compositional features (Fig.4d). The result is consistent with previous
ﬁndings that different types of interactions between IDRs drive PS,
including pi-pi, pi-cation, electrostatic, hydrophobic interactions and
so on (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 4a)
10. For example, aromatic resi-
dues (tyrosine and phenylalanine), along with glycine are relatively
enriched in Cluster 0 and Cluster Glycine. Aromatic residues are
important to mediate the pi-pi and pi-cation multivalent interactions
driving the PS of Prion-like domain (PLD) containing proteins
8,20.
Cluster Pos&Neg, Positive and Negative motifs possess basic and/or
acid amino acids, contributing to electrostatic interactions. In addi-
tion, the negatively and positive charged residues are separately dis-
tributed in Pos&Neg motifs, which are important for selective
partitioning into condensates
3,22,44. In addition, the abundance of
proline and glycine in Cluster Proline and Glycine motif clusters may
modulate IDRs disordered state, as these residues were highly enri-
ched in PLDs and low complexity domains (LCDs)
8. The result is con-
sistent with a previous report that proline and glycine are enriched in
putatively important residues in PS proteins
15. Glutamine motif cluster
encompassed repeated glutamine residues. Polyglutamine was found
to mediated condensation through abundant hydrogen bonding and
lead protein aggregation
45, examples are HTT protein and Ataxin46,47.
Among the clusters, Cluster 0 and Polar motifs are the most
common, indicating they may act as fundamental units in PS IDRs.
More than 90% of IDRs contain at least two types of motifs (Supple-
mentary Fig. 4b). The Cluster 0 and Polar motifs are usually partnered
with other types of motifs. IDRs containing both Cluster 0 motifs and
other motifs displayed higher PhaSeMotif scores, suggesting their
collaborative functions in promoting PS (Supplementary Fig. 4c). IDRs
with only Cluster Glycine, Glutamine motifs are also presented with
higher PhaSeMotif scores, consistent with their strong self-assembling
capability. Interestingly, Pos&Neg, Positive and Negative motifs are
usually presented together in IDRs, suggesting that opposite charged
residues within one IDRs but located separately are important to
electrostatic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Also, motifs with
enriched charged residues frequently partner with cluster 0 and Polar
motifs.
As motifs’ properties are related to interaction forces and thus
condensates partitioning, we also looked into the motifs preference of
proteins within known biomolecular condensates components
extracted from PhaSepDB
48,49. Most condensates were enriched in the
most common Cluster 0 and Polar motifs (Fig. 4e). Proteins with Gly-
cine motifs are enriched in paraspeckles, while proteins with Positive
motifs are enriched in nuclear speckles and spliceosomes. (Fig. 4e).
This is consistent with their functions closely associated with nucleic
acids, interactions with which would involve charged motifs. There is
emerging evidence that PS is involved in various molecular functions;
we conducted functional enrichment of proteins with different motifs.
Not surprisingly, non-membrane bounded organelles assembly is
enriched in Cluster 0, Polar and charged motifs (Pos&Neg, Positive,
Negative), suggesting their essential roles in condensate formation
(Fig. 4f). RNA-related functions are associated with Cluster 0, charged,
and Glycine motifs (Fig.4f). For example, previous studies have shown
that the IDR of FMR1 is essential for its condensation with RNA, which
regulates the translation process
50.
Together, these results indicate that PhaSeMotif identi ﬁed IDR
motifs whose combination provides hints for understanding the
underlying molecular determinants of PS. In addition, the preferences
of motifs for different condensates suggested that alteration in motifs
might represent a possible way to modulate partitioning.

Discussion
Numerous proteins with IDRs can undergo PS and promote biomole-
cular condensates formation. Illustrated by previous researches, key
regions or motifs in IDRs underlie multivalent interactions vital for PS
and condensate properties, such as charged blocks in Ddx4
22.
Historically, the putative motifs were identiﬁed from extensive muta-
tional experiments on particular high PS propensity IDRs and possibly
apply to proteins in the same family 8,20,52. However, detailed studies
into IDR motifs are still limited due to not only the dif ﬁculties in
determining possible motifs by expert observation or intuition, but
also the extensive experiments.
In order to facilitate the investigation of IDR motifs, we present a
framework that integrates interpretable motifs prediction and artiﬁcial
motifs generation through deep learning models. Under the frame-
work, the motifs within IDRs of interest are predicted computationally
and then validated using generated motifs that preserve important
sequence features (Fig. 1a). Speciﬁcally, instead of predicting motifs
directly, we mitigated the lack of labeled motifs data by leveraging
algorithms including attention-based mechanism and GBP to capture
relative importance of regions (Fig.1b). In contrast to existing protein-
wise predictors
11– 15, our sequence-based model provides region-wise
attrition without prior knowledge requirements. 82% of the validation
experiments demonstrated the necessity of motifs in driving IDRs PS
process, indicating the accuracy of our methods (Fig.3). After applying
PhaSeMotif in human PS IDRs, we revealed clusters of motifs that
display diverse sequence composition patterns (Fig. 4), including
enrichment in charged, polar amino acids or individual amino acids
such as proline and glycine, etc. The motif clusters represent the
important features that drive PS. To validate the correctness of these
sequence features, we applied deep generative models to produce
artiﬁcial motifs with similar features in each cluster. The generated
motifs were shown to restore the PS capabilities caused by original
motifs mutations in 18/21 tested cases, demonstrating the essential
role of sequence features in drivin gP S .O v e r a l l ,v i at h ep r e d i c t i o n ,
generation and validation framework with PhaSeMotif, the molecular
basis of PS IDRs would be effectively investigated.
Compared to other general PS protein prediction methods, Pha-
SeMotif accurately predicts more precise motif regions, as evidenced
in accuracy and speci ﬁcity experiments (Fig. 3). Mutations that sig-
niﬁcantly attenuated PS were more likely to be localized within the
motifs compared to mutations that have minor effects. However, due
to the limited sample size, the performance of PhaSeMotif in point
mutations should be further examined. According to a benchmark
evaluation, the performance of current sequence-based PS predictors
is still limited in predicting the inﬂuence of mutations on PS behavior,
constrained by the bias in currently available data
53. Therefore, built
upon a PS predictor, direct quantitative predictions by PhaSeMotif of
in-silico substitution with simple repeat or point mutations would be
beyond the scope.

Methods
IDRs annotations
Disordered sequence annotations were conducted using various
computational tools. The prion-like domains were identi ﬁed using
PLAAC16 with a PLAAC residue score greater than 0.5. Low complexity
regions were de ﬁned by SEG 54. Intrinsically disordered regions were
predicted using Espritz DisProt 55, with 5% false positive rate (FPR)
without PSI-BLAST. Low con ﬁdence regions were determined by
AlphaFold256 with pLDDT(predicted local distance difference test)
scores less than 50. If the distance between two IDRs in the same
proteins is shorter than their average length and shorter than twice the
length of either IDR, they were merged as one single IDR. Predicted
IDRs shorter than 50 residues were excluded for consistency across
different IDR predictions andﬁltering out possible noise
57.
High PS propensity IDRs annotations
We acquired PhaSePred scores of proteins in various species from
its web server 11. To minimize the potential noise introduced by
predicted PS labels, the threshold was determined in order to
optimize both precision and recall rate. Consequently, proteins
with scores higher than 0.6 were assigned as PS proteins and the
ones lower than 0.4 were assigned as non-PS proteins. The the-
oretical false negative rate is about 9.3% and false negative sam-
ples account for only 1.86% of the total samples. There is about 1
potential false negative sample per training batch (64 samples).
The ﬁnal dataset is consisting of 14,894 human, 11,348 mouse,
4847 Rat, 2343 Drome and 1748 Danre IDRs. IDR sequences used
in this study are detailed in Supplementary Data 1 with corre-
sponding UniProt IDs (canonical isoforms from release 2023_05).
The human dataset was divided in a ratio of 3:1:1 into training,
validation, and test datasets.
The extraction of PS-affecting mutations and z-scores
calculation
The PhaSepDB database covers mutation information that was
reported to alter PS activities 48. The mutations that exhibit effects
on PS IDRs (with PhaSeMotif predicted scores above 0.5) were
extracted from PhaSepDB. Mutational effects on PS are hetero-
geneous and sometimes biased, even though they are generally
considered to negatively affect the PS process. We have cate-
gorized mutations into major effect and minor effect groups
based on the severity of their impact on PS (criteria and refer-
ences are detailed in revised Supplementary Data 3). To allow
comparison across different IDRs, the attribution scores of each
mutation site and 1000 randomly sampled sites within the same
IDR were normalized into z-scores. This normalization provides a
statistical measure of whether mutations occur preferentially at
positions of high predicted importance for PS.
Construction of PhaSeMotif
A comprehensive summary of the machine learning dataset prepara-
tion, model construction, optimization, and evaluation, following the
checklist recommended by Walsh et al
58., is provided in Supplemen-
tary Data 7.
Data preprocessing. To represent the input sequence, we employed a
dual-channel encoding strategy for each amino acid. Weﬁrst grouped
the standard amino acids into a reduced alphabet of 9 classes based on
their physicochemical side-chain properties (Supplementary Table 1).
Each amino acid in the sequence was then converted into a 30-
dimensional feature vector by concatenating two one-hot vectors: a 21-
dimensional vector representing the speci ﬁc amino acid type and a
9-dimensional vector representing its corresponding class in the
reduced alphabet. The dual representation provides the model with
both the speciﬁc residue identity and its general biochemical context.
Model architecture. The one-hot embeddings were passed to multiple
two-dimensional convolution kernels with size of (21, 15) and (9, 15).
The step size and padding were set as 1 and (0, 1). The two resulting
feature maps were added and then transposed, with shape of (1, len, 8).
A total of 8 multi-headed attention layers were set up to fully capture
the local and global relationship between regions. The attention was
calculated as

the model was 512. The training process adopted the cross-entropy
loss function and the Adam optimization algorithm. By setting the
pos_weight parameter to 3.5, the loss function would put more
emphasis on correctly predicting the positive class, thus effectively
mitigating the imbalance of the two classes during the training pro-
cess. The step size of the Adam optimizer is set to 0.003. The model
was developed using the Pytorch framework.

Model interpretation
First, the GBP algorithm was used to reveal the important regions that
have a positive impact on prediction after the attention mechanism
reorganizes the information. The GBP ﬁltered out the regions with
negative gradients that do not contribute or contribute little to the
activation (Figs. 1B, 2A). We then selected the top 20 original regions
with the highest attention scores as the positive sets for each positive
region with positive forward and backward gradients. We deﬁned the
attribution score of each input region as the frequency of being
included in the set. The top n regions with highest attribution scores
were assigned as motifs. The cut off n can be calculated as 1 +
int(length/30), which was designed to be related to the length of IDR.
Overlapping regions were merged to ﬁnal determined motifs.
Composition features screening
We deﬁned sequence features for clustering, namely the basic single
amino acid proportion, double amino acid proportion, alphabet pro-
portion, abundant, and poly. When the proportion a type of amino acid
in a 15-residue window exceeds 0.5, it was considered as “abundant”.
When a type of amino acid appears continuously more than 6 times, it
is considered “poly” and the number of times is calculated. (Supple-
mentary Table 2). Next, the features in IDRs and motifs were compared
and we retained the ones that were either signiﬁcant between PS and
non-PS IDRs or between motifs and PS IDRs. A total of 207 features
were used for clustering (Supplementary Data 3). The statistical
examination was from python scipy (v1.10.1).
Dimensionality reduction and clustering
First, we used autoencoder for dimensionality reduction to integrate
linear and nonlinear relationships. The encoder and decoder were
composed of fully connected layers with the ReLU activation function
and 20-dimensional latent space. Next, UMAP was used for visualiza-
tion (umap-learn v0.5.5). DBSCAN was used for preliminary clustering
(scikit-learn v1.3.1). To evaluate the clustering results, we performed an
independent t-test to reveal the signi ﬁcant features of each cluster.
The clusters with fewer than 10 IDRs were removed and some clusters
with similar features were grouped together.
Generative models and artiﬁcial sequences assessment
We adopted VAE as a generative framework. Speciﬁcally, we encoded
the input sequence through convolution layers to generate multiple
channels, then sampled in the latent space, andﬁnally fuse the multiple
channels back through a decoder to generate a new sequence. Con-
sidering the differences between clusters, we train a VAE model for
each cluster respectively. The identi ﬁed motifs were re-segmented
into 15-residue sequences. The input one-hot encoding was the same as
described above. The generated result is a matrix with a shape of (15,
21) and maximum normalized to be converted as one-hot embeddings,
which can be translated into sequences. The resulting sequences were
merged based on at least 3 head or tail amino acids to get sequences of
various lengths.
The generated sequences were measured from two aspects:
accuracy and diversity. Accuracy means whether the sequences can be
correctly re-clustered using the same clustering parameters. Diversity
means Needleman-Wunsch similarity between sequences within each
cluster. The Needleman-Wunsch algorithm used was from the global
mode of the PairwiseAligner in the Biopython (v1.81).
Extraction of important regions using alternative methods
We obtained the catGRANULES scores for each amino acid in the
sequence through the catGRANULES webserver [ http://s.tartaglialab.
com/new_submission/catGRANULES]. Regions with scores greater
than 0 were considered signiﬁcant. Due to the sliding window limita-
tions of the catGRANULES method, theﬁrst 25 and last 25 amino acids
in the sequence were not scored. Using the PLACC webserver [http://
plaac.wi.mit.edu/]with the default Core Length and the Homo sapiens
background, we acquired scores where regions with values greater
than 0.5 were deemed signi ﬁcant. We obtained scores using the
PsPhunter webserver [ http://psphunter.stemcellding.org/index.php].
PsPhunter scores sites on a scale from 0 to 1, with smaller values
indicating more signi ﬁcant regions; however, no explicit cutoff is
deﬁned. Due to the sliding window limitations of the method, theﬁrst
15 and last 15 amino acids in the sequence were not scored. The Fuz-
Drop webserver [ https://fuzdrop.bio.unipd.it/predictor] directly pro-
vides the Droplet-promoting regions.
