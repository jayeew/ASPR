Rare earth-doped luminescent materials typically produce characteristic ﬂuorescence emissions
associated with the rare earth elements. Here, we report remarkable non-characteristic blue emissions
from ytterbium (Yb
3+)- and erbium (Er3+)-doped organic-inorganic metal halides, (BDPA)2MCl4 (BDPA+
= benzyldimethylphenylammonium; M2+ = Cd2+,Z n2+). Both Yb3+ and Er3+ ions are commonly used as
dopants in phosphors, which generally lead to near-infrared emissions. Theoretical calculations
suggest the unique blue emission arises from Yb
3+/Er3+-induced exciton radiative recombination from
BDPA+ to Cl−, which suppresses the characteristic emissions of these rare earth ions. More notably,
owing to structural stability of (BDPA) 2MCl4, the Yb 3+/Er3+-activated phosphors demonstrate high
water resistance, maintaining both stability and photoluminescence (PL) in water for over two months.
Further, when these crystals are dissolved in N,N-dimethylformamide or dimethyl sulfoxide, the
resulting solution continues to exhibit PL with a high quantum yield of ~90%, making it suitable for use
as liquid-phase X-ray scintillators.

It is well known that metal halide perovskites especially Pb- and Sn-
based compositions are easily damaged when exposed to moisture and those
metal halide derivatives are also less stable in water, they will promptly lose
crystallinity and decompose, ﬁnally lose photoactivity and luminescence
within seconds or minutes. Surprisingly, we found Yb
3+/Er3+-doped
(BDPA)2MCl4 crystals exhibit excellent water stability, maintaining struc-
tural phase and decent PLQYs (~40%) after 2 months ’ storage in water
(Fig. 4a, b). We attribute the extremely strong hydrophobicity of the crystal
to its unique spatial conﬁguration, in which the closely arranged BDPA+
cations form a protective, closed space around the metal polyhedron,
effectively blocking water molecules from intruding. Owing to the planar,
rigid, and uniformly large structure of BDPA⁺, molecules adsorbed on the
surface spontaneously organize intoan ordered, low-defect, dense mono-
layer or multilayer structure to maximize intermolecular interactions. This
closely packed state is“frozen” upon completion of crystallization, forming
an in-situ generated hydrophobic shell encapsulating the perovskite grains.
This remarkable durability opens new possibilities for their application in
environments where traditional blueemitters might degrade or lose func-
tionality. In addition to excellent water stability, the Yb
3+/Er3+-doped
crystals also demonstrate high thermal stability, as the main XRD peaks of
the materials remain almost unchanged after continuous heating at 127 °C
for 20 h (Supplementary Fig. S18).
The materials’ high resistance to water sparked our interest in their
stability in strongly polar solvents like DMF and DMSO. We found that,
despite their superhydrophobic nature, they are soluble in DMSO and only
slightly soluble in DMF. The resulting solutions are transparent, suggesting
that dissolution occurs at the molecular level. To con ﬁrm this, a Tyndall
effect experiment was conducted. Supplementary Fig. S19 shows that the
DMSO solution containing the lum inescent solid exhibits no signi ﬁcant
scattering, indicating the absence of nanoscale colloids in the solution. We
initially expected that, like mostﬂuorescent metal halides, the compounds
would dissolve and lose their ﬂuorescence. However, surprisingly, the

Conclusions
Unlike most rare earth-doped metal halide phosphors, which exhibit
characteristic emissions from therare earth ions themselves, the Yb3+/Er3+-
doped (BDPA)2CdCl4 and (BDPA)2ZnCl4 compounds do not display the
typical emissions of Yb3+ or Er3+. Instead, they emit intriguing blue light. We
attribute this unique emission to Yb 3+/Er3+-induced exciton radiative
recombination from BDPA+ to Cl −, which suppresses the characteristic
emissions of these rare earth ions. Interestingly, when the material is dis-
solved in polar solvents such as DMF or DMSO, the resulting solution still

Chemicals

used as received without further puri ﬁcation.
Synthesis of (BDPA)2MCl4 (M = Cd, Zn) single crystals
(BDPA)2CdCl4: 1 mmol of BDPA-Cl and 0.5 mmol of CdCl2 were placed
into a 10 mL glass vial. 2 mL of HCl (37% wt%) were added, and the mixture
was stirred vigorously at 120 °C for 10 min until the solid was completely
dissolved, resulting in a transparent solution. The solution was then cooled
to room temperature at a controlled rate of 5 ~ 10 °C/h. (BDPA)
2CdCl4
single crystals were allowed to precipitate. The mixture wasﬁltered under
vacuum, washed with 1 ~ 2 mL of hexane, and the product was dried in a
vacuum oven at 60 °C for 8 h. The preparation method for(BDPA)
2ZnCl4
was similar to that of (BDPA)2CdCl4,w i t hC d C l2 replaced by ZnCl2.
Synthesis of Yb3+/Er3+-doped (BDPA)2MCl4 crystal powders
(BDPA)2CdCl4:Yb: BDPA-Cl (1 mmol), YbCl 3·6H2O (0.25, 0.5, 1,
1.5 mmol), and CdCl2 (0.5 mmol) were mixed in a 10 mL glass vial. 2 mL of
HCl (37% wt%) was added, and the vial was sealed. It was placed on a 120 °C
heating plate until a transparent solution was formed. The seal was then
opened, and the mixture was heated at 120 °C until the solution completely
evaporated. The vial was removed, and the product was placed in a 120 °C
oven. After 24 h, the product was taken out and washed with deionized
water to remove any unreacted YbCl
3·6H2O. The washed product was then
placed in a 60 °C vacuum oven to dry, resulting in the ﬁnal product. The
synthesis method for (BDPA)2CdCl4:Er was similar, except that
YbCl3·6H2O was replaced with ErCl 3·6H2O. The preparation method for
(BDPA)2ZnCl4:Yb, Er is similar to that of (BDPA) 2CdCl4:Yb, Er, with
CdCl2 replaced by ZnCl2.
