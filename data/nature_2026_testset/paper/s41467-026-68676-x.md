Hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPSCC), an aggressive head and
neck cancer with dismal prognosis, faces persistent chemoresistance to stan-
dard TPF (docetaxel, cisplatin, 5-ﬂuorouracil) regimen. However, the immu-
nological mechanisms underlying chemoresistance remain undeﬁned. Here,
we perform longitudinal single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) proﬁling of
paired pre-/post-TPF HPSCC specimens, mapping immune cell dynamics
underlying chemoresistance. Our study identiﬁes ZNF683+ natural killer (NK)
cells as a gatekeeper of chemotherapy efﬁcacy through integrated single-cell
transcriptomics, spatial multiplex immunohistochemistry and functional
validation. Moreover, pretreatment baseline enrichment of ZNF683
+ NK cells
predicts TPF response, while GZMK+CD8+ effector memory T cells function as
the predominant immunologic effector to successful TPF intervention.
Mechanistically, bioinformatics and in vitro coculture data reveal that ZNF683
+
NK cells directly interact with CD8+ T cells, and drive an MHC-I-dependent
licensing of polyfunctional GZMK+CD8+ effector memory T cells. Collectively,
this NK-CD8+ axis provides a potential predictive biomarker and therapeutic
target to overcome chemoresistance in patients with HPSCC.
Hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPSCC) is an aggressive
subtype that represents approximately 3 –10% of all head and neck
cancer1–3. Due to the cramped anatomic space, concealed location of
primary tumor and asymptomatic nature at an early stage, patients
with HPSCC are commonly diagnosed at an advanced stage, typically
exhibiting the worst outcomes among all patients with head and neck
cancer, with a 5-year overall survival rate of 25 –35%
1,4. TPF induction
chemotherapy (docetaxel/cisplatin/5-ﬂuorouracil) serves as the front-
line organ preservation strategy with low expenditure for advanced
HPSCC patients5. While emerging combinations with EGFR targeted
therapy (e.g., cetuximab) or immune checkpoint blockade therapy
enhance laryngeal function preservation and prolong disease-free
survival time4,6–8,ap o r t i o n( 1 0–20%) of HPSCC patients exhibit innate
TPF resistance6,9, which causes them to miss critical therapeutic win-
dows. Therefore, it is urgent and essential to identify valuable bio-
markers for the prediction of induction chemotherapy response, and
to elucidate the potential mechanisms of chemoresistance for
exploiting immunotherapy strategies in HPSCC.
Genomic proﬁling has partially characterized chemoresistance in
head and neck cancer
10,11, yet conventional bulk sequencing fails to
capture the intratumoral heterogeneity and to discriminate cancer
cells from their surrounding components in tumor microenvironment

Study design
To delineate the immune in ﬁltration dynamics in HPSCC, 12 patients
receiving two cycles of TPF chemotherapy were enrolled as discovery
cohort (Jan. 2020–Dec. 2022). Primary tumor samples were collected
before and after TPF treatment for scRNA-seq and RNA-seq (Fig.1A). A
validation cohort of 41 patients (Jan. 2023–Apr. 2025) provided paired
and unpaired tumor samples for mIHC and FACS conﬁrmation (Fig.1B;
Clinical information in Supplementary Data 1).
According to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors
(RECIST) version 1.1 guidelines
24, the tumor shrinkage rate (TSR) was
calculated by enhanced MRl-measured tumor diameter changes pre-
chemotherapy and 4 weeks post-second cycle (Fig. 1C). Patients were
categorized into four groups: completely responsive (CR; Dis-
appearance of all target lesions), partially responsive (PR; At least a
30% decrease in the sum of the diameters of the target lesions), pro-
gressive disease (PD; At least a 20% increase in the sum of the dia-
meters of the target lesions), and stable disease (SD; Neither sufﬁcient
shrinkage to qualify for the PR group nor a sufﬁcient increase in lesion
size to qualify for the PD group) (Fig. 1D).
For immune inﬁltration analysis, patients were stratiﬁed into Rs (7
PR/CR patients) and NRs (5 PD/SD patients), with no signi ﬁcant dif-
ferences in clinical variables (age, alcohol consumption, smoking his-
tory, clinical stage, pathological grade, and location of the primary
lesions) between groups (Fig.1E).

Cell and tissue recovery protocol
In brief60, frozen vials were retrieved and immediately immersed in
water bath at 37 °C to ensure rapid and uniform thawing, and the vials
were occasionally shaken to aid in the process. The thawed cells were
subsequently diluted with 9 mL of prewarmed DMEM (Gibco, Thermo
Fisher Scientiﬁc, Waltham, MA, USA), gently mixed, and centrifuged at
300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells
were washed with an additional 3 mL of fresh medium and centrifuged
again to wash the suspension. The cells were then resuspended in
DMEM and counted following the staining with trypan blue to deter-
mine cell viability. Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch
Gladbach, Germany) was used to further eliminate dead cells and
debris when necessary.
Isolation of immune cells by ﬂow cytometry
As previously described45, the cell suspension was incubated with an
anti-CD45 antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and propidium
iodide (PI) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to label dead cells,
respectively. This step was performed in the dark to prevent ﬂuor-
ochrome degradation. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at
300 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was promptly removed. Cell
pellets were then resuspended in an appropriate volume of phosphate-
buffered saline (PBS) (Gibco, Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY,
USA). A 40 µm cell strainer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was
utilized to eliminate debris and cell aggregates. After a second cen-
trifugation and removal of the remaining supernatant, cells were
resuspended in PBS forﬂow cytometric analysis. Aﬂow cytometer (BD
LSRFortessa, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was used to sort and
harvest live CD45
+ immune cells. Appropriate gating strategy ensured
the highly efﬁcient isolation of live immune cells.
Single-cell sequencing of the immune repertoire
The 10x Genomics Single-Cell Immune Repertoire assay (10x Geno-
mics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) offers a powerful approach to investigate
gene expression at the single-cell level. This is achieved by leveraging a
universal primer for the 5’ adapter in conjunctionwith nested primers
designed for the invariant regions of immune molecules. Cell viability
was determined using a trypan blu e exclusion assay (Gibco, Thermo
Fisher Scientiﬁc, Waltham, MA, USA), with a threshold of 80% viability
set as the quali ﬁcation criterion for subsequent analysis. Single-cell
suspensions were processed through an automated Chromium Con-
troller (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA), in which cells were
encapsulated in gel beads in emulsions (GEMs), primed for mRNA
capture and reverse transcripted into cDNA. Each GEM received a
unique barcode derived from the primers, allowing the identiﬁcation of
individual cell postsequencing. Subsequent steps included disrupting
the GEMs and amplifying the cDNA. Following cDNA ampliﬁcation, the
products were fragmented, subjectedto end repair, A-tailing, adaptor
ligation, PCR and subsequently puriﬁed. The resulting amplicons, upon
rigorous quality control, were denatured and circularized to construct
single-stranded circularized DNA, which were then ampli ﬁed using
rolling circle ampliﬁcation to generate DNA nanoballs (DNBs). These
DNBs were deposited onto specialized nanoarrays using the DNA
nanochip technique (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced
employing combinatorial probe-anchor synthesis technology
61.
Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and quality control
Raw scRNA-Seq data were processed using the CellRanger software
suite (v.3.0.0; 10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA)
62. First, the samples
were demultiplexed based on provided indices to generate a FASTQ

aggregate gene-barcode matrix, with each column representing a
unique cellular barcode and each row representing a speci ﬁcg e n e .
Quality control (QC) metrics such as estimated cell counts, average

mitochondrial gene content, wereﬂagged as low-quality and excluded
to maintain the integrity of the dataset. Similarly, cells with fewer than
200 expressed genes and genes detected in fewer than three cells were
also excluded. These stringent QC measures guaranteed a robust

Statistical analysis
Key gene expression data from our updated dataset were processed
and visualized using GraphPad Prism software (version 9, GraphPad
Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical differences between two
distinct groups were assessed using an unpaired Student’s t test, while
differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way
ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The data are
presented as the mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) or mean ± Standard
Error of the Mean (SEM), with p < 0.05 indicating statistical
signiﬁcance.
