More than one hundred years ago, JBS Haldane noted that in a cross between
two species, if one of the sexes is absent , rare or sterile, the affected sex is
heterogametic. The underlying genetic causes for this phenomenon have been
a source of debate ever since. Here, we test how Haldane’s rule operates in
Caenorhabditisnematodes by studying (1) crosses involving sex-
determination mutants of two hybridizing species, and (2) crosses involving
tetraploids. Our results indicate that the critical feature underlying Haldane’s
rule is incompatibility between a sex chromosome derived from only one
species, and autosome pairs derived from both. In addition, we show that the
mechanisms for evaluating the X:Autosome ratio have diverged during recent
nematode evolution. We conclude that the way interactions between sex
chromosomes and autosomes are structured causes them to play an important
role in establishing genetic barriers between newly separating species.
Incipient species often diverge when two populations become isolated
from one another, and their separation is reinforced by the develop-
ment of genetic incompatibilities that prevent hybrids from surviving
or reproducing. JBS Haldane noted that when hybrid crosses affect one
sex more signiﬁcantly than the other, it is almost always the hetero-
gametic sex that is so affected
1.T h u s ,t h er i s ki sg r e a t e s tf o rXY male
hybrids in mammals, but for ZW female hybrids in birds. A century of
observations has conﬁrmed this rule2, and extended it to new systems,
like the higher risk seen among XO hybrid male nematodes than
among their XX female siblings inCaenorhabditis3,4. These sex-speciﬁc
effects are likely to play an important role in decreasing hybridization
during speciation.
What factors might cause these differences between homo-
gametic and heterogametic hybrids? Although there are many
theories
2, they generally fall into two broad classes. The ﬁrst includes
Dominance theory, which focuses on recessive incompatibility alleles
exposed on the unpaired sex chromosome
5,6. Variants of this approach
attribute these loci to faster evolution of sex chromosomes, or meiotic
or cytoplasmic drive elements
2. The common theme is that homo-
gametic hybrids, like XX females, express combined traits from two
different X-chromosomes, whereas heterogametic hybrids only dis-
play the X-linked traits of one parent species. Some researchers
suggest that this chromosomal imbalance might apply to hybrid
lethality but not to hybrid sterility7.
A second model is that sexual characteristics themselves are
involved in this phenomenon. The main idea focuses on sterility
in heterogametic males, which is observed more frequently than
lethality, and a related model focuses on male fragility
2,7. Since male
fertility genes appear to evolve rapidly, there could be a swift diver-
gence in male reproductive traits during speciation, causing pro-
blems that are exposed in hybrids. By contrast, more slowly evolving
female reproductive traits would be less likely to cause sterility in
hybrids.
Finally, Haldane’s rule might be explained by a combination of
these mechanisms acting in synergy. In addition, the existence of rare
exceptions to Haldane’sr u l e
8 suggests that other evolutionary forces
are occasionally able to counterbalance these factors.
Caenorhabditisnematodes are ideal for studying the mechanisms
that underlie Haldane’s rule. Some species are so closely related that
they produce hybrids, includingC. briggsae and C. nigoni3. Moreover,
reciprocal crosses allow different theories to be tested. Most impor-
tantly, when the father is aC. briggsaemale, the hybridXO progeny are
sterile, but when the father is C. nigoni, the hybrid XO progeny die as
embryos. Thus, both aspects of Haldane’s rule can be probed. Because

Isolating tetraploidC. nigoni
Since differences in the rates at which male and female genes evolve
seemed unlikely to explain the sterility seen in these hybrid males, we
developed a method to determine conclusively if the sterility was
caused by an X:Autosome imbalance. Unlike mammals, nematodes
can produce healthy tetraploid animals
26. Furthermore, studies of
various polyploid worms showed that they assess the X:A ratio with
great precision when determining how to develop sexually27. Thus, to
produce interspecies hybrids that do not have X:A imbalances, we
generated a C. nigoni tetraploid strain, so we could make tetraploid
hybrids.
Tetraploid nematodes can be recognized because they are longer
than their diploid counterparts. To produce a tetraploid strain, weﬁrst
used RNA interference to inactivate the rec-8 gene in C. nigoni
females28. This treatment prevents chromosome pairing during
meiosis, resulting in diploid oocytes. After fertilization, we crossed the
long progeny with each other for several generations, which eventually
produced a stable strain ofC. nigoni tetraploids. The length of theseC.
nigoni worms was within the range predicted by observing tetraploids
from other Caenorhabditisspecies
26,27. This increase in length causes
many animals to show one and a half sine waves in body position as
they move (Fig. 2a) rather than a single sine wave like diploids. Finally,
we stained females with DAPI to observe chromosome pairs in oocytes,
conﬁrming that we had isolated tetraploids (Fig. 2b).
This tetraploid strain is stable but has very small broods. For both
C. elegans and C. briggsae hermaphrodites, tetraploids have reduced
brood sizes

depression

Discussion
A key challenge for the Theory of Evolution is describing how two
populations separate to form distinct species31. The theoretical tools
needed to answer this question were created in the Modern Synthesis,
which fused the study of Genetics with that of Evolution
32. Although
physical separation plays a major role in this process, researchers
recognized that genetic barriers that make it dif ﬁcult for incipient
species to mate and produce fertile offspring reinforce and maintain
new divisions. It was in this context that Haldane developed his famous
rule, that when hybrid crosses affect one sex more signiﬁcantly than the
other, it is almost always the heterogametic sex that is so affected
1.T h e
sterility or death of one sex is common in hybrids and should sig-
niﬁcantly decrease hybridization between species.
Using two different approaches, we tested whether either of the
broad explanations for Haldane’s rule described above could explainC.
briggsae/C. nigonihybrids. First, we used sex-determination mutants to
show that the defects seen in XO hybrids were not due to incompat-
ibility between the genetic programs specifying sex or male fertility.
Most dramatically, in hybrid tra-1 null mutants, the XX animals can
develop as normal, fertile males. This result contrasts with wildtypeXO
hybrids, which become sterile males. Furthermore, it shows that the
genetic programs specifying male development, behavior and fertility
are compatible between C. nigoni and C. briggsae.

with XO hybrids, we then tested for incompatibilities between a single
X and the pairs of autosomes from both parents. Our studies with
tetraploids show that the critical problem in hybrids of the

There are several interesting models for why this type of mis-
match could matter2. For example, one species might have a harmful
recessive mutation on theX, and a compensatory recessive suppressor
on one of the autosomes. Hybrid males could then be hemizygous for
the harmful mutation but not homozygous for the suppressor, leading
to sterility or death.
However, there could be a more general reason why Haldane ’s
rule is so often observed. Recent theoretical work suggests that as
species with XY (or ZW) sex determination evolve, changes in the two
sex chromosomes will naturally lead to dosage compensation, and in
so doing produce changes that would cause the defects associated
with Haldane’sr u l e
33. These models don ’t apply completely to Cae-
norhabditis, since they areXX/XOrather thanXX/XYspecies and already
have an established dosage compensation system 34.H o w e v e r ,t h e y
make a strong case that dosage compensation could be directly
involved in Haldane’s rule. Furthermore, a recent report indicates that
a gene directly involved in dosage compensation might be responsible
for the lethality seen in C. briggsae/C.nigonihybrid XO animals
35.
Given this background and the results of widespread genome
sequencing, we suggest that a simple explanation for Haldane ’sr u l e
that also involves changes in dosage compensation. As expected,
chromosome identities, including that of the X chromosome, are lar-
gely conserved during speciation. However, there are usually many
genes that have moved either off of or onto X chromosomes when
closely related species are compared36,37. In our system, these types of
small rearrangements are found inC. briggsae and C. nigoni9.T h u s ,w e
propose that these exchanges, in combination with dosage compen-
sation systems
38,39 create the potential for harmful imbalances in gene
expression when XO hybrids are formed. Although hybrid incompat-
ibility genes, meiotic drive elements, or other factors 2 could also be
involved, they are not necessary.
For example, consider a rearrangement that results in gene B
being located on the X chromosome of C. briggsae, but on an auto-
some of C. nigoni (Fig. 4). Nematodes have a dosage compensation
system that lowers the expression of X-linked genes in XX
nematodes34. Thus, we anticipate that evolution would alter the
expression of geneB to adjust for the effects of dosage compensation.
Eventually, both species should have the same total expression of B,
relative to the expression of other genes with which it interacts. In
hybrid animals, XX individuals should still express normal amounts of
B.H o w e v e r ,XO males will make either too much or too little,
depending on which parent provided the single X chromosome. The
cumulative effect of misexpression of B and other genes like it, rela-
tive to the rest of the genome, could result in sterility or even death.
By contrast, in tetraploid hybrids, there should be twice as much gene
product made as the diploids. Furthermore, the tetraploid hybrid
males will have received an X chromosome from each parent, so all
genes should be expressed at normal levels.

We also observed an underlying change in how the X:A ratio was
evaluated in Caenorhabditis to determine sex. The threshold lies
between 0.67 and 0.75 for C. elegans, but between 0.5 and 0.67 for C.
briggsae and C. nigoni . This difference could be an example of
developmental system drift40, a process in which underlying genetic
and developmental regulation shifts while maintaining the same
phenotypic output. It suggests broad changes in either the numbers
of X or autosomal signaling elements, or how those elements regulate
xol-1.
In addition, our observation that some AAA; XX animals became
defective males, rather than hermaphrodites or females raises inter-
esting avenues for future research. One possibility is that the tipping
point for theX:A ratio in these species is very near 0.5, and that chance
causes a few animals to develop as males. However, if this were so, it
would not explain why these males were always defective, unless the
X:A ratio acted along two parallel paths to control sex determination. A
more likely model is that tetraploids have a high rate of defective
meiosis, and the rare AAA; XX males have an extra copy of one auto-
some, slightly lowering the X to A ratio into the male zone but also
causing developmental problems due to chromosome imbalances.
Thus, we suspect these tetraploids could be a fruitful model for studies
of errors in meiosis.
Our work also creates two other avenues for future experi-
mentation. First, the inability of hybrid males to mate
3,41 had inhibited
several approaches for studying hybrid animals. We have presented
two methods that circumvent this problem.
Second, we note that this model system could provide a way to
investigate allotetraploid formation during animal evolution. There are
many examples from plants where two diploid species hybridize to
form a new tetraploid species
42. By contrast, this situation is rare in
animals, with only a few known examples like the carp Cyprinus
carpio43. We have developed a model in which interspecies tetraploid
hybrid nematodes can not only be produced but are fertile. However,
these animals have low brood sizes and propagating them in the lab is
challenging. These observations suggest that they could provide a
valuable system for studying both the dif ﬁculties and potential of
allotetraploid formation in animals.
