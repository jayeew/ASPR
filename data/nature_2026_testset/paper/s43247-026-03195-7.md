Quantifying how rainwater in ﬁltration modiﬁes the stable isotope composition of soil pore water is
essential for tracing water movement through the critical zone. We monitored intact soil columns under
natural rainfall at high temporal resolution, jointly measuring seepage ﬂux and the hydrogen and
oxygen isotope composition of drainage water. The data reveal a robust, threshold-like pattern: faster
seepage is consistently associated with more isotopically depleted drainage, behavior that is not
readily explained by evaporation or simple mixing alone. We interpret this pattern as evidence for an
interfacial hydrodynamic isotope fractionation process, in which transport dynamics and soil –water
interfacial interactions partition water isotopologues without phase change. This framework re ﬁnes
how isotopes are used to infer soil water mixing and has implications for predicting recharge and
ecosystem water use under a changing climate.
Understanding how rainwater inﬁltrates and mixes with pre-existing soil
water is a long-standing challenge in hydrology. Differences in stable isotope
composition between soil water pools have traditionally been attributed
mainly to evaporative fractionation at the soil surface
1–3, to sampling arti-
facts during water extraction 4, or to physicochemical soil interactions
between tightly bound and mobile water 5,6. Nevertheless, there is limited
consensus within the research community regarding the mechanistic causes
behind this isotopic differentiation
7–9,a se x e m p l iﬁed by the ecohydrological
separation (ES) hypothesis10,11. Widespread observations suggest that non-
evaporative isotopic differentiationcan occur during soil water movement,
but the underlying physics remain incompletely resolved. A growing body
of research5,6,12,13 has advanced understanding of how inﬁltration dynamics
impact isotopic signatures, exploring soil structural heterogeneity, matric
potential, and pore-scale processes. These studies have uncovered elements
of physical mechanisms, though the picture remains fragmented. Sprenger
et al.
2 provided a systematic, phenomenological framework for these effects,
including the “subsequent mixing” concept. Nevertheless, a signi ﬁcant
disconnect persists regarding thepivotal role of seepage intensity (ﬂow ﬂux,
Q). This metric integrates rainfall features (e.g., intensity, duration) and soil
inﬁltration properties (e.g., structure, initial moisture), yet its systematic
control on the isotopic signature of drainage water lacks high-resolution
observational tests and a clear mechanistic rationale. Here we address a
fundamental question using an outdoo r, intact-core experiment: under
event timescales with minimal concurrent evaporation, does drainage-water
isotopic composition vary in a predictable way with Q? We further ask
whether any observed Q–δ pattern exhibits threshold behavior indicative of
a transport-controlled fractionation process.

Mechanistic interpretation and implications
Reexamining conventional explanations. We ﬁrst evaluate whether the
observed Q –δ pattern can be explained by conventional mechanisms.
Evaporation as primary driver? Our event-scale sampling design and time-
resolved analyses indicate minimal direct evaporative inﬂuence on drainage
during rainfall, making evaporatio n an unlikely explanation for the
observed depletions. Consistent with t his, evaporation typically enriches
heavy isotopes, whereas we observe de pletion over time (Supplementary
Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 4-2), as detailed in Supplementary Note 2.
Incomplete mixing? While incomplete mixing can produce offsets between
water pools, it fails to account for (1) the systematic covariation with Q and
the accompanying threshold, and (2) the clustering of seepage data above
the LMWL— unexpected if simple mixing of rainfall with antecedent water
(Supplementary Fig. 5) were dominant. Th i sd i s t r i b u t i o ni sc o n s i s t e n tw i t h
observations from catchment-scale seepage/groundwater
2,10 to cryogeni-

suggest subsurface fractionation pr ocesses. We therefore infer that the
observed ﬂux dependence reﬂects a transport-controlled, non-equilibrium
mechanism distinct from traditional evaporation or simple two-pool
mixing.
Interfacial Hydrodynamic Fract ionation (IHF): a parsimonious
hypothesis. To explain the data, we advance the IHF hypothesis (Fig. 2a).
Under low seepage ﬂux, slow seepage velocity and extended pore-surface
contact amplify solid –liquid interfacial interactions. Slight isotopologue
differences (e.g., zero-point energy, adsorption afﬁnity, frictional drag) lead
to preferential mobilization of lighter isotopologues and transient retention
of heavier isotopologues, producing isotopically lighter drainage. Above a
critical ﬂux, residence times shorten, matrix exchange is“short-circuited,”
and efﬂuent isotopes converge toward input rainfall. This mechanism helps
explain why plant-accessible water often appears isotopically heavier— roots
access water in smaller pores, retaining heavy isotopes— while isotopically
lighter water preferentially drains to streams and aquifers
16. The central
prediction isﬂux-controlled fractionation with a threshold, which our data
and further analysis support (Supplementary Note 2).
Although closed isotope mass balance was not feasible (absence of
initial soil-water isotopes and full soil-moisture dynamics), prior modeling
points to missing depletion processes in standard frameworks. For instance,

Methods
Experimental setup and ﬁeld deployment
In August 2018, nine undisturbed soil columns (30 cm diameter × 60 cm
height) representing three soil textures (clay loam, silt loam, sandy loam)
were collected in triplicate and installed on a rooftop in Chengdu (30.67°N,
104.07°E; ~500 m) for subsequent monitoring of natural rainfall inﬁltration
commencing in July 2019. The soil monoliths were encased in PVC pipes;
any annulus between soil and pipe was minimized by a snugﬁt and sealed to
reduce preferential wallﬂow. Initial conditions: We did not measure initial
soil–water isotopes; antecedent states were assumed to reﬂect prior rainfall

Isotope analysis and data processing
Stable isotope ratios (δ²H and δ18O) were measured by laser-based spec-
troscopy and are reported in ‰ relative to V-SMOW. Measurement pre-
cision (1σ)w a s± 0 . 3‰ (δ²H) and ±0.08‰ (δ18O). All δ values are reported
to one decimal place to re ﬂect analytical precision. Percolation ﬂux was
computed as: Q =( N × c × α)/(A × Δt), where N is the number of tips in
intervalΔt, c =0 . 2 3m mp e rt i p ,α is a calibration coefﬁcient (0.73–0.95), and
A ≈ 707 cm² is the column area.
Statistical analysis
We used mixed-effects models (R 4.4.1, lme4) to relateQ to δ²H and δ18O,
log-transforming variables to improvelinearity and variance homogeneity.
Fixed effects included ln Q and relative duration (RD); random intercepts
were speciﬁed for soil type, rainfall event, and column. Model selection used
AIC; further diagnostics and sensitivity tests are reported in the Supple-
mentary Methods 1.4.
