limited unculturable bacterium that causes citrus Huanglongbing. To date, the
mechanisms by which psyllids recognize and respond to this pathogen remain
poorly understood. Here, we show that the psyllid Toll8 receptor acts as a
pattern recognition receptor for theCLas outer membrane protein Barrel,
resulting in Toll8 dimerization and activation. Activated Toll8 subsequently
triggers an intracellular signaling cascade, involving MyD88 dimerization and
the activation of IκB kinase IKKE, that culminates in the transcriptional acti-
vation of the transcription factor NFAT and the expression of antimicrobial
effectors. Moreover, we show that CL a sc o u n t e r st h i si m m u n er e s p o n s eb y
secreting the effector protein SDE3230, which hijacks an E3 ligase to mediate
the ubiquitination and degradation of MyD88, thereby suppressing Toll sig-

arthropods and highlight an intricate counter-defense strategy employed by
pathogens for survival and propagation within insect vectors.
The innate immune system, the primary defense mechanism against
pathogen invasion, relies on pattern recognition receptors (PRR) to
detect conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMP) and
initiate immune responses
1. Among these immune components, Toll-
like receptors (TLRs) in vertebrates and their evolutionary counter-
parts, Toll receptors in insects, constitute highly conserved elements
of innate immunity, playing critical roles in pathogen recognition and
host defense. Vertebrate TLRs exhibit direct recognition capabilities
for diverse bacterial PAMPs, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS), pep-
tidoglycan (PGN), and lipoproteins, thereby triggering downstream
signaling cascades that activate immune responses
2. For instance,
TLR4 speciﬁcally binds to LPS, TLR5 interacts withﬂagellin, and TLR1/
2/6 recognizes bacterial lipoproteins, orchestrating inﬂammatory and
innate immune responses 3. Certain TLRs also identify viral PAMPs
through interactions with viral nucleic acids or structural
components
4. In contrast, the prevailing paradigm suggests that insect
Toll receptors function indirectly, requiring upstream PRRs such as
peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRP) to detect PAMPs and
activate Toll signaling through Spätzle cytokines 5–7. For instance, in
Drosophila melanogaster, the PGRP family acts as PRRs by detecting
bacterial PGN, initiating a protease cascade that activates the ligand
protein Spätzle, the ligand for Toll receptors 7.R e c e n ts t u d i e sr e v e a l
the pest insectSpodoptera lituramyeloid differentiation-like protein 11
(ML11) partners with Toll5 to sense Autographa californica multiple
nucleopolyhedrovirus
8. Similarly, in the silkworm Bombyx mori,T o l l 9
recognizes LPS by forming a complex with two ML proteins9.D e s p i t e
these advances, it remains unclear whether insect Toll receptors can
directly recognize PAMPs.
Structurally, both vertebrate TLRs and invertebrate Toll receptors
are characterized as transmembrane proteins featuring an extra-
cellular leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain, a single-pass transmem-
brane domain, and a cytoplasmic Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR)
homology domain
10. In vertebrates, ligand binding to the LRR domain

Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), the etiological agent of
the devastating citrus disease Huanglongbing (HLB), is primarily
transmitted by the Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri)
15,16. Currently,
HLB has become widespread in major citrus-growing regions world-
wide, resulting in a drastic reduction in fruit yield and incurring sub-
stantial economic losses
17–19. Despite decades of intensive research,
effective management of HLB remains a major challenge, largely due
to the unculturable, phloem-limited nature of CLas and the lack of
commercially available HLB-resistant citrus cultivars
20. CLas belongs to
a group of phloem-limited bacteria that also includes phytoplasmas
and other Candidatus species (e.g., Ca. Phlomobacters and other Ca.
Liberibacter species). Notably, these pathogens are transmitted in a
persistent-propagative manner by piercing-sucking insects, including
psyllids and leafhoppers
15,21,22. Regarding acquisition and transmission
efﬁciency, psyllid nymphs acquire CLas from infected plants more
efﬁciently, whereas adults transmit the bacterium to healthy hosts with
higher effectiveness23,24. Intriguingly, persistent transmission of CLas
does not appear to negatively impact psyllid vectorﬁtness, suggesting
that vectors have evolved immune tolerance mechanisms to sustain
bacterial infections
25. CLas, a gram-negative bacterium, possesses a cell
wall structure comprising an outer membrane containing various
proteins, a thin modi ﬁed peptidoglycan layer, and an inner
membrane
26,27. However, the mechanisms by which insect PRRs detect
CLas-derived PAMPs to activate immune defenses remain poorly
understood.
Here, we deﬁne a psyllid Toll receptor that functions as a PRR by
directly interacting with the CLas outer membrane protein Barrel, a
bacterial PAMP. This interaction initiates a previously unidentiﬁed Toll
signaling cascade that produces antibacterial effectors. Furthermore,
we elucidate a bacterial counter-defense strategy that subverts this
immune response. Ourﬁndings provide fundamental insights into the
molecular interplay between phloem-limited bacteria and their insect
vectors, offering potential avenues for developing innovative strate-
gies to control HLB and other vector-borne diseases.

Psyllid Toll receptors recognizeCLas outer membrane protein
Barrel to activate antibacterial immune response
Insect Toll immune responses typically involve PAMP recognition by
upstream PRRs such as PGRPs 28.I n D. melanogaster, PGRP-SA and
PGRP-LC function as PRRs to recognize bacterial PGN29,30.S u r p r i s i n g l y ,
only one PGRP gene (GenBank KAI5696543) was identi ﬁed in the D.
citri genome, which lacks both a transmembrane domain and a signal
peptide, unlike D. melanogaster PGRP-SA and PGRP-LC (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 1a, b). This ﬁnding suggests that D. citri PGRP is unlikely to
function as a PRR. Bioinformatics analysis identiﬁed four Toll receptor
genes in D. citri,i n c l u d i n gToll1, Toll6, Toll7,a n dToll8 (Supplementary
Fig. 2a). Among these, Toll1, Toll7,a n d Toll8 exhibited signiﬁcantly
increased expression followingCLas infection, with Toll8 showing the
highest upregulation and expression (Fig.1a, Supplementary Fig. 2b).
CLas infection also signi ﬁcantly enhanced Toll8 dimerization in psy-
llids (Fig. 1b). Functional validation through microinjection of dsRNAs
targeting individual Toll genes (dsTolls) revealed that Toll8 silencing
caused the most substantial increase inCLas 16S rRNAlevels compared
to knockdown of otherToll genes (Fig.1c, d, Supplementary Fig. 2c–e),
establishing that psyllid Toll8 plays a critical role in anti- CLas
immunity.
Structurally, psyllid Toll8 is most similar to vertebrate TLR2
(Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), which directly recognizes bacterial outer
membrane lipoproteins
31, suggesting that Toll8 may also directly
recognize PAMPs. Through systematic screening of 19CLas membrane
proteins via yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) analysis, we speciﬁcally identiﬁed
the outer membrane protein Barrel (GenBank WP_015452909) as a
Toll8 interaction partner (Supplementary Fig. 3c). This binding was
mediated by Toll8’s LRR domain (Supplementary Fig. 3d), while the
LRR domains of the other three Tolls showed no detectable interaction
with Barrel (Supplementary Fig. 3e). The speciﬁc Toll8-Barrel interac-
tion was further validated by Glutathione S-transferases (GST) pull-
down assays (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Immunoelectron microscopy
revealed that CLas Barrel localized on the membranes of CLas
particles, which ranged from 400 to 700 nm in diameter (Fig.1e). Co-
expression of Barrel with Toll8 in Sf9 cells signiﬁcantly increased Toll8
dimerization (Fig. 1f). Similarly, microinjection of puriﬁed Barrel into
psyllids promoted Toll8 dimerization without affecting overall Toll8
protein levels (Fig. 1g). These ﬁndings demonstrate that psyllid Toll8
functions as a PRR that directly recognizes CLas outer membrane
protein Barrel.
To identify key amino acids invol ved in the Toll8-Barrel interac-
tion, we performed protein structure prediction and interaction
modeling. Our analysis revealed that Asn679 (N679), Asn712 (N712),
and Asp717 (D717) in the LRR domain of Toll8 were in close proximity
to Barrel (Fig. 1h). Y2H and GST pull-down assays showed that sub-
stitution of D717 with alanine (D717A), but not N679A or N712A, dis-
rupted the interaction with Barrel (Supplementary Fig. 3g, h). Co-
expression of Barrel with Toll8 and its mutants in Sf9 cells revealed
that Toll8
D717A exhibited signiﬁcantly reduced dimerization compared
to wild-type Toll8 and the other mutants (Fig. 1i). This demonstrates
that D717 is essential for Barrel recognition and subsequent Toll8
dimerization. Thus, psyllid Toll8 acts as a PRR that directly recognizes
CLas outer membrane protein Barrel, triggering dimerization inCLas-
infected psyllids (Fig.1j).
CLas Barrel induces MyD88 dimerization via Toll8 activation
MyD88, a key adapter protein in Toll signaling, contains a TIR domain
for interaction with Toll receptors and a death domain (DD) for
recruiting downstream signaling molecules
11,32. Y2H and GST pull-
down assays con ﬁrmed that the TIR domains of Toll8 and MyD88
mediate their interaction (Fig. 2a, b). CLas infection signi ﬁcantly
increased MyD88 expression and dimerization in psyllids (Fig. 2c, d).
dsRNA-mediated knockdown ofMyD88 expression (dsMyD88) inCLas-
infected psyllids resulted in an increasedCLas accumulation (Fig.2e, f),
highlighting its role in anti-CLas immunity.
Co-expression of Barrel, Toll8, and MyD88 in Sf9 cells revealed
that Barrel did not directly promote MyD88 dimerization but did so in
the presence of Toll8 (Fig. 2g), suggesting that Barrel-activated Toll8
induces MyD88 dimerization. Similarly, microinjection of puri ﬁed
Barrel into psyllids enhanced MyD88 dimerization without altering
overall MyD88 protein levels (Fig.2h). These results indicate thatCLas
infection activates the Toll8-MyD88 signaling cascade, which exerts
anti-CLas effects (Fig. 2i).

IKKE directly interacts with and phosphorylates a homolog of
mammalian nuclear factor of activated T-cells
To identify transcription factors activated by IKKE, we screened a D.
citri cDNA library using IKKE as bait. From multiple candidate inter-
actors detected, the nuclear factor of activated T-cells (NFAT) homo-
log was uniquely detected as the only transcription factor showing
interaction with IKKE (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 2). This ﬁnding is
particularly signiﬁcant as NFAT, while well-characterized in mammals
for its roles in immunity and development, remains understudied in
insect systems despite sharing the conserved RHD domain (Rel
homology domain) with NF-κB
34,35. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that
insect NFATs are closely related (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Psyllid NFAT
contains an N-terminal domain, a central RHD domain, and a
C-terminal domain, resembling mammalian NFAT (Supplementary
Fig. 6a). Y2H and GST pull-down assays conﬁrmed that the N-terminal
kinase domain of IKKE interacted with the RHD domain of NFAT
(Fig. 3a–c). CLas infection signiﬁcantly increased NFAT transcription,
protein accumulation, and nuclear localization in psyllids (Supple-
mentary Fig. 6b, c). Microinjection of Barrel into psyllids enhanced
nuclear accumulation of NFAT (Supplementary Fig. 6d). dsRNA-
mediated knockdown of NFAT expression (dsNFAT) in CLas-infected
psyllids resulted in an increased CLas accumulation (Fig. 3d, e), con-
ﬁrming its anti-CLas activity.
Immunoﬂuorescence microscopy analysis demonstrated that
NFAT formed punctate cytoplasmic and nucleus foci in psyllid midgut
epithelium (Fig. 3f). Similarly, heterologously expressed NFAT in Sf9
cells exhibited diffuse cytoplasmic distribution accompanied by pro-
gressive nuclear accumulation (Fig. 3g). These observations collec-
tively support NFAT ’s role as a nucleocytoplasmic shuttling
transcription factor. Co-expression of IKKE and NFAT in Sf9 cells
increased NFAT phosphorylation and nuclear accumulation (Fig. 3g,
h). Phosphorylation mass spectrometry identi ﬁed enriched phos-
phorylation sites in the NFAT-RHD domain (residues 167–180) (Fig.3i).
Constructing NFAT mutants with alanine substitutions at these sites,
including NFAT

To identify NFAT-regulated antimicrobial effectors, ChIP-Seq revealed

B. mori and Procambarus clarkiiexhibit antimicrobial activity through

production, which exerts broad- spectrum microbicidal effects 38,39.
Given the high enrichment of Reeler and NOS in ChIP-Seq results
(Fig. 4a), we focused on these two candidates.
Sequence analysis predicted an N-terminal signal peptide and a
122-amino-acid mature form of Reeler (Supplementary Fig. 7a-i).
AlphaFold3 structural prediction revealed multiple alpha helices and
beta sheets in Reeler (Supplementary Fig. 7a-ii). ChIP-qPCR validated
NFAT binding toReeler and NOS promoter regions (Fig.4b). Moreover,
EMSA and Y1H assays demonstrated NFAT binding to Reeler and NOS
promoters (Fig. 4c–e). Luciferase reporter assay conﬁrmed that NFAT
directly activatedReeler and NOS transcription (Fig.4f, g). The dsNFAT
treatment signiﬁcantly reduced Reeler and NOS expression and NO
accumulation in psyllids (Fig. 4h–j). CLas infection signi ﬁcantly
increased Reeler and NOS expression and NO levels in psyllids
(Fig. 4k–m). Microinjection of Barrel also increased Reeler and NOS
accumulation in psyllids (Fig. 4n). Together, these results reveal that
CLas-activated NFAT acts as a direct regulator of Reeler and NOS.
We then investigated whether psyllid Reeler and NOS exhibited

E3 ubiquitin ligases play a crucial role in the ubiquitin-proteasome
pathway by transferring ubiquitin molecules to substrate proteins 40.
During the preliminary Y2H screening using MyD88 as a bait protein,
we identiﬁed the E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR5, containing a HECT domain,
interacting with MyD88 (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 8a, b, Supple-
mentary Table 1). CLas infection increased UBR5 expression (Supple-
mentary Fig. 8c, d), while dsRNA-mediated knockdown of UBR5
expression (dsUBR5) resulted in an increased MyD88, NFAT, Reeler,
and NOS accumulation while reducing CLas accumulation (Supple-
mentary Fig. 8e, f). Co-expression of UBR5 and MyD88 in HEK-293T
cells showed that the increased UBR5 expression led to the reduced
MyD88 accumulation (Fig. 6d). In vitro ubiquitination assays con-
ﬁrmed that UBR5 mediates ubiquitination of MyD88 (Fig. 6e, f). Co-
immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays showed that MyD88 ubiquitina-
tion was enhanced in Sf9 cells co-expressing UBR5 and MyD88 under
MG-132 treatment (Fig. 6g). Bioinformatic prediction and systematic
mutagenesis revealed three candidate ubiquitination sites in MyD88,
including Lysine 48 (K48) in the DD domain, K201 in the TIR domain,
and K301 at the C-terminus (Fig.6h). Among these, K48 emerged as the
primary ubiquitination site, as its mutation (K48R) signi ﬁcantly atte-
nuated K48-linked polyubiquitination, while mutations at K201 and
K301 showed comparatively weaker effects on ubiquitination levels

CLas transmission assay
To evaluate the effect of gene silencing on CLas transmission, we
performed inoculation experiments using adultD. citri that had been
reared for multiple generations onCLas-infected sweet orange (Citrus
sinensis) plants. Given thatCLas titer in psyllids is a key determinant of
transmission efﬁciency65,w e ﬁrst assessed bacterial levels in the insects
prior to inoculation. A random sample of 30 adult psyllids was starved
for 6 h and subjected to qPCR analysis of CLas titer in head-thorax
tissues. All sampled insects tested positive for CLas, with cycle
threshold (Ct) values below 20, indicating a high acquisition rate and
conﬁrming successful CLas infection cycle and abundant bacterial
accumulation.
Previous study showed thatCLas transmission by infected psyllids
feeding on young citrus leaves typically requires nearly 3 months
before HLB symptoms become visible
65. To expedite the assessment of
CLas transmission efﬁciency, a method has been established in which
infected psyllids are con ﬁned to young citrus leaves for a 7-day
inoculation access period, followed by a 7-day accumulation phase,
after which CLas can be detected by qPCR
23. Based on this approach,
approximately 600CLas-infected psyllids were microinjected with 46

leaf clip cage (Supplementary Fig. 4i). The insects were allowed to feed
for 7 days under controlled conditions (27 °C, 14:10 L:D photoperiod)
23.
After the inoculation period, psyllids were removed and stored for
subsequent analysis. The exposed citrus leaves were maintained in an
incubator at 27 °C for an additional 7 days to allow potential CLas
propagation. For each treatment group, ten leaf replicates were
prepared.
Total DNA was extracted from leaf vein-adjacent (midribs and
major veins) tissues on which psyllids had fed, using a CTAB-based
method. Given that the leaf veins are primary sites for CLas accumu-
lation, sampling these regions provides a partial enrichment of bac-
