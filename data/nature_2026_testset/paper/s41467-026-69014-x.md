Fibroblast growth factor 13 (FGF13) belongs to the FGF homologous factor
subfamily, members of which lack signal peptides. In this study, we demon-
strate that FGF13 is signiﬁcantly downregulated in endothelial cells and mac-
rophages in the lungs of septic patientsand septic mice. However, the role of
FGF13 in sepsis and the underlying mechanism are largely unknown. By using
mice with conditionalFgf13 knockout and FGF13 overexpression, weﬁnd that
FGF13 accelerates septic lung injury by promoting inﬂammatory activation of
endothelial cells and macrophages. Speciﬁcally, FGF13 functions as a scaffold
protein in TAK1/MEK/ERK signaling to promote hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)
−1α-regulated aerobic glycolysis in the inﬂammatory state. Meanwhile, the
protective effect of conditionalFgf13 knockout is abolished in HIF-1α-
overexpressing mice. In addition, SCH772984 (a selective antagonist of ERK
signaling) abolishes the aggravation of inﬂammation in lungs induced by
FGF13 overexpression. Ourﬁndings demonstrate that FGF13 promotes
inﬂammatory activation upon septic lung injury through the ERK/aerobic
glycolysis axis, thereby accelerating the progression of septic lung injury.
As a critical public health emergency, sepsis has had an average annual
rate of about 50 million cases globally since 2017, with a mortality rate
of approximately 20%, resulting in a ﬁnancial burden of around $20
billion in the United States alone1. Despite decades of clinical research
on sepsis, treatment strategies for this disease remain limited to anti-
biotics, steroids, supplements, and organ support. These broad-
spectrum interventions not only exhibit limited effectiveness but
also frequently lead to various side effects, which impact patient
prognosis
2. Therefore, gaining a deeper understanding of the patho-
genesis of sepsis and developing more targeted and safer therapeutic
strategies based on this enhanced understanding has signiﬁcant clin-
ical implications.
During the progression of sepsis, the direct damage caused by
pathogens and the excessive in ﬂammatory response induce multi-
organ damage, potentially culminating in organ failure and mortality.
Among these complications, septic lung injury is the most prevalent
and serious manifestation
3,4. When pathogen-associated molecular
patterns (e.g., lipopolysaccharide (LPS), mitochondrial DNA, and
lipoteichoic acid) produced by pathogens destroy the barrier integrity
of lung epithelial and endothelial cells or even kill them, they lead to

Fibroblast growth factor 13 (FGF13) belongs to the FGF homo-
logous factor subfamily, which differs from other FGF subfamilies
because its members all lack signal peptides and cannot be secreted
conventionally
11. FGF13 has been extensively studied in the nervous

FGF13 promotes aerobic glycolysis in the inﬂammatory state
Beyond its established functions in the nervous system, FGF13 plays
signiﬁcant roles in colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and several other
malignancies22– 24. Cancer cells undergo metabolic reprogramming
from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis in order to fuel
their rapid proliferation, migration, and invasion. This metabolic shift
is strikingly similar to that observed in endothelial cells and macro-
phages during in ﬂammatory activation
25,26. Notably, elevated lactate
levels resulting from enhanced aerobic glycolysis correlate strongly
with mortality rates in septic patients
27. Recent investigations of glu-
cose homeostasis revealed an inverse relationship between FGF13
expression and the peripheral blood glucose concentration
28.C o n -
sidering the critical role of aerobic glycolysis in mediating pro-
inﬂammatory responses of both endothelial and immune cells, we
postulated that FGF13 may regulate in ﬂammation progression by
modulating glucose metabolism pathways.
As expected, in both models, the levels of lactate were lower in the
peripheral blood of Fgf13△Endo and Fgf13△mye mice than in the periph-
eral blood of corresponding control mice, whereas the opposite was
true in CDH5-Cre

sistent with the ﬁndings of the in vivo studies, the results of the Sea-
horse XF glycolysis stress test suggested that the aerobic glycolysis
capacity of pro-inﬂammatory HPMECs and THP-1 cells was signiﬁcantly
reduced by knockdown of FGF13, but ampliﬁed by overexpression of
FGF13 (Fig. 4A– D). Both 2-NBDG ﬂuorescence staining and glucose
uptake experiments revealed that glucose uptake and lactate pro-
duction in pro-in ﬂammatory HPMECs and THP-1 cells were notably
disrupted by knockdown of FGF13 (Fig. 4E– L), but enhanced by over-

FGF13 in promoting aerobic glycolysis in endothelial cells and mac-

FGF13 regulates aerobic glycolysis via ERK/ HIF-1α signaling
During inﬂammatory responses, aerobic glycolysis is sophisticatedly
regulated by multiple signaling cascades, including the AMPK-mTOR,
ERK/HIF-1α,a n dN F -κB pathways25. To elucidate the precise molecular
mechanism by which FGF13 modulates in ﬂammatory aerobic glyco-
lysis, we performed unbiased proteomic analysis via mass spectro-
metry of lysates of FGF13-overexpressing HEK293 cells (Fig. 5A). This
comprehensive screening identi ﬁed MAPK1 (ERK2) as a high-
conﬁdence interaction partner of FGF13 (Fig. 5B). Based on this
protein– protein interaction, we hypothesized that FGF13 regulates
inﬂammation-induced aerobic glycolysis primarily through the ERK/
HIF-1α signaling axis, which is a well-established pathway in metabolic
reprogramming during inﬂammatory activation.
As expected, knockdown of FGF13 signi ﬁcantly reduced the ele-
vated phosphorylation levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 as well as the
protein level of HIF-1α in LPS-treated HPMECs and THP-1 cells, while
overexpression of FGF13 further upregulated the phosphorylation
levels of MEK1/2 and ERK1/2 as well as the protein level of HIF-1 α
(Fig. 5C– F). When cells were pretreated with SCH772984 (a speci ﬁc
ERK1/2 inhibitor)
30, the enhanced aerobic glycolysis capacity ofFgf13-
overexpressing HPMECs was abolished, and the increased glucose
uptake and lactate production were also abrogated (Figs.5G, S9A– E).
Consistently, SCH772984 counteracted the increased monocyte
adhesion to LPS-treated Fgf13-overexpressing HPMECs (Fig. S9F – I).
Similarly, pretreatment with SCH772984 abolished the enhanced
aerobic glycolysis capacity ofFgf13-overexpressing THP-1 cells, leading
to decreased glucose uptake and lac tate production in LPS-treated
THP-1 cells (Figs. 5H, S9J– N). Moreover, SCH772984 reduced the ele-
vated levels of iNOS protein and secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α in Fgf13-
overexpressing THP-1 cells (Fig. S9O, P). Collectively, these results
indicate that the role of FGF13 in promoting aerobic glycolysis to
exacerbate inﬂammatory activation of endothelial cells and macro-
phages depends on ERK/HIF-1α signaling.

Discussion
Septic lung injury is a leading cause of mortality in sepsis and repre-
sents a critical area for extensive research on clinical treatment
3.O u r
study illustrates the association between FGF13 and septic lung injury.
Expression levels of FGF13 were decreased in endothelial cells and
macrophages of septic mice and septic patients, which was controlled
by p53. Notably, speci ﬁc knockout of FGF13 in endothelial cells or
macrophages alleviated lung injury and reduced mortality in septic
mice, while targeted overexpression of FGF13 had the opposite effects.
Mechanistically, we revealed that FGF13 acts as a scaffold protein for
the TAK1-MEK-ERK signaling axis and thereby facilitates transmission
of this signaling upon LPS stimulation, which promotes HIF-1α-induced
aerobic glycolysis in endothelial cells and macrophages, leading to
their inﬂammatory activation (Fig.9). Therefore, we identiﬁed FGF13 as
a notable regulator of inﬂammation in the progression of septic lung
injury.
Our investigations reveal a paradoxical pattern: FGF13 expression
is signi ﬁcantly downregulated in pulmonary endothelial cells and
macrophages of both septic mice and human septic patients, but
FGF13 exacerbates septic lung injury. This seemingly contradictory
phenomenon parallels our observations in cardiac pathologies, where,
despite aggravating hypertrophic responses and cellular senescence,
FGF13 exhibits reduced expression in cardiac tissues of hypertrophic
and doxorubicin-treated mice
34,35. Taken together, FGF13 down-
regulation under pathological conditions is not con ﬁned to sepsis
alone, and this seeming contradiction can be resolved through an
examination of FGF13 ’s diverse functional roles. Contemporary
research demonstrated that FGF13 functions as a cytoprotective factor
by suppressing ribosomal RNA transcription and attenuating protein
synthesis, thereby mitigating oxidative stress and inhibiting apoptotic
pathways
32. Comparable cytoprotective effects were documented in
studies of neurogenic hearing loss in murine models 36. Collectively,
these ﬁndings establish FGF13 as a critical mediator of cellular survival
mechanisms. The molecular basis for FGF13 downregulation may be
attributable to regulatory crosstalk with p53, which transcriptionally
represses both its negative modulator, miR-504, and the miR-504 host
gene Fgf13
32,33. Notably, p53 signaling is greatly activated in diverse
pathological contexts, including cardiac hypertrophy, cardiac aging,
and sepsis 35,37– 39. We therefore postulate that the observed down-
regulation of FGF13 during sepsis stems from heightened activation of
the p53 pathway, which orchestrates apoptotic cascades and DNA
damage responses. This hypothesis was experimentally validated
through p53 silencing experiments in HPMECs and THP-1 cells

Another notable limitation of our current investigation is the absence

To rapidly meet heightened cellular energy demands during
inﬂammation, metabolic processes undergo a critical shift from ef ﬁ-
cient oxidative phosphorylation to rapid aerobic glycolysis, a phe-
nomenon known as the Warburg effect
42.T h i sm e t a b o l i c
reprogramming is a hallmark of in ﬂammatory activation of both
endothelial cells and macrophages. Importantly, therapeutic inter-
ventions targeting this glycolytic shift have demonstrated signiﬁcant
efﬁcacy in ameliorating diseases associated with inﬂammatory activa-
tion of these cellular populations
43,44. Recent clinical investigations
revealed that peripheral blood FGF13 protein levels are inversely cor-
related with blood glucose concentrations in patients with inﬂamma-
tory conditions28. Complementing these observations, our research
established that FGF13 potentiates in ﬂammatory activation of both
endothelial cells and macrophages. Theseﬁndings led us to hypothe-
size that there is a mechanistic link between the pro-in ﬂammatory
functions of FGF13 and enhanced aerobic glycolysis. Through com-
prehensive assessment of glycolytic parameters in endothelial cells
and macrophages, we revealed that FGF13 signi ﬁcantly exacerbates
inﬂammatory activation by augmenting aerobic glycolysis. Sophisti-
cated LC-MS analyses further elucidated the molecular underpinnings
of this phenomenon, revealing that FGF13 functions as a critical scaf-
fold protein for the TAK1-MEK-ERK signaling axis. This scaffolding
activity facilitates HIF-1α-induced aerobic glycolysis in both endothe-
lial cells and macrophages, consequently driving their pro-
inﬂammatory phenotype. Beyond glycolytic modulation, multiple
inﬂammatory mediators, including oxidative stress and pyroptosis,
substantially contribute to sepsis pathophysiology and have emerged
as focal points of contemporary research
45,46. FGF13 can enhance
mitochondrial function via microtubule stabilization, suggesting it
plays a regulatory role in controlling ROS production and subsequent
inﬂammatory cascades
47. Intriguingly, our LC-MS analyses identiﬁed an
interaction between FGF13 and NLRP1, raising the intriguing possibility
that FGF13 inﬂuences assembly of the NLRP1 in ﬂammasome, thereby
potentially promoting pyroptotic cell death and exacerbating inﬂam-
matory responses. Notably, our LC-MS results regarding FGF13-
binding proteins were obtained in HEK293 cells. Consequently,
beyond its established role as a scaffold protein for the TAK1-MEK-ERK
signaling axis, whether FGF13 can mediate in ﬂammatory processes
through interactions with additional proteins requires further com-
prehensive validation in physiologically relevant cell types under
pathological conditions.
Emerging evidence demonstrates that FGFs integral to pulmonary
development, notably FGF2 and FGF10, confer signiﬁcant therapeutic
beneﬁts against septic lung injury in murine models
14,16,19,48.O u r
investigative journey began by conducting comprehensive expression
proﬁling of FGFs across pulmonary tissues from both septic mice and
septic patients, ultimately identifying FGF13 as a critical regulatory
factor warranting detailed examination. Nevertheless, this targeted
approach should not overshadow the potential signi ﬁcance of other
FGF family members in sepsis pathophysiology. Sepsis is a complex,
systemic inﬂammatory condition characterized by cascading multi-
organ dysfunction. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that preserve
vital organ functions, including hepatic, cardiac, and renal systems,
may effectively attenuate disease progression
49. This multi-organ
perspective is illustrated by our ﬁndings regarding FGF21: while its
pulmonary expression is relatively similar in healthy and septic
patients, our previous investigations revealed that FGF21 orchestrates
autophagic degradation of HIF-1α, thereby suppressing macrophage-
driven in ﬂammatory responses and ameliorating sepsis-associated
hepatic injury
2. In parallel, complementary research demonstrated
that FGF5 enhances myocardial performance during septic episodes
through targeted inhibition of the CaMKII/NF κB signaling cascade
50.
These collective observations underscore the remarkable versatility of
FGF family members across diverse organ systems impacted by sepsis.
Given their multifaceted roles in modulating in ﬂammation, tissue
repair, and cellular metabolism, we propose that FGFs represent pro-
mising candidates for translational research with signi ﬁcant ther-
apeutic potential for sepsis management.
Collectively, our data demonstrate that FGF13 is a critical reg-
ulator of the ERK/HIF-1 α/aerobic glycolysis axis in the in ﬂammatory
state through acting as a scaffold protein for the TAK1-MEK-ERK
signaling axis, thereby accelerating the progression of septic lung
injury.

Ethics statement
All human participants' research in this study was conducted in
accordance with the principles of “World Medical Association
Declaration of Helsinki: ethical principles for medical research invol-

patients (patients requiring surgical removal of lung nodules) and

HEK293T cells were transfected with Flag- Fgf13 plasmids, and cells

the proteins connected with Flag-FGF13. The assay was carried out by

consisted of 5 ~ 35 % (v/v) acetonitrile in 0.1 % (v/v) formic acid at aﬂow

Sepsis model
Based on the Minimum Quality Threshold in Pre-Clinical Sepsis Studies
(MQTiPSS) guidelines, CLP was employed to establish murine sepsis
models. The speciﬁc CLP procedure was conducted according to the
methodology described in a previous investigation
56, the speci ﬁc
procedure for CLP is as follows: C57BL/6 male mice (8-10 weeks) were
anesthetized with an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of pentobarbital
sodium (50 mg/kg). Sham-operated mice underwent identical proce-
dures, except for the CLP. In the CLP-induced sepsis model, the cae-
cum of mice was ligated 50% and the ligated cecal stump was then
perforated by one“through and through” puncture (21-gauge needle).
For LPS-induced endotoxemia sepsis, mice were injected intraper-
itoneally with a single dose of LPS (10 mg/kg) for 12 h. In both models
of sepsis, to inhibit the activation of ERK signaling, SCH772984 (10 mg/
kg, i.p.) was used to treat mice 1 h after LPS injection or CLP operation.
The serum sample and lungs were harvested after 12 h for further
experimentation. After collecting the blood sample, the mice under-
went perfusion through the left ventricle with 10 ml of 0.9 % saline for
2 minutes. After perfusion, the lungs were extracted from the mice.
Following this, the lungs were positioned in a 10 cm diameter culture
dish ﬁlled with 0.9 % saline, and images of the lungs’appearance were
taken. And the remaining tissues were ﬁxed or stored at low tem-
peratures as per the experimental needs.

Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism
8.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean
(SEM). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired Student ’s
t-tests were employed. Multiple group comparisons with a single
variable were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)
followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to identify speci ﬁci n t e r g r o u pd i f -
ferences. For experimental designs with two variable factors, two-way
ANOVA was conducted, followed by Tukey ’s post hoc test to deter-
mine signiﬁcant differences between speciﬁc groups. Survival analyses
were performed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical sig-
niﬁcance was established at p < 0.05 for all analyses.
