Results
We propose that distributed visual computation harnesses spatio-
temporal patterns with three key components, illustrated in Fig.1a: (i)
low-frequency, large-scale θ waves that travel vertically (between
cortical layers) and horizontally (between cortical regions); (ii) high-
frequency, short, and localizedγ packets (spatiotemporal bursts) that
reﬂect focused processing or targeted projections; and (iii) cross-scale
coupling between θ traveling waves, γ wave packets, and neuronal
spiking. Here we investigated these rich dynamics using state-of-the-
art high-density, multi-region laminar recordings from the mouse
visual cortex, available as the Allen Neuropixels –Visual Behavior
dataset
37,42. These recordings, illustrated in Fig.1b, feature ﬁne spatial
resolution, with LFP channels spaced vertically by 40 μm, and exten-
sive spatial coverage, with probes in each of six visual cortical regions
(VISp, VISl, VISrl, VISal, VISpm,a n dVISam, ordered by increasing
anatomical hierarchy rank
9). Temporally, these data comprise high-
resolution 1250 Hz LFP and spike data from hundreds of neurons
over recording sessions lasting ~3 h, with a variety of stimuli, including:
as t a t i cg r a ys c r e e n( spontaneous), bright or dark ﬂashes
(ﬂash_250ms), and natural images paired with a change detection task

content changed. These stimuli—especially the change-detection task

nested spatiotemporal dynamics support top-down and bottom-up
processing over distributed brain areas.
We leveraged the dense vertical sampling of the Neuropixels
probes, illustrated in Fig. 1b, and a suite of wave-based methods to
quantify nested spatiotemporal dynamics in the LFP signal. After
identifying the dominant timescales of visual cortical activity, we ﬁl-
tered the raw LFP (shown in Fig. 1c) into two frequency bands: θ
(3–10 Hz) and γ (30–100 Hz). We characterized the θ component as a
traveling wave by computing the instantaneous phase, depicted in
Fig. 1d, which describes the progression of a time series through its
peaks and troughs using an angular value from−π to π. This approach
normalizes intrinsic amplitude differences between channels that may
arise from variations in laminar electrical response properties
43 (unlike
methods that quantify laminar activity using current source densities)
and is particularly suited to detecting traveling patterns. By examining
the spatiotemporal organization of the instantaneous phase, we found
that θ activity is organized as large-scale traveling waves that propa-
gate between laminae and across cortical areas. Speciﬁcally, we mea-
sured the directionality of θ-wave propagation across cortical layers
using the negative spatial gradient ofθ phase (as illustrated in Fig.1e).
To emphasize the importance of correctly orienting the derivative
under different phase conventions, and for conciseness, we refer to
this negative phase gradient as the wavenumber [see Eq. (6)]. Then, to
examine the spatiotemporal structure of the higher-frequency γ
component, we used the instantaneous amplitude, depicted in Fig.1f.
The instantaneous amplitude tracks the envelope of the high-
frequency oscillation, revealing the presence of non-stationary, spa-
tially localizedγ packets across cortical layers. Finally, we investigated
how large-scale travelingθ waves coordinate with localized γ packets
and neuronal spiking across cortical layers to form cross-scale spa-
tiotemporal structures, as illustrated in Fig.1g.
We propose that these nested dynamics form a spatiotemporal
θ–γ code, which builds on the classical univariate θ–γ code by
accounting for interactions between propagating θ waves and loca-
lized γ packets. Figure1a illustrates how this principle implements two
key features of ﬂexible computation: multiplexing and modulation.
Consider a hypothetical scenario involving paired γ packets, I and II,
that occur near the onset of a visual stimulus. Packet I arises in the deep
layers of a lower-order visual area, re ﬂecting top-down information
conveyed through feedback connections. Shortly thereafter, packet II
emerges in the super ﬁcial layers, carrying a potentially independent
bottom-up signal. Critically, packets I and II are separated in space and
time—spatiotemporally multiplexed—to minimize interference. Packet

Inter-areal θ propagation switches hierarchical directionality
Evoked θ waves travel upward across cortical laminae after stimulus
onset, and downward following stimulus offset. These bidirectional
propagations also show strong signatures of top-down and bottom-up
processing during visual tasks. To clarify the structural basis of this
dual functional role of θ, we next studied the propagation of θ waves
between regions of the visual cortical hierarchy using anatomical
and functional metrics of feedforward and feedback neuronal
connectivity
7,9.
We ﬁrst calculated anatomical and functional distance matrices
using the anatomical scores (visualized in Fig. 4a) and functional
directionality scores (Fig.4c) published by Siegle et al.7. While the two
hierarchies are correlated, they were measured from independent
datasets: the anatomical scores reﬂect the strength of feedforward and
feedback axonal connections, whereas the functional scores reﬂect the
average correlation and temporal delay between spiking activity in
different regions. To quantifyθ propagation between visual areas, we
computed the moment-by-moment phase differences between pairs
of channels at equivalent cortical depths on different probes. To better
estimate the coherence of propagation along the loose hierarchies, we
considered all pairs of probes and normalized the phase differences by
the corresponding anatomical or functional distance. As for the
translaminar wavenumber, we took the negative of the phase differ-
ences to ensure that positive values indicate propagation in a feed-
forward direction, where θ in lower-order regions leads θ in higher-
order regions. The mean of the resulting propagation directions gives
the anatomical order parameterA
θ and the functional order parameter
Fθ, which describe the coherence and directionality of θ propagation
across the respective hierarchies. Crucially, the hierarchical order
parameters do not trace the detailed structural projections between
regions and layers; instead, they are better viewed as tracking the
sequence of neural activity in equivalent layers across the hierarchy,
which are thought to have similar functional roles.

like translaminar propagation, switches directionality across a trial:
from higher- to lower-order areas (feedback-oriented) following sti-
mulus onset, but from lower- to higher-areas (feed-forward oriented)
following offset. Figure 4b shows the mean anatomical order para-
meter during the task period, which is highly negative (approaching

coupling methods; (ii) trends in PAC across layers and cortical areas,
averaging over time; and (iii) the nonstationary properties of nested
oscillations in response to a visual stimulus, by computing PAC jointly
across space and time during the course of an average trial.
First, we analyzed global phase –amplitude coupling (PAC) to
determine whether our chosen θ and γ bands exhibit cross-scale
interactions. We computed the classical phase–amplitude modulation

task and ﬂash trials. Second, phase frequencies near 4 Hz and broad-
band amplitude frequencies >30 Hz are coupled in lower-order areas
during ﬂash and task trials. These frequency bands closely agree with
the power spectral peaks in Fig. 2, suggesting a coupling between θ
waves and γ packets and supporting our chosen bands of 3–10 Hz (θ)
and 30–100 Hz (γ).

The dynamics of neural computation are a crucial bridge between the
structural and functional organization of the brain, linking underlying
anatomy to emergent cognition and behavior

revealed diverse dynamical motifs that underlie neural processing,

temporal pattern composed of slow θ traveling waves nested with
localized, high-frequencyγ packets and neuronal spiking.
We examined, in turn, the spectral, spatiotemporal, then cross-
scale properties of neural activity in the mouse visual cortex. By
mapping LFP spectra across visual cortical areas and layers, we found
that all regions have clear θ-band (3–10 Hz) and γ-band (30–100 Hz)
peaks above a 1/f baseline. Our ﬁnding that deep layers exhibit higher

Our work also puts forward methodological advances for studying
the θ–γ code in spatiotemporal settings. Conventional tools for ana-
lyzing cross-frequency coupling

Depth estimation
The original Neuropixels dataset provides probe indices (positions
along the Neuropixels probe) and CCFv3 coordinates (positions in the
Allen Common Coordinate Framework
117) for each channel, alongside
structure and layer labels. We used these data to estimate two cortical
depths for each channel: the probe depth and the streamline depth.
The probe depth is the distance of a given channel from the last
channel with a missing structure acronym, marking the outer bound-
ary of the cortex. The probe depth, measured inμm, gives an accurate
position of each channel relative to other channels on the same probe,
and hence we used this quantity for calculating spatial gradients. Note
that due to differences in the angle of probe placement relative to the
tangent plane of the cortical surface, the absolute units of the probe
depth may not represent equivalent relative positions between the
cortical surface and white matter across probes. For an estimate of
cortical depth that corrects for differences in mouse anatomies and
probe placement, we calculated a streamline depth as detailed in
Harris et al.

potential ﬁeld, estimated by applying Laplace’se q u a t i o nb e t w e e nt h e
cortical and white matter surfaces
117. The streamline depths are nor-

Session ﬁltering
The Allen Neuropixels–Visual Behavior dataset comprises 153 record-
ing sessions replicated across 81 mice, with most mice participating in
two sessions on different days; detailed descriptions of the animals
and protocols of the dataset are provided by the Allen Institute for
Brain Science
42. Each mouse belonged to one of three genotypes:
C57BL/6Jwt/wt (wild-type), Vip-IRES-Cre/wt; Ai32(RCL-
ChR2(H134R)_EYFP)/wt (Vip promoter), and Sst-IRES-Cre/
wt;Ai32(RCL-ChR2(H134R)_EYFP)/wt(Sst promoter), but we did
not study differences between genotypes. Weﬁltered down to 53 ses-
sions over 36 mice, described in Table 1, based on the following cri-
teria; the adequacy of this sample size was veri ﬁed by bootstrap
analysis of 95% conﬁdence intervals, which showed stable estimates of
average values.
First, we required six probes intersecting with the six target visual
cortical regions ( VISp, VISl, VISal, VISam, VISpm, VISrl), each
having valid channel location data. We also removed sessions from
mice with abnormal histology and behavioral activity. After estimating
layer annotations for each channel, as described above, we removed
sessions that had no channels in one or more layers, or those with poor
layer annotation consistency: we required that the maximum deviation
(scaled by the IQR) of the streamline depth of any given layer boundary
from the median depth of that boundary (across subjects) was no
greater than 3.0. We also required each session to include at least one
channel with a streamline depth greater than 0.9 and at least one
channel with a streamline depth less than 0.1, ensuring the recordings
spanned the cortical depth. Using the behavioral metadata provided
with the dataset, we required mice to have a d-prime score (measuring
the relative difference in response rates for hit trials and false alarm
trials) of no less than 1.0 in the change-detection task. Finally, we
manually removed sessions 1047969464, 1099598937, 1069461581,
and 1099869737, for which we could not infer which channel was
adjacent to the cortical surface, session 1128520325, which had no
channels in layer 3 after spatial downsampling, and 1093642839, which
was the only session with no visibleθ peak in the power spectrum (full
power spectra for all sessions and regions are available in the accom-
panying data repositories
51,52).
