Pregnant women are at higher risk of severe COVID-19, with vaccine access and
hesitancy remaining a challenge. Here, we use a pregnant female hamster
model of COVID-19 to explore the effectsof maternal infection on pregnancy,
revealing a signiﬁcant increase in intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) due to
placental inﬂammation. Viral infection causes bronchopneumonia and weight
loss in infected dams, but no vertical transmission occurs. IUGR is instead
linked to placental damage, characterized byﬁbrin deposition, thrombosis,
and elevated placental expression of IP10, IL6,a n dIL10, irrespective of fetal
sex. Enoxaparin treatment reduces placental damage and improves fetal out-
comes, while vaccination enhances viral clearance, protects the placenta, and
reduces the risk of IUGR. Theseﬁndings underscore placentitis as a key driver
of fetal complications upon SARS-CoV-2 infection and highlight the potential
of vaccination and anticoagulant therapy to protect both mother and child.
Since its emergence in late 2019, SARS-CoV-2 has disrupted daily life
and strained global health systems, causing widespread illness and
death. While advanced age, male sex, and comorbidities like cardi-
ovascular disease and diabetes have been identi ﬁed as key risk
factors
1,2, pregnancy poses particular challenges due to unique
physiological changes that increase susceptibility to severe
infection3. Moreover, mother and unborn child form an indivisible
dyad, whereby infections hit two vulnerable individuals at once,
though sequelae may differ. Clinically, pregnancy outcomes linked
to COVID-19 are in ﬂuenced by factors such as gestational age,
maternal comorbidities, and disease severity
4,5. Compared to non-
pregnant patients, expectant mothers are at a heightened risk of
hospitalization, ICU admission, invasive ventilation, and maternal
and neonatal mortality
6,7.
A range of viral infections is known to cause severe pathologies in
the unborn 8,9. The TORCH pathogens, which include rubella virus,
cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus, cross the placental barrier,
leading to intrauterine infections with devastating consequences for
the fetus. Nevertheless, the spectrum of viruses possibly interfering
with fetal development also includes respiratory viruses such as
inﬂuenza A virus and SARS-CoV-2
10,11, which affect pregnancy indir-
ectly. While direct vertical transmission is rare 12,13, SARS-CoV-2 infec-
tion appears to compromise placental function and integrity, leading
to necrosis, intervillous thrombosis, and ﬁbrin deposition, which in
turn may contribute to fetal distress or demise
14. Studies on the human
placenta have identiﬁed the syncytiotrophoblast as the primary site of
SARS-CoV-2 infection, with viral presence also reported in cyto-
trophoblasts, Hofbauer cells, villous stromal and endothelial cells, and

As research into COVID-19 and pregnancy advances, under-
standing the increased risks to pregnant women and their offspring
requires robust preclinical models. Animal models, including mice,
hamsters, ferrets, and non-human primates

regarding its impact on immune responses and placental function.
The placenta plays a central role in fetal development, mediating
gas exchange, nutrient transfer, and immune protection. Humans,
non-human primates, and rodents all possess a hemochorial placenta
in which placental trophoblast cells are in direct contact with maternal
blood. However, the overall structure of this essential support organ
differs, with humans and primates having a branched villous com-
partment, whereas in rodents, it forms a labyrinth. Such species-
speciﬁc differences in placental architecture, along with variations in
trophoblast invasion, vascular remodeling, and barrier composition,
are key factors in ﬂuencing maternal-fetal exchange and overall pla-
cental function
24,25. Seeing the convenience and availability of tools,
mice are still widely used as an experimental model of choice to study
placental physiology and pathophysiology
26. However, the murine
labyrinthine placenta is characterized by a particularly shallow tro-
phoblast invasion and limited spiral artery remodeling

pathogenesis, associated inﬂammation, and immune responses at the
maternal–fetal interface
28. Moreover, mice are naturally resistant to
clinical isolates of SARS-CoV-2, requiring exogenous expression of the
human ACE2 receptor. However, in the latter model, pathology and
mortality are skewed and dominated by neurotropic infection, ques-

litters, hamsters offer further practical advantages for the study of
pregnancy-related pathologies. As individual pups carried by one dam
are multichorionic with separate placentas and umbilical cords, each
hamster litter represents, on its own, a diverse birth cohort of multiple
mother-placenta-offspring triads.
In this study, we explored the effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on
pregnancy using a hamster model, providing insight into the virus ’s
impact on both maternal and fetal health. Our results demonstrate that
SARS-CoV-2-induced bronchopneumonia in pregnant dams leads to
maternal health complications, including gestational age-dependent
weight loss, although overall disease severity and lung pathology
remained largely unaffected. Importantly, while vertical transmission
was not observed, maternal infection signi ﬁcantly inﬂuenced intrau-
terine fetal development, markedly increasing the risk of intrauterine
growth restriction (IUGR) without affecting litter size, fetal demise, or
malformation rates. IUGR was linked to virus-induced placentitis
characterized by pronouncedﬁbrin deposition and tissue remodeling
of the decidua, and persistent in ﬂammation hallmarked by elevated
IP10 (CXCL10), IL6,a n d IL10 expression in the placentas of affected
pups. SARS-CoV-2-induced placental malfunction could be averted by
anti-coagulative therapy, and placentitis and subsequent IUGR could
be prevented by prophylactic immunization. By investigating these
interactions, we highlight the risks posed by the virus and explore
potential approaches, including treatments and vaccination, to pro-
tect both mother and child.

Persistent placental inﬂammation following viral clearance
To further investigate the impact of the maternal COVID-19-like dis-
ease and the resulting transient presence of viral RNA in the placenta,
we performed a histological analysis of the E10.5 and E4.5 groups,
focusing on structural integrity and morphological alterations of the
placental tissues. Similar to other rodents, as well as humans and non-
human primates, hamsters develop a hemochorial placenta, divided
into two anatomically distinct layers. In the case of rodents, these are
the junctional zone (JZ), responsible for energy (glycogen) and hor-
mone production, and the labyrinth, which facilitates maternal–fetal
exchange
51,52. In non-infected placentas at late pregnancy (E15.5), only
low levels of necrotic areas, ﬁbrin deposition, and thrombi were
observed, predominantly localized in the JZ. Additionally, the JZ
exhibited signiﬁcant congestion, characterized by enlarged sinuses
ﬁlled with red blood cells (Fig.3A, B), likely reﬂecting changes in blood
ﬂow and tissue remodeling associated with the progression toward
parturition (at term, i.e., E16)
53.
Despite the higher weight of placentas from animals infected late
during gestation at E10.5 (5 dpi, Fig. 2E), we did not observe any sig-
niﬁcant change in the ratio of their JZ to labyrinth areas, a parameter
associated with placental insufﬁciency52 (Fig. 3C). However, placentas
from infected animals exhibited increased perivascularﬁbrin deposi-
tion, thrombosis, and necrosis, primarily localized to the JZ. These
placentas also contained an increased number of nucleated red blood
cells (nRBCs) within the fetal vasculature in the labyrinth, generally
interpreted to result from a fetal stress response
54 (Fig. 3A, B). Con-
sequently, placentas from infected animals had signi ﬁcantly higher
cumulative pathological scores compared to those from non-infected
animals (Fig.3D). These histopathological changes and a similarly high
cumulative score were also observed in placentas from animals
infected at E4.5 (11 dpi, Fig.3A–D), indicating that tissue injury persists
despite the effective elimination of vRNA from the placentas at these
later time points (Supplementary Data 3), as would be expected since
histological lesions often lag behind viral clearance owing to ongoing
inﬂammation and repair processes.
Next, we investigated whether the presence of vRNA in the pla-
centa during the acute phase of infection correlated with an increased
expression of markers associated with immune cell in ﬁltration and
pro-inﬂammatory cytokine responses. To this end, we quanti ﬁed the
transcript levels of markers for NK cells (KLRG1, CD94), CD4⁺ (CD4)a n d
CD8⁺ (CD8) T cells, as well as genes associated with type I, II, and III IFN
responses (MX2, IP10,a n d IL28, respectively), and expression of cyto-
kines IL6 ( IL6), and IL10 ( IL10). Compared to placentas from non-
infected animals, those with detectable vRNA levels at 5 dpi (dams
infected at E10.5) exhibited signiﬁcantly increased transcript levels of
CD4 and KLRG1 (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Notably, an upregulation of
these cell identity markers was observed exclusively in placentas with
detectable vRNA. A similar trend, yet not reaching statistical sig-
niﬁcance, was observed for CD8 and CD94 transcript levels.
Our multimodal analysis of individual mother–placenta–offspring
triads revealed a certain heterogeneity. For instance, each infected

Discussion
Our study aims to examine the effects of SARS-CoV-2 on maternal and
fetal health using a preclinical hamster model, focusing on associated
risks and rationalizing potential protective strategies.
We show a signiﬁcant impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection (B.1.1.7) on
pregnancy outcomes in Syrian hamsters, with the severity of effects
varying based on the stage of gestation. COVID-19 did not directly
affect maternal fertility (Supplementary Fig. 1), with no evidence for an
elevated risk of reproductive loss or reduction in litter size (Fig. 2A).
However, infection during early pregnancy, particularly around
implantation (E4.5), was strongly associated with a higher risk of IUGR,
suggesting that this period may be especially vulnerable to the virus’s
effects on fetal development (Fig. 2). Consistently, clinical studies
indicate that maternal infection during theﬁrst trimester is associated
with an increased risk of infants being born small for gestational age
63.
In our model, pregnancy itself did not lead to an exacerbation of dis-
ease for the mother, as infected female hamsters efﬁciently cleared the
virus and showed histological signs of lung tissue repair at later stages
of infection (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2).
We identiﬁed sustained placental inﬂammation accompanied by
necrotic and ﬁbrotic changes as the primary pathological processes
associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including IUGR. During
the acute phase of infection (5 dpi), viral RNA was detected in the
placentas of infected dams. However, no viral RNA was found in fetal
tissues, suggesting that placental dysfunction, rather than vertical
transmission, underlies these complications. Quantitatively, IUGR
varied with gestational age (Fig. 2C). As the placenta continues to
develop throughout pregnancy, its susceptibility and response to
infection may vary with time. For instance, the peak of viral replication
(i.e., 3–4d p i )
31,38 following challenge at E4.5 overlaps with the key event
of placentation (around E7.5). Importantly, such gestational-age-
dependent effects appear to mirror observations in human pregnan-
cies, where high-grade inﬂammatory and vascular placental pathology
occurs more frequently after SARS-CoV-2 infection in the ﬁrst and
second (67.5% and 55.2%, respectively) than in the third trimester
(39.6%)
16. Future studies using synchronized terminal sampling across
multiple timepoints after infection could help de ﬁne the temporal
dynamics of placental susceptibility.
Our ﬁndings indicate that although viral RNA was no longer
detectable in the placentas at 11 dpi, levels of interferon-inducible
protein 10 ( IP10,o r CXCL10) and myxovirus resistance 2 ( MX2)g e n e
expression (Fig. 3), along with other markers of innate and adaptive
immune activation (Supplementary Fig. 3), remained persistently ele-
vated. Notably, the sustained in ﬂammatory response driven by IP10,
with its primarily pro-inﬂammatory and anti-angiogenic properties
64,
was strongly associated with reduced fetal and placental weight. This
suggests that a placental pro-in ﬂammatory immune microenviron-
ment, rather than a continued active viral replication, plays a critical
role in mediating adverse fetal outcomes following maternal SARS-
CoV-2 infection. Similar ﬁndings have been reported in human preg-
nancies, where elevated IP10 levels have been linked to chronic pla-
cental inﬂammation
65, preeclampsia66, preterm delivery67,a n dS A R S -
CoV-2 infection 68.T h i si n ﬂammatory setting also correlated with
marked placental histopathological changes, including increased
necrosis, ﬁbrosis, and thrombosis, further implicating placental dys-
function as a key factor in adverse pregnancy outcomes. This has also
been documented in human pregnancies affected by COVID-19
69.
The beneﬁcial effects of enoxaparin on placental health and fetal
weight evolution observed in our model highlight the potential of
anticoagulation therapies in mitigating SARS-CoV-2-induced coagulo-
pathy, which can contribute to placental insuf ﬁciency
55,70 (Fig. 4 and
Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistent with ourﬁndings, previous work has
shown that low-molecular-weight heparin administration reduces the
incidence of placental pathology in pregnant women with COVID-19
71,
further supporting the importance of hemostasis regulation in this
context. Likewise, the positive impact of anti-coagulative therapy
without any direct antiviral effect (Fig. 4G) further validates placental
obstruction and malfunction during maternal COVID-19 as a root cause
of IUGR. Interestingly, placentas from B.1.1.7-infected dams treated
with enoxaparin showed an increased number of nRBCs within the
fetal vessels of the labyrinth. While nRBCs are commonly associated
with hypoxia and subsequent growth restriction
54,72,n os u c hs e q u e l a e

known to play an important immune-modulatory role, including
pathogen recognition, cytokine production, and phagocytosis, which
are critical to both innate and adaptive immune responses
59,60.
The ﬁndings using our previously established experimental
COVID-19 vaccine 32,33 demonstrate robust protective effects, sig-
niﬁcantly reducing both maternal lung pathology and placental
damage while lowering the risk of IUGR (Fig.5). These results are in line
with other studies on COVID-19 vaccines in pregnancy, which have
shown decreased rates of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as severe
COVID-19 disease, hospitalization, ICU admission, preterm birth, and
stillbirth, along with improved neonatal health outcomes, including
fewer admissions to neonatal intensive care units
73. In addition, vac-
cination also mitigates placental damage74, a key contributor to preg-
nancy complications. Together, these ﬁndings reinforce the crucial
role of vaccination in safeguarding both maternal and fetal health
during SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Limitations of the study
Our study aimed to evaluate the impact and pathogenesis of COVID-19
during pregnancy. The identiﬁed pathomechanism does not necessa-
rily involve prolonged viral replication at the fetal-maternal interface. It
rather highlights the prevention of placentitis as a key actionable tar-
get. In this context, both anticoagulant therapy and vaccination
emerge as readily available interventions. Despite achieving this goal,
we acknowledge several limitations. (1) We used Syrian hamsters as a
small animal infection model, leveraging their natural susceptibility to
SARS-CoV-2 infection and to virus-induced acute respiratory disease
resembling bronchopneumonia in human COVID-19 patients. How-
ever, this model does not fully replicate human pregnancy physiology
or placental architecture. Hamster pregnancy lasts only 16 days,
meaning that infection in hamsters impacts a larger proportion of fetal
development than in humans. Additionally, while both species share a
chorioallantoic discoid hemochorial placenta, hamsters, like most
rodents, present a trichorial structure, where maternal blood is sepa-
rated from fetal circulation by three trophoblast layers, in contrast to
the single-layered human placenta. These obvious anatomical and
physiological differences
28 impact placentation and fetal develop-
ment, limiting the direct translatability to the timeline of human
pregnancy. Nonetheless, placentitis has been linked to poor pregnancy
outcomes in human COVID-19
87. Furthermore, hamsters are poly-
tocous, giving birth to multiple offspring per pregnancy, unlike the
predominantly monotocous nature of human reproduction. This
difference limits direct causal links between initial maternal lung
infection and fetal outcome. Nonetheless, the large litter sizes and
heterogeneity of individual placenta-fetus dyads within one mother
allow for the study of the full spectrum of diseases, facilitating the
separation of key drivers (principal components) of pathogenesis. (2)
We used the earlier strain B.1.1.7 (VOC Alpha) for infection. This strain
has been shown to be particularly aggressive in hamsters
38,w h i l em o r e
recent variants show reduced virulence in our hamster model 36,61,88.
Likewise, viral growth kinetics may not reﬂect the human situation, as
seen in a >15 times longer gestation and another developmental state
at birth in humans. Nevertheless, recently circulating SARS-CoV-2 have
not lost their pathogenic potential in humans

higher vulnerability of pregnant women90 and limited cross-protection
conferred by previous exposure or vaccination91. (3) The experimental
YF17D vaccine used in our study is one of the few transmission-

transmission does not seem to play a major role in the observed IUGR.
Likewise, due to general safety concerns, use of live-attenuated vac-
cines may remain contraindicated during pregnancy. More impor-
tantly, IUGR and small birth size are exceptionally well-established
warning signs for proper physical and mental development

our results endorse the immunization of women of childbearing age.
In conclusion, our study shows how SARS-CoV-2 infection can
harm pregnancy, mainly by causing placental damage and IUGR. These
adverse effects can be reduced by pre-emptive vaccination and
anticoagulant treatment. Ourﬁndings identify means to protect both
mothers and their unborn babies from COVID-19 and, in extension,
other acute inﬂammatory infectious diseases, and emphasize the need
for improved disease management for this particularly vulnerable
population.

and K-means clustering were performed using standardized data
(mean = 0, SD = 1). K-means clustering was performed using the
Hartigan–Wong algorithm with a maximum of 100 iterations. The
Pearson correlation distance metric was used to determine similarity
between samples. The maximum number of clusters was set to six, and
the random seed was set to “Auto” to ensure reproducibility. Cluster-
ing results were visualized in the PCA space de ﬁned by the ﬁrst two
principal components (PC1 and PC2).
To evaluate whether maternal infection in ﬂuenced the sex dis-
tribution of offspring, we ﬁtted a binomial generalized linear model
(GLM) with logit link using the number of female pups versus total
pups per litter as the outcome and treatment group as the predictor.
Estimated proportions of female pups and 95% con ﬁdence intervals
were computed using the emmeans package (type = “response”).
Pairwise comparisons between treatment groups were performed
using Tukey-adjusted contrasts. To assess deviation from the theore-
tical 1:1 sex ratio, estimated proportions for each group were tested
against 0.5 using model-based one-sample comparisons. Model diag-
nostics included dispersion assessment based on Pearson residuals.
To evaluate whether offspring sex acted as a confounding variable
in the association between PCA-de ﬁned clusters and biological out-
comes, we examined both its association with the exposure (Cluster)
and its effect on the outcome estimates. First, independence between
sex and cluster membership was assessed using contingency tables
and Fisher’s exact test due to small sample sizes. For each continuous
outcome (pup weight, placental weight, cytokine concentrations, and
placental viral RNA levels), we speci ﬁed nested linear regression

(Outcome ~ Cluster) and (ii) an adjusted model including both Cluster
and Sex (Outcome ~ Cluster + Sex). Confounding was evaluated by
comparing the estimated cluster coefﬁcients between models and by
assessing the statistical signi ﬁcance of the Sex term. Linear model
assumptions were veriﬁed through residual diagnostics.
