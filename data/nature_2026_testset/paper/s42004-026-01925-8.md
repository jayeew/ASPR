Crystallisation is fundamental to many natural and industrial processes. It is inﬂuenced by various non-
equilibrium factors such as thermal history, mechanical perturbations, andﬂow, yet the effect of imposed
mass ﬂuxes on the supersaturation ration at which crystallisationﬁrst becomes macroscopically
observable remains uncharacterised. Here, we show experimentally that thermodiffusive and isothermal
diffusive massﬂuxes can cause aqueous potassium chloride to crystallise at lower local supersaturation
ratios than in spatially isothermal reference systems. A reference supersaturation ratio wasﬁrst
established using cooling crystallisation, where temperature varies in time but remains spatially uniform.
Under thermophobic thermodiffusion, theﬁrst appearance of crystals occurred at a lower local
supersaturation ratio than this equilibrium benchmark. Likewise, under isothermal diffusion between a
supersaturated solution and a lower-concentration reservoir, crystallisation occurred at lower
concentrations and higher temperatures than expected under spatially uniform conditions. In both
conﬁgurations, crystallisation consistently initiated in regions of steep concentration gradients rather
than at locations of maximum supersaturation ratio. These results provide macroscopic evidence that
non-equilibrium massﬂuxes can narrow the metastable zone width, emphasising the importance of
spatially varying temperature and concentrationﬁelds in controlling crystallisation. Theﬁndings have
broad implications for processes requiring precise crystallisation control.

On the basis of CNT, crystallisation from spatially isothermal and
homogenous solutions has been extensively explored. The metastable zone
(MSZ) is the region between the saturation curveCsat(T), where no further
dissolution of the species occu rs, and the metastable boundary Cnuc(T),
where spontaneous nucleation occurs. Common ways of manipulating the
metastable boundary include evaporation 8,c o o l i n g9, antisolvent 10,a n d
reactive crystallisation11. These methods trigger crystallisation through a
higher local supersaturation ratio. Some methods can also control nuclea-
tion through lowering the interfacial energy, which includes heterogeneous
nucleation (on walls or with seeding)
12 or template-assisted crystallisation13
where a lattice-matching surface is used. However, it was discovered that
non-equilibrium factors could also affect the metastable boundary. These
include overheating temperature and time 14,t h ec o o l i n gr a t e15,a n d
mechanical disturbances16. The temperature T and concentration C are
among the most important factors th at affect metastable boundary, but
detailed studies on the effect of spatia lly-varying, non-equilibrium con-
centration and temperature gradients have been lacking. Fickian (or iso-
thermal) diffusion considers species massﬂux induced by a concentration
gradient, while thermodiffusion (or Soret effect) describes the species mass
ﬂux induced by temperature gradients. Despite extensive studies on crys-
tallisation under spatially uniform conditions, the in ﬂuence of sustained
mass ﬂuxes arising from imposed temperature or concentration gradients
on the macroscopic crystallisation boundary remains largely
uncharacterised.
Furthermore, we recently showed that the thermodiffusion phenom-
enon in multichannel laminar ﬂow can be used effectively to concentrate
and desalinate brine
17. We showed experimentally that thermodiffusive
separation increases with mean concentration to salinities close to satura-
tion levels18, which could result in crystallisation. A two-step process in
which the brine is ﬁrst concentrated and then crystallised could achieve a
more cost-effective method than th e traditional evaporation ponds 19.
Hence, the crystallisation phenomenon under thermodiffusion has the
potential practical application of linking with the thermodiffusive brine
concentration to produce a zero liquid discharge desalination method that is
free of chemicals, membranes, and evaporation. This practical application
also motivates a clearer understanding of crystallisation behaviour under
non-equilibrium massﬂux.
In this work, we investigated the ef fect of thermodiffusion and iso-
thermal diffusion on the crystallisa tion process of potassium chloride
aqueous solutions (KCl/H
2O). To de ﬁne the metastable boundary in a
homogeneous isothermal solution, we cooled the solution from 20 °C to a
lower temperature. We then applied a vertically positive temperature gra-
dient ∇T keeping the upper wall at 20 °C while cooling the lower wall.
Thermophobic mass transport occu rred, which produced a vertically
negative concentration gradient∇C in the cell; crystallisation occurred at
the cold lower wall. The localS at the cold lower wall was still lower than that
in cooling crystallisation (despite thethermophobic transport that results in
al o c a ld e p l e t i o no fC). Furthermore, isothermal diffusion crystallisation
experiments conﬁrmed that crystallisation occurred at a lower S than
cooling crystallisation. Interestingly, crystallisation occurred at the point of
maximum concentration gradient, rather than where the local super-
saturation ratio (S) was highest within the crystallisation cell. The crystal-
lisation process under the three different conditions, cooling,
thermodiffusion and isothermal diffusion, was all characterised in situ by
phase-shifting interferometry. Optical microscopy reveals morphologi-
cal similarities between the cooling, thermodiffusion, and isothermal dif-
fusion crystals, suggesting a simila r crystal growth process despite the
different C
nuc.

Cooling crystallisation
When the KCl/H2O solution at the initial concentration of C0 is kept at
T0 = 20. 0°C, it is under-saturated. Then, the solution is cooled from its initial
temperature T0— without spatial temperature variation — to lower tem-
peratures to determine the metastable boundary under spatially isothermal
and homogeneous conditions. The unwrapped phase differenceΔΨ between
the upper and lower cell walls ismeasured as a function of timet when cooled
fromT0 to T = 15.0 °C and 14.5 °C, as shown in Fig.3a, b, respectively. When
the crystallisation cell was cooled to 15.0 °C (Supplementary Fig. 1a), similar
cooling rates were set for the upper and lower walls of the cell, 1.18/C14Cm i n/C01
and 1.21/C14Cm i n/C01, respectively. Theﬂuctuation of the absolute temperature

distinct crystalline phases. Crystal morphology may inﬂuence subsequent
growth kinetics, but it does not alter the saturation concentrationC

Methods
Sample preparation and storage
The experimental solution was galvanically prepared, mixing KCl (Ajax
Finechem, Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc, minimum assay 99.7%) and deionised
water (Paciﬁc TII 12, Thermo Scientiﬁc). The balance has an accuracy of
1 mg and a precision of 0.1 mg (ATX224, Shimadzu). The solution beaker
was stirred with a magnetic stirrer until all crystals were dissolved, and the
beaker was covered with Paraﬁlm in the process. After the solution is pre-
pared, it is stored in a reagent bottle with a blue-polypropylene pouring ring
and screw cap and an additional gasketto minimise evaporation. To avoid
the effect of thermal history, the reagent bottle containing a ready-made
solution is stored in a temperature-controlled room at 20.0 °C and held for at
least ten days before any reported experimental data.
Crystallisation cell
A crystallisation cell, shown in Fig.1, has separate control of the upper and
lower wall temperatures. A partial–integral–derivative (PID) temperature
control with consistent proportional,integral, and derivative gains was used
in all experiments with the same Peltier modules, PT100 thermistors, heat
sinks, and water cooling system. PID temperature control ensures experi-
mental repeatability. The cell is carefully sealed with a low-hardness (60
Shore A) silicone gasket that was moulded from DOWSIL Silicone 732. The
compression rate (compressed thickness over original thickness) was 50%.
The height of the crystallisation cell is controlled with a polyoxymethylene
(POM) pillar that was machined withcomputer numerical control (CNC)
tools to ensure its constant height and thus constant compression of the
gasket. Bubbles are present at some time at the lower boundary of the cell.
The compression of the soft gasket resulted in its irregular deformation, and
thus, when the solution wasﬁlled with the KCl, some bubbles were attached
to the gasket from the beginning of the experiment. This was not due to
evaporation, as the bubble size does not change throughout the experiment,
which can last for more thanﬁve hours. Two channels of diameter 1 mm are
drilled in the upper copper block of the crystallisation cell from two diagonal
corners. Whenﬁlling up the crystallisation cell for experiments, one channel
is used for solution injection, and the other allows air to exit. In diffusion
crystallisation experiment, one channel is used for the injection of the lower
concentration solution, while the other channel allows excess solution to exit
the isothermal cell in which diffusion crystallisation was observed. Two 21-
gauge needles (outer diameter 0.819 mm) are permanently glued to the
holes with epoxy. To avoid evaporation from the injection needles, after the
cell is ﬁlled with solution, a lint-free wipe (KimWipe) is used to remove
excessive solution in the luerﬁtting (white plastic) of the needles. Then, DI
water is carefully injected to ﬁll the translucent white plastic part of the
needle (Supplementary Fig. 5) without coming into contact with the
experimental solution. In experimental photos, bubbles are usually seen at
the top corners of the crystallisation cell, but this is because of excessive
suction of the KCl solution by the Ki mWipe instead of evaporation. Eva-
poration does not occur because the bubble size does not change during
hours in a thermally controlled experiment. Moreover, for custom-made
quartz sidewalls (Yixing Purshee Optical Elements Co., Ltd), although they
are chemically resistant and quartz glass powder was used for bonding, after
hours of experimentation, traces of salt powder can still be observed on the
seams from the outside (where the quartz walls are bonded). Thus, addi-
tional epoxy is carefully applied to cover the seams both inside and outside.
This makes the left and right edges of the cell opaque as seen in the pho-
tos (Fig. 3c).
