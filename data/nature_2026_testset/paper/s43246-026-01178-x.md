Hydrogen absorption in structural alloys can induce degradation through mechanisms that depend on
its transport, distribution, and interaction with microstructure. Yet, these processes have been
accessible only through indirect, bulk-averaged, or post-mortem measurements. Here, we present an
operando high-energy X-ray diffraction microscopy approach that enables the quanti ﬁcation of
hydrogen solubility and diffusivity from real-time lattice-strain evolution with micrometre-scale spatial
resolution. The method, demonstrated on a double precipitation-hardened martensitic steel, provides
spatiotemporal maps of hydrogen ingress across entire specimens, enabling simultaneous
determination of local concentration and transport kinetics from a single experiment. Complementary
electrochemical permeation measurements reveal analogous multi-stage transients, directly
visualising the transition from lattice-fast to trap-controlled regimes and validating the diffraction-
derived diffusivity hierarchy. The results show that hydrogen diffusivity is not a ﬁxed material property
but rather evolves during uptake. Precipitation-rich microstructures con ﬁne hydrogen near the
surface, whereas precipitation-free martensite permits rapid penetration, establishing hydrogen
transport as a state-dependent, time-variant process.
Hydrogen entering structural alloys from their environment, typically via
surface reactions during corrosion , cathodic charging, or hydrogen-
containing atmospheres, can induce degradation through complex inter-
actions with the lattice and micros tructure. Among these, hydrogen
embrittlement remains one of the most catastrophic failure modes in high-
strength steels, where unexpected cracking can arise even under moderate
service loads
1,2. Although alloy design has advanced considerably in reducing
this risk3–8, our understanding of hydrogen transport remains largely con-
strained by bulk-averaged or post-mortem measurements, which obscure
the heterogeneous trapping processes that govern diffusivity9–16,A sar e s u l t ,
hydrogen mobility is commonly treated as a single, time-independent dif-
fusivity constant, despite the complexspectrum of reversible and irreversible
trapping states that operate across multiple length and time scales.
The urgency of understanding hydrogen–metal interactions extends
far beyond steels. As the global t ransition toward green hydrogen
accelerates
17,18, materials that can safely store, transport, and utilise hydro-
gen are critical. Yet, the same element that fuels the energy transition also
limits it— hydrogen drastically reduces the ductility and toughness of strong
metals through embrittlement19–25, and corrosion-induced degradation26–29,
with the latter being often overlooked. This universal phenomenon has been
reported across structural alloys, from aluminium to copper to high-entropy
systems. For instance, it was shown that converting η to T-phase nano-
precipitates in aluminium alloys dramatically suppresses hydrogen-assisted
cracking by providing strong, energetically stable traps
30, in line with other
works reporting that precipitates are effective hydrogen-trapping sites30,31.
Similarly, it was demonstrated that h igh-strength copper alloys absorb
orders of magnitude less hydrogen than steels, exhibiting almost no
degradation under hydrogen exposure
32. Conversely, it was reported that a
CoNiV medium-entropy alloy achieves both high strength and hydrogen
resistance through low hydrogen diff usivity and deformation twinning,
which impede crack propagation8. Collectively, these studies highlight that
embrittlement resistance is amicrostructure-governedproperty rather than
an intrinsic material constant.
At the atomistic level, it was demonstrated that hydrogen can either
enhance local ductility or suppress d islocation emission at crack tips,
depending on concentration and stress state— providing predictive frame-
works for brittle-to-ductile tr ansitions in Fe-based systems 33,34,T h e s e
seminal works established that hydrogen alters mechanical stability not by a
single mechanism, but through the interplay of elastic, chemical, and kinetic
factors.

Two dominant frameworks capture this interplay: (i) hydrogen-
enhanced localised plasticity (HELP) and (ii) hydrogen-enhanced decohe-
sion (HEDE). In HELP, hydrogen accumulates at dislocation cores, screens
elastic ﬁelds, and lowers the Peierls barrier, thereby facilitating dislocation
glide
33,34, In HEDE, hydrogen segregation to regions of high triaxial stress,
such as grain boundaries or crack tips, reduces interatomic cohesion and
promotes intergranular fracture
35,36, Both are strongly governed by the
diffusion of hydrogen through the la ttice and its trapping at vacancies,
interfaces, and precipitates1,2,37, Recently, we proposed that phase separation
reactions represent a more fundamental precursor to both HELP and
HEDE, providing the thermodynamic driving force for hydrogen-induced
degradation
25.
Recent in situ microscopy has begun to bridge the gap between atomic
and microstructural scales23,38–45. Direct visualisation of hydrogen-driven
dislocation motion and strain-ﬁeld evolution in stainless steel revealed lat-
tice expansion, dislocation glide and climb, and elastic shielding— hallmarks
of the HELP mechanism46. These experiments provided theﬁrst real-time
evidence of hydrogen-induced changes in local stress and dislocation
dynamics. However, such nanoscale observations probe only sub-micron
regions and cannot capture hydrogen redistribution or trap ﬁlling across
bulk microstructures, where concentration and stress gradients coexist.
Lattice spacing can, in a broader sense, serve as an informative measurable
parameter when interpreted withinan appropriate physical framework
47.
Several techniques can probe hydrogen or hydrogen-associated defects
in metals, including thermal desorption spectroscopy (TDS), atom probe
tomography, transmission electron microscopy, and nano-SIMS. These
methods provide valuable information on hydrogen trapping, local chem-
istry, or defect structures, but are inherently ex situ, destructive, or spatially
limited, and therefore cannot capture hydrogen redistribution dynamically
in bulk materials. In contrast, opera ndo high-energy X-ray diffraction
(XRD) directly probes the lattice response to hydrogen in real time and non-
destructively, while providing micrometre-scale spatial resolution across
engineering-scale specimens. This combination uniquely enables the
simultaneous mapping of hydrogen uptake and transport kinetics under
realistic loading and environmental conditions.
Here, we address this gap by establishing an operando high-energy X-
ray diffraction microscopy method
23,48, quantifying hydrogen solubility and
diffusivity in real time with micrometre-scale resolution. By converting
lattice-strain ﬁelds into hydrogen concentration maps through ab initio
calibration, we directly visualise hydrogen ingress across entire specimens.
These measurements are correlated with electrochemical hydrogen-
permeation tests and thermal-desorption spectroscopy, providing a con-
sistent framework to interpret hydrogen uptake, transport, and trapping
across scales. Using Hybrid steel
49 as a model system, we examine how
microstructure governs hydrogen dist ribution and transport behaviour.
This approach provides a basis for res olving hydrogen transport beyond
bulk-averaged descriptions and for linking local hydrogen distribution to
embrittlement-relevant processes.
Results and discussion
Real-time mapping of hydrogen transport
Our operando synchrotron XRD microscopy enables real-time mapping of
hydrogen as it moves through steel microstructures. Hydrogen absorption
produces an essentially isotropic volumetric expansion of the martensitic
(body-centred cubic (BCC)/body-centredtetragonal) lattice, detectable as a
uniform shift in the strongest diffraction peaks. Averaging over the three
most intense reﬂections, we tracked the evolution of mean lattice strain with
both depth and time. Calibrated against density-functional theory (DFT)
calculation, these strain ﬁelds were converted into local hydrogen con-
centrations, yielding spatiotemporalmaps of ingress across the specimen
thickness (Fig. 1a–c). The measured peak shifts quantify the hydrogen-
induced lattice dilation during charging. In a coupled diffusion–mechanics
problem, this dilation includes the chemical (eigenstrain) contribution and
any constraint-induced elastic component arising from hydrogen con-
centration gradients. We therefore interpret the strain-derived hydrogen
concentration as an effective, hydrog en-coupled proxy for local uptake,
rather than attempting to strictly separate chemical and mechanical
contributions.
In principle, hydrogen–lattice interactions may also reduce local strain
(i.e. microstrain), for e xample, through defect annihilation or stress
relaxation, as suggested by theoretical studies
50,51, From a statistical per-
spective, hydrogen uptake increases the conﬁgurational entropy of the lat-
tice, since each hydrogen atom const itutes at least a point defect and
substantially increases the number of accessible defect conﬁgurations. As a
result, defect-mediated lattice chan ges are intrinsically governed by the
evolving defect population. In the present experiments, however, the
dominant contribution to the measured lattice response arises from dis-
solved, mobile hydrogen, producing an elastic lattice dilation, which governs
transport and redistribution duringcharging (see Supplementary Note 1 for
the effect of hydrogen on microstrain).
The spatiotemporal lattice strain maps immediately reveal a striking
microstructural contrast. In the nano-precipitation-free martensite, which is
the solution-annealed condition, hy drogen penetrates deeply and dis-
tributes nearly homogeneously across the gauge section. In comparison, in
the nano-precipitation-rich microstructure (tempered condition), hydro-
gen ingress is conﬁned to near-surface regions, where microstructural fea-
tures act as both reversible and irreversible traps. This con ﬁnement
produces a progressively decaying penetration pro ﬁle that persists
throughout the charging duration (Fig.1d, e).

The XRD-derived concentrations represent the strain-coupled, soluble
hydrogen in martensite, primarily associated with hydrogen occupying
interstitial sites and with hydrogen residing in shallow (reversible) or deep
(irreversible) traps that impose measurable elastic strain on the lattice. These
include hydrogen located at certain mi crostructural defects, where local
lattice distortions contribute to a de tectable change in the average lattice
parameter. Hydrogen that does not produce measurable lattice strain, that
induces strain below the detection limit of the diffraction experiment
(~10
−5), or that results only in heterogen eous strain (manifested as peak
widening rather than peak shift), is not captured. The latter is caused when
hydrogen resides at phase boundariesor martensite lath interfaces, which
are sites of structural free volume.
TDS, by comparison, quantiﬁes only the hydrogen that is thermally
released from lattice sites and traps within the tested temperature range,
independent of whether the associated hydrogen imposes strain on the
lattice. Highly mobile interstitial hydrogen may partially escape before the
experiment due to rapid diffusion at room temperature, whereas hydrogen
strongly bound to deep traps may remain undetected if its release tem-
perature exceeds the experimental range.
Hence, neither technique measures the total hydrogen content. Rather,
XRD and TDS probe different hydrogen populations and, therefore, com-
plement each other. Our in-situ operando XRD microscopy approach is
particularly sensitive to hydrogen populations that remain in lattice sites and
contribute to measurable lattice strain (chemical expansion), whereas TDS
primarily characterises thermally releasable hydrogen associated with lattice
sites and trapping defects.
In principle, at steady-state hydrogen permeation, the precipitation-
rich microstructure should ultimately dissolve more hydrogen than the
precipitation-free one, as the numerous trap –matrix interfaces provide
additional binding sites. However, this extended uptake is not captured here
because the operando measurement was stopped before complete trap
saturation. The apparent lower concentration in XRD thus reﬂects a kinetic
limitation rather than an intrinsic solubility deﬁcit. Longer charging times
would likely reveal a progressiveﬁlling of trap populations and a delayed rise
in the measured lattice strain.
This comparison highlights the complementary sensitivity of the two
techniques. Operando XRD reveals the dominant, strain-coupled soluble
reservoir that controls diffusion and transient lattice expansion, whereas
TDS isolates the deeply trapped population responsible for long-term
retention. Although the trapped fraction is quantitatively minor (during the
measured period), it plays a decisive role in shaping hydrogen distribution by
throttling ingress and acting as a persistent sink near carbide-rich interfaces.
Together, the XRD and TDS results demonstrate that hydrogen transport in
steel is governed not by the total hydrogen content, but by the evolving
balance between soluble, shallow-trapped, and deep-trapped populations.

Operando diffusivity mapping as a framework for alloy design
The operando XRD methodology established here transforms the quanti-
ﬁcation of hydrogen transport in mater ials. Instead of inferring effective
diffusivities from permeation currents or desorption peaks, we measure
them directly and resolve them spatia lly via lattice-strain mapping cali-
brated by DFT. This capability reveals dynamic diffusivity regimes that
evolve as trapsﬁll, regimes that classical methods can only suggest in bulk-
averaged form. Our results show that nanoscale precipitations provide
effective trap sites, reducing hydrogen uptake and diffusion, conﬁning it to
the near-surface, and retarding bulk penetration. This conﬁnement effec-
tively delays hydrogen embrittlement.
Compared with electrochemical permeation and thermal desorption,
which yield only bulk-averaged diffusion coefﬁcients or indirect trap sig-
natures, operando XRD directly resolves the hierarchy of hydrogen trans-
port regimes in real time. The approach is broadly applicable. Beyond steels,
it can be extended to dual-phase alloys, hydride-forming superalloys, and
refractory metals such as tungsten, where hydrogen embrittlement is equally
critical. It can also be applied to hydrogen-storage materials to quantify
absorption and desorption kinetics under operando conditions.
While the present study focuses on BCC martensitic steels, the oper-
ando XRD framework is not intrinsically limited to fast-diffusing systems.
I nm a t e r i a l sw i t hs l o w e rh y d r o g e nk i n e tics, such as austenitic steels or high-
entropy alloys, the same approach can be applied by adapting the experi-
mental time window, offering a route to directly probe transport and
trapping in regimes where bulk-averaged methods are particularly limited.
The operando XRD methodology is not restricted to hydrogen. In
principle, any process that produces a measurable lattice strain or diffraction
signature can be tracked, including interstitial diffusion, substitutional dif-
fusion, and phase transformations such as hydride formation. While phase-
forming systems (e.g. metal hydrides) introduce additional complexity in
interpreting diffusivity due to multi-phase transport, the diffraction-based
measurement principle remains valid. Practical limitations for species such
as oxygen or nitrogen arise primarilyfrom experimental constraints (tem-
perature, atmosphere control, and kinetics), rather than from the method
itself.
Conclusions
We demonstrate that operando high-energy X-ray diffraction microscopy
enables direct, real-time, spatially resolved mapping of hydrogen transport
in steel. By linking lattice strain to hydrogen concentration, the method
provides simultaneous access to local quantitative hydrogen concentration
and transport kinetics within a single experiment.
The measurements reveal that hyd rogen diffusion is not a constant
coefﬁcient but rather evolves as hydrogen redistributes within the micro-
structure. Nanoscale precipitation-rich microstructures conﬁne hydrogen
near the surface and retard penetrati on, whereas precipitation-free mar-
tensite permits rapid transport.
Theseﬁndings shift the description of hydrogen transport from a bulk-
averaged parameter to a spatially and temporally resolved process. More
broadly, the ability to directly visualise hydrogen in space and time provides
a framework for linking local hydro gen distribution to embrittlement-
relevant processes and for guiding microstructure design strategies.

The DFT calculations are employed exclusively to establish an effective
elastic calibration between interstitial hydrogen content and lattice strain.
They are not intended to statistically represent all local chemical environ-
ments, hydrogen-trapping conﬁgurations, or microstructural defects, such
as dislocations or grain boundaries, present in the alloy. Owing to the
periodic nature of DFT, nominal hydrogen concentrations are necessarily
elevated even for comparatively large supercells; increasing the supercell size
reduces the hydrogen concentration only marginally while incurring pro-
hibitive computational cost. Hydrogenconcentrations are therefore delib-
erately chosen high enough to generat e a clear lattice strain signal above
numerical noise. Importantly, the calculated strain –concentration rela-
tionship remains strictly linear over the calibration range, with an intercept
effectively at zero, indicating that artiﬁcial H–H interactions do not inﬂu-
ence the extracted elastic coupling. This Vegard-like behaviour indicates
that hydrogen behaves as a dilute elastic solute within the investigated
concentration range.
The use of lattice strain as a proxy for hydrogen concentration is
supported by extensive experimental and theoretical studies on hydrogen in
BCC iron and low-alloy steels, which have shown that interstitial hydrogen
induces an approximately linear la ttice expansion and that the corre-
sponding elastic response is only weakly sensitive to local alloying chemistry
at low to moderate hydrogen contents
59,60, Because the relationship between
lattice strain and hydrogen concentration in BCC Fe is well established in the
literature
59–61, the present work does not repeat benchmark measurements
on pure iron. Instead, we apply this established framework to quantify
hydrogen transport in the chemically and microstructurally complex
Hybrid steel studied here.
While chemical disorder broadens the distribution of local site ener-
gies, the average elastic coupling governing diffraction peak shifts remains
robust. Given that operando XRD measurements average lattice strain over
diffraction volumes containing millionsof crystallites, this effective, scale-
bridging elastic calibration is appropriate for converting measured lattice
strain into hydrogen concentration, without implying a complete atomistic
description of hydroge n trapping or transport in the complex alloy
microstructure. In the dilute limit, interstitial hydrogen acts as a point defect
that produces a local elastic dilatation of the BCC lattice (chemical expan-
sion). The macroscopic lattice strain istherefore the linear superposition of
the elasticﬁelds of individual hydrogen defects. In the absence of hydrogen,
no such defects are present, and the hydrogen-induced strain must be
exactly zero. The strain–hydrogen calibration is thus physically constrained
by linear elasticity to pass through the origin (zero strain at zero hydrogen
concentration). Any non-zero intercept would imply hydrogen-induced
strain in a hydrogen-free lattice and is therefore unphysical.
