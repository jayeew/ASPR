SMT-738 is a small molecule with promising antibacterial activity against
Enterobacteriaceae, including multi-drug-resistantEscherichia coli.M u t a t i o n s
in genes encoding the lipoprotein transport complex (LolCDE) confer resis-
tance to SMT-738. Here, we report the cryogenic electron microscopy struc-
ture of the LolCDE-SMT-738 complex at a high resolution. We use mutagenesis,
drug resistance assays, biochemical assays, and molecular dynamics simula-
tions to show that SMT-738 binds to the periplasmic end of the LolCE trans-
membrane domains. This binding inducesan allosteric conformational change
in the cytoplasmic end of the LolCE transmembrane domains and the coupling
helices, leading to dissociation of one LolD subunit from the LolCDE complex.
This structural disruption results in a “deadlock” of the LolCDE complex,
rendering a transport-incompetent state. Ourﬁndings also provide insights
into the mechanism of SMT-738 resistance and selectivity.
The rapid spread of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a consider-
able threat to public health worldwide1. Particularly, infections caused
by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria have become increas-
ingly difﬁcult to treat. There is an urgent need to develop novel anti-
microbials that can effectively combat drug-resistant Gram-negative
bacteria
2–4.T h ei d e n t iﬁcation of effective antimicrobial targets is
essential for the development of new antibiotics 5. Gram-negative
bacteria are protected by a unique outer membrane (OM), which acts
as a critical barrier against environmental stressors, including anti-
biotics. Interfering with OM biogenesis has emerged as a promising
strategy for developing antimicrobial agents against Gram-negative
bacteria
6–9.
Bacterial OM biogenesis is a sophisticated biological process
involving many protein machineries. The OM β-barrel assembly
machinery (BAM) consists of the OM protein BamA and four
accessory lipoproteins, BamB, BamC, BamD, and BamE, which
play essential roles in OM protein insertion
10–14. The lipopoly-
saccharide (LPS) transport nanomachinery LptB 2FGCDEA forms a
transenvelope complex comprising the OM protein LptD and the
lipoprotein LptE, the periplasmic protein LptA, and the inner
membrane (IM) ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter
LptB
2FGC. This system is responsible for transporting LPS from
the IM to the OM and assembling it into the outer lea ﬂet of the
OM15–24.

To date, several compounds that target BamA or LptB 2FGC,
including darobactin and zosurabalpin, have been identiﬁed6,9.T h e s e
two compounds are currently undergoing clinical trials. Their
mechanisms of action have been elucidated through structural and
genetic studies. Darobactin binds to the lateral gate of BamA,
mimicking the recognition signal of BAM substrates
8,25–27. Zosurabalpin
targets LptF, trapping LPS within its intramembrane transporter7.
A third essential component of the OM is lipoproteins, which fulﬁll
diverse functions, such as OM integrity, OM biogenesis, nutrient
uptake, and antibiotic resistance mechanisms. Lipoprotein trafﬁcking
from the IM to the OM is orchestrated by the conserved Lol (locali-
zation of lipoproteins) pathway, which is formed by the LolABCDE
complex
28–30. ABC transporter LolCDE extracts lipoproteins from the
IM and transfers them to the chaperone LolA, which shuttles them
across the periplasmic space. In the OM, the receptor LolB, itself a
lipoprotein, anchors lipoproteins through lipid insertion (Fig.1a)
31–33.
Targeting the LolABCDE pathway represents a promising ther-
apeutic strategy that is a clinically unexploited target. Several com-
pounds, such as lolamicin
34,35, abaucin 36,37,G 0 5 0 738,a n dS M T - 7 3 8
(Fig. 1c)39, have been identi ﬁed as inhibitors of the OM lipoprotein
transporter of Gram-negative bacteria via a genetic approach. SMT-738
has potent antimicrobial activity in vitro and in vivo against Enter-
obacteriaceae bacteria, including multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli
NCTC 13441 and Klebsiella pneumoniae NCTC 13438, and promising
druggable properties, which is now at the preclinical stage
39. Resis-
tance to SMT-738 is mediated by mutations in LolCDE. Importantly,
SMT-738 is a microbiome-sparing agent that spares most gut
microbes39. Although the pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacody-
namics (PD) of SMT-738 have been studied, it remains unknown
how SMT-738 impairs the function of LolCDE, being the ﬁrst in this
class of antibiotics, and why this drug possesses selectivity. Com-
plemented by cryo-EM structure analysis, mutagenesis studies, in vivo
lipoprotein transport assays, drug resistance assays, biochemical
assays, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, our work revealed a
novel antimicrobial mechanism that involves the formation of an
unprecedented allosteric dead-lock LolCDE in complex with SMT-738,
which provides a novel platform for antimicrobial therapeutic
development.

SMT-738 binds to LolCDE
To determine whether LolCDE binds SMT-738, LolCDE incubated with
SMT-738 was copuriﬁed (Methods) (Supplementary Fig. 1a), and the
cryo-EM structure of LolCDE in complex with SMT-738 was determined
to have a high resolution (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 2a–ga n dS u p -
plementary Table 1). The structure reveals that SMT-738 is bound
between the regions of LolC TM1 and TM2 and LolE TM1 and TM2 at
the periplasmic end (Fig. 2b). LolC residues (L55, E195, K253, E255,
L256, A259, V260 and E263) and LolE residues (I59, V63, Y258, M261,
D264, I265, I268, and F367) were found to interact extensively with
SMT-738 via hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions (Fig.2c).
To con ﬁrm the importance of speci ﬁc residues for SMT-738
binding, we constructed a series of plasmids encoding wild-type (WT)
LolCDE or its variants in LolC or LolE. These plasmids were trans-
formed into the HD200313 strain, and the sensitivity of the trans-
formed cells to SMT-738 was assessed. The minimal inhibitory
concentration (MIC) of SMT-738 against strains expressing WT LolCDE
was 0.25 μg/ml (Fig. 2d). The resistance mutations identiﬁed in LolC
and LolE included LolC_E195R, LolC_E255A, LolC_E255R, LolC_L256D,
LolC_L256R, LolC_A259D, LolE_I59D, LolE_M261E, LolE_M261R,
LolE_D264A, LolE_D264K, and LolE_F367A (Fig.2d and Supplementary
Fig. 5b, c), indicating that these residues are critical for SMT-738
binding. To further verify these results, the two most prominent SMT-
738-resistant mutations (LolE_M261R and LolE_D264A) were selected
to assess their lipoprotein transport functions in the presence of SMT-
738. The results revealed that BamE release in these two resistant
mutants was not inhibited by SMT-738 (Fig. 2e and Supplementary
Fig. 3b, c). Additionally, laboratory-selected SMT-738-resistant strains
exhibited mutations in LolC_L256R and LolE_D264A of E. coli
39.T h e s e
data validated our structural ﬁndings. Moreover, several hypersensi-
tive and non-phenotypic mutations were also identiﬁed in LolC or LolE
(Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 5b, c).

SMT-738 allosterically deprives a LolD subunit from LolCDE
The LolCDE-SMT-738 structure shows that only one subunit of LolD is
present. This subunit, LolD, remains bound to LolE, but the interaction
between the LolD and LolC subunits is lost. To further con ﬁrm that
SMT-738 causes LolD to dissociate from the LolCDE complex, we
performed SDS-PAGE or blue native-PAGE electrophoresis to assess
the amount and assembly of LolD in puriﬁed LolCDE and the LolCDE-
SMT-738 complexes. The electrophoresis results showed no signiﬁcant
difference in the band intensity of LolCE between these two puri ﬁed
complexes on SDS-PAGE gel (Supplementary Fig. 1b). However, the
band intensity of LolD in the LolCDE-SMT-738 complex was sig-
niﬁcantly weaker than that in the LolCDE sample (Supplementary
Fig. 1b). Additionally, it was revealed that the LolCDE band appeared as
a smear and migrated signi ﬁcantly slower than the LolCDE-SMT-738
complex on blue native-PAGE gel, which indicates LolCDE has a greater
molecular weight than the LolCDE-SMT-738 complex. These ﬁndings
were further supported by western blot analysis (Fig.4a). Collectively,
the binding of SMT-738 indeed results in the dissociation of one LolD
subunit from the LolCDE complex.

Given this similarity, we conducted a detailed comparison
between the LolCDE-SMT-738 structure and the LolCDE-ADP ortho-
vanadate structure. The SMT-738 binding sites, including LolC resi-
dues (E195, E255, L256, A259) and LolE residues (I59, M261, D264, and
F367), display minor conformational changes to facilitate drug binding
(Fig. 4b). LolE exhibits slight changes in its TM2 and TM3 regions at the
cytoplasmic site, shifting its coupling helix approximately 5.5 Å toward
LolC (Fig. 4c). The LolD subunit coupled with LolE moves approxi-
mately 5.2 Å toward LolC (Fig.4c), causing a clash between LolD bound
to LolE in the LolCDE-SMT-738 structure and LolD bound to LolC in the
LolCDE-ADP-vanadate structure (Fig.4d). In contrast, the cytoplasmic
domain of LolC undergoes more pronounced changes, displacing its
coupling helix approximately 11.4 Å toward LolE (Fig. 4e). This shift
triggers a severe LolD–LolD subunit clash, prompting the dissociation
of one LolD subunit. To biochemically con ﬁrm LolD loss in LolCDE-
SMT-738, we performed an ATPase assay via a commercial kit (Meth-
ods). The results demonstrated a signi ﬁcant reduction in ATPase
activity (Fig.4f), indicating impaired complex functionality due to LolD
dissociation.

LolCDE structure (PDB code: 7ARH), excluding lipoprotein, as the
starting model. The bilayer membrane was constructed via a mem-
brane builder on the CHARMM-GUI website
40. The LolCDE complex
was embedded into the bilayer membrane environment, generating
the initial conformation of the LolCDE-membrane system. SMT-738
was initially positioned at the entrance of the LolCDE transporter. The
simulation setup involved energy minimization via GROMACS
(v2023.3), followed by system equilibration in a water box within an
orthogonal unit cell. The simulation temperature was set to 303.15 K.

Discussion
SMT-738 represents the ﬁrst member of a novel class of small mole-
cules that target the LolCDE complex in carbapenem-resistant Enter-
obacteriaceae (CREs). Our research con ﬁrms its ability to inhibit
lipoprotein transport and provides the ﬁrst structural insights into
LolCDE-SMT-738. In this study, we initially attempted to prepare a
cryo-EM sample by incubating the puriﬁed LolCDE protein with a high
concentration of SMT-738 in vitro. However, the resulting cryo-EM
structure revealed clear density for a LolCDE substrate but lacked any
density for SMT-738. We therefore hypothesize that SMT-738 cannot
bind to LolCDE when it is in the substrate-bound state. Instead, access
may occur only after ATP hydrolysis drives lipoprotein release, thereby
permitting SMT-738 entry. SMT-738 binds the periplasmic ends of the
LolC and LolE transmembrane domains, inducing allosteric con-
formational changes in the cytoplasmic domains of both LolC and
LolE. Notably, while the LolE coupling helix undergoes minor struc-
tural adjustments, the LolC coupling helix exhibits signi ﬁcant con-
formational shifts. These changes lead to severe steric clash of the
dimeric LolD structure, leading to dissociation of one LolD subunit
from LolC (Supplementary Fig. 9). This study highlights the ﬁrst
instance of a drug interacting with an ABC transporter to form a dead-
lock structure, highlighting a novel mechanism of action.
Using MD simulations and biochemical assays, we have performed
the modeling of the drug binding in which the LolD subunit is lost from
LolC (Supplementary Fig. 8). SMT-738 binding signi ﬁcantly reduced
LolCDE ATPase activity (Fig. 4f). The structure of LolCDE bound to
SMT-738 is more like that of the LolCDE-ADP vanadate structure,
suggesting that the drug binds in t h eL o l C D E - A T Pb i n d i n gs t a t ea n d
that drug binding prevents lipoprotein binding.
To validate our structural ﬁndings, mutagenesis and MIC assays
were used to validate key binding residues. The results revealed that
the LolC_E255R, LolC_L256R, LolE_I59D, LolE_M261R, and LolE_D264A
mutants conferred resistance, conﬁrming our structural ﬁndings. MD
simulations revealed that the RMSF of these SMT-738-resistant
mutants signi ﬁcantly changed, potentially hindering drug binding
and contributing to drug resistance. Consistently, SMT-738 failed to
inhibit BamE release in the LolE_M261R and LolE_D264A
mutants (Fig. 2e).
SMT-738 exhibits selectivity, effectively killing CREs while sparing
gut Gram-negative bacteria. Sequence alignment showed the variants
at the position corresponding to the E. coli LolE_D264 residue (a key
residue for SMT-738 resistance ofE. coli) in these gut commensals and
several other SMT-738-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. Our muta-
genesis and MIC assays support a hypothesis that the residue at
position LolE_D264 is a key determinant of SMT-738 selectivity across
diverse bacterial species. Moreover, this residue is conserved in H.
inﬂuenzae and H. parainﬂuenzae (non-Enterobacteriaceaemembers),
against which SMT-738 also shows potent efﬁcacy, further supporting
its hypothesis. Interestingly, lolamicin, a structurally similar LolCDE
inhibitor, also spares the gut microbiome
34. Its resistance mutations in
E. coli partially overlap SMT-738 binding sites. Our ﬁndings revealed
that lolamicin only displayed slightly higher MIC values againstE. coli
harboring LolE_D264A, D264K, and D264I substitutions compared to
the wild-type LolE, which is strikingly different from SMT-738, sug-
gesting distinct modes of action or selectivity against LolCDE between
the two compounds. In summary, our work revealed a novel
mechanism of action for a new family of antibiotics, which has a pro-
found impact on the development of novel therapeutic strategies
against drug-resistant bacteria.

Image processing
A total of 8329 micrographs were acquired for the LolCDE-SMT-738
complex, with motion correction andcontrast transfer function(CTF)
performed in CryoSPARC (v4.1). Template-free particle picking gen-
erated 16,201,931 initial particle extracts, which underwent iterative 2D
classiﬁcation in CryoSPARC (v4.1). Following three rounds of particle
subset selection through 2D averaging, 758,738 high-ﬁdelity particles
were subjected to Ab-initio reconstruction, yielding three distinct
classes. Heterogeneous reﬁnement identiﬁed a dominant class con-
taining 403,215 particles, which were re ﬁned using non-uniform
reﬁnement to generate a consensus map. Subsequently, a mask is
generated using local reﬁnement to produce a locally high-resolution
focused map. Finally, the consensus map and the focused map are
merged to generate the ﬁnal composite map.

In silico modeling and MD simulations
The LolCDE structure (PDB code: 7ARH) with lipoprotein excluded was
used as the starting model. The membrane employed in the simula-
tions mainly consists of POPE, the primary component of the E. coli
membrane, constituting 80% of its composition. The other parameters
were defaults. Using CHARMM-GUI
40, we constructed the bilayer
membrane, selected “Align the First Principal Axis Along Z ” and “Flip
Molecule along the Z axis ” to orient the LolCDE cryo-EM structure
relative to the membrane, and embedded the LolCDE complex into the
bilayer to generate the initial conformation of the LolCDE-membrane
system.
To simulate the conformational changes of LolCDE during
SMT-738 binding, we initially positioned SMT-738 at the external
entrance of the LolCDE transporter. We then used GROMACS
(v2023.3) for energy minimization. The simulation system is placed
in a water box of orthogonal unit cells, utilizing the TIP3P water
model. The equilibrium temperature is maintained at 303.15 K via a
velocity-rescale (v-rescale) thermostat (coupling time constant:
1.0 ps; the whole system is treated as a single temperature group).
The pressure is maintained at 1 bar via semi-isotropic pressure
coupling (Berendsen method; time constant: 5.0 ps; allowing
membrane thickness changes). Finally, the rigidity of the whole
system is constrained by hydrogen bonds, and the LINCS algorithm
is used to remove the centroid motion every 100 steps in linear
mode. We performed MD simulations for durations of 100 or
300 ns, recording one frame every 150 ps for subsequent analysis.
Each simulation was independently repeated three times.
To analyse the conformational changes in the ﬁve resistant
LolCDE variants, the LolCDE structure (PDB code: 7ARH) was used
as a reference. We modeled the structures of LolCDE variants using
Discovery Studio (v2019) software and then simulated these pre-
dicted apo-LolCDE variants via the methods and parameters
described above. Each simulation was independently repeated
three times.
