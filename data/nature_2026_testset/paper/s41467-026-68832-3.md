Viral diseases critically impact crop yields, necessitating insights into plant-
pathogen recognition and resistance. In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum),
tomato chlorosis virus (ToCV) employs 59 kDa protein (p59) to facilitate
movement and degrade the host Catalase1 (SlCAT1), disrupting redox home-
ostasis. Importantly, tomato has evolved an antiviral ubiquitin-proteasome
system (UPS) featuring theAntiviral E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (SlAVE2)
and Antiviral E3 ubiquitin ligase (SlAVE3) pair, which speciﬁcally recognizes
p59 to trigger antiviral defenses. During infection, this system ubiquitinates
SlWRKY6, alleviating its repression ofSlAVE3 and creating a positive feedback
loop that enhances SlAVE3 expression. SlAVE3 then targets p59 for ubiquiti-
nation and degradation, inhibiting viral spread. Notably, p59 co-opts this UPS
to degrade SlCAT1, illustrating a counter-defense interplay. Domestication
analysis reveals selection of AVE3 from S. pimpinellifoliumto S. lycopersicum,
with SpAVE3 conferring stronger ToCV resistance than SlAVE3 through
improved p59 targeting and reduced SlCAT1 degradation. This study unveils a
domesticated antiviral UPS, providing a foundation for breeding ToCV-
resistant tomatoes.

Plants employ diverse defence mechanisms against viruses,
including RNA silencing, immune receptor/sensor signaling, protein
degradation, and defence mediated by changes of redox, hormones,
and metabolism
13. RNA silencing is a major antiviral pathway, often
counteracted by co-evolving viral suppressors to enhance pathogeni-
city in susceptible hosts
14. A sophisticated two-level innate immune
system underpins plant antiviral defence15.T h e ﬁrst level, pathogen-
associated molecular pattern (PAMP)-triggered immunity (PTI),
involves surface-localized pattern recognition receptors detecting
PAMPs
16. Proteins like BRI1-associated kinase 1 (BAK1) and nuclear
shuttle protein (NSP)-interacting kinase 1 (NIK1) initiate PTI for virus
resistance
17–19. The second level, effector-triggered immunity (ETI),
relies on intracellular resistance (R) proteins that recognize pathogen
effectors, triggering antiviral defence16.T h e ﬁrst identiﬁed R protein,
conferring resistance to Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) in Nicotiana
benthamiana,i se n c o d e db y N gene20. Subsequently, numerous R
genes (Sw-5, Rx1/2, RTM1/2, RCY1, I,e ta l . )h a v eb e e nc l o n e da n du t i l i z e d
in breeding21. The plant-virus arms race hinges on reciprocal recogni-
tion: the host activates immunity upon detection, and the pathogen
launches virulence. Understanding these opposing recognition
mechanisms remains a fundamental goal in the ﬁeld.
In addition to innate immune system, another antiviral pathway
that can precisely identify viral proteins is the protein degradation
mediated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and selective
autophagy pathways
22,23. The ubiquitination process involves a three-
step enzymatic cascade mediated by E1 ubiquitin activating enzyme
(E1), E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2), and E3 ubiquitin ligase (E3).
Substrate proteins can undergo mono-monoubiquitination (single
ubiquitin at one site), multi-monoubiquitination (single ubiquitin at
multiple sites), or polyubiquitination (ubiquitin chains at a single
site)
24. Monoubiquitination and multi-monoubiquitination typically
modulate protein interactions, activation, or localization, whereas
polyubiquitination often targets substrates for degradation via the
UPS
24. E3 determines substrate speci ﬁcity and ubiquitination type 24.
Structurally, the E3 family is classiﬁed into four groups: three mono-
meric families (HECT, U-box, RING) and one multimeric family
(CRLs)
24. Tomato genome (SL4.0) contains approximately 1500–2100
E3s (5–6% of its total gene content), which is comparable in scale to
Arabidopsis (~1400) and rice (~1300). In plant-virus interactions, the
UPS performs the dual functions of viral immune suppression and host
antiviral defense
25,26. Virus can hijack UPS and UPS-related proteins to
disrupt the host immunity 27–29, preventing degradation by virus-
encoded deubiquitinating enzymes30, and SUMOylation of viral repli-
cation proteins 31. The UPS also plays key roles in plant antiviral
defense, including mediating N gene resistance against TMV and
directly degrading several viral proteins 22,32. Additionally, the UPS
activates other antiviral responses by recognizing viral proteins such
as Rice stripe virus CP via a rice E3
33. Understanding how plant UPS
components recognize viral proteins and transduce immune signals is
key, as it underpins the mechanisms of intracellular viral recognition
and host defense.
Here, we characterized a previously unstudied ToCV 59 kDa
protein (p59), which functions in virion assembly, cell-to-cell move-
ment, and pathogenicity. The p59 is targeted for degradation by
tomato’s antiviral UPS, yet paradoxically exploits this system to
suppress host catalase activity and generate pathogenicity-
promoting oxidative stress. Notably, the E3 of this UPS underwent
selection during tomato domestication, with beneﬁcial variants from
wild tomato germplasm enhancing ToCV resistance. These results
demonstrate the evolutionary arms race between plants and patho-
gens, providing genetic resources for utilizing natural variation in
biotech breeding.

Closteroviridae genomes encode a conserved ﬁve-gene module,

we constructed ToCV mutants lackingp6 (ToCV-Δp6), Hsp70h (ToCV-
ΔHsp70h), and p59 (ToCV-Δp59) (Supplementary Fig. 1b), and eval-
uated their pathogenicity. Here, we adopted a uniﬁed set of criteria to
comprehensively assess host resistance and viral pathogenicity,
including disease symptoms, disease incidence, disease index, CP
transcript, and CP abundance. Inoculation of tomato plants with wild-
type (WT) ToCV or the mutant variants showed that ToCV-Δp6,T o C V -
ΔHsp70h,a n dT o C V -Δp59 all exhibited attenuated pathogenicity
compared with the WT virus (Supplementary Fig. 1c–g). Among these,

assembly (Fig. 1aa n db ) .W et h e ne x p r e s s e dp59 in tomato (PVX-p59).

The ability of a viral protein to suppress RNA silencing is a major
determinant of infection severity, and most plant viruses encode at
least one such suppressor. Despite this prevalence, p59 deviates from
this pattern and does not function as an RNA silencing inhibitor
(Supplementary Fig 2). Subcellular localization of viral pathogenic
factors frequently in ﬂuences viral functionality
10. To determine the
localization of p59, a p59-GFP fusion protein was expressed in N.
benthamiana for ﬂuorescence observation. NaCl-induced plasmolysis
revealed that p59-GFP localizes to the cytoplasm and plasma mem-
brane, exhibiting a punctate distribution pattern (Supplementary
Fig. 3a). Given this pattern, we hypothesized potential localization to
plasmodesmata (PD). Co-expression of p59-GFP with the PD marker
TMV MP-RFP
35 conﬁrmed ﬂuorescent co-localization, demonstrating
PD targeting by p59-GFP (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Fig. 3a). As viruses
commonly disturb callose deposition and utilize PD for cell-to-cell
movement
10,36, we investigated whether p59 modulates PD pore size
and callose deposition. Upon ToCV infection, the dilation of PD aper-
tures signiﬁcantly increases as callose deposition decreases; however,
mutation of p59 impair the effect of ToCV on suppressing callose
deposition and dilating PD apertures (Fig. 1g and h; Supplementary
Fig. 3b–d). Plant viral movement proteins target PD and facilitate rapid
cytoplasmic transport to mediate intercellular viral spread
37.I nt h e
cell-to-cell movement assay, free GFP (negative control) was primarily
conﬁned to one or two cells, whereas p59-GFP spread more broadly,
reaching two or more cells, similar to the TMV MP-RFP positive control
(Fig. 1i and j; Supplementary Fig. 4a). To con ﬁrm p59 is a movement
protein, we tested its ability to complement the cell-to-cell movement
deﬁciency in aTurnip mosaic virusmutant (TuMV-GFP-P3N-PIPO-m1)
35.
p59 functionally complemented the movement-deﬁcient phenotype,
mimicking the positive control P3N-PIPO by restoring expanded
intercellular GFP distribution (Supplementary Fig. 4b and c). Thus, p59
functions as a viral movement protein.

action, MAPK signaling pathway, and plant hormone signal
transduction (Supplementary Fig. 12e). These ﬁndings demonstrate
that the transcriptional regulatory function of SlWRKY6 is consistent
with its observed disease-resistant phenotype.

Modern cultivated big-fruited tomato (S. lycopersicum var. lycopersi-

pimpinellifolium (Sp)t oc h e r r yt o m a t o(S. lycopersicum var. cer-

During plant-virus interactions, ROS play a dual role, restricting
pathogens through localized cell death at infection sites and acting as a
diffusible signal to activate antioxidant and defense responses in
adjacent cells
47. The effectiveness of these defenses depends on the
host’s response speed. Rapid ROS accumulation can eliminate viruses
early without symptoms (extreme resistance), while slower responses
allow limited viral replication, leading to oxidative stress and pro-
grammed cell death. Delayed defenses disrupt antioxidative balance,
causing systemic stress (e.g., chlorosis or necrosis) and eventual loss of
viral control (compatible infection). In studies of interactions between
animal and plant viruses with their hosts, host ROS bursts have been
observed as a notable characteristic
48,49. As the key enzyme in mito-
chondria responsible for scavenging H2O2, CAT plays a critical role in
maintaining cellular redox balance. Alterations in CAT activity are thus
expected to disrupt the redox homeostasis of host cells. However, the
regulatory mechanisms of ROS in tomato-ToCV interactions and their
compatibility relationship remain unexplored. H
2O2 accumulation in
tomato leaves positively correlates with disease severity, indicating
compatibility in the tomato-ToCV interaction during oxidative stress.
This suggests ToCV disrupts host redox homeostasis as a pathogenic
strategy. Although we previously reported that ToCV p27 can interact
with CAT and inhibit its activity, the mechanism has not been fully
elucidated
9. Similar to p27, p59 inhibits CAT activity via interaction,
promoting ToCV infection. This implies inducing cellular oxidative
stress is key to ToCV pathogenicity. Consequently, p59 functions not
only in viral assembly and movement but also as an effector disrupting
plant immunity. While viral effector hijacking of host CAT is reported
in M a i z ec h l o r o t i cm o t t l ev i r u s

the precise regulatory mechanism remains unclear. We propose a
model in which p59 disturbances host redox homeostasis by triggering
SlAVE3-mediated SlCAT1 degradation, exemplifying a co-evolutionary
exploit and counter-exploit mechanism. Given p59 ’sr o l ei nv i r a l
movement, its co-degradation with SlCAT1 may create a highly oxi-
dized cellular environment favorable for ToCV replication. This
mechanism likely enables ToCV’s stable cell-to-cell movement, making
it a key target for further investigation. Although p59 exploits SlAVE3
to degrade SlCAT1, SlAVE3 remains antiviral. The SlAVE3-p59-SlCAT1
interaction likely represents the last-ditch effort of p59 before
degradation.
The spatiotemporal dynamics of protein subcellular localization
are fundamental to the plant-virus interaction battleﬁeld. Our ﬁndings
that SlAVE3 localizes to the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, PD,
and peroxisomes highlight a compelling strategy in plant immunity: a
decentralized defense network. By positioning this key immune com-
ponent at multiple strategic locations, including the primary boundary
(plasma membrane), communication channels (PD), genetic center
(nucleus), and metabolic organelles (peroxisomes), the plant is poised
to intercept viral pathogens at various critical entry and propagation
points. This model provides a spatial context for the interactions of
SlAVE3 with partners like p59, SlCAT1, and SlWRKY6. The efﬁciency of
the UPS in the cytoplasm is thus leveraged precisely because the ubi-
quitination machinery is strategically pre-positioned. Furthermore, the
viral effector p59 ’s ability to hijack SlCAT1 from peroxisomes to
the cytoplasm is not merely a manipulation for its own bene ﬁt; it
inadvertently brings the substrate into the very compartment where
the UPS machinery, primed by SlAVE3, is most active. This spatial
hijacking is counteracted by the plant’s spatially distributed defense,
explaining the enhanced degradation of SlCAT1 during infection
compared to its relative stability in the non-infected state. Conse-
quently, understanding these intricate localization patterns is not just
about mapping proteins; it is about deciphering the tactical position-
ing of defenses and the counter-tactics of pathogens within the cellular
landscape.
Discovering and utilizing natural variation is central to crop
breeding. The SpAVE3 conferred stronger ToCV resistance than the
SlAVE3. However, the selection for SpAVE3 was overlooked during
initial tomato domestication. As ToCV was ﬁrst identi ﬁed only
recently
52, domestication of ToCV resistance began in the modern era.
This also explains why the variation from SpAVE3 to SlAVE3 was pri-
marily shaped by domestication selection rather than improvement
selection. SpAVE3 exhibited preferential recognition of the p59 but
reduced binding afﬁnity for SlCAT1. This promoted p59 degradation
while mitigating non-target effects on SlCAT1. Combined with lower
binding and ubiquitination levels of SpAVE3 to SlCAT1, we propose
SlCAT1 is likely not a primary target of SpAVE3, at least in S. pimpi-
nellifolium under normal conditions. The differences of AVE3-
mediated degradation of SlCAT1 by p59 may re ﬂect the co-
evolutionary dynamics between virus and host. Notably, SpAVE3
occurs in a small subset of cultivated accessions, providing germplasm
for its utilization. Furthermore, the functional difference between
SpAVE3 and SlAVE3 lay the groundwork for future precision editing
strategies.
In summary (Fig. 6), this study de ﬁnes p59 as a multifunctional
viral protein that participates in virion assembly, facilitates cell-to-cell
movement, and disrupts host redox homeostasis. We identiﬁed SlAVE3
as a antiviral factor recognizing p59, characterized their precise
interaction sites, and elucidated the degradation mechanism. We fur-
ther expanded the antiviral UPS by identifying SlAVE2 as a collaborator
and SlWRRY6 as a transcriptional regulator of SlAVE3, clarifying cor-
responding ToCV defense mechanism. Furthermore, we elucidated the
co-evolutionary arms race between tomato and ToCV: while SlAVE3
targets p59 for degradation, the p59 hijacks this degradation
machinery to subvert host defense by promoting SlCAT1 degradation.
These intriguing scientiﬁc questions, as they converge within the his-
tory of tomato domestication, present new opportunities for us to
utilize SpAVE3 in altering ToCV resistance breeding in tomato.
