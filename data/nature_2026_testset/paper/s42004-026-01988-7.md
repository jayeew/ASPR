Marine opisthobranch mollusks are a valuable source of structurally diverse bioactive metabolites
arising from de novo biosynthesis or dietary origins. Jorumycidine ( 4) is a novel bis-
tetrahydroisoquinoline (bis-THIQ) alkaloid with an unprecedented hexacyclic skeleton, isolated
together with jorumycin (2), renieramycin E (3), and new 21-deoxy analogues (5, 6) from the nudibranch
Jorunna funebris and its dietary sponge Haliclona sp. The structure of jorumycidine, featuring a unique
oxazolidine ring, was elucidated by spectroscopic, spectrometric, and chiroptical analyses. LC –MS/
MS diagnostic fragmentation ﬁltering (DFF), combined with re-annotation of the ren biosynthetic gene
cluster recently identi ﬁed in a Haliclona endosymbiont, supported a hybrid NRPS –PKS origin and
revealed enzymatic conversion of sponge-derived renieramycins into jorumycins by the nudibranch.
Jorumycidine exhibited potent nanomolar cytotoxicity (IC₅₀ = 13.8 nM) against multiple myeloma cells,
outperforming its congeners. These ﬁndings expand bis-THIQ chemical diversity and demonstrate
how interspecies metabolic interplay can generate bioactive scaffolds with therapeutic potential.
Marine symbioses are rich sources of chemical diversity, often yielding
bioactive metabolites with therapeutic potential. Many marine inverte-
brates lack biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) and instead obtain meta-
bolites from microbial partners or dietary sources, occasionally modifying
them for ecological defense and, in some cases, shaping entire benthic
communities
1. Opisthobranch mollusks occupy a unique niche in this
chemical landscape for their capacity to not only sequester, but also
enzymatically remodel or biosynthesize secondary metabolites inaccessible
to single organisms, thereby expanding the diversity and functionality of
natural products
2–7. These molecules can serve as chemical cues to deter
predation or mediate intra-species communication. In these mollusks,
metabolites, whether derived from dietary source or produced de novo, are
selectively accumulated in specialized mantle tissues (e.g., mantle dermal
formations or mantle rim) or released into secretions, resulting in higher
concentrations at the exposed surface or in the mucus compared to internal
organs. This spatial distribution is commonly used in chemical ecology
studies to infer sequestration and defensive function, and it underpins the
stratiﬁcation of metabolites observed in mantle, mucus, and whole-body
extracts.
Bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline (bis-THIQ) alkaloids constitute a promi-
nent subclass of natural products characterized by complex chemical scaf-
folds and signiﬁcant pharmacological potential
8–10. Found mainly in marine
bacteria, sponges, and tunicates, th e family include the anticancer drug
trabectedin (1)11 and share a conserved pentacyclic core motif assembled by
nonribosomal peptide synthetases (NRPSs). Anticancer and antimicrobial
activity, however, depends on subtle structural differences, and the bio-
synthetic routes to these variations remain poorly understood. In 2000, we
reported jorumycin (2) from the nudibranch Jorunna funebris,t h eo n l y
natural bis-THIQ alkaloid with an acetyl group at C-22
12. Its similarity to the
sponge metabolite renieramycin E ( 3) suggested a shared origin, likely
through microbial symbiosis or, altern atively, transformation of dietary
metabolites obtained from sponge producers such as Haliclona spp. This
view has been supported by the recent identi ﬁcation of the ren BGC in
Candidatus Endohaliclona renieramycinifaciens, an obligate symbiont
housed in specialized cells ofHaliclona sponges, which encodes the NRPS
machinery and tailoring enzymes necessary for renieramycin biosynthesis13.

hexacyclic bis-THIQ alkaloid featuring an oxazolidine ring that represents a

Discussion
The discovery of jorumycidine (4) expands the structural and biosynthetic
landscape of bis-tetrahydroisoquinoline (bis-THIQ) alkaloids, a family long
recognized for their biomedical potential8–10. The most well-known mole-
cule of the family is trabectidin (1)( Y o n d e l i s®) originally derived from the
tunicateEcteinascidia turbinateand currently prepared by semi-synthesis11.
Trabectedin (1) binds speciﬁcally to the minor groove of DNA at selected
guanine-rich triplet sequences, leading to DNA double-strand breaks and
a p o p t o s i si nt u m o rc e l l s
32. Additionally, the anticancer activity of1 is also
supported by immunomodulatory effects on tumor-associated macro-
phages (TAMs) and modiﬁcation of the tumor microenvironment
33,34.T h e
bis-THIQ family currently comprises more than 30 bioactive natural pro-
ducts that have also inspired the development of Zalypsis ® (PM00104)
which progressed into multiple Phase II clinical trials for solid and hema-
tologic tumors35. All these molecules maintain DNA minor groove-binding
mechanism by virtue of the reactive 21-hemiaminal group whose presence is
mandatory to preserve the anticancer activity
36. Unlike previously reported
congeners, jorumycidine ( 4) incorporates the 21-hemiaminal function
within an oxazolidine ring fused to the canonical bis-THIQ core, yielding
the ﬁrst hexacyclic scaffold of its class. This structural novelty is not simply a
curiosity, but it stabilizes a traditio nally labile hemiaminal position, pre-
serving the DNA alkylating property responsible for the potent cytotoxic
activity while circumventing the che mical fragility that has negatively
affected drug development from related scaffolds. From a chemical biology
perspective, such structure –stability–activity relationships are broadly
relevant for the development of THIQ-based therapeutics
37.
Bioinformatic re-annotation of the Haliclona renieramycin biosyn-
thetic gene cluster provides a mechanistic rationale for oxazolidine forma-
tion. The cluster encodes a hybrid NRPS –PKS system reminiscent of
quinocarcin biosynthesis24, suggesting that glycolate-derived building
blocks are funneled into the assembly line and subsequently cyclized into an
oxazolidine. This pathway underscores the versatility of NRPS–PKS cas-
settes in creating new N,O-heterocycles and suggests that similar logic may
be repurposed in other secondary met abolic contexts. In addition, our
results highlight how ecological inter actions create unique biosynthetic
opportunities in marine environments. Use of LC –MS/MS DFF
17 for
mapping bis-THIQ alakloids revealed a species-speciﬁc metabolic relay, in
which metazoan symbiont-derived renieramycins are enzymatically
remodeled by the nudibranch into jorumycins, while jorumycidine (4)i t s e l f
appears to be directly sequestered from the sponge. This interplay illustrates
how animals exploit microbial or dietary metabolites not only for chemical
defense but also as evolutionary platf orms for scaffold innovation. More
broadly, such cross-kingdom cooperation generates new molecular archi-
tectures while retaining memory of pa st biosynthetic innovations. Such
“metabolic archives” help explain the recurrent emergence of structural
variants and underscore the role of ecological interactions in shaping natural
product diversity with pharmacologically privileged motifs.

compound named jorumycidine (4) showing an unprecedented structure
with six cycles and a promising cytotoxic activity at nanomolar level against
