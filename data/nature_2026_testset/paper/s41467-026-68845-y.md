Chloromethane (CH3Cl) is a crucial chemical intermediate conventionally
synthesized under harsh conditions. Electrocatalytic methane (CH4)c h l o r -

alkali feedstocks under ambient conditions. However, the high stability and
low solubility of CH
4 limit CH3Cl’s production. To address these issues, we
develop a hierarchical system integrating catalyst and electrode designs. RuO2
catalyst is applied for surface adsorbed chlorine (*Cl) generation and CH4
activation. We also design a gas convection electrode (GCE), achieving a CH3Cl

to efﬁcient *Cl production by RuO2, coupled with the convection-dominated
mass transport and the GCE’s in-situ generation of dynamic three-phase
boundaries. Here, we demonstrate an efﬁcient catalyst for electrocatalytic CH4
chlorination and propose a broadly applicable strategy to enhance reaction
efﬁciency involving less soluble gaseous reactants.

The design and ﬂow dynamics of GCE
To optimize the three-phase contact and enhance CH4 mass transfer,
we designed a hierarchical porous gas convection electrode (GCE)
guided by the results from computational ﬂuid dynamics (CFD)
simulation, with detailed information in Supplementary Note 2. Dif-
ferent from conventional GDEs that passively rely on gas diffusion to
establish TPBs (Fig.2a), the GCEs actively generate dynamic gas-liquid-
solid interfaces through forced counterﬂow and convective liquid-gas
mixing (Fig. 2b). The gas-liquid contact mechanism in GDEs is limited
to surface-based triple-phase boundaries, while enhanced gas-liquid
contact in GCEs can be achieved through convective mixing, creating
volumetric triple-phase interfaces (Fig. 2c). The simpli ﬁed model of
GCE for CFD simulations mainly consisted of 3 parts: GCE framework
integrated with gas channel, gas blocking interface, and liquid channel
(Fig. 2c). Our design employed an axis-segregated ﬂow strategy: low
density gas ﬂowed along the w-axis while the denser liquid was chan-
neled along the orthogonal v-axis, with a controlled portion of liquid
permeating through the hydrophilic coating layer along the u-axis to
reach the catalyst (Fig. 2c). This con ﬁguration created shear-driven
interfacial turbulence that ampliﬁed contact areas from 1 cm
2 (GDEs)
to 34.90 cm 2 (GCEs) (Supplementary Table 1). This axis-segregated
ﬂow strategy led to the dynamic generation of TPBs, ensuring full
electrochemical accessibility across the electrode volume and
enabling near-complete utilization of the catalyst’sa c t i v es i t e s .

pore walls, inertial effects, and shear stresses between gas and liquid
layers, which are governed by Newton ’s law of viscosity, slowed and
turned the ﬂow angle of the mixed ﬂuid. Outside the GCE framework,

gravity. These interactions induced a sharp decline in velocity in both

a high-velocity region for bothﬂuids away from the blocking interface

Fabrication and characterization of GCE
Building on insights from CFD simulations and conventional GDE
designs, we have fabricated GCEs with optimized hierarchical struc-
tures. Reticulated vitreous carbon (RVC) was selected as the porous
substrate (corresponding to the multidirectional resistance) for the
GCE, due to its properties: high surface area (517 cm
2 g−1, Supplemen-
tary Table 1), electrical conductivity (309.6 S m −1), and corrosion
resistance in acidic media39. The GCE fabrication process involved two
steps: (1) catalyst deposition and (2) hydrophilic layer coating (Fig.3a).
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization conﬁrmed
the uniform dispersion of RuO2 across the 3D porous architecture of
the porous substrate, with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)
analysis revealing a Ru atomic percentage of 9.51% (Fig. 3b). X-ray
computed tomography (CT) characterization further demonstrated
well-preserved pore interconnectivity (100% void fraction retention)
within the porous substrate after RuO
2 catalyst deposition (Supple-
mentary Fig. 13), which was critical for maintaining mass transport
efﬁciency. To implement the CFD-optimized design, a hydrophilic
layer was applied to the porous electrode surface to replicate the CFD-
simulated gas-blocking interface. SEM analysis demonstrated seamless
layer integration with the porous substrate without pore collapse
(Fig. 3c, d). This coating layer enables electrolyte access to TPBs while
effectively preventing gas leakage. Wettability analysis (Fig. 3e) cou-
pled with high-speed videography (Supplementary Video 1) revealed
rapid wetting layer hydration, with initial droplet absorption in 4.98 s,
followed by secondary wetting within 0.1 s. This ultrafast wetting
behavior ensured continuous electrolyte replenishment at active cat-
alyst coverage sites. However, the larger contact angle of the GCE
interior (Supplementary Fig. 14), compared to the hydrophilic coating
layer, enabled the conﬁnement of gas ﬂow upward within the porous
structure of GCE, establishing a directed convective pathway for efﬁ-
cient gas delivery to the catalyst.
To fully leverage the GCE architecture, we developed and 3D-
printed an integratedﬂow reactor comprising two functional modules:
Module A incorporated the GCEs chamber with optimized gas ﬂow
channels for ef ﬁcient reactants delivery, while Module B featured
liquid channels for electrolyte circulation (Fig. 3f, Supplementary
Fig. 15). A dual-faced polytetra ﬂuoroethylene (PTFE) membrane was
positioned atop the GCE (Supplementary Fig. 16) to achieve spatial
separation between gaseous reactants (delivered through porous
channels) and liquid species (transported via the hydrophilic layer).
During electrochemical CH
4 chlorination, CH4 ﬂowed upward through
Module A ’s channels and subsequently permeated the PTFE mem-
brane. Simultaneously, electrolyte was transported through Module
B’s channels, permeating the GCE ’s porous structure via capillary
action. The PTFE membrane conﬁned the electrolyte within the porous
structure, redirecting it to diffuse back rather than exit through the gas
outlet. This mechanism maintained a dynamic liquid presence, avoid-
ing a static, fully ﬂooded condition. After a brief stabilization period
(experimentally observed within minutes), the system achieved con-
tinuous gas-liquid separation and established a dynamic gas-liquid
mass transport equilibrium.

Characterization
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS GeminiSEM 300) coupled
with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to examine
the morphological structure and the catalyst loading distribution. Ex-
situ electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy (EPR, Bruker
EMXplus-9.5/12) was used to detect chlorine radical generated and
then desorbed from the catalyst surface. The leaching behavior of Ru
was quantitatively assessed through inductively coupled plasma mass
spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7800) analysis of anolyte samples col-
lected every 1.5 h.
DMPO (5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide) was employed as a spin-
trapping agent to detect Cl radical generated during electrolysis in
EPR. The anolyte was pre-mixed with 100 mM DMPO to facilitate in-situ
chlorine radical trapping. Electrochemical experiments were per-
formed using a RuO ₂-GDE as the working electrode, with a constant
applied potential of 1.8 V vs. RHE for 10 s. The anolyte ef ﬂuent was
subsequently collected for EPR analysis to identify Cl radicals. Simi-
larly, control experiments with blank GDE with identical procedures
were repeated under the same conditions.
In-situ surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS-Raman)
measurements were performed using a Horiba LabRAN Odyssey sys-
tem integrated with a commercial GDE-based ﬂow cell, employing a
532 nm excitation laser and a 50 × microscope objective (NA = 0.55).
The three-electrode conﬁguration consisted of a SERS-GDE functio-
nalized with RuO
2/Au NPs as the working electrode, a Pt wire as the
counter electrode, and a Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) as the reference
electrode. The SERS substrate was prepared by dispersing 1 mg of
RuO₂ NPs into a mixture of 400μL Au sol (2.5 mg mL
−1), 50 μLI P A ,a n d
40 μL5 w t . %N aﬁon solution, followed by 1 h of ultrasonication and
subsequent drop-casting onto the GDE surface. During operando
measurements, CH4 was continuously supplied at 10 sccm through a
mass ﬂow controller while maintaining potentiostatic control via a
Donghua electrochemical workstation. Spectra were recorded across
the potential range of 1.8 –2.3 V (vs. RHE) with 0.1 V increments, pre-
ceded by a 5 min electrochemical stabilization period at each potential
to ensure steady-state operating conditions, with real-time frequency
calibration achieved using silicon wafer standards throughout the
experiments.
The gaseous Cl
2 was quantiﬁed using the N, N ′-diethyl-p-pheny-
lenediamine (DPD) colorimetric method. First, the outlet gas was
bubbled for 5 min through 50 mL of 4 g/L NaOH solution to generate
Solution A. Then, 1 mL of the anolyte (Solution B) was mixed with
Solution A, and the mixture was diluted with DI water by a speci ﬁc
factor to stay within the detection range (Solution C). Finally, 10 mL of
Solution C was mixed with one packet of DPD reagent (0.5 g), shaken
vigorously for 20 s, and analyzed immediately using an ultraviolet-
visible spectrophotometer (UV-Vis, Shimadzu UV-2600) within 1 min.
