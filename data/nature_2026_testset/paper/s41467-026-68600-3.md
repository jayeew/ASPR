Electrochemical CO2 reduction (ECR) in acidic electrolytes minimizes CO2 loss
and carbonate formation issues, allowing for high CO2 utilization efﬁciency
and showing good potential for practical CO2 upgrading applications. How-
ever, in the membrane-based electrolyzer, the proton transfer efﬁciency
across the membrane from the anolyte to the catholyte is crucial for the sta-
bility of the catholyte pH in acidic ECR, especially at high current density and
during long-term electrolysis. Here, we investigate the effects of proton
transfer efﬁciency and salt precipitation in different acidic ECR electrolyzer
and propose a membrane-free CO
2 hydrogenation electrolyzer, which couple
CO2 reduction and hydrogen oxidation. This electrolyzer design effectively
maintains a stable electrolyte pH during long-term electrolysis, and simulta-
neously achieves high Faradaic efﬁciency for HCOOH production, high single-
pass carbon utilization efﬁciency, and a lower cell voltage. At a current density
o f1 0 0m Ac m−2, the system requires only 1.7 V to achieve a 90% HCOOH Far-
adaic efﬁciency and demonstrates stable operation for 208 hours.
Electrochemical CO 2 reduction (ECR) powered by low-cost renew-
able electricity offers a promising route for producing valuable
chemicals and feedstocks
1–3, such as formic acid 4–7, carbon
monoxide8, and ethylene 9,10, while simultaneously mitigating
greenhouse gas emissions 9–13. Although high selectivity and activity
for ECR have been achieved in alkaline9,14 and neutral electrolytes15,16,
the formation and precipitation of carbonates impede the imple-
mentation of ef ﬁcient and stable CO
2 conversion systems 17–21. Spe-
ciﬁcally, during ECR, one CO 2 molecule reacts with two hydroxide
ions (OH−) to form one equivalent of carbonate (CO3
2−) for every two
electrons transferred 22. Consequently, the carbon utilization
efﬁciency, deﬁned as the fraction of the CO 2 feed converted to ECR
products, is critically low (less than 50%) due to the majority of CO 2
reacting with hydroxide ions to form carbonates 23. The recovery of
CO2 from a carbonate electrolyte is an energy-intensive process 22.
Furthermore, after long-term electrolysis, the precipitation of car-
bonates, such as potassium carbonate, can block CO
2 diffusion,
thereby inhibiting ECR 23–25. In addition, for certain products such as
formic acid (HCOOH) and acetic acid, electrolysis in alkaline or
neutral electrolytes presents an additional challenge: the acids
cannot be directly obtained because their corresponding anions
exist in salt form
26,27, leading to high separation costs 28.

for ECR, no OH− is generated17,30. Additionally, when H2Oi st h ep r o t o n

electrolyte, converting it back to CO2

be readily recovered by distillation in acidic conditions23. Efforts have
been dedicated to developing electrolytes and catalytic surfaces that
minimize the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and
increase ECR selectivity, activity, and stability
32,33. For instance, intro-
ducing alkali cations (such as K +) and functionalizing electrocatalysts
with cationic molecules can alter the local environment around the
electrocatalysts, promoting acidic ECR performance
17,34–38. However,
the membrane 39, an essential component in ECR, which crucially
impacts the composition of the catholyte and thus affects acidic ECR
performance, has received less attention. In acidic ECR electrolyzers, a
cation exchange membrane (CEM), such as Naﬁon 117 and Na ﬁon 211
membrane, is commonly used, allowing proton transport from the
anode to the cathode and avoid the oxidation of liquid ECR products in
the anode side
17,34–37. The use of a CEM introduces another often-
overlooked issue: the proton transport efﬁciency from the anolyte to
the catholyte can inﬂuence the pH of both compartments, potentially
leading to salt precipitation over long-term electrolysis.
Here, we explored the crucial role of the membrane in acidic ECR.
In systems using a cation exchange membrane, the catholyte pH was
found to increase progressively during electrolysis
23. To address the
limited H + transport caused by the membrane, a membrane-free
electrolyzer was developed, effectively minimizing the pH increase of
the electrolyte. To prevent oxidation of ECR products at the anode, we
utilized hydrogen oxidation reaction to replace the conventional
oxygen evolution reaction, which operates at a much lower oxidation
potential than that of formic acid. Coupled with a Bi
0.5-Ag0.5 catalyst
engineered for fast charge transfer, the membrane-free CO 2 hydro-
genation electrolyzer enabled stable HCOOH production for 208 h,
maintaining over 90% Faradaic ef ﬁciency (FE) at 1.7 V in acidic elec-
trolyte at 100 mA cm
−2.

Membrane H+ transfer efﬁciency impact catholyte pH
In acidic ECR, the catholyte pH typically ranges from 0.5 to 4, exhi-
biting negligible CO 2 loss17. This is evident from the pH-dependent
distribution of CO2, bicarbonate (HCO3
−), and CO 3
2− (Supplementary
Fig. 1) 40. The fraction of HCO 3
− ranges from 10 −6 to 10 −2 when the
solution pH is between 0.42 and 4.38. However, as the pH continues to
increase, the fraction of bicarbonate and carbonate increases expo-
nentially, eventually becoming the dominant species and resulting
carbonate formation issues at the cathode. Therefore, it is crucial to
maintain stable catholyte pH, preferably without any ﬂuctuations, to
effectively address carbonate formation issues, especially in long-term
and/or high current practical applications.

2 electrolysis system typically employs a ﬂow-type
reactor (Fig. 1a), where a certain amount of anolyte and catholyte are
recycled at a ﬁxed ﬂow rate. The anolyte and catholyte consist of dif-
ferent concentrations of protons (H+), alkaline metal cations (M+), and
anions (A−). During CO2 electrolysis, H+ and/or M+ can crossover from
the anolyte to the catholyte driven by the electricalﬁeld and diffusion
and then inﬂuence the catholyte and anolyte H+ and M+ concentration.

We then employed COMSOL to further analyze the in ﬂuence of
membrane and electrolyte composition to proton transport (Supple-
mentary Fig. 2 and Methods). We ﬁrstly investigated the inﬂuence of
membrane thickness (Supplementary Fig. 3), a thicker membrane
restricted proton transfer, resulting in a non-uniform protonﬂux and
hindering transport from the anode to the cathode (right to left).
Consequently, the proton concentration distribution across the cell
was non-uniform, and theη
H + at the cathode side was low. In contrast,
in the absence of a membrane (bottom panel), proton ﬂux was faster
and more uniform from the anode to the cathode, leading to a uniform
proton concentration distribution and a signiﬁcantly higher ηH + near
the cathode compared with the system with membrane. In addition,
higher proton selectivity could also promote proton transport from
the anode to the cathode (Supplementary Fig. 4). As proton selectivity
decreased, H
+ ﬂux, H+ concentration, and ηH + at the cathode side all
declined, indicating that a membrane with high proton selectivity is
more effective at maintaining catholyte pH. We also investigate the
inﬂuence of Electrolyte composition (Supplementary Fig. 5). As H
+
concentration increases, proton transport from the anode to the
cathode becomes more difﬁcult. When both the anode and cathode
have high proton concentrations, reduced chemical potential, strong
ionic shielding, and near-saturation of proton-conducting channels in
the membrane collectively limit the effective proton migration rate.
Taken together, a thinner membrane with high proton selectivity and
an electrolyte with low proton concentration are more favorable for
proton transport.

efﬁciency on ECR performance
To support the above analysis, we evaluated the ECR performance in
a conventional ECR and OER paired electrolyzer with cation
exchange membrane (ECR//CEM//OER), as shown in Fig. 2a. Na ﬁon
117 and Pt/C were employed as the CEM and OER catalysts, respec-
tively. In the initial electrolyzer con ﬁguration, the distance between
the anode and cathode was set to 10 mm. CO
2-to-HCOOH conversion
was selected as the model reaction due to the high economic value of
HCOOH. We selected Bi-based materials as the catalyst due to it’sl o w
toxicity and high abundance, which also exhibit high formate
selectivity
43. However, the electrical conductivity of Bi is relatively
low, leading to the higher cell voltage. Considering this, we intro-
duced high electrical conductive silver into the Bi-based materials to
enhance the charge transfer and lower the ECR potentials
44.W e

method. According to the different precursor stoichiometric ratios,
the catalysts are designated as Bi, Bi

respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission

diffraction (XRD) patterns conﬁrm the co-existence of metallic Bi and
Ag in the structure for all Bi-Ag samples (Fig. 2d). X-ray photoelec-

Electrolyzer design to enable stable catholyte pH
The ηH + and stability of catholyte pH are crucial for acidic ECR. A
membrane-free electrolyzer provides an alternative that bypasses the
limitations in H
+ transfer from the anolyte to the catholyte caused by
the membrane. We then designed the ECR and OER paired electrolyzer
without membrane (ECR//OER, Fig. 3a). As expected, the electrolyte
pH maintained nearly unchanged during the long-term test and the
η
H + was close to 100% (Fig. 3b), owing to the rapid neutralization of
hydroxide ions formed at the cathode and hydrogen ions generated at
the anode through electrolyte ﬂow. These results highlight the
advantages of a membrane-free electrolyzer compared to a
membrane-based electrolyzer. However, the average FE
HCOOH
decreased to 54% after 8 h electrolysis (Fig. 3b), possibly due to the
oxidation of HCOOH at the anode, as the equilibrium potential for
formic acid oxidation reaction (FAOR, 0.2 Vvs.R H E )51 is lower than the
standard potential for OER (1.23 V vs.R H E )4,52.
Motivated by recent HOR-coupled electrosynthesis systems for
nitrogen reduction53,54, we developed a membrane-free CO2RR − HOR
conﬁguration that similarly utilizes the thermodynamic advantages of
hydrogen oxidation to reduce the cell voltage. Compared to OER
(1.23 V vs. RHE), hydrogen oxidation reaction has a much lower stan-
dard equilibrium potential (0 V vs. RHE, Supplementary Table 4, Sup-
plementary Fig. 21), which is also lower than the standard equilibrium
potential for HCOOH oxidation (0.2 Vvs. RHE). Inspired by pioneering
work
21 on bicarbonate reduction for CO production using membrane-
free electrolyzers coupled with HOR in the anode, we coupled HOR
with ECR in a membrane-free CO
2 hydrogenation electrolyzer (ECR//
HOR, Fig. 3c) to address the issue of HCOOH oxidation at the anode54
caused by the absence of a membrane (Supplementary Fig. 22). As
shown in Fig. 3d, the cell voltage of the ECR//HOR electrolyzer (3 V) is
lower than that of the ECR//OER electrolyzer at a current density of
100 mA cm
−2 with the same electrolyte composition and anode to
cathode distance. The pH of the electrolyte remained nearly constant,
around 1.8, during 8 hours of electrolysis, and the FE
HCOOH in the ECR//
HOR electrolyzer stayed above 90%, highlighting the advantages of the
membrane-free CO
2 hydrogenation electrolyzer.

possibly due to the higher ECR//CEM//OER cell voltage, which creates a
stronger electric ﬁeld between the anode and cathode, thereby

Integrated system for efﬁcient electron utilization
Although the ECR and HOR coupled membrane-free system can sig-
niﬁcantly reduce the cell voltage, the production cost of HCOOH
remains high due to the expensive hydrogen source. While H 2 pro-
duction from water splitting could lower the H 2 cost, the OER at the
anode still requires a high potential and generates low-value oxygen as
a byproduct. In contrast to OER (1.23 V vs. RHE), MOR (methanol oxi-
dation reaction, 0.04 V vs. RHE) has a much lower thermodynamic
equilibrium potential
57. Additionally, by optimizing the electrocatalyst,
the MOR product can be tuned to high-value formate instead of CO2
58.
Therefore, we propose to couple the ECR//HOR electrolyzer with the
HER//AEM//MOR electrolyzer, using the H
2 produced by HER as the
hydrogen source for HOR, and producing both formate and HCOOH in
the two electrolyzers (Fig. 5a). Thus, electron utilization and the
HCOOH production rate could be doubled.
The MOR performance was evaluated in an anion exchange
membrane-based membrane electrode assembly (MEA) electrolyzer.
NiFe LDH and commercial 40 wt% Pt/C were used as the anode and
cathode catalysts, respectively (Supplementary Note 3, Supplemen-
tary Figs. 35-40). The FE
formate maintained ~ 90% during a 5-hour test at
100 mA cm−2 using 1 M KOH + 1 M MeOH as the anolyte, demonstrating
the good stability of this system (Supplementary Fig. 41). We then
integrated the HER//AEM//MOR electrolyzer with the ECR//HOR elec-
trolyzer (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 42). The hydrogen generated at
the cathode of the HER//AEM//MOR electrolyzer was directly fed to the
anode of the ECR//HOR electrolyzer as the hydrogen source, elim-
inating the need for an external hydrogen supply. Both the ECR//HOR
and HER//AEM//MOR electrolyzers were operated at a current density
of 100 mA cm
−2 with a reactor area of 1 cm2. The measured cell voltages
for the HER//AEM//MOR and ECR//HOR electrolyzers were 1.7 V and
1.7 V, respectively (Fig.5b). The coupled system operated stably for 5 h,
achieving a combined FE for formate and HCOOH exceeding 180%
(Fig. 5c). As expected, the pH of the electrolyte in the ECR//HOR
electrolyzer remained stable at ~1.8 (Supplementary Fig. 43).
A comprehensive TEA was carried out (Supplementary Note 2,
Supplementary Figs. 44-52). Assuming a CO
2 feedstock cost of 40 USD
ton−1 and a methanol feedstock cost of 470 USD ton−1, and an electricity
price of 0.03 USD kWh −1, integration with a separation process invol-
ving electrodialysis followed by extraction–distillation yields an esti-
mated levelized production cost of formic acid of approximately
2041.1 USD ton−1 (Fig. 5d). The major cost components contributing to
this value include: separation cost, electrolyzer capital cost, and elec-
tricity expense. The separation cost (1267.7 USD ton
−1) accounted for
approximately half of the total cost. This resulted from the low initial
product concentration (0.1 M) and the presence of a KOH and formate
mixture from the HER//AEM//MOR electrolyzer, which required an
energy-intensive electrodialysis process to concentrate the produced
formate to 2 M. In addition, because electrolysis was conducted at a
low current density (100 mA cm
−2), achieving the targeted production
rate requires a larger electrolyzer area (electrolyzer cost 59:3 1 0U S D
kW−1 at 1.7 V and 1.7 A cm−2, corresponding to 8959 USD m−2), leading to
a higher capital investment. Although the production cost higher than
the current market price
60 (830 USD ton−1), it is expected to decrease
further with reductions in electrolyzer capital cost and electricity price
(Supplementary Fig. 52). In addition, compared with direct H
2 supply
(Supplementary Note 4), this approach reduces the costs associated
with H
2 transport and storage (factors not considered in our TEA) and
also enhances operational safety. Future work should therefore focus
on increasing the electrolysis current density to lower electrolyzer
capital costs and on developing new separation methods to reduce
separation costs.
Discussion
In this work, we reveal the critical role of the cation exchange
membrane in acidic ECR and propose proton transfer ef ﬁciency as a
key parameter in ﬂuencing catholyte pH and salt precipitation dur-
ing long-term electrolysis. To overcome the limitations associated
with membrane-induced H ⁺ transfer impediments, we developed a
membrane-free ECR//HOR paired electrolyzer that enables a stable
catholyte pH during long-term electrolysis. The incorporation of
HOR at the anode effectively mitigates the overoxidation of liquid
products. As a result, we achieved a high SPCE of up to 77% and long-
term operational stability for 208 hours at 100 mA cm
−2, maintaining
aF EHCOOH above 90% at a low cell voltage of 1.7 V. By integrating the
HER//AEM//MOR electrolyzer with the ECR//HOR electrolyzer using
H
2 as the nexus molecule, both electron utilization ef ﬁciency and
product yield can be effectively doubled. Future work should focus
on increased the electrolysis current density of the integrated
design to promote the production rate and increase the eco-
nomic pro ﬁt.
