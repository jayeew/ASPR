Catechol offers switchable adhesion in response to electrochemical redox reaction. However,
electrochemistry requires water for effective proton transport, but water weakens adhesive
performance. Here, we incorporate proton and electron conducting elements (sulfonic acid-
containing monomer and multiwalled carbon nanotube, respectively) into a water-free catechol-based
adhesive to create a high-strength adhesive that is also susceptible to electrochemical control. These
additions increase the proton and electrical conductivity by over 100-fold. The adhesive also exhibits
elevated lap shear adhesion strength (4.6 MPa) to metal substrates and outperforms a commercial
epoxy glue. Under mild electrical stimulation (9 V), the adhesive strength decreases by over 90%. X-ray
photon spectroscopy conﬁrms the deactivation of the adhesive is achieved by catechol oxidation to its
poorly adhesive quinone form. When the adhesive is used to create two adjacent adhesive joints
connected by a single metallic substrate, the adhesive can be selectively deactivated without affecting
neighboring joint. These results present a promising approach for switchable adhesives with high
adhesive performance and precise electrical control.
Switchable adhesives with on-demand tunable adhesive property have
garnered signiﬁcant attention due to their pot ential to transform various
ﬁelds, ranging from biomedical applications to robotic locomotion and
sustainable manufacturing1– 7. By allowing bonds to form and release under
mild external stimuli, these materials can reduce material waste, streamline
assembly or disassembly processes, and minimize potential damage during
debonding8,9. In wearable sensors or medical patches, for instance, a
reversible adhesive enables easy repositioning and pain-free detachment10,
while automated production lines beneﬁt from the ability to temporarily
bond components and then deactivate the adhesive for rapid
reconﬁguration
2,3. Switchable adhesives are designed to respond to various
externally applied stimuli, such as lightexposure, or changes in temperature
or pH. Light-responsive adhesives o ften suffer from limited substrate
penetration of the light source, makingthem ineffective for use with opaque
surfaces or beneath layered assemblies11, while thermally responsive systems
typically require prolonged heating orelevated temperatures, which can be
energetically inef ﬁcient and potentially damaging to surrounding
components12. pH-triggered adhesion relies on solvent diffusion and
environmental control, restricting their use in dry or nonaqueous settings
and limiting temporal precision13. Compared with these external stimuli, the
use of applied electricity offers superior controllability, ease of operation,
and the potential for integrating with electronic devices. However, electro-
responsive switchable adhesives are limited by the need for elevated voltage
to induce change (>100 V), designed to bond between speciﬁcs u r f a c et y p e s ,
or required constant applied electricity to generate adhesion
14– 18. Conse-
quently, balancing robust interfacial bonding with a user-friendly, low-
energy debonding mechanism is difﬁcult.
Recently, our lab reported the use o f electrochemistry to control the
adhesive property of catechol-containing adhesives
19– 21. Catechol is one of
the main adhesive molecules that contributes to the robust adhesion of
mussel adhesive proteins
22,23. Catechol offers versatile bonding interactions,
such as metal chelation, hydrogen bonding,π– π and cation– π interactions,
thus conferring remarkable bonding strength to both organic and inorganic
surfaces (e.g., metals, polymers, tissues) 24– 27. Incorporating catechol into
both synthetic and biologic polymers imparts these materials with adhesive
property for designing adhesives and coatings for a wide range of
applications28– 30. The adhesion strength of catechol is highly dependent on
its oxidation state31,32. The reduced form of catechol is responsible for strong

mechanical integrity, proton transport would be severely hindered and
prevent efﬁcient redox control of adhesion.
To create a strong adhesive that is also susceptible to electrochemical
redox reaction, it is necessary to promote proton and electron transport in a
water-free polymer network. Proton transport in an anhydrous condition
can occur through the Grotthuss m echanism, where the protons are
transported between a closely associated proton donor (acidic group) and
proton acceptor (basic group)
35,36. Particularly, polymer containing sulfonic
acid (– SO3H) side chains are often used to create proton exchange mem-
branes and have exhibited excellent proton transport capability in low
relative humidity environments
37,38. By introducing sulfonic acid moieties
into a polymer backbone rich in hydrogen bonds, one can establish densely
spaced acidic sites that facilitate proton hopping via the Grotthuss
mechanism. This minimizes the need for bulk water as a proton transport
medium. In parallel, multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) serve as
highly electrically conductiveﬁllers and can substantially enhance electron
transport
39. Unlike spherical nanopartic les, carbon nanotubes (CNTs)
possess a high aspect ratio, creating percolation networks for electron
transfer at comparatively lowﬁller contents.

Lap shear adhesion testing
The inﬂuence of exposure to applied electricity on lap adhesion strength was
initially examined using an adhesive formulated with 91 wt% P(HEMA-
DMA-AMPS), 10:1:1 and 9 wt% MWCNTs adhered to titanium (Ti) sheets
as the test substrate (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 10). Before voltage
application, the adhesive exhibited lap shear adhesion strength of 4.6 MPa,
signiﬁcantly surpassing the values observed from hydrogel-based adhesives
by 100-fold
21,33,34. This adhesive strength value is also comparable with other
high-strength, catechol-containing adhesives reported in the literature
(2– 11 MPa)43– 45. As a qualitative demonstration of adhesive strength, a 2.3-
kg (5-lb) barbell plate was successfully suspended from an adhesive joint
with an area of 4 mm × 6 mm (Fig. 2b). To expose the lap shear joint to
electricity, electrodes were directly connected to each Ti sheet and 9 V was
applied using a direct current (DC) power supply for up to 4 min prior to
adhesion testing (Fig.2c, d). During the deactivation process, the measured
current ﬂuctuated between 40 and 80 mA and gradually decreased during
continuous voltage application. Upon application of 9 V, a sharp drop in
adhesion strength was observed within the ﬁrst minute, decreasing to
approximately 2 MPa. As the duration of voltage application was extended
to 4 min, the adhesion strength was measured at 0.5 MPa (Fig. 2a). These
observations conﬁrmed our initial hypothesis and demonstrated effective
modulation of adhesion via electrical input in a dry state.
Next, we systematically evaluatedthe effect of adhesive composition,
such as the MWCNT content, DMA and AMPS ratios, and backbone
polymer composition, on the adhesive ’s performance and electro-
responsiveness. As shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 11, increas-
ing CNT content from 0 to 9 wt% decreased the initial lap shear strength
from 7.2 MPa (0 wt% MWCNT) to 4.6 MPa (9 wt%) prior to electrical
stimulation. The observed decrease in adhesion strength is potentially due to
the dilution of the adhesive polymer content in the composite. However,
higher MWCNT content correlated with more efﬁcient and near-complete
adhesion deactivation. Speciﬁcally, an adhesive formulated with 9 wt%
MWCNT exhibited a 91% decrease in adhesion after exposure to 9 V for
4 min, while the CNT-free adhesive retained 4.9 MPa (decreased by 32%) of
adhesion strength with the same electrical stimulation. When the CNT-free
adhesive was exposed to 17 V for 4 min, adhesive strength only reduced to
3.3 MPa (decreased by 54%). Electrical conductivity ( σ
e) was measured
using an Ossila four-point probe (Supplementary Table 3). The CNT-free
adhesive exhibited conductivity that was too low to be detected by the four-
point probe, indicating insufﬁcient electron transport for effective electro-
chemical deactivation. The conductivity increased nearly 20 folds when
CNT content was increased from 5 to 9 wt% (86 and 1600 S/m, respectively).
These results highlighted the critical role of CNTs for electron transport.
While the adhesive strength of CNT-free and poorly electrically
conductive adhesive could be partially reduced using applied electricity,
increasing electrical conductivity enabled a more rapid redox switching of
catechol groups across the adhesive i nterface while utilizing reduced
applied voltage.

Compatibility with various metallic substrates
To explore the versatility of the electrochemically switchable adhesive, its
performance was evaluated on different metal substrates, including Ti,
stainless steel, and aluminum (Al) (Fig.3e and Supplementary Fig. 16). Prior
to voltage application, strong adhesion strengths were achieved for all the
surfaces tested, with the highest being Ti (4.6 MPa), followed by Al
(3.6 MPa) and stainless steel (2.2 MPa). Upon exposure to 9 V for 4 min, all
adhesive demonstrated signiﬁcant adhesion reduction. For the steel surface,
there was a near-complete deactiva tion (0.01 MPa), achieving a 99.5%
reduction in adhesion strength. Similarly, adhesion strength dropped sub-
stantially on Ti (0.4 MPa) and Al (0.44 MPa). These results indicate that the
adhesive’s electro-responsiveness is not limited to a single substrate and can
be deactivated effectively across mu ltiple conductive metallic surfaces,
which may be critical for broader practical applications where diverse
substrates are commonly encountered.

dry or minimally hydrated polymer systems; their removal diminishes the
link between catechol redox chemistry and proton transport
61,64.
Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) characterization
DSC was utilized to determine the glass transition temperatures (Tg)o ft h e
adhesive polymers, which were found to be around 57 – 60 °C (Supple-
mentary Table 7). These values are lower than those reported for the
homopolymers of poly(HEMA) and poly(AMPS) (80 and 90 °C,
respectively)
65,66, indicating an increased chainﬂexibility arising from ran-
dom copolymerization. Given that all the adhesion and proton-transport-
related measurements were conducted at room temperature and well below
the measured T
g values, the glassy state of the polymer contributed to the
elevated adhesive strength of these polymers. Additionally, large-scale seg-
mental motion is suppressed and did not contribute to the increase in
proton transport. As such, the increased proton conductivity can be
attributed to localized hydrogen-bond-mediated hopping between sulfonic
acid groups (AMPS) and neighboring polar sites (– OH in HEMA and amide
groups in DMA) rather than bulk chain mobility. Similar Grotthuss-type
proton hopping mechanisms operated at a temperature below T
g in
hydrogen-bonded and anhydrous polymer systems have been previously
reported61,67,68, supporting proton conduction without requiring polymer
motion or high humidity.

Demonstration of electrical deactivation
Four videos highlighted the adhesiveperformance and selective control of
the electrochemically switchable adhesive. In Supplementary Movie 1, an
adhesive joint was formed using P(HEMA-DMA-AMPS), 10:1:1+ 9w t %
MWCNT covering an area of merely 4 mm × 6 mm using two Ti sheets as
the substrates. A 2.3-kg (5-lb) barbell plate was suspended from the bottom
Ti sheet. The adhesive joint maintained its mechanical integrity without
failure throughout the entire 1-min video. In Supplementary Movie 2, the
same 2.3-kg (5-lb) barbell plate was suspended from the bottom Ti sheet and
electricity was applied to the Ti sh eets using 9 V DC voltage at 1 min 5 s
mark. The adhesive joint broke after ~10 s due to the suspended weight.
Supplementary Movies 3 and 4 demonstrated the selective deactivation of
an adhesive. The sample in each video was assembled by three Ti sheets and
2 adhesive joints connected in series. A 200-g weight was suspended from
the bottom sheet. In Supplementary Movie 3, electricity was passed across
the top adhesive joint, resulting in selectively breaking this joint while
leaving the bottom adhesive joint intact. Conversely, electricity was passed
across the bottom adhesive joint in Supplementary Movie 4, resulting in
breaking the bottom joint while the top adhesive joint was not affected by the
applied electricity. Adhesive failure occurred exclusively on the cathodic
substrate side. This decoupled response conﬁrmed that the electrochemical
deactivation mechanism was inherently con ﬁned to the adhesive joint
through which current was actively passing. This selective debonding
behavior strongly supported the hypothesis that the electrochemical trigger
will be exclusively electrically gated, further reinforcing the unique capability
of this adhesive system for integratingwith electronic devices. Such precise
controllability of the adhesive platform allows for on-demand, site-speciﬁc,
and programable release. This capability is expected to enable advanced
integration in modular electronics, biomedical devices, adaptive robotics,
and structurally dynamic systems, where components must be detached
without affecting adjacent bonded areas.
This project uniquely combined design elements from proton
exchange membranes and catechol-based adhesion chemistry to create an
electro-responsive adhesive system that demonstrated adhesion strength
that is comparable to commercial epoxy glue and selective deactivation.
Looking ahead, several key directions may further enhance the functionality
and adaptability of this system. First, the reversibility of adhesion cycles
warrants deeper investigation. While the current work demonstrates strong
adhesion and effective one-time deactivation, achieving full bond-debond-
rebond repeatability could enable the adhesive to function in long-term,
reconﬁgurable applications, such as wearable electronics, robotic gripping
surfaces, or dynamic biomedical interfaces. Exploring the use of temporary
protecting group in the form of phenyl boronic acid to prevent irreversible
catechol oxidation and crosslinking may be required
13,20.A d d i t i o n a l l y ,
improving the sensitivity to electrical stimuli will be critical for reducing
energy consumption and broadening operational compatibility with low-
power electronics. This may involve engineering the polymer micro-
environment to reduce activation voltage thresholds,ﬁnding a more elec-
trically conductive metallic subs titute for MWCNTs to improve charge
transfer efﬁciency, and further enhancing the proton conductivity of the
adhesive. While we have shown that the deactivation of our adhesives
occurred mainly through electrochemical oxidation of catechol, application
of electricity can disrupt hydrogen bonds that exist within the adhesive
network and may contribute to a decrease in its bulk cohesive properties
71.

Conclusions
In conclusion, this study presents a new approach to enable high-strength,
catechol-based adhesives to become electrochemically switchable through
incorporating proton-conducting sulfonic acid and electrically conductive
MWCNT. These proton and electron-conducting elements enabled elec-
trochemical redox reaction in an anhydrous adhesive network, which
eliminated the need for the oft-used water as the medium that also reduces
the cohesive property of an adhesive. Through systematic analysis of the

Synthesis of adhesive copolymers
The copolymers were prepared via thermo-initiated free radical poly-
merization by combining monomers (DMA and AMPS) with either HEMA
or MEA as the backbone monomer. The numerical subscripts following the
copolymer abbreviations indicate the mole ratio of each monomer added
during the reaction (Supplementary Table 1). For example, P(HEMA-
DMA-AMPS), 10:1:1 was prepared as follows. 8 mmol HEMA, 0.8 mmol
DMA, 0.8 mmol AMPS, and 0.12 mmol AIBN were dissolved in 12 mL
dimethylformamide (DMF), followed by nitrogen purging for 10 min. The
reaction mixtures were stirred at 500 rpm and heated at 70 °C in a silicone oil
bath overnight under a nitrogen atmosphere. After cooling to room tem-
perature, the resulting polymers were precipitated into 120 mL diethyl ether,
collected by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and vacuum-dried for
24 h. MEA-containing copolymer was synthesized following the same
procedure, replacing HEMA by MEA. Control polymers were prepared
similarly by regulating the ratio of DMA and/or AMPS as necessary.
