Membrane processes offer a promising pathway for selectively separating
organics and salts to enable water reuse and resource recovery. While poly-
meric membranes incorporating macrocyclic molecules that feature amphi-
philic nature and tunable cavities are well suited for this purpose, traditional
macrocycles with limited reactive sites and uncontrolled diffusion are chal-
lenging to be assembled into highly interconnected membranes. Here, we
introduce tetra-aldehyde appended calixarene (TACA), a macrocyclic mono-
mer featuring three-dimensional cavity and moderate reactivity, for creating
loose-structured nanoﬁlms via unidirectional diffusion assisted interfacial
polymerization (UDIP). Precise positioning of the lipophilic TACA at the
organic phase boundary allows it to polymerize with aqueous-phase diamines
on the hydrogel surface, facilitating an undisturbed environment for con-
trolled polymerization. The resultant thin macrocycle-assembled membranes
featuring intrinsic water-facilitated through-cavity exhibited high water per-
meability of 63.8 L m
-2 h-1 bar-1, and exceptional dye/salt selectivity and struc-
tural robustness, as evidenced by efﬁcient diaﬁltration of binary dye/salt
mixtures and superior operational stability. This work highlights the potential
of macrocycle-assembled membranes for high-salinity organic wastewater
treatment.
The treatment and reuse of industrial wastewaters are central to
addressing global water scarcity and enabling circular water
economies
1,2. In particular, saline organic wastewaters produced by
industries such as textiles, petrochemicals, and pharmaceuticals pose
as i g n iﬁcant challenge to conventional treatment processes due to the
co-existence of high concentrations of organic contaminants and
inorganic salts
3,4. Membrane-based separations provide promising
solutions for precise separation of organics and salts, facilitating water
reuse, resource recovery, and zero-liquid discharge
5–7. Currently, the
treatment of organic-laden saline wastewaters primarily relies on
polyamide-based membranes, which exhibit limited selectivity for
separating organic molecules from inorganic ions
8,9. This limitation
arises from the rapid interfacial polymerization of planar, highly
reactive monomers, resulting in membranes with low microporosity

The three-dimensional stereochemical con ﬁgurations and diverse
chemical functionalities of macrocyclic molecules enable precise
control over monomer diffusion kinetics in both bulk and interfacial
environments16,17. This controlled monomeric diffusion facilitates the
formation of thin polymeric nanoﬁlms with tunable crosslinking den-
sity and a narrow pore size distribution 18,19. Importantly, the well-
deﬁned intrinsic micropores of these macrocycles evolve into inter-
connected nanopores within the crosslinked membrane, thereby
enhancing the membrane microporosity and improving solvent
transport ef ﬁcacy
20,21. Functionalized macrocycles (e.g., amine-
cyclodextrin22, pillararene23 and Noria24) have been utilized to fabri-
cate microporous membranes exhibiting exceptional performance in
gas separation, organic solvent nano ﬁltration, and reverse osmosis.
However, the commonly used amide reaction during fabrication often
produces an excessively dense crosslinked network with sub-
nanoscale pores. This limits its ability to separate molecular/ion mix-
tures and hinders its effectiveness in treating saline organic-rich
waters. In contrast, calixarenes maintain the structural characteristics
of traditional macrocyclic molecules while offering tunable con ﬁg-
urations and easy chemical modiﬁcation. This provides a foundation
for precise control over membrane architecture. By altering chemical
bonding type, polymer membranes with adjustable network structures
are fabricated.
Here, we identify tetra-aldehyde-functionalized calix[4]arene
(TACA) as promising three-dimensional building blocks to construct
high-porosity polyimine membranes for improved molecule/ion
separation. TACA, with four aldehyde groups, enables moderate Schiff
base reaction with diamines, facilitating the formation of loosely
structured networks. Moreover, TACA contains four phenolic hydroxyl
groups positioned para to the ald ehydes, and four methylene that
connects four benzene rings in a cone-shaped arrangement. This
nonplanar, rigid framework possesses internally connected calix-
shaped cavities, naturally forming a continuous pathway for water
transport. In contrast to other contorted macromolecules, TACA offers
several advantages for the synthesis of thin- ﬁlm composite (TFC)
membranes. First, the hydrophobicity and relatively large size of TACA
conﬁne it to the organic phase, while the aqueous-phase m-phenyle-
nediamine (MPD) diffuses unidirectionally across the phase boundary,
enabling precise spatial control over interfacial polymerization (Fig.1a,
b). Second, the less reactive ald ehyde groups in TACA allow better
control over the Schiff base reaction, enabling moreﬂexible regulation
of ﬁlm compactness. Third, hydroxyl groups on the lower rim of the
calix-shaped cavities promote the formation of hydrophilic channels
that facilitate water transport
25. Last, TACA exhibits low toxicity and
biocompatibility, making it a highly attractive material for membrane
fabrication26.

tribution of aqueous-phase monomers, resulting in non-uniform ﬁlm
thickness and a broad pore size distribution
28– 30. In contrast, Kevlar

Leveraging these properties, we introduce a strategy that com-
bines the moderately reactive, three-dimensional contorted TACA
macrocycle to fabricate loosely structured TFC membranes with
highly interconnected porous networks. Kevlar hydrogel was
employed as the porous substrate to in situ fabricate polyimine-
based TACA-MPD composite membranes via unidirectional
diffusion-assisted interfacial polymerization (UDIP). In contrast to
traditional interfacial polymerization, the UDIP process uses a Kevlar
hydrogel to prevent direct contact between the two-phase solutions,
thereby achieving superior control over the reaction. Furthermore,
UDIP provides a sustained and stable monomer supply for ﬁlm for-
mation, which promotes structural self-healing conducive to the
formation of defect-free thin ﬁlms. A systematic exploration of cri-
tical synthesis conditions (e.g., monomeric molar concentration/
ratio and reaction duration) was conducted to elucidate their role in
morphological structure and physicochemical properties of the
synthesized TACA-MPD membranes. Our macrocycle-assembled
membranes exhibited impressive dye/salt selectivity, remarkable
structural robustness, and operational stability, as demonstrated by
the ef ﬁcient separation of binary dye/salt mixtures. Combined
experimental and molecular simulation results revealed that the
exceptional water permeability and separation performance stem
from the well-de ﬁned, rigid interconnected cavities formed within
TACA-MPD nanoﬁlms (Fig. 1c, d).

tribution of a compound between an organic phase and an aqueous
phase. A higher log P value corresponds to an increased lipophilicity
and preferential partitioning into the organic medium, while a lower
value denotes enhanced aqueous solubility and a dominant afﬁnity for
the aqueous phase. Partition coefﬁcient measurements revealed that
MPD, with a logP of −0.45, is hydrophilic but still moderately soluble in
DCM, while TACA, with a logP > 3, is highly hydrophobic and insoluble
in water (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Note 4)
47.
These properties suggest that TACA concentrates at the organic
(DCM) interface, where MPD diffuses from the aqueous phase to
initiate interfacial polymerization.
To con ﬁrm this directional polymer growth, MPD and TACA
were placed on opposite sides of the Kevlar support, composed of a
hydrogel layer and a non-woven fabric. When the TACA/DCM phase
contacted the hydrogel side, nodular structures emerged on its
surface, while the non-woven fabric remained smooth (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 22a, b). In the reversed conﬁguration, with the MPD/aqueous
phase facing the hydrogel, nodule formation was observed on the
non-woven fabric (Supplementary Fig. 22c, d). These observations
conﬁrm the directional growth of imine-linked polymers at the DCM
interface.
UV-Vis spectroscopy further veri ﬁed the partitioning of MPD
molecules into the DCM phase (Supplementary Fig. 23). Increasing the
initial MPD concentration enhanced its interfacial accumulation and
diffusion rate (Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 24). These ﬁndings suggest
that extending the reaction time or increasing the monomer con-
centration could enhance polymerization degree and consequently
affect the surface properties of the polyimine membrane
48.
To further investigate the inﬂuence of fabrication conditions on
UDIP, the morphology of the membrane was characterized under
varying TACA concentrations (Supplementary Figs. 25 –28). At low
TACA concentrations, the reaction proceeded mildly, yielding smooth,
particle-free surfaces. Increasing TACA concentration led to an inten-
siﬁed interfacial polymerization process, leading to the gradual for-
mation of surface particles and wrinkles. At excessively high TACA
concentrations, the vigorous reaction combined with the rigid ben-
zene ring structure led to the formation of wrinkles and cracks on the
membrane surface.
Based on the experimental observations, a mechanistic pathway
for TACA-MPD membrane formation is proposed (Fig.3j). The process
begins with the con ﬁned diffusion of MPD toward the organic inter-
face, where it undergoes a Schiff base reaction with TACA, initiating
the nucleation and growth of polymer nodules. These particles
aggregate and coalesce on the Kevlar support, forming a continuous
nanoﬁlm through the dynamic breaking and reforming of imine
bonds
49. As the reaction progresses, continued particle growth and
accumulation lead to an increased nanoﬁlm thickness50. The diffusion
rate of MPD and the concentrations of both monomers govern the
polymerization kinetics and resulting membrane morphology. Higher
monomer concentrations and longer reaction times promote ﬁlm
growth but may also induce defects such as wrinkles and cracks due to
excessive particle formation.
Separation performance of TACA-MPD membranes
The separation performance (water permeance and dye rejection) of
various TACA-MPD membranes was evaluated using a cross ﬂow
apparatus with feed solutions containing organic dyes and/or inor-
ganic salts. As depicted in Suppl ementary Fig. 29, extending the
reaction time from 1 to 7 h led to a gradual decrease in the water
permeance but signiﬁcantly improved dye rejection, reaching 99.8%
for Congo red and 99.1% for Methyl blue. This enhancement is attrib-
uted to increased membrane thickness and crosslinking degree
51.
Notably, the TACA0.5MPD0.5−3 membrane (prepared with 0.5 mM of
each monomer and 3 h of reaction time) exhibited a high water per-
meability of 63.8 L m−2 h−1 bar−1 and an ultra-high Congo red rejection

Discussion
High-salinity organic wastewaters pose a signi ﬁcant challenge for
treatment and reuse due to the coexistence of high concentrations of
salts and organic compounds. In this work, we present loosely struc-
tured macrocycle-assembled membranes as a promising solution for
treating saline, organic-rich wastewaters. Through rational structural
design, we identify TACA as a suitable building block for membrane
fabrication, owing to its rigidly contorted structure, intrinsic through-
cavity, and moderate reactivity. Using unidirectional diffusion-assisted
interfacial polymerization, we fabricated macrocyclic membranes that
exhibit high water permeance, effective salt/organic fractionation, and
low fouling propensity. These favorable properties are attributed to
the smooth membrane surface, interconnected porous network, and
multiple water and ion transport pathways.
The macrocycle-assembled membranes developed in this study
offer a distinct advantage over conventional polyamide nanoﬁltration
membranes by coupling high water permeance with exceptional
organic/salt selectivity. This combination facilitates energy-ef ﬁcient
diaﬁltration, eliminating the need for the high hydraulic pressures
commonly required in traditional separation processes. Their capacity
for the selective recovery of organic compounds and salts makes them
particularly well-suited for treating high-salinity organic wastewaters,
providing a sustainable, cost-effective solution that strongly supports
resource recovery and the circular economy.
Methods
Fabrication of Kevlar hydrogel support membrane
Kevlar hydrogel support membranes were fabricated via a wet phase
inversion method
42,77. A mixture of 1.2 g Kevlarﬁb e r s ,1 . 2gK O H ,1 . 2m L
water, and 60 mL DMSO was magnetically stirred at room temperature
for 24 h to obtain a homogeneous and transparent dark red solution.
The solution was subsequently centrifuged at 6578 x g to remove air
bubbles and KOH precipitates. The non-woven fabric wasﬁxed onto a
clean glass plate, and then the treated solution was cast onto it with a
casting knife set to 250 μm thickness. The coated glass plate was
immediately immersed in a deionized water bath (26 °C). The Kevlar
hydrogel support membrane was formed by phase inversion for 6 min.
Preparation of free-standing TACA-MPDﬁlms
Freestandingﬁlms were fabricated by interfacial polymerization at the
free water-DCM interface. TACA was dissolved in 50 mL of DCM to
prepare a 0.5 mM organic phase solution. MPD was dissolved in 50 mL
of deionized water to form a 0.5 mM aqueous solution. The organic
phase solution was poured into a glass petri dish, followed by the slow
addition of the aqueous phase onto its surface to form a stable water/
DCM interface. The petri dish was then covered to prevent disturbance
from airﬂow. After a 3-h interfacial polymerization reaction at room
temperature, theﬁlm at the interface was carefully transferred using a
silicon wafer or anodized aluminum for subsequent characterization.
Preparation of TACA-MPD macrocyclic-based membranes
The TACA-MPD membrane was fabricated by UDIP using the Kevlar
hydrogel as substrate. The Kevlar hydrogel membrane was secured
between the two chambers of a diffusion cell. 30 ml of 0.5 mM MPD/
aqueous solution was added to one chamber, followed immediately by
adding 30 ml of 0.5 mM TACA/DCM solution to the chamber on the
opposite side. The TACA
0.5MPD0.5−3 membrane was synthesized with
a 3-h reaction under undisturbed conditions. A series of TACA-MPD
membranes was fabricated by adjusting the monomer concentrations
in the organic and aqueous phases, as well as the reaction time. The
prepared membranes were designated as TACA
XMPDY-Z, where X
represents the TACA concentration (0.4, 0.5, 0.6, and 0.7 mM), Y
represents the MPD concentration (0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 1.0 mM), and
Z represents the reaction time (1, 3, 5, and 7 h). By balancing dye
rejection and water permeance, we identiﬁed the optimal membrane,
which was fabricated using 0.5 mM TACA and 0.5 mM MPD under a 3 h
reaction condition (referred to as TACA
0.5MPD0.5−3), and used it for
subsequent dye/salt separation tests.
