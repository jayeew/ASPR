While Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) offers signiﬁcant pain relief, its psychoactive side effects limit its
clinical utility. The discovery of cannabidiol (CBD) as a negative allosteric modulator (NAM) of
cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1R) establishes allosteric targeting as a viable framework for re ﬁning
cannabinoid-based therapeutics. Cannabidiphorol (CBDP), a naturally occurring homolog of CBD
identiﬁed from the FM2 chemovar of Cannabis sativa, is structurally similar to CBD but its
pharmacology is largely unexplored. This study investigated the potential of CBDP as a NAM of CB1R.
Site-directed mutagenesis, in vitro CB1R allosterism assays and molecular dynamics analyses
revealed that CBDP acts as an NAM by stably engaging two distinct allosteric sites: the established
ORG27569 site and an intracellular site located between transmembrane helices 1, 2, 6, 7, and helix 8.
In vivo, tetrad assays and novel object recognition (NOR) testing in male C57BL/6NHsd mice
suggested that CBDP co-administration with THC was associated with modest modulation of
cognitive performance relative to THC alone, while THC ’s antinociceptive effects were preserved.
These ﬁndings identify CBDP as a possible dual-site CB1R NAM and support future studies to
evaluate its potential therapeutic and mechanistic utility.
Cannabis sativa, one of the most extensively studied medicinal plants,
produces a diverse array of bioactive compounds, including phytocanna-
binoids, which are notable for thei r complex polycyclic scaffolds and
pharmacological potential. Over 100phytocannabinoids have been identi-
ﬁed to date, all sharing a conserved architecture composed of a monoterpene
unit and a phenolic ring with a lipophilic alkyl chain1,2 (Fig. 1a).
Among these phytocannabinoids, ( − )-cannabidiol (CBD) and
(−)-trans-Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) are the most well-characterized
and have been shown to modulate th e endocannabinoid system (ECS)
through distinct mechanisms3,4. CBD and THC share a common pentyl-
chain motif on the phenolic ring; however, subtle differences in their cyclic
architectures dictate their unique pharmacological behaviors and receptor
afﬁnities. Despite the anti-inﬂammatory beneﬁts of Δ
9-THC, its widespread
clinical applications have been limited because of its psychoactive side
effects, including memory impairment and intoxication
5,6.
In 2019, Citti et al. reported the disc overy of novel heptyl analogs of
CBD and THC —cannabidiphorol (CBDP) and tetrahydrocannabiphorol
(THCP), respectively—from the FM2 chemovar of C. sativa7. While Citti
et al. ﬁrst isolated and structurally characterized CBDP as a natural phy-
tocannabinoid from C. sativa,i tw a si d e n t iﬁed only in trace amounts;
consequently, its broader natural distribution and biosynthetic prevalence
warrant further investigation.
Cannabinoid receptors CB1R and CB2R, both classiﬁed as Class A G
protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), have been shown to play key roles in
mediating the physiological effects of both endogenous and exogenous
cannabinoids. While THCP was shown to exhibit high binding af ﬁnity
with cannabinoid receptors (CB1R and CB2R), the pharmacological
properties of CBDP have remained largely uncharacterized despite the
development of synthetic approaches for its production. Recent studies
indicate that CBDP may share several biological properties with CBD,
including antioxidant, anti-in ﬂammatory, and anti-epileptic
properties
7–10. Further research has shown it to possess proliferative
effects of CBDP in breast cancer models 9 and to act as a weak antagonist
of CB1R at micromolar concentrations 11. However, to date, no studies
have investigated CBDP ’s allosteric modulation at cannabinoid recep-
tors or its behavioral pharmacological properties.

Emerging evidence gathered from in vitro models of CB1R over-
expression imply that CBD does not act at the CB1R orthosteric site but
instead functions as a negative allosteric modulator (NAM), decreasing
the receptor ’sa f ﬁnity for agonists via a distinct intracellular allosteric
site
12–15. The NAM activity of CBD at CB1R is one of many in vitro
polypharmacological activities described for CBD to date, including
modulatory activity at other GPCRs and ion channels
16,17. Therefore,
CBD-like compounds that retain CB1R NAM activity, but avoid the
pharmacological promiscuity of CBD, represent a new avenue for can-
nabinoids with high therapeutic potential. Unlike orthosteric antago-
nists, NAMs do not bind to the primary ligand-recognition site of the
receptor but instead bind to spatially distinct sites (allosteric sites) to
modulate receptor conformation and ultimately reduce the potency or
efﬁcacy of agonists. As a result, a more subtle regulation of receptor
activity is expected, often preserving basal physiological signaling while
attenuating further activation. In the case of CB1R, NAMs such as CBD
and ORG27569 have been shown to decrease the receptor ’s responsive-
ness to agonists like Δ⁹-THC and mitigate agonist-induced psychoactive
effects without abolishing endocannabinoid signaling completely
18. This
mechanism opens a new avenue for the development of cannabinoid-
based therapeutics with reduced psychoactive liabilities. Because of the
close structural similarity with CBD and the emerging understanding of
allosteric modulation at CB1R, we hypothesized that CBDP may also
allosterically modulate CB1R.
Herein, we present theﬁrst comprehensive investigation of CBDP as
a NAM of CB1R. In vitro allosterism assays demonstrated that CBDP
decreased CB1R activity by reducing CP55,940-dependent inhibition of
cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) via CB1R, consistent with
NAM characteristics. To elucidate its binding mechanism, single-point
site-directed mutagenesis was performed to explore CBDP interactions
with CB1R at two distinct allosteric sites. The results revealed that key
residues at both allosteric sites contribute to CBDP-mediated modula-
tion of CB1 receptor activity. Furthermore, molecular dynamic simula-
tions supported a possible dual-site binding model, with analysis
revealing that CBDP forms strong and stable binding poses at both sites.
Finally, in vivo behavioral assays provided preliminary evidence con-
sistent with CB1R modulation by CBDP in the novel object recognition
test (NORT) at a single dose, while acknowledging that more in-depth
future in vivo evaluation is necessary.

Computational prediction of the CB1R binding site for CBDP
To further investigate the interaction proﬁle and stability of the CBDP in
the CB1R –THC–complex at the ORG27569 and intracellular allosteric
sites, we conducted a 500 nanosecond (ns) all-atom MD simulation using
Desmond. All the simulations were performed in triplicate using dif-
ferent initial seed velocities. Supplementary Figs. 3 –4 illustrate that both
protein and CBDP conformations remained stable throughout 500 ns
simulations, with minimal deviations across three independent MD
simulation runs at the ORG27569 and intracellular allosteric sites. Since
no signi ﬁcant variation was observed across the three runs, only
ensemble averages at both sites are discussed and shown in Fig. 5. The
C-α RMSD of CB1R equilibrated within ~50 ns and remained stable
thereafter, with average values of 2.21 ± 0.38 Å at the ORG27569 site and
1.79 ± 0.19 Å at the intracellular site (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 2).
The ligand RMSD values were also low, averaging 1.44 ± 0.44 Å and
1.27 ± 0.46 Å at the ORG27569 and intracellular sites, respectively
(Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 2), indicating that CBDP maintained a
well-deﬁned binding pose at both sites (Supplementary Figs.3–4, Fig. 5b).
Minor ﬂuctuations in the positioning of CBDP were observed due to
movements in the terminus atoms of ﬂexible heptyl chain, and iso-
propylidene moieties. Nevertheless, consistently low ligand RMSD values
(<2.0 Å) con ﬁrmed that CBDP remained stably bound to CB1R
throughout the simulations at both allosteric sites (Fig. 5b).
Residue-level stability of the allosteric pockets at ORG27569 and
intracellular sites
The ensemble averaged root mean squareﬂuctuation (RMSF) analyses of
C-α atoms of CB1R provided insights into the stability of
CB1R–THC–CBDP complexes at the ORG27569 and intracellular
allosteric sites (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Figs. 5 –10). Both complexes
exhibited overall stability, with no signiﬁcant ﬂuctuations in the RMSF of
critical residues involved in CBDP binding at either site (Fig. 5c, Sup-
plementary Figs. 5 –10). The ensemble RMSF values for C- α atoms of
CB1R at ORG27569 site (1.13 ± 0.70 Å) and at the intracellular site
(1.01 ± 0.51 Å) (Supplementary Table 2) indicated that the protein
structure remained conformationally stable throughout the simulations,
apart from the residues within unstructured loop region, especially in
intracellular loop 3 (IC3). The overall secondary structure content
remained high, particularly in α-helical regions, consistent with a com-
pact and stable receptor conformation during CBDP binding. The
ensemble averaged RMSF of CBDP itself was also analyzed, revealing
marked stability across its cyclohexene and phenol ring, while higher
ﬂuctuations were observed along the distal heptyl chain and the iso-
propylidene substituent at the ORG27569 (0.79 ± 0.27 Å) and intracel-
lular (0.96 ± 0.55 Å) allosteric sites (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 5d). A
signiﬁcant increase in ﬂuctuation was noted sequentially along the heptyl
chain extending from the phenol ring (Fig. 5d), as expected. Additional
contribution to ligand ﬂexibility arose from the methyl and iso-
propylidene moieties attached on the cyclohexene ring, collectively
accounting for the observed RMSF variations in CBDP.

Discussion
THC exhibits therapeutic beneﬁts, yet its psychoactivity limits clinical use.
CBD can function as a NAM of CB1R in vitro, suggesting that structurally
related compounds could enhance therapeutic outcomes by mitigating
CB1R-mediated psychoactivity. CBDP, a naturally occurring CBD homolog
isolated fromC. sativaFM2 chemovar, has remained uncharacterized in this
context. This study identiﬁed CBDP as an NAM of CB1R. In vitro assays
demonstrated that CBDP displays minimal agonist activity, inhibits
CP55,940-induced CB1R activation in a manner consistent with non-
competitive antagonists (e.g., sub-unity Schild slope and functional proﬁle
in cAMP CB1R mutant studies), and alters orthosteric ligand binding
sa t
CB1R. Mutagenesis studies revealed altered binding proﬁles for CBDP at
both extrahelical and intracellular sites within the CB1R. Alanine sub-
stitutions at the extrahelical ORG27569-binding pocket (H154
2.41, W2414.50,
and F2374.46)s i g n iﬁcantly enhanced [³H]-SR141716A binding in the pre-
sence of CBDP, suggesting that these residues are critical determinants of its
allosteric modulation. Similarly, mutation of Y153
2.40 and D4038.49 at the
intracellular site impaired CBD P-mediated displacement of [³H]-
SR141716A, emphasizing the central role of these residues in intracellular
recognition. Together, theseﬁndings support a possible dual-site binding
model, wherein CBDP can engage boththe TMH4/5 extrahelical region and
an intracellular site near helix 8.
Computational analyses further su bstantiated this dual-site model,
revealing stable binding poses of CBDP at both allosteric sites, with key
hydrophobic and water-mediated interactions supporting the mutagenesis
ﬁndings. Residues such as W241
4.50 and D4038.49 formed transient or water-
mediated contacts with CBDP, providing a mechanistic explanation for the
augmented effects observed upon alanine substitution.
The NAM properties of CBDP were further evaluated in vivo. At a 1:1
ratio (10 mg kg−1 each), co-administration of CBDP with THC was asso-
ciated with modest improvements in recognition index performance
relative to THC alone in the NORT, notably without compromising other
pharmacological effects, such as antinociception in the hot plate test. These
ﬁndings suggest that CBDP may selectively modulate speciﬁcT H C - i n d u c e d
behavioral outcomes under de ﬁned experimental conditions, while pre-
serving its analgesic attributes. However, as CBDP pharmacokinetic proﬁles
and brain penetration were not characterized in this study, a direct corre-
lation between in vitro potency and in vivo ef ﬁcacy remains to be
established.
A limitation of this study ’s in vitro work is that our focus was nar-
rowed to radioligand binding using mutant hCB1R with an antagonist
(SR141716A) and one functional assay (modulation of CP55,940 ’s
inhibition of cAMP accumulation). Although a comprehensive assess-
ment of multiple signaling pathways (e.g., β-arrestin2 recruitment, Ca
2+
release, [3H]-CP55,940 radioligand binding with hCB1R mutants, and/or
use of other radioligand agonists) was not conducted, these data provide
clear proof of concept for the NAM activity and putative binding sites of
CBDP. Additional radioligand studies assessing [ 3H]-CP55,940 binding
at hCB1R mutants will be pursued in future work. Similarly, computa-
tional data used a single intermediate structure of CB1R (6KQI) and these
data could be expanded upon by future work exploring other CB1R
activation states. Lastly, our in vivo ﬁndings suggest that CBDP may
selectively modulate speci ﬁc THC-induced behavioral outcomes under
the experimental conditions tested. However, other allosteric modulators
are known to display probe dependence when using different orthosteric
agonists. While testing probe dependence exceeds our current resources,
the in vitro and in vivo NAM potential of CBDP could be further
explored in future work by assessing other agonists and directly testing
the role of CB1R in our observations (i.e., through use of CB1R knockout
animals). To our knowledge, this study represents the ﬁrst in vitro
through in vivo reporting of CB1R NAM activity by a plant-derived
cannabinoid, CBDP.
The combined experimental and computational ﬁndings provide
converging evidence that CBDP exhi bits NAM-like effects through dual
allosteric engagement of CB1R in vitro, with modest but informative in vivo
correlates. Consequently, these data support further investigation of CBDP
and related scaffolds as chemical tools and lead-like modulators for CB1R
allosteric mechanisms relevant to CNS and pain-related pharmacology.

Open ﬁeld test (total horizontal beam breaks). Immediately following

Hot plate and tail ﬂick assessments. For the hot plate assay, mice were
placed on a hot plate (IITC Life Science, CA, USA) with a 30-s resting
interval, and the latency to ﬂutter or lick hind paws or perform an escape
response was recorded. The heat source was increased from room tem-
perature to 60 °C at a rate of 2.5 °C/s. If no attempt at these measures were
made, animals were removed from the hot plate after 45 s. Each mouse
was measured twice on the same hot plate. For the tail ﬂick assay, mice
were lightly restrained and placed on a tailﬂick analgesia meter (IITC Life
Science, CA, USA) twice with 20-s resting intervals and were exposed to
radiant heat. The latency of the tail to ﬂick was recorded. If no attempt at
this response was made, animals were removed from the tail ﬂick meter
after 10 s.
Catalepsy and core body temperature . Each mouse had its forepaws
placed on a raised bar (4 cm high) for the catalepsy bar test, and their
latency either to withdraw forepaws from the bar or to climb onto the bar
was recorded to the nearest second. Mice were removed from the bar after
30 s if no response was observed.
Core body temperature was recorded by inserting a rectal probe with a
connected thermocouple thermometer (Cole-Parmer, Clifton, IL, USA) to
an approximate depth of 2 cm and was expressed as the difference between
basal and post-injection temperatures.
Novel object recognition . Thirty minutes prior to the training phase,
mice were acclimatized to the testing room. For the training phase, each
mouse was placed in an open ﬁeld with two identical objects (Stoelting,
IL, USA) placed approximately ﬁve cm apart. Mice were given ten
minutes to explore the objects. Mice were then returned to the home cage
and given a 4-h inter-trial interval (ITI). After the four-hour ITI, one of
the identical objects was replaced with a novel object, and mice were then
given ten minutes to explore the objects. The length of time exploring
each object in both the training and testing phase was recorded.
Statistical analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad
Prism (v. 10.4.1). All values are presented as mean ± SEM with n =7 –12
animals/group. Analgesia in the hot plate assay was expressed as the
percent maximal possible effect [% MPE = (post drug latency —basal
latency) / (45 s − basal latency) × 100]. Antinociception in the tail ﬂick
assay was expressed as the percent maximal possible effect [% MPE =
(post drug latency—basal latency) / (10 s − basal latency) × 100]. Novel
object recognition was expressed as the proportion of time spent with the
novel object as a function of all the time spent exploring [% time with
novel object = time with novel object / (time with familiar object + time
with novel object) × 100]. The change in novel object recognition between
the training and testing sessions was expressed as object recognition in
the testing session minus object recognition in the training session.
