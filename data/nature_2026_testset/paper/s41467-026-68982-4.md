Hydroxyl Radical Protein Footprintingis a powerful tool to probe protein
higher-order structure, protein-protein and protein-ligand interactions. It is
mostly performed in vitro, but recent advances have extended its use to live
cells, nematodes, and 3D cultures. However, to the best of our knowledge,
application in living mammalian tissues has not been accomplished. Here, we
present the successful use of radical protein footprinting (RPF) in mammalian
whole blood from wild-type and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) mice. Using
persulfate photoactivated with the FOXPhotolysis System, we achieve effec-
tive protein labeling without signiﬁcant disruption to blood cell morphology.
An optimized quenching protocol eliminates background labeling. We report
oxidative modiﬁcations for the eleven most abundant proteins detected,
revealing disease-associated conformational changes in multiple proteins. We
validate that in-blood RPF identiﬁes changes in protein structure resulting
from complement activation and increased transferrin iron saturation in T2DM
mice. These ﬁndings demonstrate the feasibility of RPF in mammalian blood
and create opportunities for structural proteomics in preclinical models and
clinical samples.

Selection of a stable radical precursor for whole blood
Most previous cell-based and in vivo radical footprinting experiments
have used hydrogen peroxide-based FPOP for fast radical
generation
10,15. However, blood notably displays high catalase
activity16, which complicates the use of hydrogen peroxide as a radical
precursor. We tested four catalase inhibitors to determine if catalase
and/or peroxidase activity in whole blood could be suf ﬁciently
reduced without altering the gross physiological properties of the
blood sample. We also assessed gas generation in blood from Na
2S2O8,
previously utilized for in-cell FPOP 13. Of the four inhibitors, hydro-
xylamine was the most effective, with a 95% reduction in catalase
activity at 200 mM concentration, 5:1 v/v blood to inhibitor stock, and
room temperature, as seen in Supplementary Fig. 2a. Thiosemicarba-
zide (TSC) and hydroxylamine induced gross changes in blood col-
oration and constitution, clearly disrupting the native blood
environment. At a more physiologically relevant 20:1 v/v, substantial
catalase activity remained. Importantly, none of the four inhibitors
prevented foam generation in the blood at concentrations compatible
with physiological conditions. Barring the identiﬁcation of some other
effective inhibitor, HRPF is likely not feasible in whole blood with H
2O2
as a radical precursor. Conversely, when Na 2S2O8 was spiked into
blood at ﬁnal 200 mM concentration as shown in Supplementary
Fig. 2b, c, no gas generation or gross changes in blood properties were
observed. Additionally, a key advantage of Na
2S2O8 is the absence of
intrinsic signaling activity within cells. In contrast, hydrogen peroxide
presents a limitation due to its established involvement in intracellular
signaling pathways that can elicit biological responses 13. Therefore,
Na2S2O8 was used for all in-blood RPF experiments.

However, compound changes of Na 2S2O8 concentration and either
lamp voltage or scavenger concentration must carefully account for
the inherent Abs
265 of Na2S2O8. We recommend only changing lamp

Selection of an effective quench solution for SO4
−
Insufﬁcient scavenging of slower-acting reactive oxygen/nitrogen
species (ROS/RNS) could allow for secondary oxidations, which are not
formed directly during the fast-labeling event by reaction of SO₄
−• and
•OH with the protein target, but instead arise from less reactive ROS/
RNS generated as secondary intermediates from the initial radical
chemistry
24,25. An effective quench is critical as secondary oxidation
after primary radical modiﬁcation can introduce artifactual modiﬁca-
tions to non-native structures 26. Recent observations suggest that
standard HRPF quenching protocols are not suf ﬁcient for SO 4
−• RPF
(L.M. Jones, unpublished results), contrary to previous reports 12,13.I n
this study, two quench solution systems were tested. Quench solutionⅠ
contained 200 mM N, N ′-Dimethylthiourea (DMTU) and 70 mM Met
amide, a standard HRPF quench that has been demonstrated effective
for photoactivated H
2O2
27. Quench solution Ⅱ included 200 mM imi-
dazole in addition to 200 mM DMTU and 70 mM methionine amide.
The efﬁcacy of the Na
2S2O8 quench solutions was assessed by quan-
tifying secondary oxidation products. To induce secondary oxidation,
Na2S2O8 was photoactivated and subsequently collected into quench
solutions already containing myoglobin and GluB. Quench efﬁcacy was
assessed at ﬂash lamp voltages of 900 and 0 V. Persulfate can oxidize
some organics directly, without involving radical species19, generating
background oxidation. In the 0 V condition, myoglobin or GluB was
injected into the FOX with Na
2S2O8 before collecting in a quench to
test background oxidation and the ability of an effective quench to
limit it.
The results of these quenching experiments are shown in Fig. 3
and Supplementary Fig. 3. We detected seven peptides from myoglo-
bin. During electrospray ionization, signi ﬁcant in-source oxidation
occurred for peptide 2–17, even in samples without Na
2S2O8.T h i si n -
source oxidation can be differentiated from pre-existing oxidation by
the perfect chromatographic co-elution of the in-source oxidation
product with the unmodiﬁed peptide. These in-source oxidation pro-
ducts are consistent in intensity and can be observed in the no-
Na
2S2O8 controls. Some peptides exhibited a signi ﬁcant increase in
background oxidation in quench solution I, which only contains DMTU
and methionine amide. For instance, peptide 120–134 showed a three
to fourfold increase in modi ﬁcation in quenches lacking imidazole.
Quenches with 200 mM imidazole showed no signiﬁcant difference in
peptide modiﬁcation with or without Na
2S2O8. Moreover, there was
not a signiﬁcant increase in oxidation when myoglobin was injected in-
line with Na 2S2O8 without illumination as reported by Gau et al. 12.
These results indicate that a quench solution containing DMTU,
methionine amide, and imidazole effectively prevents secondary oxi-
dation artifacts and limits background oxidation in SO
4
−• RPF experi-
ments on the FOX system.

Effect of Na2S2O8 on blood cell morphology and hemolysis
To determine if SO4
−• RPF preserved native-like conditions in stabilized
whole blood before photoactivation, murine blood was mixed (9:1, V/
V) with either 2 M Na
2S2O8 or NaCl. Murine blood cell morphology was
visualized with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope and RBCs cell size was
measured using ImageJ software. Results are shown in Fig. 4.I nc o n -
ditions of 2 M NaCl or Na
2S2O8 addition, a slight morphological
alteration of the erythrocytes was evidenced by few spicules pro-
truding from the surface of the cell membrane attributable to echi-
nocytes. However, no signiﬁcant changes were observed in RBCs cell
size caused by treatment of 2 M Na
2S2O8 compared to that of the equal
concentration of NaCl (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). To further
determine the effect of Na 2S2O8 on RBCs membrane integrity, the
hemolysis ratio (%) was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4g, blood cells
mixed with 2 M Na2S2O8 caused less than 2% hemolysis and showed no
signiﬁcant differences (Supplementary Table 3) from treatment with
equal concentration of NaCl, indicating that treatment of 2 M Na2S2O8
is non-hemolytic to RBCs.

the clearance of immune complexes. Activation of complement pro-
ceeds through three distinct pathways, all of which intersect at the
proteolytic processing of complement component C3, yielding the
effector fragment C3b. Conversion of C3 into C3b represents the
pivotal event that drives complement activation
31–37. This transforma-
tion from C3 to C3b is accompanied by extensive conformational
changes. As shown in Fig.6, the native C3 is organized into 13 structural
domains. Upon activation, these domains undergo varying degrees of
conformational reorganization. Some regions display relatively subtle
positional shifts, exempliﬁed by the macroglobulin (MG) 7 and MG8
domains, whereas others undergo pronounced, large-scale rearran-
gements, most notably the thioester-containing domain (TED) and the
complement C1r/C1s, UEGF, BMP1 (CUB) domain
33,36.
Two key conformational changes stand out during the comple-
ment activation. The most dramatic involves the TED domain, which
undergoes a substantial ~85 Å translation, thereby uncovering the
intramolecular thioester that is buried in the native C3 structure. In the
activated C3b form, the TED shifts into proximity with the MG1
domain, leading to the exposure of previously inaccessible surfaces
while also creating new interaction interfaces. These newly formed and
revealed regions enable binding to other complement components
that participate in both ampliﬁcation and regulation of the cascade. A
second major change is initiated by cleavage of C3 at the
Arg726–Ser727 bond by C3 convertases. This reaction releases the
anaphylatoxin (ANA) domain from the N-terminus of the α-chain,
producing the larger C3b molecule alongside the small soluble frag-
ment C3a. In the intact C3 protein, the ANA domain contributes
indirectly to structural stability by maintaining the relative positioning
of the MG8 domain and supporting the TED–MG8 interface. Loss of the
ANA domain destabilizes this arrangement, triggering rotational
movements of the MG7 and MG8 domains and permitting outward
displacement of both the CUB domain and the TED
34–36. By our RPF
result, peptide 75 –85 and 905 –913, which locates in MG1 and MG7
domain respectively, was signiﬁcantly protected in T2DM. In native C3,
peptide 75–85 is fully solvent-exposed, making it readily accessible to
oxidative radicals. In C3b, however, the relocation of the TED domain
brings it into direct contact with the MG1 domain at this region. As a
result, peptide 75–85 becomes buried within the interface and is no
longer solvent-accessible (shown in Fig.6a–d). While peptide 905–913
lies at the junction between the MG7 and CUB domains. In C3b, release
of the ANA domain alters the position of the CUB domain, causing this
region to become shielded and less accessible to solvent (Fig.6a, b, g,
and h). On the other hand, we found that peptide 741–748 was strongly
exposed in T2DM. In native C3, this region is buried within the core of
the MG ring, located immediately adjacent to the C3 convertase clea-
vage site and positioned against the ANA and TED domains, which
together shield it from solvent access. In C3b, however, convertase
cleavage removes the entire ANA domain and repositions the TED
domain, generating an open conformation that renders peptide
741–748 fully exposed (Fig. 6a, b, e, and f). The conformational
alterations detected across these three peptides are collectively con-
sistent with increased activation of C3 in T2DM relative to WT.
To validate that the conformational changes in C3 detected by our
RPF approach reﬂect complement activation in T2DM, we assessed the
amount of the activation of the complement cascade by measuring
circulating native C3 and its activated products (C3b, iC3b, and C3c,
hereafter collectively referred to as C3b in reference to the epitope the
ELISA antibody was raised against) in T2DM and WT mice by ELISA. The
results are shown in Fig. 6i, j and Supplementary Tables 9 –11. Inter-
estingly, both plasma C3 and C3b levels were signiﬁcantly elevated in
T2DM mice compared with WT controls. However, while C3 level was
only about twofold higher in T2DM, C3b exhibited a striking sixfold
increase. This disproportionate rise in C3b indicates markedly
enhanced activation of the complement cascade in T2DM relative to
WT. These ﬁndings align with prior studies showing that complement
signaling is altered in diabetes. Increased complement activity results
in greater cleavage of C3 to C3b, which contributes to local in ﬂam-
mation, increased opsonization, and the development of insulin
resistance
37–41. Evidence of activation of C3 has been reported to
enhance pancreatic β-cell function and survival and to help maintain
glucose homeostasis in T2DM 42. Overall, the markedly activated
complement cascade in T2DM explains the conformational changes in
C3 detected by our in-blood SO4

suggest that distinct, disease-associated alterations in protein surface
solvent accessibility are detectable by in-blood RPF.

Tf, commonly referred to as serotransferrin, is a circulating glyco-
protein responsible for regulating systemic iron balance by delivering
iron from sites of uptake to cells throughout the body via the
bloodstream43–47.A ss h o w ni nF i g .7a, b, structurally, Tf is composed of
an N-terminal and a C-terminal lobe. Each lobe is further divided into
two subdomains that together form a metal-binding cleft, enabling a
single Tf molecule to coordinate up to two ferric ions. Tf pre-
dominantly adopts two distinct and stable conformational states
depending on its iron-loading status
43,47–50. In the iron-free (apo) form,
the protein assumes an open con ﬁguration in which the lobes are
exposed to the surrounding solvent. Upon iron binding, Tf transitions
to the iron-loaded (holo) form, characterized by a closed architecture
in which each lobe folds around its bound metal ion. In this closed
state, the coordinated iron becomes sequestered approximately 10 Å
beneath the protein surface
43,50,51.
Conformational changes were detected in three peptides by our
RPF approach, two of which (peptide 548 –554 and 555 –565) showed
pronounced protection in T2DM, followed by a moderately exposed
peptide immediately downstream. As shown in Fig. 7c, these two
adjacent, strongly protected peptides reside in the C-lobe, directly
adjacent to the iron binding site. Based on their position and protec-
tion pattern, we hypothesize that Tf iron saturation is increased in
T2DM, leading to enhanced structural stabilization around the metal-
binding cleft. Such elevated iron saturation would expand the dynamic
pool of molecules undergoing the open-to-closed transition and could
account for the unexpectedly strong protection observed in this
region. In order to test this hypothesis, we evaluated Tf iron binding in
T2DM and WT mice by measuring serum iron, TIBC, and Tf iron
saturation. As illustrated in Fig.7d, serum iron levels were signiﬁcantly
elevated in T2DM, whereas TIBC was markedly reduced. Consequently,
the percentage of Tf saturation was substantially higher in T2DM
(Supplementary Table 12), reﬂecting a shift in the distribution of apo-
versus holo-Tf. Interestingly, the C-lobe typically loads ironﬁrst under
physiological conditions
44, which explains the conformational changes
we observed in C-lobe under heavier iron saturation condition of
T2DM. These ﬁndings are consistent with previous studies demon-
strating perturbed iron metabolism, increased Tf turnover, and a
positive association between Tf iron saturation and diabetes risk
52–56.
Together, these results validated that the conformational changes
detected by our RPF approach in Tf arise from elevated Tf iron
saturation in T2DM. More broadly, they highlight that our RPF method
is capable of detecting complex, physiologically driven conforma-
tional processes that depend on the native biological matrix.

Discussion
A driving motivation for this study was to address unmet technological
needs in the structural characterization of biotherapeutics and
disease-related proteins in complex clinical matrices. T2DM represents
a prime example of a condition where structural proteomics could
yield transformative insights. T2DM is characterized by complex sys-
temic changes involving chronic inﬂammation, insulin resistance, and
metabolic dysfunction, processes in which protein conformation and
post-translational modiﬁcations play critical roles
57–59.C o m p l e m e n tC 3
has emerged as a contributor but also a potential effector of disease
p r o g r e s s i o ni nT 2 D M60–63. Elevated complement C3 levels are asso-
ciated with insulin resistance, chronic low-grade in ﬂammation, and
metabolic dysfunction, hallmarks of T2DM 60,61,64. Its activation pro-
ducts, such as complement C3a, can impair insulin signaling and beta-
cell function, suggesting a direct role in metabolic dysregulation62.I n -
blood RPF found signiﬁcant differences in complement C3 topography
in T2DM mice compared to WT, consistent with increased activation of
complement C3 in T2DM. Na2S2O8-based RPF offers a powerful means
to probe conformational changes in proteins like complement C3
directly within blood, enabling the study of their structural states in
both healthy and diabetic conditions. Such structural insights could
reveal hidden aspects of disease biology, leading to the identiﬁcation
of early biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Beyond T2DM, in-blood
RPF holds potential for applications in a wide range of diseases where
protein structure and interactions are altered. Neurodegenerative
diseases, autoimmune conditions, and cancer are all characterized by
protein misfolding, aggregation, or abnormal interaction
networks
65–70, phenomena that can be effectively studied through in
situ structural proteomics.
Future technical enhancements, including improved intracellular
labeling, integration with other bio ﬂuids, and development of an
automated RPF workﬂow, will broaden the method’s applicability and
reproducibility. Work is ongoing to enhance the ability of Na2S2O8 to

Another current limitation of note is the inability to robustly

modiﬁcations (PTMs). Additions of oth er potential differential mod-
iﬁcations to the RPF search space quickly increases the complexity of
the search in a geometric fashion. Because of the near-global reactivity
of the hydroxyl radical to all amino acids, just searching for these
oxidized peptides in the absence of differential PTMs is a challenging
problem. In this study, all detected peptides were searched against
UniProt for previously reported PTMs, and none of the peptides
identiﬁed from C3 or Tf contained reported modi ﬁcations. Future
work improving search methods to allow for robust measurement of
RPF results in the presence of PTMs would allow for a broader subset
of the proteome to be probed with this technique.
In-blood RPF holds signiﬁcant potential for both pre-clinical and
clinical applications by enabling direct structural analysis of proteins
within complex biological matrices. This approach offers a powerful
means to identify conformational biomarkers of disease, revealing
structural changes in key proteins that may underlie or reﬂect patho-
logical processes. It is well-suited for use with clinical samples, such as
blood, and can be extended to pre-clinical animal models to track
disease progression or therapeutic response. Additionally, in-blood
RPF provides valuable insights into the structural stability, aggrega-
tion, and degradation of protein pharmaceuticals under near-
physiological conditions, supporting structural pharmacology efforts
in drug development.
In-blood RPF represents a signi ﬁcant advancement in structural
proteomics, enabling detailed analysis of protein conformation and
interaction in complex clinical matrices such as whole blood. Its
application to disease-relevant proteins in T2DM underscores its
potential to provide mechanistic insights and identify early biomarkers
and therapeutic targets.

Statistics and reproducibility
All experiments were performed in a minimum of three independent
replicates. No data points were omitted from the analyses. Experi-
ments were conducted without randomization, and investigators were
aware of group assignments throughout both experimental execution
and outcome evaluation. All data are expressed as the mean ± standard
deviation (SD), unless otherwise stated. A two-tailed unpaired t-test
was used to compare two groups. One-way analysis of variance
(ANOVA) followed by Dunnett ’s test was used to compare three or
more groups ( P < 0.05 was considered statistically signi ﬁcant differ-
ence, NS denotes not signi ﬁcant). GraphPad Prism 10.0 was used for
analysis.
