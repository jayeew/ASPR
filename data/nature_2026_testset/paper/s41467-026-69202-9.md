Realizing multi-mode programmable dynamic tunable persistent lumines-
cence within a single solid is promising for multi-dimensional information
storage and encryption applications. However, coupling luminescent centers
with differentiated defect states remains a challenge. Here we report trans-
parent glass ceramics that exhibit photo/thermally dynamic tunable afterglow.
A lithium-ion doping-assisted phase separation principle is developed to
control the precipitation of defective non-stoichiometric nanocrystals
(Zn
1.7SiO4: Li) in glass matrix, constructing a biphasic microenvironment with
differentiated defect states. The persistent luminescence color can be
manipulated by simultaneously engineering the distributions of Mn2+ activa-
tors in the amorphous glass matrix and nanocrystals, and the mechanism is
discussed. The developed transparent composites exhibit excellent hardness
of up to 10 gigapascal and high thermal stability, which is applicable for harsh
conditions. This work pioneers a strategy for modulating dynamic afterglow in
a single solid and inspires more potential applications in muti-dimensional
information storage and encryption.
Persistent luminescence (PersL) is an intriguing photophysical phe-
nomenon with the gradual charge recombination after
photoexcitation1. With the ability to storage energy for periods of time,
applications of PersL materials have spread from civil cases, i.e.,
emergency lights, decoration displays, etc., to advanced scienti ﬁc
applications including information storage, optical encryption, medi-
cal delivery identiﬁcation and bioimaging
2– 8. However, conventional
PersL materials, encompassing inorganic, organic, and hybrid materi-
als, generally exhibit static monochromatic emission, limiting their
ability to address the multifunctional requirements in advanced
optoelectronic devices.
Currently, great efforts have been made to develop dynamic PersL
materials, which can ﬂexibly respond to diverse external stimuli such
as excitation light, temperature, humidity, electricﬁeld or mechanical
stress, and translate these responses into detectable afterglow outputs
with changeable properties (intensity, color or lifetime)
9– 12. Typically,
the host-guest doping strategy and molecular engineering have been
proved as two effective methods for dynamic tunable PersL in organic
materials
13– 15. However, these materials inevitably suffer from both
unstable triplet excitons that restrict phosphorescence to seconds and
poor environmental tolerance (e.g., heat/light) that results in degraded
luminescence efﬁciency during long-term use. In another case, doping
ions to engineer trap states and modifying energy transfer process
between multi-emission centers are employed to realize dynamic
tunable PersL in inorganic materials and hybrid materials
16– 20.F o r

Transparent glass, with excellent physicochemical stability
(including corrosion/heat resistance) and mechanical property, has
long been established as an exceptional medium for three-dimensional
(3D) information storage and anti-counterfeiting applications
21– 23.
Especially, controllable nanocrystal precipitation and ﬂexible ion
doping in the rigid glass matrix provide feasible pathways to tune the
optical properties and functionalities within a monolithic glass/glass
ceramic24,25. Engineering trap states among different emission centers
in the glassy phase or crystal phase to modify the trapping/de-trapping
properties of photo-generated carriers promises to realize the
dynamic color-tunable PersL in these transparent solids, potentially
enabling applications in high-dimensional information storage and
encryption
26. However, current crystal-engineered PersL glass ceramic
usually yields static PersL color due to the similar environments and
homogeneous defect states among emission centers in crystal
domains. Consequently, no reliable spatiotemporally dynamic tunable
PersL has been explored in a single-component glass-ceramic system.
Herein, we report a type of transparent glass-ceramic character-
ized by the precipitation of non-stoichiometric nanocrystals. Using a
phase-separation strategy, Mn
2+ activators are spatially conﬁned within
the glassy phase and the non-stoichiometric nanocrystalline phase,
which grants them distinct coordination environments and defect-
state properties to enable the multi-mode programmable dynamic
tunable PersL within a single glass-ceramic solid. The obtained PersL
color can be ﬂexibly manipulated by altering the excitation wave-
lengths and ambient temperatures. Furthermore, the transparent
composites show excellent mechanical properties and thermal stabi-
lity, enabling great potential for applications under harsh conditions.
Results
Li+-doping assisted nonstoichiometric phase transformation
The phase-separation has been demonstrated as an effective principle
for tuning optical properties of materials
27,28. Here, a type of trans-
parent glass ceramic with non-stoichiometric crystals in the glass
matrix was created based on a phase separation method, and distinct
crystal-ﬁeld and defect-state environments were generated for acti-
vator ions through ion doping or thermal process adjustment. The
ﬂexible network structure of non-stoichiometric crystals offers far
greaterﬂexibility in defect-state design, thus having more potential for
designing dynamic tunable PersL than their rigid stoichiometric
counterparts. The detailed procedure is schematically displayed in
Fig. 1. Through heat-treatment-induced phase separation, defective
non-stoichiometric nanocrystals are deliberately designed into the
glass matrix, with activators being selectively incorporated into the
biphasic system featuring differentiated defect states. Therefore,
activators at different doping sites will exhibit distinct carrier
dynamics (trapping and de-trapping), thereby enabling dynamic tun-
able PersL. Where Mn
2+ ions were chosen as the activators to modulate
the luminescence properties through controlled phase separation and
defect engineering, whose emissions are highly dependent on the
crystal ﬁeld strength
29,30.

on Mn2+-doped (0.5 mol%) zinc silicate glass material were conducted.

Dynamic tunable PersL properties in KLI3
As anticipated, obvious dynamic tunable PersL is realized in this Mn2+-
activated defective Zn 1.7SiO4: Li nanocrystal-precipitated glass-cera-
mic, conﬁrming the effectiveness of the non-stoichiometric strategy
based on phase engineering. The programmable trapping of charge
carriers at the heterogeneous defect states within the dual-phase
structure (glass and Zn
1.7SiO4: Li nanocrystals) can be achieved by
manipulating either the excitation wavelengths to alter the carrier
transition pathways or the charging temperatures to control the cri-
tical depth for stable trap ﬁlling. By which, the carriers ’ de-trapping
properties (population and duration) after excitation can be modiﬁed.
This in turn alters the intensity ratio of the two emission bands, thereby
enabling continuous tuning of the afterglow chromaticity. Concretely,
as shown in Fig. 2d, a reversible PersL color from green to orange was
detected by changing the excitation wavelength from 254 nm to
365 nm. And, as the charging temperature increased, afterglow color
pre-excited by 365 nm for 5 min shifted from orange to green (Fig.2e),
with the relevant CIE color coordinates changing (Fig.2f) from (0.483,
0.489) to (0.410, 0.551). The corresponding normalized integrated
temperature-dependent PersL spectra (Supplementary Fig. 7) revealed

Across tested temperatures (298 K, 373 K and 423 K), the sample
exhibited a progressive afterglow color shift to yellow/green within the
ﬁrst 5 s of decay time (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Movies 1– 3) after being

was necessary to induce PersL. There was also no additional light
irradiation to induce de-trapping after cessation of excitation. Conse-
quently, the PersL in the current work is characterized by persistent
luminescence but not thermally stimulated luminescence or photo-
stimulated luminescence.
Compared with reported dynamic PersL materials (Table 1), this

systems, ≤423 K), (ii) an expansive operating temperature range of
~200 K (more suitable for practical applications), and (iii) a prolonged

Mechanism investigations of dynamic tunable PersL
The excitation spectra analyses (Supplementary Fig. 17) of dual-
emissions reveal that the operated excitation wavelengths (254 nm and
365 nm) separately activate two different kinds of charge transfer
transitions: (1) the charge transfer band (CTB) of O
2−-Mn2+ (appearing
as a broad band below 330 nm), and (2) the d-d transitions of Mn 2+
(manifested as a series of discrete bands beyond 330 nm) 51. Com-
paratively, the remaining Mn2+ ions in the glassy phase undergo rapid
excited-state energy transfer via phonon assistance, causing the
characteristic discrete excitation peaks to merge into broad bands
52.
Due to different charge transition paths, all glass ceramics exhibit
excitation-wavelength-dependent afterglow, displaying an orange-to-
green color evolution by changing excitation wavelength from 365 nm
to 254 nm. Carriers excited by high-energy photons (254 nm) can
approach the conduction band directly and receive the rapid and
saturated trapﬁlling from shallow to deep levels. The green afterglow
dominance in 254 nm-excited three-dimensional thermoluminescence
(3D-TL, Fig. 3g) heatmap of GC700 at all temperatures indicates a
higher trap concentration across the entire depth range in the crystal
phase than that in the glass phase. However, charge carriers photo-
excited by low-energy (365 nm) excitation in Fig.3f tend to induce less
efﬁcient trap ﬁlling from shallow to deep levels, mediated by con-
duction band (CB) transport and quantum tunneling (QT) in crystal
phase
53. The unavoidable continuous de-trapping of carriers from
deep traps throughout the entire temperature range also complicates
the identi ﬁcation of dominant luminescence centers coupled with
deep traps. This leads to the fact that the contribution of green
afterglow emission at high temperatures is not as pronounced as that
shown in Supplementary Fig. 7.

To clarify the potential mechanism of the dynamic PersL pro-
cess, a reasonable hypothesis has been proposed in Fig.4g. Under UV
excitation, charge carriers in the crystalline and glassy phases are
pumped to high-energy levels near the conduction band (CB)/
extended states (ES) via Mn
2+-mediated d-d transitions (under
365 nm) and localized CTB absorption (under 254 nm), respectively.
A portion of these carriers then transfers and relaxes to Mn
2+ 4 T1(G)
level, making a radiative transition to the6A1(S) ground state to cause
PL emissions. When shifting the excitation wavelength from 365 nm
to 254 nm, the much stronger Mn-O CTB absorption in the crystalline
phase compared to the glassy phase drives more ef ﬁcient energy
transfer to tetrahedrally coordinated Mn2+ ions, decisively enhancing
the dominance of green emissions to enable the tunable PL behavior
dependent on excitation wavelengths. Meantime, the other portion
of charge carriers is captured by localized trap states while migrating
through the conduction band and extended states. Under 254 nm
excitation (State 1), abundant photogenerated carriers rapidly
populate trap energy levels to approach saturation through CB/ES
transport in both phases. After the cessation of excitation, suf ﬁcient
carriers are released from shallow traps and return to Mn
2+ lumi-
nescent centers, generating the afterglow predominant by green
emissions due to the higher density of shallow traps in the crystalline
phase. In contrast at room temperature, 365 nm excitation (State 2)
with lower photon energy limits the number of carriers with suf ﬁ-
cient energy to overcome the potential barrier for CB/ES transport.
Instead, quantum tunneling to deep levels becomes the dominant
trapping route. The resulting non-saturated ﬁlling of trap states
signiﬁcantly suppresses the afterglow contribution of carriers
released from the lattice shallow traps at tetrahedral coordinated
Mn
2+, leading to an orange afterglow color. Furthermore, elevated
charging temperatures (State 3 and State 4) provide suf ﬁcient ther-
mal energy for carriers to overcome the potential barrier, promoting
the ﬁlling of the trap energy levels via CB/ES transport. However, the
inherent instability of shallow traps at high temperatures also
enhances the probability of carriers being captured by the deep trap
energy levels dominantly situated within the crystalline phase and
facilitates the dominance of green emissions, ultimately leading to
the charging-temperature-dependent tunable afterglow perfor-
mance. In addition, with the prolonged decay time, afterglow emis-
sions manifest as a gradual transition from shallow to deep traps.
Since the lattice Mn
2+ couples with deeper trap energy levels, it
exhibits a longer afterglow duration, thus driving the temporal shift
in the afterglow chromaticity.
Applications for information storage and dynamic encryption
Finally, the optical information storage and encryption applications of
the glass ceramic can be developed by leveraging its excitation-
dependent and temperature-dependent characteristics. The pattern-
ing design was readily achieved through a simple photomask process.
As demonstrated in Fig. 5a, under 365 nm excitation, the system
enabled multicolor information writing and reading from orange to
green as the temperature increased from 298 K to 423 K. Additionally,
in Fig. 5b, by rapidly switching the excitation wavelength (between
254 nm, 295 nm, 365 nm) within 3 s, reversible writing of different
PersL color information from dark green to yellowish-green and then
to orange could be realized. Importantly, unlike current materials,
which typically require the incorporation of multiple luminescent
components to achieve multicolor afterglow display, glass matrix
offers potential for the real-time controllable multicolor afterglow
owing to its excellent bulk excitation characteristics. As shown in
Fig. 5c, distinct thermalﬁelds were separately applied to the petal and
stem regions with a soldering iron. The temperature gradient between
areas induced differential PersL colors across the entire patterned
structure. Meanwhile, when implementing spatially controlled excita-
tion with a 254 nm and a 365 nm laser in Fig. 5d, the eagle-patterned
area excited by 254 nm exhibited green afterglow, while the branch-
patterned region displayed yellow afterglow after combined 254 nm
(1 s) and 365 nm (1 s) excitation or orange afterglow after single 365 nm
excitation. Finally, exploiting the intrinsic transparency of the glass
matrix enabled the successful realization of 3D PersL patterning
(Supplementary Fig. 21) through femtosecond laser direct writing,
which demonstrates its unique potential for advanced 3D optical
applications. Thus, our developed non-stoichiometric crystallization
strategy based on phase separation in glass matrix opens a new avenue
for the design of dynamic tunable PersL. The developed material
overcomes the limitations of multi-component material, enabling the
integration of multicolor PersL within a monolithic system by simply
modifying the excitation conditions. Combined with the intrinsic
transparency of glass, such material is highly promising for three-
dimensional information storage and encryption. And its inherent high
thermal stability and mechanical strength address key obstacles for
practical applications.

Methods
Material synthesis
The glass precursor powder, comprising 40ZnO-30SiO 2-10Al2O3-
10H3BO3-xLi2CO3-(10-x)K 2CO3-0.5MnCO3 (Aladdin, >99.9% purity,
x =0 – 4 mol%), was uniformly mixed for 10 min in an agate mortar. The
mixture was then loaded into a furnace and reacted at 1430 °C for
40 min under a reducing atmosphere. The fully reacted glass melt was
cast onto a preheated 300 °C steel plate and immediately transferred
to a 400 °C annealing furnace for thermal stress relief. The reducing
atmosphere throughout the experiment was maintained by using
coconut-shell activated carbon granules (1– 2 mm diameter) to prevent
the oxidation of Mn
2+. The obtained glass/glass ceramics were then cut,
ground, and polished for the necessary measurements. All glass/glass
ceramic samples for optical imaging in this work were 12 mm in
diameter.
Material characterizations
DSC tests were carried out with a heating rate of 7 K/min on a differ-
ential scanning calorimeter (NETZSCH, DSC404F3). XRD analyses were
performed by employing an Empyrean 200895 diffractometer with
Cu-Ka radiation. The TEM and HRTEM images were performed with a
transmission electron microscope (JEOL, JEM-2100F) at 200 kV accel-
erating voltage. FTIR spectroscopy analysis was carried out by a
Nicolet IS50 Fourier infrared spectrometer to detect the vibrations of
chemical bonds. XPS technique was carried out using a Thermo 250XI
equipment. Portable hand-held UV lamps (3 W, 254 nm; 3 W, 295 nm;
3 W, 365 nm) were employed to irradiate the glass /glass ceramics
while taking PL and PersL photographs. Room-temperature PL/PersL
spectra and variable-temperature PL/PersL spectra were gained from a
self-built confocal ﬂuorescence spectrophotometer equipped with a
365 nm lamp and a 254 nm lamp as the excitation light. EPR spectro-
scopy was performed to identify the unpaired electrons associated
with vacancy defects based on a Bruker A300 spectrometer equip-
ment. 3D-TL spectra and 2D-TL spectra were gained by using a
Guangzhou TOSL-3DS equipment at a heating rate of 5 K/s, with the
tested samples being pre-excited by UV excitation forﬁve minutes. The
afterglow decay curves of the glass ceramic at different charging
temperatures were obtained based on an Edinburgh FLS equipment.
The three-dimensional (3D) patterning within the glass ceramic was
achieved via a femtosecond laser (365 nm, power 2 W, repeti-
tion 11 kHz).
