Long-term pharmacological intervention is essential for managing chronic diseases, yet continuous
administration of antithrombotic agents often increases bleeding risk, posing a major clinical
challenge. Here, we designed a multifunctional drug delivery platform composed of large-aperture
mesoporous silica (Mas) coated with silk ﬁbroin (SF) or sustained heparin sodium (HS) release. The
well-deﬁned mesoporous structure enables high drug loading, while the biocompatible SF coating
regulates release kinetics, suppresses burst release, and prolongs therapeutic ef ﬁcacy. This dual
structural system enhances antithrombotic performance while mitigating hemorrhagic risk. In vitro
studies demonstrated improved hemocompatibility and reduced cytotoxicity of SF-Mas/HS
compared with free HS. In vivo thrombosis models further con ﬁrmed effective inhibition of thrombus
formation without excessive bleeding. By integrating inorganic mesoporous carriers with natural
protein coatings, SF-Mas/HS provides a safe and ef ﬁcient strategy for long-term antithrombotic
prevention, highlighting the potential of biomaterial-assisted delivery systems to balance efﬁcacy and
safety in chronic disease management.

Results and discussion
The synthesis and characterization of SF-Mas
The entire process to synthesize particles was shown in Fig. 1A. We ﬁrst
synthesized uniform Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Mes) with a diameter
of 80 nm (Fig.1B and Supplementary Fig. 1). Mesoporous silica with bigger-
aperture (Mas) was then obtained by using1,3,5-Trimethylbenzene (TMB),
and its diameter was about 100 nm (Figs.1C and S1). From the comparing
of transmission electron microscopy(TEM) images, we found the surface of
Mas was rougher than Mes, indicating the larger aperture on the surface. SF-
Mas (silk ﬁbroin-coated Mas) was obtained by the further modiﬁcation of
SF and the SF layer formed a coating on the surface of Mas (Fig.1D). The
scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of Mas and SF-Mas also
indicated the successful coating of SF (Supplementary Fig. 2). Following
pore enlargement and SF modiﬁcation, the particle size of the nanoparticles
gradually increased. The aperture size of mesoporous silica was changed
from 2 nm to 10 nm, representing the stronger drug loading ability (Fig.1E,
F). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) showed the character-
istic peak in different groups. The peak of amide in SF was observed in SF
and SF-Mas rather than Mas, conﬁrming the existence of SF coating in SF-
Mas (Fig.1G). The change of zeta potential also indicated the synthesis and
modiﬁcation of particles (Supplementary Fig. 3).
The drug loading and release of SF-Mas
SF-Ma/HS was constructed by loading HS into the porous structure of SF-
Mas. HS was incorporated into the solution during the modiﬁcation of the
SF coating, ensuring effective encap sulation of HS within the apertures.
Moreover, FTIR spectroscopy showe d the new absorption peak corre-
sponding to HS, the region highlighted around 1634 cm−1 represented the
characteristic peak of C=O, and the broad peak around 3281 cm−1 repre-
sented the binding stretching peak of OH, indicating the characteristic
associated with HS (Fig.1H). The result of microplate reader indicated that
SF-Mas/HS demonstrated increased absorption at the same wavelength as
HS, while the supernatant exhibited a decreased absorption at that wave-
length, conﬁrming the loading of HS after incubation (Fig.1I). The result of
release showed that SF-Mas had a slower release rate than Mas, indicating
the SF coating controls the release of HS (Fig.1J). Mas group released rapidly
within 24 h, while SF-Mas group kept releasing after 72 h. This indicated
that the SF coating effectively prolongs the retention of heparin sodium HS,
preventing the burst release of HS in a short time.
The biocompatibility test of SF-Mas
The process of cell culture was shown in Fig. 2A. CCK-8 and live/dead
staining were applied to evaluate the cytotoxicity of SF-Mas (Fig. 2B, C).
When the concentration was 20μg/mL, the cell viability between Mas and
SF-Mas was similar. However, when the concentration was up to 200 μg/
mL, the cell viability of Mas decreased signi ﬁcantly (Fig. 2B). We further
veriﬁed the cytocompatibility by live/dead staining at the concentration of
200 μg/mL. The result showed that cells were almost green in the SF-Mas
group, but more dead cells existed in the Mas group (Fig.2C). The hemolysis
test suggested that SF-Mas showed be tter blood compatibility when the
concentration increased (Fig.2D, E). The Mas easily brought damage to the
erythrocyte, causing hemolysis (Fig. 2F, G). The results validated that SF
coating improved the cytocompatibility and blood compatibility of SF-Mas,
reducing the risk for intravenous injection.
The antithrombotic activity of SF-Mas in vitro
The antithrombotic activity of SF-Mas was tested by blood anticoagulation
in the centrifuge tube, and the weight of blood clots was measured. We
found free SF-Mas had no antithrombotic function, which was the same as
the PBS group (Fig.3A). The weight of clots in PBS and SF-Mas group were
both about 83.3 mg (Fig.3B). When Mas or SF-Mas were loaded with HS,
they exhibited antithrombotic capacity, and the weight of clots was about
18.0 mg and 16.3 mg, respectively. The free HS performed the best antith-
rombotic ability in vitro, and the weight of the clot was about 10.0 mg. The
results indicated that Mas/HS, SF-Mas/HS, and HS all worked in preventing
thrombus in vitro. The superior performance of HS in vitro can be attributed
to its independence from the constraints of its short half-life, allowing it to
exert a sustained effect within the centrifuge tube.
The antithrombotic activity of SF-Mas in vivo
The antithrombotic activity of SF-Mas was evaluated in the carotid
thrombus of mice. H&E staining of vessels revealed distinct antithrombotic
efﬁcacy across the different treatment groups (Fig.3C). The antithrombotic
rate is determined by calculating the ratio of the vacant area to the total area
in the section. We found PBS and SF-Mas were ineffective in antith-
rombosis, and the antithrombotic rate was calculated about 4.9% and 8.5%,
respectively (Fig. 3D). Free HS had an antithrombotic effect, and its
antithrombotic rate was about 46.1% (Fig. 3D). It illustrated that free HS
prevented the formation of thrombus within a limited capacity. Free HS
would be metabolized rapidly in the mouse with poor effect, and a large
amount of HS was required for efﬁcient antithrombosis. Mas/HS improved
the utilization of HS, and its ant ithrombotic rate was 57.3% (Fig. 3D).
Compared with free HS, Mas/HS regulated the release of HS in the mice.
The interaction between the drug and the delivery system is limited to
adsorption. The SF-Mas/HS exhibited the best antithrombotic effect, and
the antithrombotic rate was about 85.1%. SF-Mas/HS improved the sus-
tained release effect of the HS, enhancing the prevention of the thrombus.
This could be because the movement of drug molecules is not only restricted
by the adsorption forces but also by the size of the membrane pores. The
thrombus weight measurements across the groups further conﬁrmed that
SF-Mas/HS exhibited the most potent antithrombotic effect (Fig.3E). After
treatment with SF-Mas/HS, the mass of thrombi detached from blood
vessels decreased from 8.3 mg to 1.1 mg.

The drug loading and release of SF-Mas
The drug loading occurred at the same time of coating SF to Mas. 5 mg of

trifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min to obtain the SF-Mas precipitation, and the
loading capacity was calculated by the amount of HS in the supernatant.
10 mg of Mas/HS and SF-Mas/HS were put into 10 mL of PBS solution
(pH = 7.2), shaking at 37 °C and 150 rpm. After shaking for 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 20,
24, 30, 40, 48, 60, and 72 h, the supernatant was collected, and the con-

The cytotoxicity and hemolysis test of SF-Mas
Fibroblast L929 cells were used to test the cytocompatibility of SF-Mas. Cells
were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per plate. 24 h
later, PBS, SF-Mas, and Mas (20, 200μg/mL) were added, respectively. After
incubation for 24 h, the medium in the plate was removed, and the cells were
washed with PBS for 3 times. Then 100μL of medium containing 10% CCK-
8 kit was added into plates and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. The medium
was collected, and its absorbance was measured at 450 nm wavelength.
Living/Dead staining was realized byCalcein-AM and PI staining kit. After
adding SF-Mas and Mas (200 μg/mL) into plates with cells for 24 h, the
medium was removed, and the premix solution (2μM Calcein-AM, 8 μM
PI) was added to plates, incubating for 30 min. Then the solution was
removed, and the cells were washed third with PBS. Living cells and dead
cells were observed by green and red ﬂuorescence, respectively. For
hemolysis evaluation, 1 mL of fresh blood was centrifuged to remove
supernatant (1500 ×g, 5 min) and re-suspended with 5 mL of PBS. 200μL
of Mas and SF-Mas (0, 10, 50, 100, 200 μg/mL) were added into 1 mL of
blood. Pure water was used as positive control. After incubator for 2 h at
37 °C, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation (2000 ×g,5m i n )a n d
the absorbance was measured at 540 nm. Hemolysis rate was calculated by
the following formula: hemo lysis % = (sample absorbance − PBS absor-
bance)/(positive control absorbance− PBS absorbance) × 100%.
