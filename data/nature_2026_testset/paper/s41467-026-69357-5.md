High electrical conductivity is crucial for electrics, electronics, communica-
tions. Eliminating defects such as grain boundaries and impurities is a primary
approach to enhancing electrical conductivity in metals. Although reducing
electron-phonon interaction can further contribute to electrical conductivity,
only a marginal enhancement is obtained by imposing external pressure even
high up to gigapascal. Here we report a contrary strategy to transforming
defects into conductive beneﬁts, by forming abundant defects of severely
distorted lattice via heterogeneous interface-assisted plastic deformation in
metal (copper). Such lattice distortions are generated under a non-extreme
condition, and cause internal giant local stress signiﬁcantly suppressing
electron-phonon coupling and thus reducing phonon-caused electron scat-
tering. Consequently, a striking bulk electrical conductivity of > 110% IACS is
obtained in the copper at room temperature, which is equivalent to the effect
of an external high pressure of about 10 gigapascals. Our work provides an
extendable approach for developing high-performance metal conductors.
Electrical energy is the main form of energy transport and con-
version. The development of high-performance conductor mate-
rials has the potential to signi ﬁcantly improve the ef ﬁciency
of systems and devices, reduce energy consumption, and increase
integration and power density. Taking an example, the electrical
conductivity of interconnects exceeding that of pure copper
can effectively improve signal transmission rate, reduce bit
error rate, and chip power consumption
1–5. According to Mat-
thiessen’s rule, electron scattering caused by phonon and
lattice defects, including vacancies, grain boundaries (GBs),
impurities, and dislocations, collectively deteriorates electrical
conductivity in metals
1,6–8. To minimize the deleterious effects of
the above electron scattering factors on electrical conductivity,
a multitude of methods have been employed, such as increasing
lattice integrity and inhibiting grain re ﬁnement, as well as
puriﬁcation.
Copper is the most widely used metal conductor material owing
to its high conductivity and low cost. However, a very limited incre-
ment in the electrical conductivity of Cu has been achieved since the
International Annealed Copper Standard (IACS, electrical conductivity
of Cu is 100% IACS) was established in 1913. For example, electrical
conductivity is less than 103% IACS in oxygen-free Cu with a purity as
high as 7N-level, and ~110% IACS in Cu single crystal without GBs
9,10.I n
recent years, some explorations have shown the potential in improving
electrical conductivity to a higher level by forming composites either
in bulk or on surface
2,11–13, and the enhancement of electrical con-
ductivity was mainly attributed to the doping effect, reducing electron
scattering on the Cu–C interface, enhancing thermal heat dissipation,

Essentially, the intrinsic electrical conductivity of some phase is
dominated by its electronic structure and energy band, which can be
inﬂuenced by applying external ﬁelds, including force, temperature,
and magnetism, etc., leading to some strategies such as strain engi-
neering and band engineering
14–17. For example, the compression-
shear stress on diamond changed charge redistribution and softened
phonon, resulting in the production of robust superconductivity
18.
Regarding metals, it was proved that applying pressure on Cu can
suppress electron-phonon coupling (EPC) and thus improve its elec-
trical conductivity, but a very limited increment by 2% was achieved
19
when subject to high pressure, even up to 2 gigapascal (GPa). More-
over, in most cases, these bene ﬁts are not integrated as intrinsic
properties of the materials, and they disappear as the appliedﬁelds are
removed.
Contrary to the conventional wisdom of eliminating structural
defects, we proposed introducing nanolayers of severely distorted
copper lattice at GBs in nanograined Cu to act as highly conductive
channels by means of suppressing electron-phonon interaction
strength and reducing phonon-caused electron scattering. The local
giant stress is caused by accumulated copious strain energy upon cold
drawing ( ε ≈ 12) and subsequent annealing-induced anisotropic
expansion due to the thermal expansion coef ﬁcient (TEC) mismatch
between the Cu matrix and strained graphene at GBs. Additionally, it
has high thermal stability (at least up to 773 K), and no external
extreme ﬁelds are required to maintain it.
Fabrication and ultrahigh electrical conductivity
To obtain sufﬁcient deformability and grain reﬁnement to the nanos-
cale simultaneously, we incorporated trace graphene (Supplementary
Note 1, about several tens of parts per million in volume fraction) at
GBs to assist plastic deformation of coarse-grained Cu
20. The ultimate
nanograined Cu wires were obtained through a sequential process
involving in-situ growth of graphene on Cu foils (Supplementary
Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), laminated structure assembling,
hot-pressing molding, hot extrusion, and cold drawing, followed by a
subsequent thermal
annealing treatment in the ﬁnal step (Fig. 1a and Supplementary
Fig. 2). Such trace graphene at GBs enables Cu exceptional plastic
deformability, i.e. the extruded bar (diameter d = 13 mm) can be con-
tinuously cold drawn to wires ( d < 0.03 mm) without intermediate
thermal annealing (corresponding to a strain of ~12, Supplementary
Figs. 2 and 3). Because of the constraint role of graphene at GBs, Cu
samples containing trace graphene (Gr–Cu) have stronger ability on
grain reﬁnement and higher thermal stability than that without gra-
p h e n ei n s i d e( F i g .1b, f, Supplementary Fig. 4, and Supplementary
Fig. 5). After thermal annealing treatment, with the same diameters
(d ≈ 0.4 mm), grain size coarsened up to ~50 ± 13.4 μmi nt h ep u r eC u
wire (Fig. 1b), much larger than the value of ~0.72 ± 0.21 μmi nt h e
Gr–Cu wire (Fig. 1c). As the drawing process continued, nanograined
structures were formed and retained in the ﬁnal cold drawn Gr –Cu
wires and annealed Gr –Cu wires, respectively (Supplementary
Figs. 11 and 12 and Fig. 1d–f).
We compared the electrical conductivities of Gr–Cu wires and Cu
wires prepared using the same raw materials and method (Fig.1ga n d
Supplementary Fig. 6). For pure Cu wires, as expected, their electrical
conductivities decrease gradually with increasing strain, and slightly
recover upon thermal annealing treatment. In contrast, the electrical
conductivity of the Gr –Cu wires remains almost unchanged in the
whole process of cold drawing, and witnessed drastic increases after
thermal annealing treatment, with a certi ﬁed exceptional electrical
conductivity larger than 110% IACS (Supplementary Note 2 and Sup-
plementary Fig. 7), which is more than 10% higher than that of pure Cu.
It is worth emphasizing that, contradictory to the conventional
belief that as the grain size is reﬁned to nanometer scale, the electrical
conductivity will signiﬁcantly decrease
21,22, the electrical conductivity
inversely increases with decreasing grain size in the plastically
deformed Gr–Cu here (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 8). Ourﬁndings
strongly suggested that grain re ﬁnement not always decreases elec-
trical conductivity, which contradicts the notion established in con-
ventional materials.
While Ag is well known as the best electrical conductor among all
the metals, the Gr–Cu here shows comprehensive advantages over Ag
as indicated by the expanded area in the performance radar chart
(Fig. 1j). Ag has a higher electrical conductivity than Cu, but Cu is used
in much larger quantities because Ag is too expensive. The Gr –Cu
material provides an attractive choice with electrical conductivity
higher than Ag but price around Cu. Moreover, pure Cu has a higher
current-carrying capacity than Ag, and incorporating graphene will
further improve this performance
23,24, indicating higher power and
safety in applications. Additionally, the density of Gr–Cu is almost the
same as that of pure Cu, about 85% that of Ag, implying direct beneﬁt
for lightweighting in practical devices and systems. In many applica-
tions, high electrical conductivity and high mechanical strength are
often required simultaneously, but methods used to strengthen metals
generally also cause a pronounced decrease in electrical conductivity
1.
Fortunately, the Gr–Cu material has both higher electrical conductivity
and mechanical strength than either Cu or Ag, which provides a
pathway to develop high-performance metal conductors.

lamella grain coarsening in the process of hot-pressing sintering. By
subsequent hot extrusion and multi-pass cold drawing, the few-layer
graphene at GBs was thinned and further dispersed via shear-induced
interlayer sliding (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Video 1),

Microstructure evolution inducing local strain
To explore the microstructure evolution and formation of lattice dis-
tortion during annealing, we conducted in-situ heating experiments in
TEM. The low-magniﬁcation TEM images revealed that the nanolami-
nated structure was thermally stable during in-situ heating (Supple-
mentary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Video 2). Closer observations in
HRTEM (Fig. 3a–d) show some meaningful details on microstructure
evolution. In the randomly selected nanolamella (about 50 nm in
width) in the TEM specimen cut from the as-drawn Gr–Cu wire, dense
strain moiré fringes were observed in the lamellae before heating
34,35.
As the heating temperature increased, the strained zone became nar-
rower gradually, and the fringes in the middle region of the nanola-
mella disappeared ultimately, which seems like the strain was
concentrating towards GBs on two sides upon heating. As
revealed by HRTEM, two nanolayers with clear contrast and com-
pressive strain ( ε
xy) formed along the GBs on both sides (Supple-
mentary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Video 3), which is consistent with
the features of microstructure and nanolayers as observed in the ex-
situ annealed Gr–Cu samples (Fig. 2f).
We also conducted Raman measurements of graphene in the
interface to estimate the interfacial stress (Fig. 3e). We observed a
slight blue shift of the Raman G peak after sintering (without defor-
mation), and a larger shift to higher wave numbers in the as-extruded
Gr–Cu sample (with an extrusion ratio of 9.5 and a strain ε ≈ 2.2).
Raman peak position can be used as the index of strain state in
graphene; a signiﬁcant red shift of ~14.2 cm
−1 per 1% strain for Raman
G peak was observed in graphene under uniaxial tensile strain 36.I n
contrast to the tensile strain, the reported in-plane biaxial
compression-induced blue shifts37 of the Raman G peak for the gra-
phite or graphene vary from 4.1 to 4.9 cm −1·GPa−1. Compared to
pristine graphene38 or our graphene transferred from Gr/Cu foil to
silicon substrate, the blue shift of the Raman G peak in the as-
extruded sample is estimated to be around 30 cm
−1 in our work. Thus,
it can be approximately calculated that the compressive stress
caused a shift of the G peak of 6.1 –7.3 GPa. It is reasonable to expect
that even more signi ﬁcant Raman shifts and thus actual stress much
larger than 8 GPa in the annealed Gr –Cu wires (the strain ε as high as
12 in the wire with d < 0.03 mm), although it is challenging to further
collect Raman spectra for the highly dispersed graphene in the Cu
matrix.
This interfacial stress induced by the TEC mismatch between the
severely strained graphene and Cu matrix was elucidated in Fig. 3f.
Upon elevating temperature during sintering and annealing, along the
direction parallel to the nanolamella, graphene contracted due to its
negative TEC in-plane
39 while the Cu matrix oppositely expanded.
Subsequently, graphene tends to expand while Cu tends to shrink,
resulting in the formation of graphene wrinkles (described as ripples
or folds) during the cooling process. The interfacial compressive stress
was partially released by forming folds or ripples
40,41, while the majority
of the compressive stress was still retained, resulting in a biaxial
compressive stress on the Cu matrix. In the other direction, perpen-
dicular to the nanolamella, graphene itself expanded and also con-
strained the expansion of the Cu matrix, thus leading to a uniaxial
compressive stress. Consequently, a multi-directional compressive
stress formed near the Cu –C interface, which is consistent with that
reported in the graphene/Cu micro-laminated composites
42. However,
only in the drawn Gr –Cu wire does such a compressive stress effect
contribute to a substantial increase in bulk electrical conductivity.
Firstly, copious strain energy was accumulated in the process of cold-
drawing, and graphene was highly strained, resulting in a larger com-
pressive stress generated at the Cu –C interface. Additionally, the
proportion of the strain-affected area becomes large enough as the Cu
lamellae are reﬁned to the nanoscale. For instance, the width of the
distorted nanolayer ranged from 5 to 20 nm for the nanolamellae with
at h i c k n e s so f~ 5 0n mi nF i g s .2fa n d3d. Such microstructure evolution
and strain accumulation can well explain the strain-dependent
enhancement of electrical conductivity (Fig.1g, h).
Giant stress in the annealed Gr –Cu wire was also con ﬁrmed by
severe lattice distortion of copper, as shown in the atomic-scale images
of the nanolayer (Fig. 3g, h) collected by high-resolution high-angle
annular dark-ﬁeld (HAADF) imaging in an aberration-corrected scan-

Further ﬁrst-principles calculations indicated that such a large lattice
expansion requires local stress of several tens of GPa. We have also
conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to elucidate the
maintaining effect of graphene on the mechanical properties of the
graphene-reinforced copper composites and stable interfacial bond-
ing (Supplementary Fig. 19). The results show the remarkable increase
in the tensile strength due to the hindering effect on the dislocations.
Especially, comparing with the microstructure at a strain of ~0.5, the
microstructure at a strain of ~0.7 was kept stable without interfacial
separation. Additionally, the detachment phenomena are less likely to
occur under compressive loading
43. Thus, based on the above experi-
mental and MD results, interfacial compressive stress was induced and
kept without interfacial separation.

camera at a shooting interval of ~1 s.

In situ TEM heating testing was carried out in a Talos F200X TEM using

situ heating processes were recorded in real time.
First principles calculations
The ﬁrst-principles calculations were conducted using the QUANTUM
ESPRESSO package53, based on the density functional theory (DFT)
with the optimized Norm-Conserving Vanderbilt Pseudopotential54,55.

equilibrium lattice constant of 3.60 Å. The atomic coordinates of the
optimized computational models corresponding to the four strain
states are provided in the Supplementary Data. The energy cutoff of
100 Ry was chosen for the wave functions. The electron charge density
was calculated by the Methfessel-Paxton method with a smearing
parameter of σ = 0.02 Ry (4). To consider the Gr/Cu composite, we
constructed the systems by following two steps. Firstly, we relaxed the
bulk Cu withﬁxed different lattice parameters to simulate the effect of
hydrostatic pressure. Then, we cleave the Cu (111) surface to construct
the heterojunction of Cu (111) @ Gr with two different stacking orders.
The heterojunctions are optimized with ﬁxed Cu position and lattice
parameters, while the C atoms are fully relaxed. The hydrostatic
pressure is simulated by change the crystal parameters and fully
relaxed the atomic positions, where the
Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) quasi-Newton algorithm
was used. Phonon dispersion curves were calculated based on the
