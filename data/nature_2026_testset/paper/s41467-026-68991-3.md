Solar-driven photocatalytic reduction of CO 2 into value-added che-
micals has attracted considerable interest, as it offers a promising
route to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions and alleviate resource
constraints

(e.g., triethanolamine or triethylamine) has signi ﬁcantly enhanced

To address these challenges, photocatalytic systems must overcome
two inherent limitations in CO 2 reduction with H 2O: both half-
reactions involve multiple electron transfers and require simulta-

Natural photosynthesis achieves remarkable ef ﬁciency in cou-
pling CO
2 reduction and H2O oxidation through sophisticated charge
management: Photosystem II (PSII) and Photosystem I (PSI) are spa-
tially and temporally separated and combine with reversible electron
carriers for directional energy transfer
18,19. In this system, PSII exclu-
sively drives H2Oo x i d a t i o n( 2 H2O → O2 +4 H+ +4 e⁻), while the liberated
electrons are temporarily stored and buffered by quinone carriers like
plastoquinone (PQ) through reversible redox reactions before being
delivered to PSI for the synthesis of reduced nicotinamide adenine
dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)
20,21, which is essential for subsequent
CO2 reduction (Fig. 1a). In this process, PQ enables the temporary
storage of short-lived photogenerated electrons, ensuring ef ﬁcient
electron transfer between PSI and PSII. Inspired by this natural strat-
egy, we considered introducing a similar charge storage strategy into
an artiﬁcial photosynthetic system through a redox medium, enabling
the reaction of the composite material to decouple the functions of
H
2O oxidation and CO 2 reduction in time, and developed a general
interfacial charge transfer strategy.
Our previous studies demonstrated that the conversion of W 6+
to W5+ in tungsten oxide (WO 3) allows for the transient trapping and
storage of photogenerated electrons 22,23. If these stored photo-
generated electrons can be extracted to recombine with photo-
generated holes in the CO
2 reduction active components, they
would function analogously to PQ. To validate this, we synthesized a
silver single-atom-anchored WO
3 material (Ag/WO 3) and demon-
strated that Ag/WO 3 can effectively store photoexcited electrons
through irradiation-induced W 6+/W5+ valence oscillation (Fig. 1b).
As veri ﬁcation experiments, subsequent separate coupling of
Ag/WO3 with extensively studied active components, including
cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc), graphitic carbon nitride (C 3N4), and
cuprous oxide (Cu 2O), yields composite catalysts exhibiting sub-
stantially enhanced performance relative to the pristine catalysts.
Detailed characterization of the most effective model catalyst,
CoPc/Ag/WO
3, reveals that stored photoexcited electrons in Ag/
WO3 migrate to recombine with photogenerated holes in CoPc.
This process maintains a high density of reducing electrons at
the CO
2 reduction sites of CoPc. The CoPc/Ag/WO 3 catalyst
achieves a 100-fold enhancement in photocatalytic CO 2 reduction
activity compared to pure CoPc, matching the performance of
optimized CoPc systems employing organic sacri ﬁcial agents. This
bioinspired charge reservoir strategy demonstrates universal
applicability across different catalytic systems, providing a sus-
tainable and universal strategy for photocatalyst development in
CO
2 conversion.

Sustainable CO2 conversion performance of catalysts containing
Ag/WO3 composites
To determine whether the electrons stored in the Ag/WO 3 nano-
particles can effectively recombine with the photogenerated holes
produced by the active components, we constructed a model catalyst,
CoPc/Ag/WO
3, by combining Ag/WO 3 with the widely studied CoPc.
The fundamental physicochemical properties of the CoPc/Ag/WO 3
composite catalyst were characterized using XRD, XPS, SEM, Fourier-
transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and UV-Vis DRS (Supple-
mentary Figs. 11–15). All these characterization results conﬁrm that the
synthesized composite material CoPc/Ag/WO
3 is composed of Ag/WO3
and CoPc, which is consistent with the expected structure.
The CO2 reduction coupling with H2O oxidation performance of
synthesized samples was evaluated under simulated solar irradiation by

The universality of the charge reservoir strategy
Following the discovery that stored photogenerated electrons
enhance the catalytic activity, assessing the universality of this charge
storage mechanism becomes crucial. Therefore, we employed XPS VB
spectra to investigate the distribution of accumulated electrons
39.
Through the XPS VB spectrum test, we found that after the sites of W5+
were introduced into the catalyst, a new peak appeared in the range of
0.3–1.5 V (centered at 0.9 V) (Fig. 5a, b). This means that as long as
the oxidation potential of the photogenerated holes produced by
the active components is greater than 0.3 V, accumulated electrons
can be consumed, which greatly broadens the selection of active
components.

Discussion
In summary, this work designed a bioinspired charge storage strategy
centered on the valence oscillation of tungsten between W
5+ and W6+ in
Ag single-atom-loaded WO3 nanoparticles and realized efﬁcient CO2
photoreduction coupling with H2O oxidation. When coupling Ag/WO3
with representative materials, including CoPc, C 3N4,a n dC u 2O, all
composite systems exhibited markedly enhanced catalytic perfor-
mance compared with their pristine components. In particular, CoPc/
Ag/WO
3 catalyst achieves a CO production rate of ~1.5 mmol per gram
of CoPc per hour, which represents a 100-fold increase over pure CoPc
and is comparable to the performance of systems utilizing organic
sacriﬁcial agents. Demonstration experiments under natural sunlight
further conﬁrmed its potential for practical application. Our mechan-
istic studies, primarily employing fs-TA, KPFM, XPS, and EPR, among
others, revealed that the material functions via a charge-discharge
cycle mechanism, characterized by the following signi ﬁcant advan-
tages: First, Ag/WO
3 acts as a charge storage device, reducing W 6+ to
W5+ under light irradiation to achieve “charging”;t h e n ,d u r i n gt h e
catalytic process, the stored electrons are transferred to the active
component and recombined with the photogenerated holes of the
active component, thus preventing charge recombination in the
reduction catalyst. This mechanism achieves spatiotemporal decou-
pling: WO
3 is solely responsible for H 2O oxidation and electron sto-
rage, while the active component for CO2 conversion can be selected
purely based on its reduction performance, without the stringent VB
requirements for H

that the VB of the reduction catalyst can accept electrons from the W5+,
making it highly universal and compatible with various catalytic

Methods
Synthesis of the WO3 catalyst
1 g of tungsten hexachloride (WCl6) was dissolved in 60 mL of ethanol.
The solution was continuously stirred at room temperature for 30 min.
Subsequently, the solution was transferred to a Teﬂon-lined autoclave,
where it underwent treatment at 180 °C for 12 h. Following this pro-
cess, the suspension was subjected to centrifugation, followed by
repeated washes with ethanol and deionized H
2O. Finally, the obtained
material was dried at 70 °C and subsequently calcined in an air atmo-
sphere at 300 °C for 1 h, resulting in the formation of the desired WO3
catalyst.
Synthesis of Ag/WO3 catalyst
100 mg of WO 3 was added to 50 mL of deionized H 2O, followed by
ultrasonic dispersion for 1 h to achieve a homogeneous dispersion
solution; the mixture was purged with Ar for 1 h and irradiated using a
300 W xenon lamp for 1 h, during which the color changed to light
blue, indicating the formation of reduced W
5+.S u b s e q u e n t l y ,3 m Lo f
silver nitrate solution (AgNO3, 1 mg/mL) was introduced into the dis-
persed solution and stirred at room temperature for 1 min; after stir-
ring, the solid catalyst was separated via centrifugation and alternately
washed three times with absolute ethanol and deionized H2O. Finally,
the washed catalyst was dried at 70 °C in air and calcined in an air
atmosphere at 300 °C for 1 h, yielding a light-yellow product corre-
sponding to Ag/WO
3; the procedure was replicated to prepare samples
with varying Ag contents by adjusting the AgNO 3 addition amount
accordingly.
Synthesis of composite catalyst
10 mg of cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc, analytical grade) was dissolved
in 50 mL of absolute ethanol, followed by 3 h of ultrasonic treatment at
room temperature. Subsequently, 90 mg of Ag/WO
3 was added to the
mixture, and an additional hour of ultrasonic treatment was applied.
The dispersion was then ball-milled at 500 rpm for 5 h. Next, the
ultrasonically treated catalyst was stirred at 85 °C until all the ethanol
had evaporated. The resulting dried catalyst was washed three times
with absolute ethanol and air-dried at 70 °C. The resulting catalyst is
denoted as CoPc/Ag/WO
3. The synthesis steps for other proportional
CoPc catalysts remain the same as described above, with the only
variation being the ratio of CoPc added. By substituting Ag/WO
3 with
WO3, while keeping the other steps unchanged, the resulting catalyst is
CoPc/WO3. The detailed synthesis procedures for Cu 2Oa n dh i g h l y
crystalline C3N4 are described in the Supporting Information. Upon
obtaining the individual Cu2Oa n dC3N4 monomers, the Cu2O/Ag/WO3
catalyst and the C 3N4/Ag/WO3 composite catalyst were respectively
prepared following a synthesis procedure analogous to that used for
CoPc/Ag/WO
3.
