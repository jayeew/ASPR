analyze satellite altimetry data from 1993 to 2024 across African marine domains. The 2023 –2024 El
Niño produced the largest detrended sea level anomaly on record (27 mm), exceeding even the
stronger 1997–1998 event. This exceptional response arose from ocean preconditioning: record-
positive Indian Ocean Dipole, Atlantic Niño, and Tropical North Atlantic indices preceded and
ampliﬁed El Niño forcing, while anomalous winds suppressed upwelling and record strati ﬁcation
trapped surface heat, quadrupling ocean heat content. Thermal expansion accounted for over 70% of
this event’s total anomaly, reaching nearly 30 mm across the African marine domain. Sea levels rose

-2, exceeding the global average. These ﬁndings
reveal compound threats to low-lying deltas and Small Island Developing States from ﬂooding,
ongoing land subsidence, and declining marine productivity.
The escalating frequency and intensity of climate extremes, forced by both
anthropogenic warming and natural climate variability, are posing severe
challenges to global ecosystems, economies, and societies1. Among recent
events, the 2023 –2024 El Niño episode stands out as one of the most
powerful in recent decades. It trigge red widespread climate anomalies,
including extreme heatwaves, severe d roughts, intense tropical cyclones,
and signiﬁcant sea level anomalies (SLAs) worldwide2–6.
Ad eﬁning feature of this event was the exceptional warming of global
ocean surfaces. Since April 2023, gl obal ocean surface temperatures
(60°S–60°N) have reached record-high levels, with annually averaged sea
surface temperatures (SSTs) b etween April 2023 and March 2024
approximately 0.25 °C higher than the previous record7,8. During the boreal
summer, SST anomalies exceeded the 2015–2016 El Niño record by up to
0.3 °C, surpassing previous peaks observed in 1997–1998 and 2009–2010.
This record-breaking thermal anomaly,likely exacerbated by a reduction in
low-cloud cover and albedo9, coincided with unprecedented global atmo-
spheric surface temperatures in late 2023 and early 2024, leading to new
records in annual global temperatures in both 2023 and 2024
7,9.

Spatial sea level trends and accelerations
Figure1a presents the SLA trend across the African marine domain over the
past three decades (1993–2024), covering the Atlantic and Indian Oceans,
Mediterranean Sea, Red Sea, as well as the Gulf of Aden and Gulf of Oman.
The spatial distribution reveals prono unced variability, with statistically
signiﬁcant (p < 0.05) regional mean sea level (RMSL) trends. The color scale,
spanning from blue (–6 mm/yr) to reddish (+6 mm/yr), distinguishes areas
of sea level decline from those experiencing rapid rise, contextualized against
the GMSL rise (3.45 ± 0.3 mm/yr). This global benchmark identiﬁes regions
with above- or below-global average changes.
In the Atlantic Ocean basin, SLA trends exhibit signi ﬁcant spatial
heterogeneity. The Gulf of Guinea and equatorial Atlantic display elevated
trends above the GMSL, ranging between 3 and 5 mm/yr. These elevations
are modulated by ocean circulation features such as the Equatorial Coun-
tercurrent, Guinea Current, and th ermal expansion associated with
warming surface waters. Furthermore, climatic drivers like the West African
Monsoon, ENSO, and the Atlantic Niño force sea level through the interplay
of wind patterns and redistribution of ocean heat content. The Atlantic
Niño, the tropical Atlantic’s analogue to ENSO, drives interannual sea level
variability by in ﬂuencing zonal wind stress and thermocline depth
25,26.
Coastal cities such as Lagos (Nigeria), Douala (Cameroon), Accra (Ghana),
and Abidjan (Côte d’Ivoire) are increasingly exposed toﬂooding, erosion,
and salinization, with these risks exacerbated by land subsidence and urban
expansion
27–30. Along the northwestern African margin, the Canary
Upwelling System, extending fro m Guinea-Bissau to Morocco, shows
contrasting SLA trends ranging between 3.5 and 4 mm/yr. Persistent coastal
upwelling, driven by trade winds, cools surface waters and suppresses local
SLR. Further south, the South African coastline exhibits trends of 2–4 mm/
yr, shaped by the warm water transport of the Agulhas Current from the
Indian Ocean. The South Atlantic is also inﬂuenced by the South Atlantic
subtropical gyre, which further co ntributes to observed SLA patterns by
redistributing ocean heat
17.

In the Mediterranean Sea basin, SLA trends are marked by spatial
heterogeneity. The western margin generally ranges from 2 to 4 mm/yr,
whereas the eastern basin, notably near Egypt’s coast and around Cyprus,
exhibits maxima exceeding 4 mm/yr.
The Indian Ocean basin displays predominantly above-average SLA
trends, particularly along the easte rn African coast and western basin,
including the Mozambique Channel and Madagascar, with values between
3.5 and 6 mm/yr. Low-lying islands such as the Comoros and Seychelles
experience increased exposure to ﬂooding, with trends often exceeding
4 mm/yr. The Mozambique Channel records some of the highest SLA
trends in the region, driven by the interaction of the South Equatorial
Current and the East Madagascar Current, which intensify regional SLR
31,32.
Additionally, ENSO and the IOD play critical roles in modulating SLA
patterns, with positive IOD phases, in particular, being linked to elevated sea
levels along the eastern African margin. Thermosteric contributions from
sustained ocean warming further amplify regional trends.
The Red Sea basin exhibits relatively uniform SLA trends above the
GMSL, ranging between 2 and 4 mm/yr. The semi-enclosed conﬁguration
of the Red Sea, in association with high evaporation and limited exchange
with the Indian Ocean, leads to distinctive and stable SLA patterns. The
region’s sea level is also modulated by variability in surface heatﬂuxes, wind
forcing, and mesoscale circulation
33. These patterns highlight the impor-
tance of including marginal seas in re gional SLR assessments, given their
unique oceanographic characteristics, sensitivity to regional forcing, and
local vulnerabilities.
Figure1b shows SLA trends with the Niño3.4 signal removed. The
overall spatial distribution remains broadly similar to Fig.1a, suggesting that
most regional SLA trends are driven byoceanographic and thermodynamic
processes independent of ENSO. However, ENSO-adjusted trends reveal
notable reductions, with maximum differences ranging from 0.5 to 0.8 mm/
year (p < 0.05), indicating that ENSO can account for up to approximately
13% of the total observed trend in some regions (Fig.1c). These effects are
primarily linked to ENSO-driven changes in wind stress, heat ﬂux, and
ocean heat content, acting through dynamic and thermosteric mechanisms.

These ﬁndings collectively demonstrate that the African marine
domain is undergoing a statistically signiﬁcant and spatially diverse accel-
eration in SLR, embedded within a global transition. The 2023–2024 period,
characterized by a record annual SLA of 8.39 cm and monthly anomalies
exceeding 9.5 cm, underscores the urgency of this transformation. Niño3.4-
corrected trends, ranging from 3.36 mm/yr in the Mediterranean Sea
(Fig. 3c) to 3.85 mm/yr in the Eastern Central Atlantic (Fig.3a), conﬁrm the
robustness of long-term SLR, minimally inﬂuenced by ENSO and driven by
sustained climate forcings. Comparing GIA- and Niño3.4-corrected time
series across all basins shows high correlations ( r > 0.95), indicating that
removing ENSO variability does not alter the overall trajectory of climate-
driven SLR but helps isolate its long-term signal. Here, the GIA correction
accounts for land motion and vertical reference frame changes, while the
Niño3.4 correction removes ENSO-related interannual variability. This
highlights the utility of Niño3.4 correction in clarifying the contribution of
sustained climatic forcing to regional sea level trends, which is critical for
reliable coastal planning. The persistent upward trend, signiﬁcant thermal
contribution, and exceptional El Niñoforcing indicate that the region is in
an advanced stage of sea level transformation, necessitating enhanced sci-
entiﬁc, infrastructural, and policy responses.

Multi-modal linear regression analysis of regional sea level
drivers
To assess the relative inﬂuence of different climate modes on regional sea
level variability along the African coast and adjacent basins, we applied a
multi-modal linear regression (see Data and Methods) using Niño3.4, the
Indian Ocean Dipole (DMI), the Tropical North Atlantic (TNA), Atlantic
Niño (ATL3), the Paci ﬁc Decadal Oscillation (PDO), and the Southern
Annular Mode (SAM) as predictors. This comprehensive framework,
analyzing the 1993 –2024 period, speci ﬁcally addresses potential con-
founding between climate modes to isolate their individual contributions.
Standardized coefﬁcients were derived to allow direct comparison of their
inﬂuence across ﬁve domains: Africa (AF), Eastern Central Africa (ECA),
Southern Africa (SA), the Mediterranean (MD), and the Western Tropical
Indian Ocean (WTIO).
The regression analysis, based on the 1993–2024 period, reveals that
ENSO has consistently been the dominant climate mode inﬂuencing Afri-
can sea level variability (Fig.8). Niño3.4 displayed very high coefﬁcients in
SA (0.907) and the Africa domain (0.804), and remained strongly inﬂuential
in ECA (0.794), the Mediterranean (0.745), and the WTIO (0.791). These
values indicate that a one-standard-deviation increase in Niño3.4 corre-
sponded to 0.75 –0.91 standard deviation increases in SLAs. The DMI
emerged as the most notable addi tional contributor, with coef ﬁcients
reaching 0.199 in Eastern Central Africa, suggesting that Indian Ocean
variability can locally amplify or attenuate ENSO-driven changes. Con-
tributions from TNA, PDO, SAM, and ATL3 were modest and inconsistent,
indicating that their inﬂuence on interannual variability in this region is
secondary to that of ENSO. However, note that repeating this same analysis
for each individual grid cell reveals additional localized responses to these
climate modes (Supplementary Fig. 7). For example, SLA variability off the
coast of Morocco is most strongly associated with the TNA and shows little
connection to ENSO, despite an overall strong connection between ENSO
and the ECA’s regional SLA (Fig.8).
To evaluate how well the combined inﬂuence of these climate modes
explains observed sea level variability, Supplementary Fig. 8a compares the
observed detrended African regional SLA with a regression-based recon-
struction derived from all six climate indices. The two time series display
clear co-variability, with a Pearson correlation coefﬁcient of r = 0.64,i n d i -
cating a robust linear relationshipat interannual timescales. The coefﬁcient
of determination (R² = 0.405) shows that the regression explains 40.5% of
the detrended SLA variance, represe nting a substantial fraction of the
observed interannual variability. The RMSE of 6.84 mm quanti ﬁes the
average amplitude mismatch between observed and reconstructed SLA,
indicating reasonable reconstruction accuracy given the exclusion of local
and high-frequency processes. Supplementary Fig. 8 displays the individual
climate index time series (Niño3.4, ATL3, TNA, DMI, PDO, and SAM),
revealing that not all interannual SLA peaks align exclusively with El Niño
events. Instead, several peaks corresp ond to constructive interference
among multiple modes, for example, concurrent positive phases of Niño3.4,
TNA, ATL3, and DMI, suggesting tha t multi-modal interactions can
amplify the total sea level response beyond what any single index would
predict. This temporal perspective complements the spatial regression
analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7) and helps explain why certain non-El El-
Niño years also exhibit elevated SLAs.
To ensure the regression captured v ariability rather than long-term
change, the secular trend was removed prior to analysis, allowing the results
to reﬂect interannualﬂuctuations alone. Thisﬁnding aligns with evidence of
non-linear ampliﬁcation, in which the elevated baseline of regional sea levels
due to long-term warming enhances the impact of interannual ENSO
variability. Taken together, th e regional mean analysis (Fig. 8), spatial
regression maps Supplementary Fig . 7), and temporal reconstruction
(Supplementary Fig. 8) provide a physically consistent picture of how
remote and regional climate modes joi ntly structure sea level variability
around the African continent: ENSO exerts the strongest inﬂuence across
the Indian Ocean and tropical Atlanti c; Atlantic modes contribute sig-
niﬁcantly along the western margin; the IOD and Paciﬁcm o d e si nﬂuence
the Indian Ocean. This hierarchy of overlapping drivers suggests that during
extreme events like the 2023 –2024 El-Niño, these regional modes can
constructively interfere to precondition the ocean surface and amplify the
total sea level response. The implications are signiﬁcant: regions with strong
ENSO sensitivity, particularly along the Southern African coasts, face
heightened risks of ﬂooding, erosion, and ecolo gical disruption during
strong events.
Discussion
This study reveals a complex and accelerating pattern of SLR across African
oceanic domains, shaped by long-term anthropogenic forcing and inter-
annual variability associated with ENSO. Over 1993 –2024, RMSL trends
consistently exceeded the global a verage of 3.45 ± 0.3 mm/yr, with the
Western Indian Ocean (3.88 ± 0.08 mm/yr) and Eastern Central Atlantic
(3.90 ± 0.05 mm/yr) recording the most rapid increases. Acceleration rates
in the Indian Ocean (0.16 ± 0.02 mm/yr²) also surpassed the global mean
(0.11 ± 0.05 mm/yr²), conﬁrming Africa’s coastlines are warming faster and
more unevenly than previously documented
11.
Ad eﬁning feature of this analysis is the sharp regional contrasts in how
El Niño events modulate SLAs. The period 2023–2024 was exceptional, with
detrended SLAs of 27.04 mm and non-detrended anomalies of 83.02 mm,
exceeding the 1997–1998 benchmark event (19.24 mm and −17.02 mm,
respectively). Spatially, 2023–2024 anomalies exceeded 90 mm across nearly
all African basins the Indian Ocean, Atlantic, Mediterranean, and Red Sea
(Fig. 5i), compared to more localized 75 mm peaks during 1994–1995 (Red
Sea and Southern Indian Ocean, Fig.5a). In contrast, the 2002–2003 event
showed mostly negative anomalies, likely dampened by a neutral IOD
phase
49.
Regionally, the Western Indian Ocean had the strongest ENSO-driven
variance (8.85%), likely due to IOD interplay and thermocline deepening.
The Eastern Central Atlantic showed weaker variability (1.55%), modulated
by Atlantic Niño and changes in zonal wind stress 19.D e s p i t eb e i n gs e m i -
enclosed, the Mediterranean and Red Sea registered ENSO signals, likely
transmitted via atmospheric teleconnections, altering pressure and wind

Conclusion
This study demonstrates that SLR across the African marine domain is not
only faster than the global average but also increasingly variable, driven by
the combined inﬂuence of long-term anthropogenic forcing and episodic
climate variability. Sea level trends across all major ocean basins exceed the
global average, with the Western Indian Ocean and Eastern Central Atlantic
standing out as acceleration hotspots. Acceleration rates in the Indian Ocean
surpass the global mean, reinforcing concerns about the intensifying pace of
regional SLR. A deﬁning insight from this analysis is the diverse and evol-
ving regional response to El Niño events. The 2023–2024 event emerged as
the most extreme on record, producing unprecedented SLAs that extended
across the Indian Ocean, Atlantic, Mediterranean, and Red Sea. These
ﬁndings reveal a nonlinear intensi ﬁcation regime, where the elevated
baseline sea level, resulting from persistent ocean warming, steric expansion,
and reinforced by subsurface heat ac c u m u l a t i o na n de n h a n c e ds t r a t iﬁca-
tion, interacts with ENSO-driven variability to produce compounded
impacts disproportionately larger than those from ENSO signals alone. It is
important to clarify that these imp acts do not stem solely from ENSO
anomalies; rather, they reﬂect the cumulative effect of ENSO-driven SLAs
superimposed on this elevated baseline, with the preconditioned ocean state
enhancing the response to even moderate ENSO forcing. This interpretation
is corroborated by thermal and stratiﬁcation anomalies unmatched in the
satellite record, including warming of the upper 300 m exceeding+0.5 °C
and basin-wide SST anomalies approaching +1.5 °C. Correcting for the
Niño3.4 signal isolates this effect and conﬁrms that while ENSO contributes
only modestly to long-term variance, its episodic anomalies can push coastal
systems past critical thresholds, exacerbatingﬂooding, erosion, and salini-
zation. The detection of a statistically signiﬁcant regime shift around 2009,
with a 71% increase in trend from 2.72 ± 0.14 to 4.66 ± 0.17 mm/yr, aligns
with broader climate system changes, including accelerated ice mass loss
and shifts in the PDO. Theseﬁndings mark a turning point in regional SLR
trajectories, emphasizing that Africa is entering a new and more hazardous
sea level regime.
