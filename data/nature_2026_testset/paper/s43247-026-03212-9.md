Seismic anisotropy in the Earth ’s lower mantle, particularly around subduction zones, is commonly
attributed to deformation by dislocation creep, yet much of the lower mantle appears nearly isotropic.
This contrast complicates interpretations of mantle rheology. Here we report the temperature
dependence of lattice preferred orientation in bridgmanite, the most abundant lower mantle mineral,
through high-pressure deformation experiments at 25 gigapascals and 1700 – 2100 kelvin. Both iron-
free and iron-bearing bridgmanite develop lattice preferred orientations across this temperature range,
with distinct slip systems occurring below and above 1800 kelvin. Low-temperature fabric produces
strong seismic anisotropy, whereas high-temperature fabric yields weak, near-isotropic signatures
under horizontal shearing. These results provide a uni ﬁed explanation for strong seismic anisotropy
near subduction zones and globally near-isotropic behavior of the lower mantle. They suggest that
dislocation creep could dominate lower mantle deformation while generating diverse seismic
signatures, providing important understanding for lower mantle rheology and dynamics.

Comparison with previous studies
Our results demonstrate a temperature-dependent transition in the LPO
of bridgmanite within the temperature range of 1700– 2100 K (Fig. 2 and
Supplementary Figs. 7– 8). Our observations of the (100) slip plane at low
temperatures (<1800 K) are consistent with the results of uniaxial
deformation experiments conducted at 1473 – 1673 K by Tsujino et al.
6
and at 1773 K by Xu et al. 26 under the lower mantle conditions (Sup-
plementary Table 2). We also performed a simple shear deformation
experiment on bridgmanite aggregates at 1873 K using the same com-
position of (Mg
0.97Fe0.03)SiO3 (run D103 in Supplementary Table 1), as
in Tsujino et al. 16. In contrast to their ﬁndings, we observed the domi-
nant slip system of [100](010) rather than [001](100) which slip plane is
dominant at lower temperature in this study. One explanation for this
discrepancy might be the temperature uncertainty in Tsujino et al. 16,
where the temperature was estimated based on the electric power rather
than measured directly with a thermocouple
16. In addition, in Fe-bearing
system of Tsujino et al. 16, stishovite was observed as a coexisting phase.
Silica activity could affect dominant slip direction of bridgmanite. At
high temperature (>1800 K), our results of the dominant [100](010) slip
system are in good agreement with the microstructural observation of
dislocations belonging to the [100](010) slip system of bridgmanite
deformed at 27 GPa and 2130 K
27,28.
Previous theoretical studies predicted slip systems in MgSiO3 bridg-
manite by assessing the lattice friction through the Peierls – Nabarro
model33– 35. These studies suggest that [010](100) and [100](010) are the two
easiest slip systems for a wide pressure range, which is consistent with our
results. The effects of pressure on the slip systems transition in bridgmanite
were revealed through atomistic calculations36,37 and compression experi-
m e n t si nd i a m o n da n v i lc e l l s38,39, though the temperature-induced slip
systems transition has not been previously reported. Although the precise
mechanism for such a transition revealed in this study remains unclear,
similar transitions in dominant slip systems with temperature have been
reported in other orthorhombic minerals, such as olivine
40,41.

The maximumP-wave velocity for both LPO types (Fig.3a) aligns with
the maximum density of (010) poles (Fig.2). In horizontal shearing, high-

For the low-temperature LPO,P-waves propagate fastest subparallel to the

Inﬂuence of Al on the deformation and elastic anisotropy of
bridgmanite
In the Earth ’s lower mantle, Al can also be incorporated in bridgmanite
(as (Mg,Fe)SiO 3– Al2O3) through charge-coupled (CC) or oxygen-
vacancy (OV) mechanisms 43,44, while its effect on LPO development of
bridgmanite appears limited compared to primary variables like
temperature
45. Experimental study has shown that Al-bearing bridgma-
nite with CC substitution exhibits a similar temperature-induced LPO
transition from (100) to (010) with increasing temperature, as observed
in both Fe-free and Fe-bearing bridgmanite
45. Although Al-bearing
bridgmanite with OV substitution shows different slip system at high
temperature
45, the content of OV component in (Fe,Al)-bearing bridg-
manite is thought to be limited due to the preferential formation of the
FeAlO
3 component46,47. In addition, the dominant deformation
mechanism, dislocation creep, remains largely unaffected by aluminum
content45. Although Fe substitution alone was reported to reduce the
elastic anisotropy of bridgmanite 48, both ab initio molecular dynamics
simulations49 and high-pressure experimental studies50,51 indicate that the
elastic anisotropy of (Fe,Al)-bearing bridgmanite is comparable to that of
MgSiO
3 bridgmanite. Therefore, the presence of Al is not expected to
signiﬁcantly modify the LPO transition behavior, elastic anisotropy, or
the dominant role of dislocation creep in the deformation of bridgmanite
under the uppermost lower mantle conditions.
Implications for the seismic anisotropy in the Earth’s
lower mantle
Ubiquitous seismic anisotropy has been observed in the uppermost lower
mantle8– 10. Interpreting seismic anisotropy based on present results could
provide critical insights into the ﬂow patterns in the lower mantle. For
instance, P-wave azimuthal anisotropy with subduction-normal fast
velocity directions, observed under the Paci ﬁc slab (stagnant at 660 km)
beneath the Japan Arc9, corresponds well with the low-temperature LPO
of bridgmanite under subduction-normal mantle ﬂow (Fig. 3a, b). The
stronger VSV azimuthal anisotropy observed in the circum-Paciﬁc region
compared to the central Paciﬁc15 is consistent with our ﬁnding that low-
temperature LPO of bridgmanite exhibits stronger anisotropy than that
formed at higher temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore,
the observation of the stronger S-wave polarization anisotropy in the
western Paci ﬁc (such as Kurile and Honshu) compared to that in the
eastern Paciﬁc (such as Central America)
18 is also in agreement with our
result of the stronger anisotropy of low-temperature LPO compared to
that of high-temperature LPO of bridgmanite (Fig. 3c). The magnitudes

such as near subduction zones, can be attributed to the low-temperature type lattice
preferred orientation (low-T LPO) of bridgmanite with [010](100), induced by
mantle ﬂow aligned with the subduction direction. In contrast, nearly isotropic S-
wave seismic behavior in the uppermost lower mantle in regions far from the sub-
duction zones can be explained by the high-temperature type LPO (high-T LPO) of
bridgmanite with [100](010) under horizontal shearing, without requiring the
absence of LPO or deformation by non-dislocation creep. These ﬁndings suggest
that dislocation creep may be the dominant deformation mechanism in the
uppermost lower mantle. Moreover, the predominance of horizontal mantle ﬂow in
this region, further indicated by the dominant V
