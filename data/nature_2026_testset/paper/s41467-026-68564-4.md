Immune-mediated inﬂammatory diseases (IMID) are chronic conditions with a
well-established multifaceted association with the gastrointestinal diseases
Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), commonly known as inﬂam-
matory bowel disease (IBD). In this study, we leverage Danish nationwide
pedigree and health data, genome-wide association studies, and fecal micro-
biota data to characterize the association between IBD and other IMIDs and
disentangle genetic and environmental contributions. We show that CD and
UC have distinct patterns of correlations with other IMIDs within families. By
evaluating genetic and gut microbial correlations, we highlight a UC
microbiota-linked correlation with multiple sclerosis and systemic lupus ery-
thematosus despite a negative genetic correlation, suggesting a key role for
environmental factors. In contrast, weﬁnd consistent genetic and microbial
convergence between both IBD subtypes and rheumatoid arthritis, whereas
genetic factors mainly drive the correlation with psoriasis. Thus, our ﬁndings
uncover heterogeneity in shared etiological pathways across immune diseases,
underscoring the need for stratiﬁed approaches to diagnosing and treat-
ing IBD.
Inﬂammatory bowel disease (IBD), comprising Crohn ’s disease (CD)
and ulcerative colitis (UC), is part of a broader spectrum of immune-
mediated inﬂammatory diseases (IMID), which also include rheuma-
toid arthritis (RA), psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE),
multiple sclerosis (MS), and type 1 diabetes (T1D), among others.
Clinical and epidemiological studies indicate a higher prevalence of
IMIDs among patients with IBD and their ﬁrst-degree relatives, with a
signiﬁcant proportion of patients with IBD also diagnosed with con-
ditions such as asthma, psoriasis, and RA
1,2. These patterns suggest
shared genetic and/or environmental risk factors.
The underlying mechanisms driving these associations may
involve converging alterations in innate and adaptive immune
responses that are common to both IBD and other IMIDs. Pharmaco-
logical exposures, particularly the use of tumor necrosis factor

Results
Familial genealogy of the Danish population
The Danish Civil Registration System (CPR) contains data on 9,831,644
individuals (Supplementary Table 1). Using this registry, we identiﬁed
9,563,570 genealogical links connecting 7,213,857 individuals, while
2,617,999 remained genealogical singletons (Supplementary Fig. 1). In
total, 247,295 family clusters were de ﬁned; the largest comprised
5,991,233 individuals (60.9% of the population), spanning six genera-
tions and including 1,828,320 founders— individuals without recorded
parental links. Additionally, 103,870 trios and 66,764 quartets were
reconstructed.
To ensure diagnostic completeness and relevance, we restric-
ted our analyses to individuals born between 1910 and 2010, an
interval with the highest density of medical records of IBD and other
IMIDs in the DNPR (Supplementary Fig. 2). Within this birth cohort,
we identi ﬁed 29,387,026 pairwise relationships, including
7,693,480 parent – offspring pairs and 2,545,428 full siblings. For
downstream analyses, we focused on full siblings due to their
comparable age distributions and disease incidence, minimizing
bias introduced by changes in diagnostic criteria or healthcare
access over time.

Discussion
This nationwide, multi-omics integration study across Danish citizens
reveals that CD and UC, while often grouped under the umbrella of
IBD, display markedly distinct patterns of correlation with other IMIDs.
By leveraging genealogical, genetic, and microbial pro ﬁles across a
harmonized population framework, we demonstrate that CD and UC
exhibit disease-speciﬁc correlations in both familial aggregation and
multi-omics architecture. These ﬁndings build upon prior evidence
suggesting shared pathophysiological pathways across IMIDs, yet
highlight the nuanced divergence between the two IBD subtypes in
their broader immune landscape
1,2. Notably, the strongest and most
consistent associations for both CD and UC were observed with
ankylosing spondylitis and psoriasis, whereas diseases such as MS and
SLE showed subtype-speciﬁc or even inverse genetic correlations
5.T h i s
data underscores the importance of dissecting IBD subtypes inde-
pendently when exploring shared mechanisms of immune
dysregulation.
Our genetic correlation analyses reinforce the phenotypic diver-
gence between CD and UC, revealing both shared and opposing
patterns of correlations across IMIDs. As expected, CD and UC showed
a strong positive genetic correlation, consistent with overlapping
susceptibility loci identiﬁed in prior GWAS studies, including NOD2,
IL23R, and variants in the HLA region
8,9. Beyond this shared core, dis-
tinct proﬁles emerged. CD demonstrated positive genetic correlations
with psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, and asthma, while UC showed
stronger associations with T1D and primary biliary cholangitis — dis-
eases with traditionally distinct immunological axes. Intriguingly, we
observed signiﬁcant negative genetic correlations between both IBD
subtypes and MS, as well as between UC and SLE. Theseﬁndings align
with large-scale pleiotropy maps of immune traits that have shown
both convergence and antagonism in genetic architecture across
autoimmune conditions
9, and mirror cell-type speci ﬁc enrichment
analyses, suggesting that tissue-speci ﬁc regulatory programs may
underlie divergent genetic risk patterns across IMIDs10.
Microbiota-based disease proﬁles further distinguished CD and
UC from one another and from their genetically associated comor-
bidities. Although CD and UC shared a strong microbial correlation, we
observed condition-speciﬁc associations with other IMIDs. For exam-
ple, CD displayed positive microbial correlations with celiac disease
and RA, while UC showed additional associations with MS and Graves’
disease— diseases for which the genetic links were weaker or absent.
These discrepancies underscore the potential role of environmental
and mucosal interfaces, particularly the gut epithelium, in shaping
microbial-driven immune modulation independently of host genetics
8.
Such decoupling of genetic and microbial correlations has been
observed previously, suggesting that microbial signatures may reﬂect
disease-state plasticity or shared ecological disruptions rather than
direct heritable risk
11.
Importantly, our ASV-level analyses allowed higher taxonomic
resolution than genus-level summaries used in earlier studies12,13.T h e
sensitivity analyses included more samples but were unable to adjust
for sex and age, as in the main microbiota analyses. Though direc-
tionally consistent, the sensitivity analyses revealed that some micro-
bial associations— such as that between UC and celiac disease — were

The distinct yet overlapping comorbidity patterns revealed
through familial, genetic, and microbial correlation analyses offer a
new framework for interpreting disease clustering in IBD and its
related immune-mediated conditions. Our results suggest that tra-
ditional diagnostic categories may obscure shared pathophysiolo-
gical networks, while also failing to account for subtype-speci ﬁc
risks. For instance, the robust associations between CD and airway-
related diseases such as asthma, versus UC ’s links with systemic
autoimmunity (e.g., SLE, MS), support the concept of immune dis-
ease endotypes driven by differential genetic and environmental
pressures. These ﬁndings echo recent efforts to rede ﬁne auto-
immune conditions based on molecular phenotypes rather than
clinical labels
9.
In a clinical context, recognizing such divergence may help stra-
tify patients with IBD according to their risk of developing speci ﬁc
IMIDs, thereby informing personalized screening protocols, ther-
apeutic decisions, and counseling strategies. This is particularly rele-
vant for biologic therapies such as TNF inhibitors or JAK inhibitors,
whose ef ﬁcacy and safety pro ﬁles differ across IMIDs. Integrating
multi-omics risk proﬁles could therefore guide targeted interventions,

We developed a method to enable testing of correlation across
different diseases’ microbiota characteristics. This was done by gen-
erating microbiota disease proﬁles. Its validity is supported by internal
consistency checks. For example, in both celiac disease and CD, 21
ASVs had nominal p < 0.05 with the same direction of association and
none in the opposite direction, consistent with their positive micro-
biota disease proﬁle correlation. In contrast, CD and psoriasis shared
only one ASV with the same direction of association but nine with the
opposite, re ﬂecting their negative correlation. Sensitivity analyses
using broader inclusion criteria (without sex/age data) produced
broadly consistent patterns. However, because these analyses did not
adjust for sex and age (only for geographical location and sequencing
instrument), the potential for residual confounding cannot be exclu-
ded. To address this, we performed the sensitivity analysis restricted to
controls from the same studies as the patients with the disease of
interest, thereby ensuring closer matching for sex and age. The sen-
sitivity analysis conﬁrmed the robustness of the associations, although
the lack of sex/age adjustment remained a methodological limitation
that may partly explain differences observed for some disease pairs.
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our ﬁnd-
ings. First, although the Danish registries provide unparalleled cover-
age for longitudinal and familial analyses, diagnostic codes may vary in
accuracy across diseases and time periods, especially for milder phe-
notypes or those managed outside hospital settings
16. While our
inclusion of only full siblings reduces bias from environmental het-
erogeneity, it may also underrepresent broader familial aggregation
patterns beyond ﬁrst-degree relationships. Second, the genetic cor-
relation analyses were restricted to publicly available GWAS summary
statistics based on European ancestry, which may limit generalizability
to non-European populations or underpowered phenotypes. Third,
despite rigorous quality control of microbiota data and ASV-level
inference across studies, residual batch effects and unmeasured con-
founders— such as dietary habits, antibiotic use, treatment use, or host
immune status — may have in ﬂuenced microbial correlations. Many
IMIDs share pharmacological treatment, which holds the potential to
alter the gut microbiota. Furthermore, we had to establish a method
for generating microbiota disease pro ﬁles and calculating their cor-
relations for this project. Thus, this method has not previously been
evaluated by the scientiﬁc community. However, altering the design in
the sensitivity analysis broadly resulted in similar results, supporting
this approach.
It is important to acknowledge that the analyses of genetic and
microbiota correlations were constrained by data availability. Conse-
quently, statistical power varied not only between the three layers of
analysis (genealogical, genetic, and microbiota) but also across the
different IMIDs. In some instances, a limited sample size precluded the
estimation of reliable correlations. As a result, genealogical correla-
tions were assessed for 17 diseases, genetic correlations for ten, and
microbiota correlations for seven. Moreover, since the data were
derived from different cohorts (Danish citizens for genealogy,
European-ancestry GWAS for genetics, and globally sourced micro-
biota data), direct comparisons of effect magnitude across analytical
layers were avoided. Finally, the focus on Danish and European
ancestry populations may limit the generalizability of ourﬁndings, and
future studies extending these analyses to more diverse global popu-
lations are warranted.
Another important consideration is that the cross-sectional nat-
ure of our microbiota data precludes inference about causality or
directionality in disease – microbiota interactions. Longitudinal or
interventional studies, ideally with integrated host transcriptomic and
metabolomic layers, will be essential to disentangle cause from con-
sequence in microbe-mediated IMID risk.
Overall, this study provides a comprehensive, multi-layered view
of the complex relationships between IBD and a broad spectrum of
IMIDs, combining national registry data, genomic correlation, and
high-resolution microbiota proﬁling. By identifying both shared and
divergent patterns of familial, genetic, and microbial comorbidity
across immune diseases, we offer insights into the mechanisms
underlying disease clustering and immune system crosstalk. Impor-
tantly, our ﬁndings demonstrate that CD and UC — though often co-
studied— harbor distinct immunogenetic and environmental associa-
tions, reinforcing the need for subtype-speciﬁc approaches in clinical
management and research design.
Although not designed to produce direct clinical interventions,
our identiﬁcation of shared genetic loci and microbial proﬁles across
IBD and multiple IMIDs underscores the need for more integrated,
multidisciplinary management of patients with immune-mediated
comorbidities. For instance, our ﬁnding that loci such as JAK2 are
associated with several IMIDs in either concordant or discordant
directions may have implications for risk modeling, suggesting that
polygenic risk scores should account for cross-disease pleiotropy
rather than being applied in isolation. Similarly, recurrent microbial
taxa such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Fusicatenibacter sac-
charivorans, which demonstrated consistent yet disease-speci ﬁc
associations, may serve as future targets for microbiota-guided diag-
nostics or adjunctive therapies. As clinical care increasingly shifts
toward personalized and stratiﬁed medicine, these insights may help
clinicians better anticipate, monitor, and manage immune comorbid-
ity in patients diagnosed with IBD.
Looking ahead, future studies should build upon our integrative
framework by combining longitudinal registry data with high-
resolution molecular layers such as single-cell immune proﬁling, host
transcriptomics, and microbial metabolomics. This would enable
deeper mechanistic insight into the observed divergence between CD
and UC, particularly regarding their distinct genetic and microbial
correlations with other IMIDs. Moreover, incorporating temporal and
functional data could help clarify causal directions and disease ther-
apeutic strategies, addressing one of the main limitations of our cross-
sectional microbiota analysis. Such efforts would strengthen subtype-
speciﬁc risk prediction models and may ultimately support the
development of precision strategies for early detection, therapeutic
targeting, and prevention of immune-mediated comorbidities in
patients with IBD.
Methods
Danish registers
The register data are protected by the Danish Act on Processing of
Personal Data and are accessed through an application to and approval
from the Danish Data Protection Agency and the Danish Health Data
Authority. Informed consent by the participants was not required.
The Danish Civil Registration System (CPR)
17 was established in
1968, including all people living in Denmark since then. The registry
includes a personal identiﬁcation number, used to link across Danish
registries. 9,831,856 individuals were identiﬁed (born between January
1859 and December 2024). The registry includes information on sex,
date of birth and parental links.
The Danish National Patient Register (DNPR)
16 was established in
1977 and includes medical records of all patients treated in Danish
hospitals. In 1995, the DNPR switched from ICD-8 diagnosis codes to
ICD-10.
Genealogy and relatedness
We used a similar approach for calculating genetic relationship as
previously described by Athanasiadis et al.
18. We excluded parental
links involving twins, individuals with duplicate parental identi ﬁers,
same-sex parent pairs, and cases with only one parental link available.

Filtering of samples for analysis
To ensure data quality, we excluded all samples with fewer than

across ten IMIDs. Beta diversity visualization using Jaccard and

samples— thereby reducing the number of ASVs from 151,558 to 399.
This ﬁltering step markedly reduced the visual batch effects (Supple-
mentary Fig. 7C, D). A detailed overview of the prepared data is pro-
vided in Supplementary Table 5.
Confounding variables
We evaluated the in ﬂuence of potential confounders — including
sequencing instrument, geographic region, age, and sex— on microbial
beta diversity. Associations with Bray – Curtis and Jaccard dissim-
ilarities were assessed using PERMANOVA, implemented via the adonis
function (vegan14 v2.6.6.1) with 999 permutations with the combined
variables. All variables were identiﬁed as signi ﬁcant sources of varia-
tion (Supplementary Table 7) and were therefore accounted for in
downstream analyses. We restricted further analyses to the subset of
samples with complete metadata, resulting in 780 samples spanning
eight diseases (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Fig. 8). Type 1
diabetes was subsequently excluded due to limited representa-
tion (n =1 0 ) .

Sensitivity analysis
To evaluate the robustness of ourﬁndings, we conducted a sensitivity
analysis that included all available microbiota samples, irrespective of
missing data on sex and age. In this analysis, cases were compared
exclusively to healthy controls from the same study, thereby mini-
mizing potential confounding by age or sex through design rather than
adjustment. This approach enabled the inclusion of a broader set of
samples and diseases.
For each disease pair, we applied random down-sampling to
homogenize the number of cases and controls between groups.
Sequencing instrument and geographic region were included as con-
founding variables. Microbiota disease pro ﬁles were then recon-
structed for each disease, and their correlations assessed as described
