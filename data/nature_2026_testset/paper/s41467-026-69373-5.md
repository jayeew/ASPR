Overall, the results demonstrate that the incorporation of CNF
and the partial replacement of cement with LF enhance the rheological
performance of the cementitious system for 3D printing. A higher
static yield stress directly improves buildability by enabling the
material to better resist the load imposed by subsequent printed lay-
ers. Thixotropy plays a crucial role in 3D-printed concrete. A well-
balanced thixotropic behavior helps maintain the shape stability and
buildability of printed layers
12,39. LF can serve as an effective additive to
control the thixotropy in 3DP concrete. A higher structuration rate is
observed with LF replacement, which beneﬁts 3D printing by enabling
rapid stiffening of freshly deposited layers and improving shape sta-
bility and buildability. However, rapid stiffening may compromise
interlayer bonding when printing la rge objects with extended layer
intervals; thus, maintaining an optimal structuration rate is essential
40.
In addition, the high critical strain may be especially bene ﬁcial for
printing complex geometries, such as large overhangs, where the
material must withstand considerable deformation due to limited
support from the underlying layers

helps the material resist unexpected extrusion inconsistencies, which
may impose temporary high loads and deformation on the printed
layers

Hydration kinetics, particle interaction, and evolution of
microstructure
To elucidate the inﬂuence of LF and CNF on the rheology of cement
paste, we adopted the framework of soft colloidal and rigid interaction
described by Roussel et al., and Douba et al.
17,42,43. The cement paste
system is highly complex, involving both colloidal interactions and
hydration-induced effects that govern its rheological performance.
The soft colloidal interactions include effects such as electrostatic
forces among particles, while the rigid interactions are primarily
associated with hydration reactions, such as C–S–H precipitation and
growth. In this study, zeta potential measurements were performed to
probe the soft colloidal interactions between cement, LF, and CNF. On
the other hand, the origins of the rigid interaction were examined
through the combination of storage modulus measurements, iso-
thermal calorimetry to monitor the heat of hydration, and scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) to visualize the microstructure. Ultimately,
the cement system represents a highly intricate system in which
both soft and rigid interactions originate and evolve concurrently
within a three-dimensional network. Although a complete mechanistic
understanding of how new additives (such as CNF and LF) in ﬂuence
the rheological behavior of complex cementitious systems
remains elusive, the combined experimental evidence highlights cor-
relations that make alternative explanations unlikely
17,41,44,45.W i t h i n
these bounds, the adopted framework, which is also used in the cur-
rent literature
17,42,43, provides a valuable ﬁrst-order understanding of
the dominant mechanisms in the system.
Figure 2a presents the heat ﬂow curves from isothermal calori-
metry normalized by the weight of cement for all mixtures. The heat
ﬂow curves indicate that, overall, CNF has a negligible inﬂuence on the
primary hydration kinetics of cement, regardless of the level of LF
replacement. In contrast, the replacement of PLC with LF considerably
inﬂuences hydration kinetics. The primary peak in the curves shifts to
the left with LF replacement, indicating that LF accelerates cement
hydration, with a more pronounced acceleration effect at higher LF
replacement levels. This acceleration effect was unaffected by differ-
ent CNF additions in the system. Supplementary Fig. 9 illustrates the
cumulative heat release normalized by the weight of cement. The
results indicate that CNF has a negligible effect on total hydration heat.
In contrast, total heat release increases with higher LF replacement
levels. Furthermore, LF can in ﬂuence the duration of the induction
period of cement hydration. This period is important because the
mixing, pumping, and extruding processes of 3D-printed concrete are
typically carried out during this time. Figure 2b shows that the accel-
eration effect remains evident during the induction period due to LF
replacement, with the PLC-LF29 system exhibiting the highest heat
ﬂow and the shortest induction period. On the other hand, Fig. 2c
indicates that CNF exhibits a slight retardation of hydration during the
induction period. In addition, the XRD patterns (Fig. 2d) of different
mixtures show comparable phase assemblages, dominated by typical
hydration products and calcite, with residual unhydrated alite (C
3S)
and belite (C2S) peaks. No distinct additional crystalline phases were
detected, implying that the combination of CNF and LF is unlikely to
introduce any new reactions.
There are several mechanisms reported in previous studies
through which cellulose nano-materials could in ﬂuence cement
hydration. First, the high surface area of CNF could provide nucleation
sites for hydration products that enhance cement hydration
24. Addi-
tionally, the “short-circuit diffusion” effect has been proposed for
cellulose nanocrystals (CNC), where CNC forms channels within the
C–S–H layer, facilitating moisture transport from the pore solution to
the cores of unreacted cement particles, thereby enhancing
hydration
46. In contrast, the hydroxyl and carboxyl groups in CNF
could bind with calcium ions, limiting the number of nucleation sites
and thereby retarding hydration
24.I nt h i ss t u d y ,t h ei nﬂuence of CNF
on cement hydration is minimal. The in ﬂuence of LF on cement
hydration is consistent withﬁndings reported in the literature28,29.T h e
acceleration is primarily attributed to its ﬁller effect29. LF particles
accelerate the hydration process by providing additional surface area
for the nucleation of hydration products and by improving particle
packing, which reduces the average distance between cement grains
and alters the local cement-water environment, collectively accel-
erating the hydration process
47.

are governed by the same nucleation and growth processes associated

Zeta potential measurements were performed following the
methods reported in previous studi es on cellulose nanomaterials in
cement systems25,46. Measurements for cement, CNF, and LF were
conducted in both simulated pore solution (SPS, pH ≈ 13) and deio-
nized (DI) water (pH ≈ 7) (Fig. 2e). The results show that cement par-
ticles are near neutral in both DI water (+0.5 mV) and simulated pore
solution (SPS) ( −3.7 mV), suggesting a tendency to ﬂocculate. CNF
remains strongly anionic ( −35 mV in DI water and −27 mV in SPS),
indicating that self-adsorption is limited as it is considered “moder-
ately stable ” and is within the range of measurements of other
studies
25,46,49,50. The reduction of the zeta potential of CNF in SPS
reﬂects electrostatic screening, whereby cations accumulate closer to
CNF surfaces, with possible speci ﬁc adsorption onto carboxyl and
hydroxyl groups. Given the Ca 2+-rich environment of cement pore
solution and the near-neutral zeta potential of cement particles, these
ions could mediate CNF adsorption onto cement surfaces, similar to
other cellulose nano particles
46,49. Previous studies have also shown
that the hydroxyl and carboxyl surface groups on CNF can effectively
bind to the cement particles, inducing CNF –cement
interactions
25,26,49,51. In contrast, LF exhibits zeta potential shift from
−26 mV in DI water to weakly negative ( −13 mV) in SPS, suggesting
reduced electrostatic stabilization and a higher tendency for aggre-
gation. Because both CNF and LF are negatively charged in SPS, direct
electrostatic attraction between them is unfavorable. Furthermore,
LF’s limited surface functionality allows only minimal ion-mediated
bridging. In this system, CNF acts as high-aspect-ratio ﬁbrils, char-
acterized by micron-scale lengths and nanoscale diameters. Their
geometry makes them less effective at generating classical depletion
zones and, therefore, less likely to induce depletion ﬂocculation.
Consistent with these observations, rheological measurements show
only a limited increase in shear stress upon blending LF with CNF (see
Supplementary Notes 5 and Supplementary Fig. 6 for details), sup-
porting the absence of a strong direct CNF–LF interaction. Moreover,
in SPS, cement is nearly neutral ( −3.7 mV) while LF remains weakly
negative (−13 mV), suggesting that CNF appears more likely to interact
with cement than with LF. Taken together, these results suggest that
CNF ﬁbers tend to adhere to cement particles, with CNF –cement
interactions dominating in the system and contributing to the
observed rheological performances.
The storage modulus of the mixtures shows an insigniﬁcant effect
on CNF addition. Previous studies have reported that the storage
modulus in the cement system is primarily sensitive to hydration-
driven rigid interactions, while colloidal interactions are considered
less stiff
17,18,48,52. Therefore, the limited effect of CNF can be attributed
to its minimal in ﬂuence on hydration, sometimes even slightly
retarding it, which constrains the development of rigid C–S–Hb r i d g e s
and limits enhancement of the storage modulus. Similar trends have
been reported when incorporating other nanomaterials into cement
systems
48,53, where colloidal interactions improve yield stress without a
corresponding increase in storage modulus. Although the underlying
mechanism remains not fully understood, these studies suggest that
colloidal interactions can effectively enhance static yield stress but do
not necessarily lead to an increase in storage modulus for cement
systems. Ultimately, the mechanisms governing the inﬂuence of CNF
on the fresh-state properties of cementitious systems remain complex,
and their effect on the storage modulus also warrants further
investigation.
Consistent with the interpretation of the rheological and calori-
metric results, the zeta potential measurements conﬁrm electrostatic
interactions between CNF and cement particles. The increase in static
yield stress without a corresponding rise in heat of hydration or sto-
rage modulus, combined with the strongly anionic charge of CNF and
the near-neutral charge of cement particles, collectively indicate that
the dominant mechanism governing CNF’si nﬂuence in the fresh state
is colloidal rather than hydration-driven. Theseﬁndings align with the
limited structuration rate measured for CNF and the substantial
increase observed for LF, con ﬁrming that the former acts primarily
through colloidal interactions wh ile the latter promotes hydration-
driven interactions. These ﬁndings leave alternative explanations
inconsistent with the experimental evidence and support the conclu-
sion that CNF –cement interactions originate from electrostatic and
physical associations rather than from the formation of new hydration
products. At the same time, SEM observations (Fig. 2f) revealed CNFs
distributed around cement particles and hydration products, sug-
gesting that these early colloidal networks may gradually transition
into localized rigid interactions. The timing of this transition likely
occurs beyond the fresh-state window relevant to extrusion and layer
deposition in 3D printing. Accordingly, while this study establishes
that CNF enhances rheological performance primarily through colloi-
dal mechanisms during the early stage, the potential evolution of these
interactions into rigid networks at later ages remains an open question
for future investigation.

adequately characterized by a single rheological property
54.T h e
buildability challenges can be characterized by two common failure

the material’s ability to withstand the load deposited on top of it

material’s resistance to deformation under applied force, and critical
strain, which indicates the maximum elastic deformation a material
can undergo without permanent deformation that compromises the
structure’s functional geometry, are important factors in ﬂuencing
buckling behavior.

geometries with overhangs.

Discussion
This study advances material development for 3D-printed concrete by
addressing key challenges related to rheology, mechanical perfor-
mance, sustainability, and cost ef ﬁciency. The incorporation of only
0.3% cellulose nano ﬁber (CNF) results in a ninefold increase in static
yield stress. The replacement of cement with LF increases the storage
modulus and structuration rate. Combined rheological and micro-
structural analyses reveal that LF promotes fresh-state stiffening
through accelerated hydration, while CNF enhances rheological per-
formance via colloidal interactions. CNF also enhances the
compressive and ﬂexural strength, which effectively mitigates the
dilution effect associated with the high-level replacement of
cement with LF. CNF does not exhibit a notable inﬂuence on hydration
kinetics but improves the mechanical performance of the material by
forming a more cohesive matrix or by bridging micropores and
microcracks.
The proposed mixture formulation distinguishes itself in terms of
sustainability, achieving performance improvements while reducing
both cost and carbon footprint. CNF was found to be the most effec-
tive and cost-efﬁcient additive for enhancing static yield stress com-
pared with other modiﬁers reported in previous studies. The TEA and
full cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment demonstrate that the com-
bined use of CNF and LF reduces both the MSP and GW impact. When
considering compressive strength as the functional unit, the MSP and
GW impact of the PLC-LF29-CNF0.30 mixture are reduced by 12.1% and
34.4%, respectively, relative to the reference case.
Robotic-arm 3D printing demonstrated the feasibility of fabri-
cating large-scale elements with challenging overhang geometries. The
developed material exhibited superior buildability compared to two
commercially available 3D-printing mixtures under identical condi-
tions. This demonstration indicates the scalability of the proposed
mixture and its potential for full-scale construction applications.
Future work on full construction-scale 3D printing will be valuable for
further demonstrating the scalability and practical applicability of the
developed mixture.
Methods
Materials and mixtures
All mixtures were prepared with a water-to-binder ratio of 0.4 (by
weight). For the mortar used in 3D printing and mechanical evaluation,
a sand-to-binder ratio of 1 (by weight) was used. Two levels of LF
replacement were studied: (1) PLC with an additional 14% LF replacing
cement, denoted as PLC-LF14, resulting in a total of 25% LF replace-
ment compared to OPC without LF; (2) PLC with an additional 29% LF
replacing cement, denoted as PLC-LF29, resulting in a total of 40% LF
replacement compared to OPC without LF. ASTM C595 permits lime-
stone replacement up to 15 wt% in Portland-limestone cements (Type
IL), and EN 197-1 allows up to 35 wt% replacement. Our selected values
are aligned with these established limits and intended to investigate
the effect of CNF across a practical range of LF incorporations. CNF
was incorporated into the mixture as an addition, with its dry weight
calculated as a percentage of the weight of the binder. The selected
CNF dosage range was determined based on preliminary screening,
which indicated that low dosages effectively in ﬂuence the fresh
properties of cementitious mixtures. Previous studies have also
reported that small amounts of CNF can effectively affect cement
performance
51,58; therefore, a low dosage range (0 –0.3 wt%) was
adopted in this study to evaluate the in ﬂuence of CNF on the rheolo-
gical and mechanical behavior of the mixtures. For the rheology tests
and isothermal calorimetry, paste material (without sand) was used.
For the small-scale printing and mechanical tests, river sand with a
maximum particle size of 0.6 mm was used as the ﬁne aggregate for
mortar. For large-scale printing, river sand with a maximum particle
size of 2.36 mm was used as the ﬁne aggregate. Methylcellulose (Mil-
lipore Sigma, viscosity 15 cP) was incorporated in all mixtures. A high-
range water reducer (HRWRA, Master Glenium 7700) was used only for
the PLC-LF0-CNF0.3 mixture during small-scale printing to overcome
the extrusion limitations associated with syringe-based printing. The
water reducer was not used for large-scale printing. Physical and che-
mical properties of the materials are detailed in Supplementary Note 1;
Supplementary Tables 1; 2 and Supplementary Figs. 1; 2. Detailed mix
proportions are included in Supplementary Notes 3 and Supplemen-
tary Table 3.
Mixing procedure
High shear mixing was used to effectively disperse the CNF. The
mixing water was ﬁrst added to the CNF slurry, which was then
subjected to high-speed shear mixing using a regular blender (Ninja
Generation II, 1000W) for 2 min to ensure proper dispersion of the
CNF. For rheology tests, paste materials were prepared with a con-
sistent shear history. The dry LF and cement were mixed until a
homogeneous blend was achieved. The pre-mixed CNF solution
(containing mixing water and CNF after high shear mixing) was
measured and added to the dry materials, which were then mixed
for two minutes using a hand mixer at a consistent speed. The
paste was subsequently loaded into the cup and hand-tamped to
remove air bubbles before transferring to the rheometer. A similar
mixing procedure was used for isothermal calorimetry tests. For the
mortar used in small-scale 3D printing and mechanical testing, all
dry solid materials were measured and mixed in a Hobart 5-Qt bowl
mixer at low speed for 2 min. The pre-mixed CNF solution was then
added to the solids and mixed at medium speed for an additional two
minutes. For large-scale 3D printing, a large 3-1/2 cubic ft. mixer was
used. The dry solid materials were ﬁrst mixed in the mixer until a
homogeneous blend was achieved, after which the pre-mixed CNF
solution was added and mixed until a uniform consistency mortar was
reached.

Rheology test
Detailed rheological protocols used to evaluate the in ﬂuence of
different additives on cement paste are described in Supplementary
Notes 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5. Following the protocol described
in Supplementary Fig. 5, at least three measurements were collecte-
d using fresh mixtures prepared following the same mixing
procedure and controlling the time between water addition and
rheological characterization. For the tests using the protocol
described in Supplementary Fig. 5, an Anton Paar MCR 702 rheometer
were used with a vane geometry 24 mm in diameter and 30 mm
in length. The measurement cup had a diameter of 28.92 mm.
For the structuration rate tests, due to limited access to the
Anton Paar rheometer, a TA Instruments AR-G2 rheometer was used.
A similar vane geometry with a diameter of 28 mm and length
of 42 mm was used, and the measurement cup had a diameter
of 30.4 mm. All rheological measurements were conducted with an
8 mm gap at 25 °C under standard laboratory relative humidity
conditions.
Mechanical test
Compressive tests were conducted on cast cube specimens
(50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm) after 28 days of curing in the regular
laboratory environment. The material preparation followed the
mixing procedure described in the materials and mixing procedure
section. To ensure accurate testing, the surfaces in contact with the
mold were used, providing a ﬂat surface for proper contact with the
loading and support ﬁxtures. Compressive strength was assessed
using an MTS machine with a 300 kN capacity, at a displacement rate
of 0.5 mm/min. Flexural tests for cast and 3D printed element were all
performed under 3-point bending con ﬁguration with similar sample
dimensions. To evaluate the anisotropic properties of the 3D-printed
elements, two types of ﬁlament conﬁgurations were tested: one with
interfaces parallel to the loading direction (90°) and the other with
interfaces perpendicular to the loading direction (0°). These ﬂexural
tests were conducted after 7 days curing in the regular laboratory
environment. The top and bottom surfaces of the 3DP beams were
smooth, ensuring adequate contact with the loading and support ﬁx-
tures. Consequently, the specimens were tested in their “as-printed”
orientation. For the cast elements, the surfaces in contact with the
mold were used, as the smooth interface provides proper contact with
the ﬁxtures. All ﬂexural tests were carried out using an MTS machine
with a 10 kN capacity and a displacement rate of 0.2 mm/min. At least
three measurements were collected for each test group for mechanical
testing.
Isothermal calorimetry
Isothermal calorimetry was used to evaluate the in ﬂuence of CNF
and LF on cement hydration kinetics, which also provides insight
into the results observed in the rheology tests. Hydration kinetics
of the hydrating mixes were investigated at 23 °C using isothermal
calorimetry (TAM air). Following the mixing procedures
described above, approximately 10 g of paste was loaded into each
standardized glass ampule. The hydration of PLC and two LF replace-
ment levels (PLC-LF14 and PLC-LF29) with three CNF addition levels
(0.15, 0.3, and 0.5%) was studied. The heat ﬂow data was recorded
for 5 days.
Thermogravimetric analysis
TGA was performed to assess the inﬂuence of CNF and LF on cement
hydration under air-cured conditions. A Thermogravimetric Analyzer
(Q50, TA Instruments) was used to heat the samples to a maximum
temperature of 900 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min to assess weight
loss and quantify chemically bound water. Samples for TGA analysis
were taken from broken pieces of specimens tested for compressive
strength after 28 days of dry curing. To arrest hydration, the samples
underwent solvent exchange with isopropanol. Mortar pieces were
immersed in 120 ml of isopropanol for 5 days, and the isopropanol was
replaced after the ﬁrst 3 days. The mortar pieces were then dried and
stored in a vacuum desiccator for an additional 4 days to evaporate the
isopropanol. The dried samples were ground into powder and sieved
through a #200 mesh before TGA testing. The chemically bound water
was quantiﬁed as the weight loss occurring between 100 and 600 °C
during the TGA test.
