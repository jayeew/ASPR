Potency (IC50) prediction of small molecules is pivotal for anticancer drug development. This study
benchmarked ﬁve deep learning (DL) models for IC 50 prediction— DeepCDR, DrugCell, PaccMann,
Precily, and tCNN — against a simple mean-based Baseline using standardized GDSC datasets and
recently published anticancer compounds. To ensure practicality, conventional error metrics were
supplemented with percentage error, log error, three-sigma limit, and a newly proposed Experimental
Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy statistic. The models performed well on randomly split data and
unseen cell lines but showed sharply reduced accuracy for unseen compounds. Though all DL models
exhibited similar performance trends, DeepCDR, DrugCell, and tCNN held a slight edge in most testing
scenarios. Interestingly, several DL algorithms could not signiﬁcantly outperform the Baseline model in
many tests. Assessing prediction error against physicochemical and biological properties of
compounds and cell lines revealed weak correlation, highlighting an underexplored aspect of model
performance. A user-friendly web server (https://nlplab1.isical.ac.in/ic50.php) was also developed for
IC
50 prediction of new compounds against cancer cell lines.

Results and discussion
Evaluation metrics
To assess the performance of the prediction models, we initially use standard
evaluation metrics as followed in the ML community:
1. Pearson Correlation ( r): Measures the linear relationship between
predicted and actual values, ranging from–1 (perfect negative corre-
lation) to 1 (perfect positive correlation), with 0 indicating no linear
correlation.
2. Coef ﬁcient of Determination (R
2): Quantiﬁes the proportion of var-
iance in the observed data that is explained by the model. While values
close to 1 indicate a strong predictive ﬁt, R
2 can also be zero or
negative–implying that the model performs no better, or worse, than
using the mean of the data as a prediction.

3. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): The square root of the average of

While these metrics provide an overall assessment of a model ’s
performance, they do not capture the practical acceptability of predic-
tions in laboratory settings. For instance, a model with R
2 = 0.7 and
RMSE = 1.0 may still leave a lab chemist uncertain about the actual
reliability of its IC
50 predictions. To address this limitation, we adopt the
use of simple percentage error and log base-10 error, which offer
improved interpretability for experimentally measured quantities such as
IC
50. Speciﬁcally, we calculated the Absolute Percentage Error (APE) for
each prediction and the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) as a
summary error metric across all predictions. The absolute base-10
logarithmic error (ALE) and its mean, MALE, across all predictions, were
also computed. On the ALE scale, a value of 0.3 corresponds to an error
by a factor of 2, 1 corresponds to an error by a factor of 10, and a value of
2 corresponds to an error by a factor of 100.
Another key consideration in evaluation is normalization and rescal-
ing. ML model outputs are typically normalized before training. For IC
50 or
ln[IC50] prediction, values are scaled to the [0,1] range, and error metrics are
computed on this normalized scale. However, such evaluation does not
reﬂect the true error in IC 50 values, making it less meaningful for practi-
tioners. For example, in a dataset where IC50 values range from 0.01μMt o
20 μM, the corresponding lnIC50 values span [−4.6052, 2.9957]. If a model
is trained to predict ln[IC50], these values are min-max normalized to [0,1].
Suppose the model predicts 0.2 for a given compound –cell line pair; this
corresponds to 0.3158 in the ln[IC50] scale, which converts back to an IC50 of
1.3714μM. If the experimental IC50 is 0.05 μM( l n [ I C50]= −1.9957, map-
ped as 0.0654 on the [0,1] scale), then APE in normalized scale is 206
(experimental: 0.0654, predicted: 0.2), and APE in actual IC
50 values is 2643
(experimental: 0.05 μM, predicted: 1.3714 μM). This drastic difference
arises due to the nonlinearity in the scale conversion between IC 50 and
normalized outputs. Unlike traditional ML approaches, we evaluated the
APE and ALE directly on IC 50 values, free from any normalization or
rescaling, as the ultimate objective of these models is to provide accurate IC50
predictions for real-world applications.
While MAPE and MALE give useful ideas in measuring IC 50
values, these metrics, along with r, R2, and RMSE, implicitly assume the
experimentally derived IC50 to be the absolute ground truth. To date, no
popular accuracy metric incorporates any tolerance for the inherent
variability associated with IC
50 values determined by cell viability
assays, which are in ﬂuenced by a variety of intricate and often
uncontrollable factors. Predictions with high APE and ALE may
be considered practically very useful in laboratory settings, as IC 50
for the same compound –cell line may vary across experiments
and laboratories. For example, if the experimental IC 50 is 0.05 μM,
and the model predicts 0.15 μM, the APE is high, 300%, but the pre-
diction is still relevant as the IC 50 is within the error range commonly
faced in labs. Given these limitations, we developed a new evaluation
measure, Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy
(EVAPA), which incorporates empirical estimates of IC
50 variability to
offer a more practical and context-aware assessment of model
performance.

Benchmarking performance on GDSC dataset
The performance of the ﬁve deep learning models and the simple mean-
based Baseline model was evaluated under three data partitioning strategies:
Random (R), No Common Cell lines (NCC), and No Common Drugs
(NCD) splits. In the R split, models are trained and tested on randomly
sampled subsets, allowing overlap between compounds and cell lines. This
idealized setup offers a representative view of the dataset and yields reliable
overall performance estimates but does not evaluate generalization to unseen
chemical or biological entities. The NCC split assesses generalization to new
cancer cell lines by ensuring that training and test sets contain disjoint cell
line subsets, simulating predictions f or previously unobserved biological
samples. The NCD split evaluates dr ug generalization by partitioning
compounds into non-overlapping sets, testing a model’s ability to predict the
efﬁcacy of entirely new molecules in established cell lines: a scenario aligned
with real-world drug discovery tasks. The evaluation results, as presented in
Fig. 3, include the Pearson correlation coefﬁcient (r), coefﬁcient of deter-

In essence, the results on the GDSC dataset, employing diverse and
rigorous evaluation strategies, reporting performance across multiple sce-
narios, highlight a critical limitation: although models perform well (much
better than the Baseline) under ideal conditions typically used for testing
machine learning models, i.e., random split, their extrapolative power to
unseen chemical structures is signiﬁcantly limited. Notably, the R and NCC
test sets remain largely in-distribution (ID), whereas the NCD split presents
out-of-distribution (OOD) test samples

fewer in the GDSC database (only ~200 compounds). This distinction in the

cell line–speciﬁc patterns for predicting IC

be inferred with certainty, as the Baseline, without any such “learning",
performs equally well in NCC.
Inﬂuence of compound and cell line properties on performance
We analyzed how chemical and biological properties of compounds and
cell lines in ﬂuence model performance, offering insights for medicinal
chemists and pharmacologists. Speci ﬁcally, we assessed the impact of
compounds’ physicochemical properties, target pathways (re ﬂecting
modes of action), and cell line tissue types on IC 50 prediction accuracy.
Based on the average error metrics computed across ﬁve-fold random
splits, DeepCDR and DrugCell emerged as the best-performing models,
and thus further analysis on these is reported. The three other models
also exhibited similar trends. The error metric chosen for evaluating
compound properties was the log
10-scaled drug-wise MAPE(IC 50),
deﬁned as the MAPE of IC 50 values, calculated across all drug –cell line
pairs corresponding to each individual compound (or drug). The
variability trends were conﬁrmed by considering other metrics like drug-

Conclusions
This study evaluated the performance of ﬁve deep learning models for
predicting the IC50 values of small-molecule compounds against cancer cell
lines. To more effectively benchmarkmodel performance on heterogeneous
biological data, we incorporated metrics such as MAPE, MALE, and
EVAPA, rarely used in ML evaluatio ns, to yield more interpretable and
experimentally meaningful insights. The development of EVAPA, which
accounts for inherent experimental variability in IC
50 and similar measures
like GR50, underscores a fundamental limitation of drug potency data 47.
Although the heterogeneity of pharmacological databases is well recognized,
proposed solutions such as adopting e xperimentally consistent and less
variable parameters have yet to gain widespread acceptance48. Constructing
databases around novel, more standardized measures could substantially
improve both the performance and evaluation of ML models.
The DL models’IC50 prediction accuracy was analyzed against a simple
mean-based Baseline model, on stan dardized datasets derived from the
GDSC database, as well as on recentl y published anticancer compounds,
which serve as a surrogate for experimental validation. The DL models
demonstrated strong performance on randomly split data, which they were
originally trained to tackle, and on unseen cell lines. However, predictive
accuracy dropped substantially wh en evaluated on unseen compounds,
both from the GDSC and the literature. This underscores a critical limitation
of existing IC
50 prediction models: the inadequate ability to extrapolate to
structurally novel compounds, whichconstitute OOD samples. Since non-
ML, simple mean-based predictions unexpectedly match and occasionally
exceed the accuracy of several DL modelsin tests involving unseen cell lines
and unseen drugs, incorporating such a Baseline model is proven to be
essential when reporting ML performance. This benchmarking further
highlights that the simple Baseline offers a competitive alternative to the
complex, computationally expensive DL algorithms. The analysis of model
performance with respect to the chemical and biological properties of
compounds and cell lines indicated t hat prediction accuracy was largely
independent of these factors.
In future work, we aim to apply improved deep learning archi-
tectures, incorporating OOD learning strategies such as domain gen-
eralization and meta-learning

compounds by enabling better chemical feature representation and
leveraging more diverse training data. We also plan to collaborate with
industry and academia to assess the real-world utility of these models
in drug discovery pipelines. Enhancing model explainability will also

EVAPA
The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC 50), experimentally mea-
sured in cell viability assays, can vary signi ﬁcantly. To address this issue
while evaluating IC50 predictions, we introduced the performance metric
Experimental Variability-Aware Prediction Accuracy (EVAPA), extending
the method of Kalliokoski et al.
49 from protein–ligand to drug –cell line
measurements.
Using the ChEMBL_35 web interface (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/),
we ﬁltered “SMALL MOLECULE ” and “L01 – ANTINEOPLASTIC
AGENTS” by descending “Bioactivities” and selected the top compounds
with the highest number of reported bioactivity measurements. Theﬁlters
“IC50”, “Homo sapiens”,a n d“cell lines” were then applied, and the resulting
bioactivity data were downloade d. Redundant or null columns were
removed, retaining only rows with“<”, “=“,o r “≤” standard relations and
“nM” standard unit.
According to the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC)
database ( https://www.cancerrxgene.org/help#t_curve), Syto60, Resa-
zurin, or CellTiter-Glo assays were used to screen compounds with a
72-h drug-cell incubation period. We extracted both assay name and
incubation time from the “Assay Description ” for each drug –cell line
pair. Because Syto60 assays did not appear in ChEMBL data, we
focused on Resazurin and CellTiter-Glo assays. AlamarBlue and
CellTiter-Blue were treated as equivalent to Resazurin. Notably, the
assay name and duration could appear in various formats (e.g.,
“CellTiter-Glo”, “Celltiter-Glo”, “cell titer glo ”), and 72 hr or 3 days
were considered identical to 72 h. For each of the compounds with the
highest number of anticancer bioactivities reported, the data were
ﬁltered to include only these assay names and incubation times. We
then collated IC

Datasets
Genomics of drug sensitivity in cancer (GDSC)-derived dataset. The
GDSC database is a widely used pharmacogenomic resource representing
the IC
50 of a drug on a given cancer cell line. It (GDSC2 is an updated
version with additional compounds, cell lines, and follows more stan-
dardized and precise assays) includes over 1000 cancer cell lines from
various tissue types and more than 250 anticancer compounds, including
chemotherapeutic agents and targeted inhibitors. For IC
50 prediction,
GDSC provides genomic features such as mutations, copy number var-
iations, and gene expression data, along with SMILES representations,
molecular ﬁngerprints, and chemical descriptors for drugs. The full
dataset initially contains approximately 200,000 cell line-compound
pairs, with each cell line tested against up to 250 compounds and each
compound screened across 500 –1000 cell lines.
A key consideration is the range of drug concentrations tested. Many
IC
50 values exceed the maximum tested co ncentration, leading to extra-
polated estimates rather than direct measurements. To ensure reliable
training, we ﬁltered out these extrapolated values, reducing the dataset to
approximately 51,000 experimentally measured cell line–compound pairs,
which were used for training and evaluation. This ﬁltered GDSC dataset
provides a strong foundation for evaluating deep learning models in drug
response prediction.
Novel anticancer compounds from literature . We conducted a lit-
erature review within the ACS Journal of Medicinal Chemistry and the
European Journal of Medicinal Chemistry to identify research articles
focused on the discovery of anticancer or antitumor small molecules.
Speciﬁcally, studies reporting IC
50 values obtained from cell viability or
cytotoxicity assays against cancer cell lines available in GDSC were
selected. Nine papers published from 2021 onwards met these inclusion
criteria
30–39. From each selected paper, chemical structures of molecules
tested against cell lines documented in the GDSC database were manually
extracted and converted to SMILES representations using ChemDraw
Professional v16.0.1.4. Corresponding IC
50 values and their reported
standard deviations were recorded in μM units. Entries presenting IC 50
values expressed only as lower bounds (e.g., > x μM) were excluded from
the dataset. Only those compound –cell line pairs for which predictions
could be generated by all four models were selected. Consequently, a test
set comprising 209 compound –cell line pairs was assembled. Some
compounds were represented as generic or canonical SMILES as well as
isomeric SMILES, to examine the prediction accuracy variation with
SMILES. This test set of previously unseen compounds included data
derived from diverse assay protocols and comprised both covalent and
non-covalent inhibitors. None of these compounds was present in the
GDSC database.
Models
In this experiment, the task involves predicting IC50 from the disease state
(cell line) and therapeutic or drug (s mall molecule), where cell lines are
described by omics pro ﬁles (e.g., genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics)
and molecules by chemical structures or SMILES. We focus only on the
methods that are based on deep learning methodologies, as deep neural
models have outperformed traditional machine learning algorithms by a
large margin for different decision -making tasks. The key difference
between the two paradigms lies in fea ture representation: traditional
machine learning algorithms rely on ha ndcrafted features, whereas deep
learning leverages large datasets to learn representations automatically. The
main challenge in applying deep learning based models for IC
50 prediction is
choosing the appropriate input representations for drug and cell line. DL
models for the prediction of IC50 mostly rely on embedding techniques that
originated in theﬁeld of Natural Language Processing (NLP)52.T h ei d e ai st o
generate a semantically meaningful vector of a much lower dimension for a
given sequence. Since generation of embedding for a large sequence such as
genomics or transcriptomics proﬁles is still a technical challenge
53, different
strategies are followed by differentmethods. To calibrate the DL methods’
performance, we designed a simple mean-based“Baseline" model based on
the training and testing sets following the splits used for the DL models. The
following sections discuss how the methods addressed here feed inputs to
the deep models and what type of DL algorithms are used to predict IC
50.
DeepCDR. DeepCDR22 is a hybrid graph convolutional network (GCN)-
based approach that integrates multi-omics pro ﬁles of cancer cells with
structural drug representations to predict IC50 values. Unlike models that
rely on handcrafted chemical descriptors or SMILES-based embeddings,
DeepCDR uses a Uniform Graph Convolutional Network (UGCN) to
automatically learn the latent topological representation of drug mole-
cules. On the genomic side, it processes multiple layers of biological data,

including genomic mutations, transcriptomic expression levels, and
epigenomic modi ﬁcations. These multi-omics features are passed
through specialized subnetworks, where high-level feature extraction is
performed before merging them with the graph-based drug representa-
tions. The integrated features are then processed using a 1D CNN to
generate the ﬁnal IC
50 predictions. The distinguishing feature of
DeepCDR is its application of GCNs to represent drug structures,
enabling direct learning of molecular interactions.
DrugCell. DrugCell 23 is a deep learning framework that predicts IC 50
values by modeling cancer cells as a hierarchical system of biological
processes. Instead of treating genomic features as independent entities,
DrugCell organizes them into a structured Visible Neural Network
(VNN) where neurons represent biological subsystems, such as signaling
pathways, organelles, and cellular functions. This approach ensures that
the model ’s decision-making process aligns with known biological
structures, enhancing interpretability. The model takes in binary muta-
tion data as input, encoding genomic features based on their hierarchical
relationships rather than as a simple ﬂat vector. Drug representations are
processed separately using Morgan ﬁngerprints, a well-established
method for encoding chemical structures. These two representations-
one derived from a biologically structured VNN and the other from a
traditional arti ﬁcial neural network (ANN)-are merged at a late fusion
stage to predict IC
50 values. DrugCell’s key distinction is its biologically
structured VNN, which represents cellular processes in a hierarchical
manner.
PaccMann. PaccMann 24 is a multi-modal deep learning model that
integrates three essential components for drug sensitivity prediction: the
molecular structure of compounds, transcriptomic pro ﬁles of cancer
cells, and prior knowledge of protein-protein interactions. Unlike tra-
ditional models that treat gene expression as a ﬂat feature vector, Pacc-
Mann employs an attention-based encoding mechanism to assign higher
weights to the most informative genes, thereby reducing the dimen-
sionality of the input space while preserving critical biological signals.
The model provides three different encoding strategies for SMILES-based
drug representations: a bidirectional recurrent neural network (RNN), a
convolutional encoder, and an attention-based encoder.
Precily. Precily 25 is another deep neural network (DNN)-based frame-
work designed to predict IC50 values by leveraging gene expression data.
Instead of using raw gene expression pro ﬁles, it computes pathway
enrichment scores from bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-
seq) data, allowing for a more biologically meaningful representation. On
the drug side, Precily processes molecular descriptors derived from
SMILES representations using SMILESVec, which converts chemical
structures into 100-dimensional vector embeddings. The model inte-
grates these pathway enrichment scores and drug descriptors as input
features to train a regression model that predicts log-transformed IC
50
values (ln[IC50]). A distinct feature of Precily is the use of pathway-based
representation, which groups genes into functional pathways rather than
treating them as independent variables.
tCNN. tCNN, or twin Convolutional Neural Networks 26, is designed to
improve IC50 prediction by directly extracting features from both drug
and cell line data using convolutional architectures. The model processes
genetic feature vectors from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer
(GDSC) database for cell line representation, while drugs are represented
using their SMILES notation, which is passed through a dedicated con-
volutional neural network. These two parallel CNNs independently
extract meaningful features from the genetic and molecular input spaces
before merging them into a fully connected network that predicts IC
50
values. Unlike models that rely on prede ﬁned molecular descriptors or
pathway scores, tCNN learns from raw sequence-based representations,
capturing intricate patterns from high-dimensional genomic and che-
mical data. Its key novelty lies in its use of CNNs to process SMILES
representations, allowing the model to learn spatially relevant molecular
interactions in an end-to-end fashion.

Baseline. For each split (random or R, no common cell lines or NCC
and no common drugs or NCD), we built a simple mean-based pre-
dictor on the training data, excluding the validation and test data, used
for the DL models: (i) for R the Baseline prediction is the global mean of
IC
50 across all training drug –cell pairs (only one IC 50 value); (ii) for
NCC the prediction is the drug –wise mean IC 50 (mean of drug D over
all corresponding cell lines: D − C1, D − C2, ...); (iii) for NCD the
prediction is the cell line –wise mean IC 50 (mean of cell line C over all
drugs or compounds: C − D1, C − D2, ...). Evaluation employed the
same metrics as for DL models (including EVAPA on IC 50) for the test
sets derived from GDSC and the “unknown” compounds taken from
literature. Following the used ﬁve-fold cross-validation for the DL
models, the Baseline was recomputed from the training portion of each
fold to avoid test leakage. For reporting results, we consider the mean of
the error metrics, as the standard deviations are quite low (see sup-
plementary data).
Implementation details
The ﬁve deep learning models— DeepCDR, DrugCell, PaccMann, Precily,
and tCNN— were trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU. All models
were optimized using the Adam optimi zer, with the mean squared error
(MSE) employed as the loss function. All other hyperparameters were
retained as speci ﬁed in the respective original implementations. The
genomic mutation data, gene expression data, and DNA methylation data
used for training the models were downloaded from the DeMap portal
(https://depmap.org). We performed ﬁve-fold cross-validation on the ran-
dom, cell line, and drug splits, and the model corresponding to the lowest
mean squared error value on the validation set was chosen for evaluation.
