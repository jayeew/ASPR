Photochemical sensing demands systems that adjust their optical properties without altering
chromophore structure. Here, we report CPCQ, a thermally activated delayed ﬂuorescence (TADF)-
active macrocycle, as a responsive platform for optical sensing. CPCQ shows a photoluminescence
quantum yield (PLQY) of 78%, a delayed lifetime of 243 ns, and an ef ﬁcient reverse intersystem
crossing rate of 2.68 × 10 ⁷ s⁻¹. Unlike conventional ﬂuorescent and TADF emitters, CPCQ with cyclic
array of four TADF active donor-acceptors enables reversible state switching of its emission
mechanism. Electron-deﬁcient guests form exciplexes with reducing ﬂuorescence and shifting
emission. In contrast, electron-rich guests enhance delayed ﬂuorescence (248 ns) via the heavy atom
effect. Oxygen quenches charge-transfer emission and restores blue-shifted locally excited (LE)
emission. Strikingly, hydrogen ﬂips the effect, triples LE emission (k
r = 2.07 × 108 s-1, PLQY = 93%), and
hints at superradiance from cooperative behavior of donor– acceptor units. These adaptive responses
establish CPCQ as a highly sensitive and selective molecular tool for next-generation sensing and
optoelectronic applications.
In photochemistry, the prevailing strategy has long involved designing
chromophores tailored to perform a single, predeﬁned function1–3.T h e s e
molecules are synthesized with precision, often guided by a speciﬁce m i s s i o n
proﬁle or energy transfer characteristic. Yet nature operates with a strikingly
different approach. In living organisms, a single chromophore can carry out
multiple roles, switching behavior depending entirely on its environment4.
Microbial rhodopsins serve as a compelling example. Despite all containing
the same all-trans retinal chromoph ore, their emission spans a broad
spectral window from 435 to 590 nm
5–8. This natural adaptability is driven
by steric and electrostatic interactions within their protein scaffolds, which
modulate the photophysical properties without altering the chromophore
itself. Inspired by this biological strategy, researchers have begun to explore
systems where function arises from context rather than static molecular
design
9,10.
Supramolecular chemistry provides the ideal platform for this vision11.
In particular, macrocyclic hosts such as calixarenes12–14, cyclodextrins15–17,
cucurbiturils18–20,p i l l a r a r e n e s21, and metallosupramolecular cages22–27 offer
structurally robust, well-deﬁned cavities capable of engaging in selective
molecular recognition. These hosts ar e often praised for their chemical
stability, modularity, and capacity for reversible interactions, all essential
traits for constructing dynamic, responsive systems. Literature has long
established its utility in diverse areas, including catalysis
28–33,m o l e c u l a r
sensing34,35, and targeted drug delivery36–38. More recently, their applications
in light-driven processes have gained attention30,32. The ability of macro-
cyclic hosts to form host–guest complexes in solution enables researchers
to probe excited-state behaviors with high ﬁdelity. In contrast, solid-state
systems often suffer from uncontroll ed packing, unpredictable inter-
molecular interactions, and quenchi ng due to impurities or long-range
energy migration. A particularly rich area of exploration lies in the charge-
transfer (CT) states that arise during host –guest interactions32,39.T h e s e
states, formed in both the ground and excited electronic conﬁgurations, are
central to tuningﬂuorescence and energy transfer processes. Foundational
studies with cucurbiturils and cyclodextrins have shown how conﬁnement
within a rigid cavity can restrict v ibrational relaxation and enhance
radiative decay, incr easing emission ef ﬁciency39–42. Guest-dependent
behavior further allows for dynamic modulation- certain guests can acti-
vate ﬂuorescence, while others suppress it, depending on their electronic
nature. These properties have been exploited in the development of sensors
for small molecules, ions, and gases. Literature reports demonstrate mac-
rocyclic systems capable of detecting O
2,t r a p p i n gC O₂, and even capturing
toxic gases such as CH2 =C H2,S O₂,S F₆,a n dN O₂43–47. However, reported
systems are either non-emissive or rely solely on ﬂuorescence, limiting
them to short-lived singlet excited-state pathways, narrowing their func-
tional range.

Thermally activated delayedﬂuorescence (TADF) offers a solution48–53.
TADF-active materials harvest longer-lived triplet excitons by thermally
upconverting them to singlet states, producing delayed emission without
relying on heavy metals. When incorporated into macrocycles, this
mechanism enables highly sensitive , tunable optical responses. While
emerging reports have demonstrated TADF in host–guest frameworks, few
studies have explored how the cavity environment itself could be leveraged
to modulate TADF behavior
54–61. This presents a clear opportunity. By
adapting nature ’sm o d e l—where structure de ﬁn e sf u n c t i o nt h r o u g h
environmental tuning—we can design supramolecular systems that shift
their optical output with molecular precision. The result is a new class of
smart, light-responsive materials with broad utility in sensing and
optoelectronics.
Here, we set out to explore the TADF-active cyclic conjugate,CPCQ
(Photoluminescence quantum yield, PLQY = 78%, τ
DF = 243 ns, reverse
intersystem crossing rate, kRISC =2 . 6 8×1 07 s-1, transition dipole moment,
μ= 6.74 D), introduced in our earlier report (Fig.1)53. With its rigid cavity
designed to hold small organic guests, CPCQ offers a way to tune optical
signals. By exploitingCPCQ’s electronic traits, we chose terephthalonitrile
(TPN) and phthalonitrile (PN) as electron-deﬁcient guests to disrupt the CT
state. This triggered exciplex formation (CPCQ ⊃TPN, ϕPLQY = 32%;
CPCQ⊃TPN, ϕPLQY = 45%) when electron-poor guests entered the cavity.
In contrast, electron-rich guest with heavy atoms (1,4-diiodobenzene, 1,4-
DIB) boosted delayed emission and raised the overall PLQY (ϕ
PLQY = 81%).
Surprisingly, exposingCPCQ to hydrogen gas (H2) caused superradiance-
like behavior (ϕPLQY = 93%), while oxygen gas (O2) dynamically quenched it
(ϕPLQY = 39%), shifting the emission from CT to local emission (LE). Other
gases, like sulfur hexa ﬂuoride (SF6), sulfur dioxide (SO 2), and methane
(CH4) caused irreversible quenching. These results open doors to sensing
technologies by harnessing CPCQ’s unique interactions with different
molecules.
Results and discussion
The host compoundCPCQ shows strong blue TADF, with a high PLQY of
78%, impressive k RISC of 2.68 × 107 s−1,w i t hal o w ΔEST of 0.13 eV and
prompt (PF) and delayed ﬂuorescence (DF) lasting 12.1 ns and 243 ns,
respectively. This performance arises from an increased local triplet (LE)
density near the lowest singlet charge-transfer (CT) state, generated by both
through-bond and through-space CT, which enhances spin –orbit and
vibronic couplings. Because of its cyclic structure, CPCQ forms a clear
internal cavity around the quinoxali ne acceptor - an ideal site for guest
inclusion. Our approach in choosing guest molecules had three clear aims.
We anticipated guests capable of disruptingCPCQ’s internal CT pathways
by forming inclusion complexes. Then, we aimed to capture how these
complexes altered CPCQ’s optical signals. Lastly, we explored CPCQ’s
potential as a gas sensor by observingquenching and coherence effects. To
support these aims, we used cyclic voltammetry to examineCPCQ’sr e d o x
proﬁle (Supplementary Fig. S1). Based on energy alignments, we identiﬁed
electron-poor guests like phthalonitrile (PN) and terephthalonitrile (TPN),
and electron-rich ones like 1,4-diio dobenzene (1,4-DIB). We also chose
gases such as oxygen (O
2), hydrogen (H 2), sulfur dioxide (SO 2), sulfur
hexaﬂuoride (SF6), and methane (CH4)t ot e s tCPCQ’s gas-sensing abilities.
These choices allowed comprehensive exploration of CPCQ’s sensing
potential through measurable optical responses.
Analyzing host-guest interactions using NMR proved dif ﬁcult, as
aromatic signals from both host and guests overlapped signi ﬁcantly,
appearing close together at δ 7.0–7.3 ppm. However, matrix-assisted laser
desorption/ionization (MALDI) analysis successfully conﬁrmed that com-
plexes did form in every case tested. Each aromatic guest reliably produced a

naturally shows broad absorption spectra, adding one equivalent of guest
did not change its absorption features. This unchanged absorption also
excludes the possibility of a CT sta te (ground state) forming upon com-
plexation, as highlighted in Supplementary Fig. S3. Molecular dynamics
simulations later provided deﬁnitive conﬁrmation of host-guest binding,
discussed further in a subsequent section.

upon adding guests. This absence of spectral change indicates that no CT
phenomenon occurs in the ground state. However, host-guest binding
interactions can still effectively b e explored using PL spectroscopy. To
investigate these interactions, we carried outﬂuorescence titration experi-

Conclusions
In summary, the optical properties ofCPCQ were systematically studied in
the presence of small molecular guests and under various gaseous envir-
onments, revealing its strong potential as a versatile platform for energy
transfer and sensing applications. The formation of exciplexes with
electron-deﬁcient guests demonstrated its sensitivity to electronic interac-
tions, while the incorporation of a he avy atom-containing guest led to a
marked enhancement in thermally activated delayed ﬂuorescence. Both
oxygen and hydrogen purging caused a transition in emission proﬁle from a
broad charge transfer band to a more l ocalized spectral feature, with
hydrogen exposure producing a threefold increase in emission intensity.
Additionally, hydrogen gas induced a superradiance-type behavior. Expo-
sure to sulfur-based gases and methane triggered a complete “turn-off”
response. These distinct responses to different analytes demonstrate
CPCQ’s capacity to modulate its excited-state properties based on its che-
mical environment. The reversibility of these effects (for H
2 and O2 gases),
particularly with inert gas purging, further strengthens its practicality for
real-world sensing systems. Theseﬁndings establishCPCQ as a responsive
and adaptable photophysical system,offering a promising foundation for
the development of next-generation materials in photosensitization and gas
sensing technologies.
