In the late twentieth century, nighttime land surface temperatures are observed to rise faster than
daytime temperatures, resulting in high diurnal temperature ranges and considerable diurnal
asymmetric warming. Nevertheless, the projected changes in diurnal temperature ranges under global
warming remain with large uncertainties across statistical multi-model simulations. Here, we identify
an emergent relationship between historical diurnal temperature ranges variability and future
projections on both global and regional scales. By leveraging this spatially heterogeneous
relationship, we present a framework to re ﬁne regional projections, reducing model uncertainties by
15– 68% across the 27 IPCC AR6 reference regions. Our ﬁndings suggest a sustained in ﬂuence of
external forcing on diurnal temperature ranges from past to future, driven primarily by related
widespread reductions in cloud cover, rather than internal variability. This study provides valuable
insights into constraining regional extreme responses to a warming climate, laying the groundwork for
informed regional climate policy decisions.
Under global warming, the frequenc y and intensity of various climate
extremes have been observed to change signiﬁcantly, attracting considerable
attention. The diurnal temperature range (DTR), deﬁned as the difference
between daily maximum and minimum temperatures by the Expert Team
on Climate Change Detection and Indices
1, is usually regarded as a repre-
sentative climate index for assessing extreme climate change. In addition,
DTR holds signi ﬁcant implications for human and natural systems,

agricultural yields6. Moreover, the increased DTR linked to a lower daily
minimum temperature may cause freez ing temperatures at nighttime,
which is detrimental to the crops7,8.I nt h a tc a s e ,t h es i g n iﬁcant changes of

The Fifth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Cli-

decades
10, commonly known as night warming or asymmetric warming11.
Previous studies have suggested that anthropogenic (ANT) forcing can
largely explain the observed decrease in the global DTR and has detectable
effects on the declining changes of DTR in both global and regional features.
The greenhouse gases (GHG), acting as a major component of the ANT
signal, have dominated contributions to the decreasing trend in the DTR
12.
Recently, a higher warming rate of daily maximum temperature than that of
daily minimum temperature, i.e., the opposite-altered asymmetric warming,
has been detected, implying an increased DTR
13. This reversed asymmetric
warming is proposed to be closely related to the increase in surface solar
radiation driven by the changes in cloud cover since the late 1980s
13.
Among multi-simulations, the DTR shows various changes in different
General Circulation Models (GCMs)14, especially in the subtropics, sug-
gesting a large model uncertainty14. In particular, the models show great
disagreements in the DTR changes in both the sign and the magnitude, which
should be further analyzed to comprehend the regional differences and raise
conﬁdence in multi-model mean projections. Nowadays, few studies have
focused on constraining the uncertai nty in projecting DTR, despite the
potential implications of changesin the DTR for humans and nature.
The emergent constraint (EC) has been widely used to reduce model
uncertainties

HadISD data and those from all model grids

The physical mechanism
It is widely recognized that changes in DTR are inﬂuenced by several factors,
including cloud cover, precipitation, and soil moisture through heat ﬂux.
The heat ﬂux affects DTR through daytime heating and nighttime cooling.
The processes include driving the minimum temperature by downward
longwave radiation, the maximum temperature by solar radiative forcing,
and modulating the DTR through the balance between sensible and latent
heat ﬂux. Therefore, to uncover the physical mechanism behind the rela-
tionship between historical DTR trends and DTR changes, it is reasonable to
examine the relationships between the related historical heatﬂux trends and
DTR changes.
Positive surface downwelling shortwave radiation (RSDS) contributes
to the DTR increase as it only occurs during the day. This can raise the daily
maximum temperature more than the minimum, leading to a rise in DTR.
RSDS primarily contributes to DTR changes in mid-to-high latitude regions
of the Northern Hemisphere, including Northwestern North America,
Northeastern North America, Eastern North America, Northern Europe,
the Russian Arctic Region, Western Siberia, Eastern Siberia, Southwestern
South America, and Central Africa. In contrast, negative surface down-
welling longwave radiation (RLDS) reduces nighttime outgoing radiation,
decreasing minimum temperatures m ore than maximum temperatures,
which consequently leads to an increase in DTR. Negative RLDS primarily
inﬂuences DTR changes in Western and Central Europe, Western Central
Asia, Southern Eastern Africa, and Northern Eastern Africa. Additionally,
both RSDS and RLDS signiﬁcantly contribute to the increasing DTR over
the Tibetan Plateau (Fig.3a).
It is well established that RSDS and RLDS are strongly inﬂuenced by
cloud cover. A reduction in cloud cover increases daytime RSDS and
decreases nighttime RLDS at the surface, resulting in a higher DTR. Since
the CTL trend shows large model uncertainty (Fig. S13), the corelated
radiative processes in different models also lead to a large model uncertainty
of DTR changes. This relationship is evident and persistent in both historical
and future periods, as a notable decrease in cloud cover corresponds with
increasing DTR (Figs.4a and S14). Given the positive feedback of clouds on
global warming through radiativeﬂuxes, their impact on DTR is expected to
be further ampliﬁed under warming conditions
30 (Figs.4b, c and S15, S16). It
can also be seen that, in the future, t he contribution of RSDS becomes
prominent in a growing number of reg ions compared to the historical
period (Fig. 3b), underscoring the critical role of clouds in inﬂuencing the
variation of DTR.

Discussions
So far, there have been relatively few proposed constraints on future DTR
change compared to those on mean temperature. Despite recent
advancements in DTR observations that have improved our under-
standing of its changes, the con ﬁdence in the global EC proposed here
remains somewhat constrained by persistent uncertainties in the obser-
vational DTR data. Therefore, it is essential to sustain and elevate related
observational resources moving forward. Additionally, there are theore-
tical limitations of the EC approach itself, which extend beyond the scope
of this study, as well as various uncertainties related to the construction of
the emergent relationship. We hope that this study provides valuable
insights into the potential application of an EC framework on DTR
change. Besides, considering the effects of DTR changes discussed in the
introduction, further research is needed to apply constraints in related
areas, such as disease transmission rates, agricultural production, and
other sectors affected by DTR variations. It is also important to note that
the EC relationship does not hold in certain land regions, particularly in
the Southern Hemisphere. This may be due to our constraint relationship
primarily reﬂecting external forcing inﬂuences, particularly the impact of
GHGs, while other factors, such as atmospheric dynamics, also affect DTR
changes
33.
Conclusions
Our results show a strong linear corre lation between the historical DTR
trend and the DTR future change per 1 K of global warming. This emergent
relationship is evident across various emissions scenarios and land areas,
accentuating its robustness despite regional differences. Based on this
relationship, we leverage historical DTR trends from the CRU TS dataset to
propose an EC on future DTR changes, while considering regional varia-
tions to constrain model uncertainty. Notably, the EC substantially modiﬁes
the multi-model mean DTR change pro jections, reducing model uncer-
tainties by 15 – 68% across the 27 IPCC AR6 reference regions. We also
underscore the spatial difference and demonstrate that EC is more effective
in speciﬁc regions where the GHG forcing is the primary driver of DTR
changes
12.T h e s eﬁndings further point out the persistent in ﬂuence of
external forcing on DTR from the past to the future, rather than internal
variability.

Methods
Data
The daily maximum and minimum temperature data in this study are from
the Climatic Research Unit Time-Series version 4.07 (CRU TS) dataset35.
The CRU TS temperature dataset integrates data from multiple sources,
including historical weather station records, ship logs, and recent satellite
observations. This dataset is carefully calibrated to address potential biases
and variations in measurement methods, making it a widely used and
trusted resource for analyzing long-term temperature trends andﬂuctua-
tions. It offers a spatiotemporal resolution of 0.5° × 0.5° monthly, covering
the period from 1901 to 2022. We utilize CRU TS (tasmin and tasmax)
because it offers a long, globally consistent record (1901 – present) at 0.5°
resolution, with rigorous station homogenization and interpolation proce-
dures that have been validated in numerous studies of daily extremes
35.C R U
TS is the basis for many analyses of DTR change36 and has been shown to
produce trend estimates highly consistent with HadEX family of products
(HadEX2, HadGHCND) and ISTI+ PHA at large scales37, as concluded in
IPCC AR438.
To investigate future DTR changes, we employ data from 26 models in
the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) 39.T h e s e
models offer future climate simulations under different Shared Socio-
economic Pathways (SSPs) spanning 2015– 2100 (see Table S1). Speciﬁcally,
we analyze data from historical, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 experiments.
Table S1 in the supplementary material provides details on each model,
including modeling institution, number of ensemble members, and
respective letter labels. We leverage all available ensemble members, using
daily minimum and maximum temperatures, monthly radiation ﬂuxes,
precipitation, and cloud cover data forboth historical and future periods up
to 2100. Radiation ﬂuxes consist of incoming longwave radiation (RLDS),
net solar radiation absorbed at the surface (RSDS), as well as surface latent
and sensible heatﬂuxes (LH and SH). All data are regridded to a horizontal
resolution of 1° × 1°, and the multi-model mean (MMM) is calculated as the
arithmetic average across the 26 model simulations.
In our study, the future changes in each variable are standardized by the
global mean of surface air temperature change for each model (for seasonal
analyses, the corresponding seasonal global mean temperature is
employed). This approach eliminates the inﬂuence of inter-model uncer-
tainty on climate sensitivity, as outlined by Huang et al.
19 and Liu et al.16.T h e
patterns of change for each variable are then normalized based on local
variations per 1 K of global warming, providing a framework for quantifying
externally driven shifts in spatial distribution
