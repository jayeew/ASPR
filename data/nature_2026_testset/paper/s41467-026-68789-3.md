Cas9 can process poly(T) single-stranded DNA molecules upon activation in an
RNA-guided manner. Here, we uncover key determinants underlying this
function. First, we show that unﬂanked R-loops in the RNA 5′ side favor trans-
cleavage activity, which occur when targeting short double-stranded DNA
molecules. Second, we show that elongated guide RNA spacers beyond the
canonical 20 bases, even by a few bases, severely impair this collateral activity.
Third, althoughtrans-cleavage is mediated by the RuvC domain, we show that
a catalytically active HNH domain contributes to an efﬁcient process. Analysis
of structural models provides tentative mechanistic insights. Together, these
ﬁndings illustrate that ﬁne modulation of Cas9 function can be achieved.
Several prokaryotes have adaptive immune systems encoded by clus-
tered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and
genes expressing CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins
1. Due to their ever-
growing functional versatility, these systems are being repurposed for
a series of biotechnological applications, including gene editing, reg-
ulation, and detection
2. Indeed, they have become essential tools for
precise gene editing, enabling targeted modiﬁcations in the genomes
of different organisms, including humans 3. Increasing efforts have
signiﬁcantly enhanced the precision of the process to achieve ther-
apeutic applications4. CRISPR-Cas systems have also allowed for the
regulation of gene expression by activating or repressing speci ﬁc
genes without altering the DNA sequence 5. RNA-guided dead Cas
proteins offer opportunities for circuit engineering and gene function
screening
6. Moreover, the trans-cleavage activity upon nucleic acid
targeting of both Cas12 and Cas13 has been instrumental to boost the
ﬁeld of CRISPR diagnostics
7,8, allowing the sensitive detection of
pathogens and genetic mutations.
Recently, the trans-cleavage activity of Cas9 has been disclosed
over poly(T) single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) molecules9.C a s 9c a na l s o
process C-rich ssDNA substrates and even small RNA molecules. This
ﬁnding contrasts with a long-held opposite view based on structural
considerations. Cas9 is a large multidomain protein that contains two
endonuclease domains, RuvC and HNH, along with a protospacer
adjacent motif (PAM)-recognizing domain
10, which introduces double-
stranded breaks in DNA at speci ﬁc sites in an RNA-guided manner.
Such a function of Cas9 is accomplished by the RuvC domain 9 and
apparently enhanced when the nuclease is guided in its native form
with two RNA molecules, i.e., with a CRISPR RNA (crRNA) and a trans-
activating crRNA (tracrRNA). Nevertheless, the sequence and struc-
tural determinants of this collateral activity are not fully appreciated.
In this work, we further investigate the trans-cleavage activity of Cas9
through a series of biochemical assays involving different targets,
guides, and nucleases. We characterize how function is modulated by
fundamental structural variations in nucleic acids and changes in
protein activity.

ﬂuorogenic 18 nt poly(T) ssDNA probe, we scrutinized thecis-cleavage

To distill a clear structural determinant, we next considered
longer dsDNA molecules to target (35–36 bp stretch from the 5′ end to
the PAM). In this case, the DNA region ﬂanking the resulting R-loop in

trans-cleavage activity when using long dsDNA inputs compared to the
signals obtained with the shorter inputs (Fig. 1E, F; see also absolute

#4, e.g., the reduction was 90% by guiding Cas9 with the crRNA-

We assayed crRNAs with imperfect spacers for systems #1 and #2,
introducing mismatches at various positions to probe R-loop forma-
tion and downstream cleavage activities. Single-nucleotide mis-
matches were introduced at positions 5, 8, 13, and 19. A two-nucleotide
mismatch at positions 5, 6 and a three-nucleotide mismatch at posi-
tions 4, 5, 6 were considered too. Cas9 tolerated single-nucleotide
mismatches across all positions with respect to cis-cleavage (Fig. 2A,
C). Yet, collateral activity was more sensitive and sequence-dependent;
it was substantially reduced by the mismatch at position 5 in system #1
and by the mismatch at position 13 in system #2 (Fig. 2B, D; see also
Supplementary Fig. 2F). Different basal activities were also noticed for
each crRNA sequence. Furthermore, we observed that while the three-
nucleotide mismatch impeded the formation of the complete R-loop in
both systems, the two-nucleotide mismatch only did so in system #2
(Fig. 2A, C). These high-order mismatches nonetheless avoided a sig-
niﬁcant increase in trans-cleavage activity in both systems (Fig.2B, D).
These data underscore complexities associated with responsiveness
and ﬁdelity in the CRISPR-Cas9 system
14.
To further study the dynamics of the response, we monitored
over time the ﬂuorescence signals associated with dsDNA targeting
and subsequent ssDNA trans-cleavage (for system #1 with the crRNA-
tracrRNA).Cis-targeting was fast, with a mean reaction time of ~7 min,
but a delay of ~10 min was found in trans-cleavage together with a
slower reaction rate (Fig. 2E). Of note, ssDNA collateral catalysis was
completely abolished, even the basalactivity of the ribonucleoprotein,
using an extralong dsDNA target (49 bp stretch from the 5′ end to the
PAM), in agreement with the formation of a more stable ﬂanked
R-loop. Examination of trans-cleavage activity for increasingly long
dsDNA targets was in tune (Supplementary Fig. 2G). Indeed, this
behavior was reproduced when targeting a plasmid (Fig. 2F). This
suggests that this feature might play a limited role in the natural in vivo
context, especially considering the lack of apparent impact on
immunity in the case of Cas12
15.

which aligns with the fact that these two nuclease domains act in

CRISPR-Cas9 reactions
Reactions were performed in 1x Tris/Acetate/EDTA (TAE) buffer pH 8.5
(Invitrogen), 0.05% Tween 20, and 12.5 mM MgCl2 at a ﬁnal volume of
20 µL17. 200 nM CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein, previously assembled
at room temperature for 30 min, was mixed with 40 nM target DNA (in
the form of dsDNA, ssDNA, or plasmid) and 100 nM ssDNA probe.
Control reactions lacking target DNA or CRISPR components were also
considered. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in tubes in a
thermocycler (Mastercycler, Eppendorf). For time-dependent activity
analyses, reactions were directly incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in a micro-
plate in the multimode plate reader.
Fluorescence quantiﬁcation
CRISPR-Cas9 reaction volumes were loaded in a black 384-well
microplate with clear bottom (Falcon), which was then placed in a
multimode plate reader (CLARIOstar Plus, BMG) to measure green and
far-red ﬂuorescence. For FAM, excitation was at 490/8 nm and emis-
sion at 526/12 nm (green); for Cy5, excitation was at 640/8 nm and
emission at 675/12 nm (far-red). The ﬂuorescence values of the reac-
tion buffer were subtracted to correct the signals. Absolute or relative
ﬂuorescence values were represented in theﬁgures.
Nucleic acid detection assays
Samples of total RNA from human embryonic kidney (HEK293T) cells
at 5 ng/μL, extracted using RNAzol RT (Merck), were spiked with the
complete and puriﬁed RNA genome of SARS-CoV-2 (Vircell) at theﬁnal
concentration of 5·103 copies/μL. A pre-ampliﬁcation step by RT-RPA
was performed with TwistAmp basic kit (TwistDX). For that, 500 U
RevertAid (Thermo), 50 U RNase inhibitor (Thermo), and 480 nM
custom forward and reverse primers (targeting the E gene) were added
to 29.5 µL rehydration buffer. The TwistAmp basic reaction pellet was
resuspended, and 2 µL spiked sample and 14 mM magnesium acetate
were added to start the reaction. Reactions were incubated at 42 °C for
30 min in a thermoblock (Thermomixer, Eppendorf). RT-RPA products
were puri ﬁed and quanti ﬁed. Subsequently, CRISPR-Cas9 reactions
were performed as previously described, and ﬂuorescence readouts
were obtained. In this case, theﬂuorescence value of the unprocessed
probe was subtracted to correct the signals. Control CRISPR-Cas9
reactions were performed with RT-PCR products. Moreover, RT-RPA
products were validated by quantitative PCR (qPCR). In MicroAmp fast
reaction tubes with cap (Applied), 1 ng puriﬁed material, 500 nM pri-
mers, and Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied) were com-
bined for a total volume of 20 μL. Tubes were loaded into a qPCR
system (QuantStudio 3, Applied) for ﬂuorescence measurement, and
the following protocol was employed: an initial activation step (95 °C
for 10 min) followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C for 15 s) and
annealing and extension (65 °C for 1 min). Samples with C
T >3 0 w e r e
considered negative.
Gel electrophoresis
Nucleic acid cleavage by Cas9 was con ﬁrmed by agarose gel electro-
phoresis. For that, CRISPR-Cas9 reactions wereﬁrst treated with 10µg
proteinase K (Ambion), incubating at 50 °C for 1 h. The digestion was
inactivated at 95 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, 1 U RNase H (Ambion)
and 2 µg RNase A (Invitrogen) were added, incubating at 37 °C for 1 h.
Samples were then loaded on a 1% or 3% agarose gel prepared with 0.5x
Tris/Borate/EDTA (TBE) buffer, which was run for 45 min at room
temperature (110 V). Gels were stained using GreenSafe (NZYtech). The
GeneRuler DNA ladder mix (100 –10000 bp, Thermo) and ultra-low
range DNA ladder (10–300 bp, Thermo) were used as markers.
