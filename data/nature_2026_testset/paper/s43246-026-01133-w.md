The global transition toward ef ﬁcient, sustainable, and cost-effective energy storage is accelerating,
driven by efforts to decarbonize key sectors. Among emerging technologies, sulfur-based conversion
cathodes have garnered signi ﬁcant attention as promising candidates for next-generation batteries
due to their exceptional theoretical energy density, low cost, and material abundance. Their successful
deployment could advance critical applications, including electric mobility, renewable energy
integration, and grid stabilization. Despite this potential, sulfur cathodes face persistent limitations that
have prevented commercialization. Unlike reviews focusing primarily on materials innovations in
idealized settings, this work provides a critical, user-focused assessment that prioritizes challenges of
scalable manufacturing and operation under practical conditions. We analyze fundamental failure
mechanisms under realistic parameters, including high sulfur loading, lean electrolyte, and limited
lithium anode excess, that cause performance to diverge dramatically from target metrics. By
synthesizing recent advancements in mechanistic understanding, host design, and interface
engineering, we identify key bottlenecks hindering large-scale production. The review concludes with
strategic pathways spanning materials design, device architecture, and market integration to bridge
the gap between laboratory research and real-world application.

Advantages and limitations of sulfur cathodes
Sulfur cathodes operate based on the redox chemistry of elemental sulfur in
reaction with metal ions supplied by the anode (Fig.1). During discharge,
sulfur, typically in the form of cyclic S₈ molecules, reacts with the metal ions
in solution and is progressively reduced to sulﬁde ions (S²⁻) through a series
of intermediate polysulﬁde species (e.g., S₈²⁻,S ₆²⁻,S ₄²⁻), while metal ions are
simultaneously released from the anode. These metal ions may originate
from metal stripping in the case of a metallic anode (e.g., Li, Na, Zn),
deintercalation from intercalation-type anodes (e.g., graphite, hard carbon),
or conversion reactions involving materials such as silicon. Upon charging,
the process is reversed: S²⁻ is oxidized back to elemental sulfur, and metal
ions are either electrodeposited onto the anode or reincorporated into the
host material through intercalation or conversion mechanisms.

and its discharge products, typically metal sul ﬁdes (M

Sulfur cathodes have attracted considerable interest in recent years due
to their exceptional potential to exceed the energy density of conventional
intercalation-type cathodes such as NMC (LiNiMnCoO ₂), NCA (LiNi-

two-electron redox process (S ₈ +16e-↔8S²⁻), enables a high theoretical
speciﬁc capacity of 1675 mAh/g and a gravimetric energy density
approaching 2600 Wh/kg, nearly ﬁve times that of typical LIB cathodes.
These characteristics make sulfur a highly attractive candidate for applica-
tions that demand lightweight, high-energy storage solutions, including
EVs, aerospace technologies, and portable electronics, where energy-to-
weight ratios are crucial.
Beyond energy density, sulfur cathodes offer key advantages in terms of
elemental abundance and cost-effectiveness. Sulfur is plentiful, non-toxic,
and inexpensive, especially when compared to critical and supply-risk
materials such as cobalt and nickel, which dominate many current LIB
chemistries. Furthermore, sulfur cathodes are often composed of additional
low-cost and potentially sustainable components, including porous carbon
matrices, possibly derived from biomass, and, in some con ﬁgurations,
minimal amounts of transition metal catalysts to facilitate redox kinetics.
The low cost and widespread availability of materials also make sulfur
cathodes a highly attractive candidate for stationary and large-scale energy
storage systems, both front-of-the-meter and behind-the-meter, aimed at
supporting renewable energy integration and grid stabilization.

Despite these promising features, the large-scale commercialization of
SBBs faces several technical and practical challenges, in part rooted in the
intrinsic characteristics of sulfur redox chemistry (Fig.2), including:
• Low charge/discharge rates and related power density, with moderately
long response times, stemming fromsluggish redox kinetics between
sulfur and metal ions, as well as limited electronic and ionic transport
within the cathode and electrolyte.
• Moderate round-trip energy ef ﬁciency, due to signi ﬁcant over-
potentials required to drive redox reactions.
• Low practical energy densities, constrained by limited sulfur loading,
incomplete M-S reaction, and the use of excess electrolyte in current
designs priorizing cycling stability.
• Limited cycling stability, driven by active material loss at the cathode
and the degradation of both the anode and electrolyte.
• Limited operation temperature range with stability problems at high
temperatures and poor low-temperature performance.
• High self-discharge rates and resting capacity fade, largely attributed to
the migration of intermediate polysulﬁdes.
• Poor thermal dissipation, both internal and external, resulting from the
inherently low thermal conductivityof porous sulfur-based cathodes.
• Safety concerns mainly related to the use of toxic and highly volatile
electrolytes and lithium or other metal anodes, which are commonly
employed to match the high capacity of the sulfur cathode.
• Limitations with current battery infrastructure and high manufactur-
ing costs stemming from the use of lithium metal anodes and ﬂam-
mable ether-based solvents, the associated requirement for stringent
processing conditions, and the lack of an established supply chain.
• Environmental and recycling challenges, emphasizing the need for
sustainable and cost-effective end-of-life strategies.

Moderate round-trip energy efﬁciency
The round-trip energy efﬁciency of SBBs typically ranges between 70–85%,
which is considerably lower than that of conventional LIBs. This limitation
is primarily attributed to the difference between charge and discharge vol-
tages, which averages around 10% of the discharge potential at low current
rates, intrinsically capping the round-trip energy ef ﬁciency by the same
margin (Fig. 4). At higher current densities, this hysteresis becomes more
pronounced, further reducing the energy ef ﬁciency of the system. This
voltage difference is directly related to the overpotential required to drive the
sluggish multi-step sulfur redox reaction (S₈↔Li₂S).
Improving this situation demands the development of high-
performance electrocatalysts to reduce the energy barriers associated with
the sulfur redox processes
41. Beyond catalytic improvements, the incor-
poration of redox mediators has shown potential for enhancing reaction
kinetics by facilitating electron tra nsfer through lower-energy shuttle
mechanisms42. However, these mediators must be carefully chosen and
conﬁned to the cathode side to avoid additional overpotentials and parasitic
redox reactions that can offset their beneﬁts.
An additional limiting factor is the inherently insulating nature of both
the charged and discharged products, S₈ and MyS, which introduces addi-
tional signiﬁcant impedance in the system. This can be addressed by
designing highly conductive hierarchical cathode frameworks22 incorpor-
ating porous carbon networks43. Besides, enhancing the ionic conductivity
of electrolytes44 and reducing their viscosity45 is also critical to minimizing
voltage drops.
The sharp increase in overpotential with rising charge/discharge rates,
resulting in greater energy loss per cycle, is largely attributed to mass
transport limitations at the electrode-electrolyte interface, which hinder the
local supply of active reactants and impede reaction kinetics. One way to
alleviate this issue is by suppressing polysul ﬁde dissolution, thereby
retaining active species near the reaction site. However, as noted above, this
approach shifts the dominant mechanism toward solid-state charge trans-
port through electronically and ionically insulating phases, which imposes
an additional overpotential that is detrimental to the energy efﬁciency of the
process.
Another contributor to energy loss is the shuttle effect, in which
dissolved polysulﬁdes migrate between the electrodes, leading to parasitic
reactions at the lithium anode. This contributes to overall energy inef-
ﬁciency by lowering the Coulombic ef ﬁciency. Suppressing this shuttle
effect through the use of separator coatings
46, electrolyte additives47–49 or
solid-state barriers 50,51 is therefore essential for improving energy
efﬁciency.

the actual sulfur fraction in the cathode to 70 wt% or less. On top of this, in

Even under these conditions, the sulfur conversion reaction is often
incomplete. This issue is especially pronounced in non-Li-based systems,
where the lower reduction potential of the metal compared to lithium leads
to even slower polysul ﬁde conversion kinetics, ultimately reducing the
achievable cell capacity.

transport, and compensate for lithium loss during cycling. These conditions,

Low cycling stability
Capacity fading remains a critical barrier to the long-term viability of SBBs,
particularly in EV applications where a typical performance benchmark is
the retention of at least 80% of initial capacity over 1000 cycles, corre-
sponding to a maximum average fade rate of ∼0.02% per cycle. Current
high-sulfur-loading and lean-elect rolyte LSBs fail to meet this target,
exhibiting signiﬁcant degradation after only 100–200 cycles and capacity
fade rates in the range of 0.1% per cycle. This degradation is strongly
inﬂuenced by cell design, including cathode architecture, electrolyte for-
mulation, and operational parameters such as current rate and depth of
discharge.
The predominant cause of capacity loss in LSBs using conventional
ether-based electrolytes is the dissolution of intermediate polysulﬁdes into
the electrolyte, leading to irreversi ble loss of active material. Migrated
polysulﬁdes can reach the anode and participate in parasitic side reactions,
which not only consume metal ions and polysulﬁdes but also degrade the
anode surface, promoting further instability. Additionally, the accumulation
of polysulﬁdes in the electrolyte increases its viscosity and decreases ionic
mobility, while their deposition, potentially mediating chemical compro-
portionation and disproportionation chemical reactions, in the separator
can further impede mass transport and increase internal resistance. Poly-
sulﬁdes can also chemically react with the lithium nitrate (LiNO₃) electrolyte
additive typically used in LSBs and with the organic solvent, degrading it
65.
These failure mechanisms, includingDOL polymerization, the triggering of
solvent side reactions by S3

imide (LiTFSI anion decomposition, and LiNO3 depletion, have been stu-
died in detail using in situ and operando analytical techniques14,66–69.

several mitigation strategies have been explored, as already outlined in“Low

Incorporating electrocatalysts into the cathode is an effective strategy to
reduce the residence time of soluble intermediates by accelerating their
conversion to insoluble species, thereby reducing polysulﬁde diffusion and
shuttle effects. Although catalysts may introduce new failure modes or add
complexity, experimental studies consistently show enhanced cycling sta-
bility. The performance beneﬁts, particularly in terms of reduced capacity
fade and improved Coulombic efﬁciency, often outweigh the added cost and
fabrication complexity
73–76.
However, metallic compound catalysts are themselves susceptible to
degradation under extended high-rate cycling. A primary mechanism is
metal leaching, where polysul ﬁde interactions cause the dissolution of
transition-metal ions (e.g., Co, Fe, Ni, Mn), progressively depleting active
sites77. Catalysts also degrade through phase segregation and agglomeration;
for example, unconﬁned nanosized FeS₂ particles can coalesce or recon-

desorption to mitigate this passivation80.

due to the dominance of solid-solid reaction pathways, which are kinetically
limited. As a result, while such strategies may reduce active material loss,
they typically introduce new limitatio ns in stability, rate capability, and
energy density, and thus must be carefully optimized81.

Limited temperature operation range
Due to the complex cathodic conversi on reactions and the use of metal
anodes, SBBs exhibit strongly temperature-dependent performance, mak-
ing them particularly vulnerable in extreme thermal environments. These
temperature effects signiﬁcantly inﬂuence ion transport, reaction kinetics,
and structural stability across the cell components (Fig.7)98.
At low temperatures, the reaction kinetics is further slowed down, and
thus the rate performance and capacity. Besides, the increased viscosity of
the electrolyte leads to reduced metal-ion mobility and sluggish polysulﬁde
diffusion, which in turn exacerbates polarization and lowers overall elec-
trochemical performance. Metal deposition at the anode becomes uneven,
promoting dendrite growth that may pierce the separator and cause internal
short circuits. Simultaneously, localized polysulﬁde deposition at the cath-
ode is aggravated, compromising the mechanical integrity and conductivity
of the electrode.
At elevated temperatures, the electrolyte becomes thermally unstable
and susceptible to oxidative decomposition, which may result in gas gen-
eration, pressure buildup, and electrolyte leakage. High temperatures also
accelerate polysulﬁde dissolution and shuttling, leading to severe active
material loss and rapid capacity fa ding. The metal anode undergoes
intensiﬁed side reactions with the electrolyte, initiating repeated SEI
breakdown and repair cycles that continuously consume active metal ions
and destabilize the interface. Moreover, the separator experiences
mechanical stress from both cathode volume expansion and metal dendrite
penetration. Besides, the conventio nal heat-labile polypropylene (PP)
materials also prevent batteries from maintaining long-term cycles beyond
60 °C, further increasing the risk of short circuits and thermal runaway
99.

the battery system. At the electrolyte level, it is crucial to extend the
operational temperature range by selecting solvents with low freezing and
high boiling points, and by introducing functional additives that stabilize the
SEI layer and suppress ﬂammability. Flame-retardant additives based on
phosphorus compounds

larly important for mitigating the risk of thermal runaway. In the long term,
the development of SSEs may representa promising direction for enhancing
thermal and electrochemical stability.

engineered to minimize ion diffusion distances, improving low-temperature

High self-discharge rate and resting capacity fade
Conventional SBBs, particularly those employing lithium metal anodes and
ether-based electrolytes, suffer from notably high self-discharge rates.
Without effective measures to suppress polysul ﬁde migration, capacity
losses can exceed 50% within just a few days or even hours at rest, a stark
contrast to conventional LIBs, which typically exhibit less than 5% self-
discharge over an entire month.
The primary cause of this elevated self-discharge is the dissolution of
intermediate polysulﬁde species into the electrolyte, initiating the so-called
shuttle effect105. In this parasitic process, dissolved polysulﬁdes migrate to
the anode where they undergo reduction, then diffuse back to the cathode
and are reoxidized. This redox cycling occurs continuously, even when the
battery is idle, leading to steady volt age decay, irreversible loss of active
material, and long-term capacity fade.

when the battery is stored at intermediate states of charge (SOC), a
condition in which a signi ﬁcant fraction of sulfur exists in soluble
polysulﬁde form in a conventional ether-based LSB. This behavior is
fundamentally different from that of LIBs, where intermediate SOCs are
typically considered optimal for storage. In sulfur-based systems,
intermediate SOCs increase the concentration of mobile, redox-active
species, intensifying the shuttle effect. However, both high and low
SOCs also introduce challenges

places greater stress on both electrodes, increases parasitic reaction rates,

hinder their commercial deployment (Fig.10). The primary concerns stem
from the conventional use of reactive alkali metal anodes, particularly
lithium and sodium, to match the high capacities of sulfur cathodes. While
these anodes offer exceptionally high theoretical capacities, they are inher-
ently prone to dendrite formation during repeated charge/discharge cycles, a
challenge not unique to SBBs but common to all lithium- and sodium-metal
battery systems
116. Over time, dendrites can grow across the separator and
reach the cathode, causing internal short circuits. In severe cases, this can
lead to thermal runaway, resulting in cell rupture,ﬁre, or even explosion.
Safety risks in conventional SBBs are further exacerbated by the
properties of other cell components. The ether-based organic electrolytes,
particularly 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME), com-
monly used in LSBs due to their super ior compatibility with LiPSs, are
highly volatile and combustible. Moreover, the carbon-sulfur composite
cathode, though critical for ensuring electrical conductivity and accom-
modating high sulfur loading, is typically composed of ﬂammable carbo-
naceous materials. In the event of an internal short circuit or thermal
runaway, these components substantially elevate the risk of ﬁre or cata-
strophic cell failure.

thermal runaway behavior often initiates at the cathode side and dramati-
cally accelerates when molten sulfur and lithium metal interact, creating

On the other hand, to overcome the safety concerns of organic solvents,
aqueous SBBs have also been proposed146. Aqueous SBBs aim to combine
the high theoretical energy density of sulfur redox chemistry with the
inherent safety, low cost, and environmental friendliness of water-based
electrolytes. The use of aqueous electrolytes eliminates the risk ofﬂammable
organic solvents and signiﬁcantly enhances thermal and chemical stability,
making them attractive for grid-scale and stationary applications.
In typical aqueous sulfur battery de signs, elemental sulfur or poly-
sulﬁde species are used as the cathodic active material, while the anode must
be carefully selected to maintain chemical stability in aqueous environ-
ments. Materials such as Zn, Sn, and Al have been widely investigated as
aqueous-compatible anodes due to their favorable electrochemical stability
in neutral or mildly acidic aqueous electrolytes
147–149. These elements are also
abundant, low-cost, and capable of undergoing reversible redox reactions,
making them particularly suitable for coupling with sulfur-based cathodes.
Additionally, sulfur has also been proposed as an anode material coupled
with an iodine cathode and a Na ⁺ charge carrier150,151,o f f e r i n gan o v e l
electrochemical conﬁguration for aqueoussulfur batteries.
As for Li, Na, and K systems, although Li, Na, and K metals react
violently with water and are thus not stable in aqueous environments, their
ionic forms (Li + ,N a + ,K + ) have been successfully utilized as charge
carriers in certain aqueous battery conﬁgurations. These typically rely on the
use of insertion-type anode materials capable of reversibly intercalating
these alkali ions
152,153. However, structural stability of these electrodes
remains a critical bottleneck, and related research is still at an early
exploratory stage.
Aqueous SBBs offer signi ﬁcant advantages in terms of safety and
environmental compatibility, making them strong candidates for next-
generation low-cost, long-life, and large-scale energy storage systems.
Nonetheless, their practical development faces several technological hur-
dles. Most notably, the theoretical electrochemical stability window of water
is limited to 1.23 V, which signi ﬁcantly constrains the operating voltage
range of aqueous battery systems and, by extension, their achievable energy
density. To overcome this limitation, “water-in-salt” super-concentrated
electrolytes have been developed to partially extend this window to above
2.5 V, i.e., in the range of SBBs based on organic electrolytes
154.
In addition, sulfur’s low solubility in water and the sluggish kinetics of
aqueous sulfur redox reactions can limit performance. Furthermore, poly-
sulﬁdes may exhibit diverse speciation b ehaviors within aqueous electro-
lytes, potentially leading to complex reaction pathways, side reactions, and
interfacial instability. Current research efforts are addressing these issues
through multiple approaches. The inc orporation of electrocatalysts has
proven to be an effective strategy for accelerating redox kinetics and redu-
cing overpotentials, especially through the design of cathode interfaces that
promote synergistic electronic and ionic transport
155. Tuning the pH
environment is also a critical method, as the decomposition potentials of
water shift under different acidic or alkaline conditions, potentially
expanding the usable electrochemical window of the cell156. Besides, hybrid
electrolytes and redox mediators are also being developed to enable more
efﬁcient aqueous SBB systems28,157.
Additionally, ongoing research aims to develop non- ﬂammable or
ﬂame-retardant cathode additives, se parators, and liquid electrolytes 158,
incorporate thermal shutdown mechanisms, and apply protective coatings
to both anode and cathode materials to further minimize the risk of thermal
runaway andﬁre
159,160. These solutions, while promising, must be integrated
without compromising the high energy density and cost-effectiveness that
make LSBs attractive in the ﬁrst place. Ultimately, improving the safety of
LSBs will require a multifaceted approach, combining materials innovation,
cell design optimization, and advanced thermal and mechanical manage-
ment systems to ensure stability during both operation and storage.

stringent manufacturing conditions,including moisture- and oxygen-free
environments, to prevent degradation and safety hazards. These require-

process.
In addition, LSBs typically employ ether-based electrolytes, which,
while effective at dissolving LiPS and thus accelerating and extending the Li-
S reaction, are more expensive and pose several drawbacks. These solvents
are toxic, highly ﬂammable, and unstable at elevated voltages, limiting the
operating window of the battery and increasing safety concerns. Their
volatility also demands careful handling and controlled processing condi-
tions, which further escalate production costs.
Another cost-related factor is the lithium salt conventionally used in
LSB electrolytes. LiTFSI is commonly used due to its high chemical and
thermal stability, as well as its compatibility with polysul ﬁde chemistry.
However, LiTFSI is signiﬁcantly more expensive, often more than twice the
cost of LiPF₆, the standard salt used in commercial LIBs, placing additional
pressure on the overall cost structure of LSBs.
Addressing these cost issues will require a shift toward alternative
electrolyte systems, ideally based on non-ﬂammable, non-toxic solvents that
are stable across wider voltage windows and compatible with lower-cost
lithium salts. Moreover, replacing l ithium metal anodes with alternative
materials such as silicon or even graphite, and potentially lithium ions with
alternative elements such as sodium, could alleviate some of the safety and
cost concerns. However, such approa ches introduce new challenges, as
noted above.
Furthermore, as a low TRL technology, SBBs currently lack a mature
manufacturing ecosystem. This, combined with their dependence on some
costly materials and sensitive, often low-throughput processing techniques,
signiﬁcantly elevates production costs. To enable commercial viability, the
manufacturing infrastructure for SBBsmust be optimized for scalable, high-
throughput, and cost-effective production. In parallel, the supply chain for
specialized components, including catalytic additives, advanced binders,
and functionalized separators, must be established and industrialized.
However, some of these components may prove difﬁcult or costly to scale,
presenting additional barriers to widespread adoption.
More speci ﬁcally, the dry processing t echniques emerging in LIB
manufacturing, offering cost, environmental, health, safety, and sustain-
ability beneﬁts (discussed in “Prospects for SBB manufacturing”), funda-
mentally alter the microstructure and transport properties of sulfur cathodes
compared to conventional slurry-cast electrodes. Dry processing typically
yields denser, less porous electrodes. While beneﬁcial for volumetric energy
density, this reduced porosity can impede electrolyte inﬁltration and ionic
transport, posing a challenge for thick, high-loading cathodes. To address
transport limitations, highly porous, binder-free electrodes offer a promis-
ing alternative. These structures form open, interconnected networks that
enhance electrolyte access and ionic percolation. However, their lack of
polymeric binders can compromise mechanical integrity and electronic
contact. Therefore, a key design challenge lies in balancing high porosity for
transport with sufﬁcient robustness for stability, whether through advanced
binder systems or tailored dry-processing parameters.

Environmental impact and recycling challenges
Like other battery technologies, SBBs face signiﬁcant environmental chal-
lenges related to recycling, disposal, and materials sustainability. While
established recycling practices already exist for metal current collectors (Cu
and Al foils) and metal anodes (e.g., Li), two additional aspects require
particular attention in the context o f SBBs: the electrolyte and binder
materials commonly used in current LSB liquid-cell designs.
Ether-based solvents, particularly DOL and DME, pose substantial
safety risks, as discussed above, and environmental issues, especially when
evaluated through the lens of green chemistry and long-term sustainability.
Both DOL and DME are volatile organic compounds (VOCs) with low
boiling points (∼75 °C), making them prone to evaporation and thus sig-
niﬁcant contributors to air pollution. Additionally, these ether solvents are
not readily biodegradable, raising concerns about environmental persis-
tence and contamination of soil and water during battery production or
disposal. Their use requires strict solvent recovery and ventilation systems,
and improper handling can lead to ground-level ozone formation, which is
regulated in many jurisdictions.
To reduce the environmental impact of SBB electrolytes, researchers
are exploring various alt ernatives. Fluorinated ethers, while showing
excellent performance and being more electrochemically stable
164,a r e
expensive and not necessarily greener. H igh-concentration electrolytes
(HCEs) and localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) have
emerged as promising approaches, as they reduce the total solvent volume
and improve electrochemical stability
165,166. Other efforts focus on non-
ﬂammable orﬂame-retardant solvents, including sulfone-, phosphate-, and
ionic liquid-based formulations167. Ultimately, aqueous-based electrolytes,
which are suitable for applications prioritizing low cost and safety, such as
large-scale energy storage, and SSEs , which are aimed at achieving high
energy density, both eliminate organic solvents entirely and represent the
most promising solutions for long-term environmental compatibility and
safety, although each has its own limitations as discussed above.
The binder also presents environmental concerns. Polyvinylidene
ﬂuoride (PVDF) is widely used in both LIBs and LSBs due to its outstanding
chemical and electrochemical stability. However, PVDF is aﬂuoropolymer
containing highly stable carbon-ﬂuorine bonds, making it persistent in the
environment. Like per- and polyﬂuoroalkyl substances (PFAS), PVDF is
considered a “forever chemical”, contributing to long-term environmental
pollution. Furthermore, PVDF is typ ically processed using NMP, which
requires careful handling and expensive recovery systems. These processing
demands increase health risks, production costs, and environmental foot-
print, particularly in industrial-scale battery manufacturing. PVDF also
complicates battery recycling: it is difﬁcult to separate from active materials,
resistant to thermal decomposition, and incompatible with biodegradable or
green recovery processes.
To overcome these limitations, several water-processable, envir-
onmentally friendly binders are bein g developed. For example, carbox-
ymethyl cellulose (CMC) is a biodegradable binder commonly used in LFP
cathodes and can be processed in aqueous solutions
168,169.O t h e rp r o m i s i n g
alternatives include polyacrylic acid (PAA), which provides strong adhesion
and water compatibility, and biopolymer-based binders such as alginate,
chitosan, and starch derivatives
170.
In the context of recycling, the path toward sustainability for SBBs
remains complex171. On the one hand, SBBs beneﬁt from a low content of
critical metals, apart from lithium in LSBs, which reduces their environ-
mental footprint compared to cobalt- and nickel-rich LIBs. On the other
hand, this scarcity of valuable metals also diminishes the economic incentive
for recycling, as the recoverable material value is signiﬁcantly lower. In fact,
recycling LSBs is even less cost-effective than LIB recycling, which itself is
only marginally viable when comparedto the cost of raw material extraction
through mining.
Nonetheless, SBBs offer several promising opportunities for circular
materialﬂows. LSBs can be produced from recycled lithium recovered from
end-of-life LIBs, and the sulfur can be sourced from industrial waste
streams, such as petroleum reﬁning or low-cost pyrite mining, which has
limited commercial value today due to the low market price of sulfur and
iron
16,172,173. The transition metals used in small quantities as catalytic
dopants (e.g., Fe, Co, Ni) may also be sourced from ferroalloy or steel by-
products, provided that purity levels are sufﬁcient

underway to produce conductive and porous carbon materials from bio-
mass or polymer waste, further aligni ng SBBs with sustainable material
sourcing175,176.
However, to ensure long-term viability, it will be essential to under-
stand how impurities in recycled or low-grade materials affect electro-
chemical performance, and to develop the required cost-effective
puriﬁcation strategies to meet battery-grade speciﬁcations. Only by inte-
grating materials sustainability, safe and green processing, and innovative
recycling pathways can SBBs fully realize their promise as a next-generation,
environmentally friendly energy storage technology.
Overall, while it may be possible to address individual limitations of
SBBs in isolation, the strong interdependence among various factors, such as
electrochemical performance, material stability, manufacturing constraints,
safety, and sustainability, makes ho listic optimization of these systems
extremely challenging (Fig.11). Improvements in one area often introduce
trade-offs in others, underscoring the need for integrated, system-level
design strategies to achieve practic al and scalable sulfur-based battery
technologies.

viable successor to the LIB throne.

Outlook
To enable SBBs to evolve from a promising research concept into a
commercially viable energy storage technology, a comprehensive and
strategic development roadmap is essential. As the automotive industry
accelerates its shift toward electriﬁcation, the successful adoption of next-
generation battery systems such as SBBs will depend on targeted tech-
nological innovations, robust industrial partnerships, and investments in
scalable manufacturing infrastructure. A key element of this transition
involves tailoring SBB products to meet the speciﬁc demands of different
market segments. For instance, maximizing energy density will be critical
for EVs, while cost-effectiveness and long service life will be paramount
for stationary storage applications. Customizing battery conﬁgurations to
align with these diverse application requirements will be essential to
accelerate market adoption.
Early deployment, combined with rigorous performance testing across
various use cases, will be crucial to building conﬁdence and ensuring that
SBBs meet commercial reliability standards. Strategic collaborations among
industry, research institutions, and policymakers will be instrumental in
addressing technical and scalability b arriers. In parallel, investments in
specialized manufacturing processes and resilient supply chains will support
long-term growth. Reducing production costs through process optimization
and component simpliﬁcation will be vital for enhancing market compe-
titiveness. Additionally, coordinated policy support, through subsidies,
regulatory frameworks, and sustained research funding, can play a decisive
role in accelerating the commercialization of SBB technologies.
From a technological standpoint, overcoming current limitations
will require continued advances in cathode engineering (including the
integration of conductive hosts, catalytic additives, and polysul ﬁde-
trapping architectures), electrolyte development (especially stable,
higher ionic conductivity and lower viscosity formulations incorporat-
ing proper additives), and protective strategies for anodes (ranging from
coatings to alternative materials). Recent progress in SBB cathode design
has already addressed some of the core issues, such as the low con-
ductivity of sulfur, its sluggish redox kinetics, and its large volume
expansion during cycling, but further optimization is needed to enable
high sulfur loadings, long cycle life, and consistent performance under
practical conditions.
In particular, realizing the full potential of SBBs will require the
development of multifunctional mate rials that improve redox kinetics,
enhance interfacial stability, and support reversible sulfur conversion under
ultra-lean electrolyte conditions. This includes advanced conductive
matrices, selective redox mediators, robust arti ﬁcial SEIs, and adaptive
electrode architectures capable of withstanding mechanical and chemical
stresses. Scalable and cost-effective manufacturing techniques will also be
critical to translating laboratory-scale materials into viable commercial
products. Moreover, emerging strategies such as self-healing binders and
dynamic electrolyte systems show promise in extending battery lifespan and
reliability.
However, when assessing the future development of SBBs, it is
essential to align research with realistic laboratory and industrial con-
straints. Several common experimental parameters are impractical for
commercial scale-up, including but not limited to the following: i)
Cathode loading and thickness: While low-loading, thin cathodes sim-
plify laboratory research, they fail to deliver the areal capacity and energy
density required for practical applications. Industrial cells demand high-
loading, thick electrodes that introduce signi ﬁcant mechanical and
transport challenges absent from typical lab studies. ii) E/S ratio: Excess
electrolyte is frequently used to lower resistance and extend cycle life in
experiments, but it in ﬂates cost, weight, and reduces volumetric energy
density, making it unacceptable for commercial cells, which must operate
under lean-electrolyte conditions. iii) Non-scalable materials and
designs: Strategies reliant on rare and expensive additives, low-
conductivity 3D substrates, or structurally unstable cathodes often can-
not be translated to the large-area, robust formats needed for manu-
facturing. iv) Pressure requirements for SSEs: Although SSEs can
enhance safety and suppress polysul ﬁde shuttling, many ceramic or
sulﬁde-based variants require impractically high stack pressures to
maintain interfacial contact in large-format cells. Therefore, future SBB
development must prioritize SSE designs that function under low or zero
external pressure, employ scalable fabrication methods, and are validated
under industrially relevant testing conditions.
Ultimately, the transition from fundamental research to commercial
deployment will require a multidisciplinary approach that bridges materials
science, electrochemical engineering, and system-level design. By aligning
technological development with end-user needs and scalable manufacturing
practices, SBBs have the potential to become a cornerstone of next-
generation energy storage, offering a sustainable, high-performance alter-
native to conventional LIB technologies.
