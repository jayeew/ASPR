Tipei Jia1, Yongzhen Peng 2, Lishan Niu1, Zheng Qi1 &J i n y i n gX i1
Sulﬁde oxidation and sulfate reduction are opposing processes in the micro-
bial sulfur cycle, typically separated toavoid futile cycling. Here, we show that
Mycobacteriumsp. MAG-M116, identiﬁed from a biodesulfurization system
under low pH, sulfate-rich, and H2S-overload conditions, simultaneously per-
forms sulﬁde oxidation and assimilatory sulfate reduction (ASR), challenging
this paradigm. Under aerobic conditions, MAG-M116 oxidizes sulﬁde to ele-
mental sulfur, channeling electrons into the quinone pool to drive forward
electron transfer for ATP synthesis and reverse electron transfer (RET) for
NADPH generation. ASR, acting as a redox homeostat, captures electrons from
leak-prone RET to mitigate electron leakage and reduce reactive oxygen spe-
cies production by 57.5%. This bidirectional sulfur metabolism enables rapid
sulﬁde detoxiﬁcation while maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis,
allowing Mycobacteriumto dominate the community (abundance increasing
from 3.5% to 99%). Theseﬁndings reveal an adaptivestrategy wherein coupled
opposing redox reactions contributeto maintaining intracellular redox
homeostasis under substrate-excess conditions.
Sulfur metabolism represents a fundamental and ubiquitous biogeo-
chemical process in nature. This metabolic network can be divided into
two core but counteracting pathways: sul ﬁde oxidation and sulfate
reduction
1–5. In the sulﬁde oxidation pathway, sulﬁde oxidizers oxidize
reduced sulﬁde species (H2S/HS−/S2−) into higher oxidation states, such
as elemental sulfur (S8; All abbreviations in Supplementary Table S1)
and sulfate, to derive energy 6. Conversely, in the sulfate reduction
pathway, sulfate reducers reduce SO4
2− to sulﬁde via the dissimilatory
sulfate reduction (DSR) or assimilatory sulfate reduction (ASR)
pathway
1,7 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Sulﬁde produced via ASR is then
used to synthesize cysteine (Cys), a critical precursor for methionine
(Met) and iron–sulfur clusters
8.
Identifying concurrent opposing metabolic processes within
unicellular systems could recalibrate our understanding of metabolic
networks and uncover new environmental adaptation mechanisms
9–11.
In the ﬁeld of sulfur metabolism, a prevailing view is that
microorganisms with the capacity for sul ﬁde oxidation and sulfate
reduction belong to separate species 12–17. Nevertheless, identifying a
single microorganism capable of simultaneously executing these
opposing sulfur metabolic pathways continues to attract considerable
research interest. Recent studies have reported the existence of
microorganisms that harbor genes encoding both sul ﬁde oxidation
and dissimilatory sulfate reduction pathways
3,18–22.H o w e v e r ,t h e s e
processes are tightly regulated by oxygen availability and occur
sequentially rather than concurrently. No microbial species has been
conclusively shown to simultaneously perform bidirectional sulfur
metabolism to date. In contrast to the oxygen-sensitive DSR, ASR is
less constrained by oxygen levels, and it is widely distributed across
taxa
7,23,24. Could a single microbial species simultaneously
perform both sulﬁde oxidation and ASR-type sulfate reduction, and if
so, what are the physiological implications for such counteracting
processes?

The paradox of simultaneous sul ﬁde oxidation and ASR in uni-
cellular systems include: (1) The co-occurrence of these opposing
processes may establish a futile cycle
10, thereby restricting cell growth;
(2) for bidirectional sulfur metabolizers, the sophisticated transcrip-
tional cascades may enforce strict pathway segregation to prevent co-
activation of these counteracting modules10,25,26; (3) bidirectional sulfur
metabolizers must compete with niche-specialist sul ﬁde-oxidizing
microorganisms for the shared substrate sulﬁde, contributing to their
rarity in sul ﬁde-limited environments; (4) in sulfur-nutrition acquisi-
tion, the prevalence of metabolic cross-feeding allows sulﬁde oxidizers
to utilize external sul ﬁdes or sulfur-containing amino acid for
biosynthesis27–29, bypassing the need for sulfate reduction. Despite
these challenges, the bidirectional sulfur metabolism is justiﬁed by its
potential to mitigate the overproduction of deleterious reactive oxy-
gen species (ROS), particularly under high-sul ﬁde conditions: high-
sulﬁde levels are toxic to microorganisms, necessitating rapid detox-
iﬁcation via oxidation
30. This process unleashes a substantial electron
ﬂux, heightening the risk of electron leakage in the respiratory chain
and thereby fostering ROS generation 31,32. Theoretically, ASR could
counteract this by diverting excess electrons to sulfate reduction,
thereby buffering the intracellular redox balance and preventing the
overproduction of ROS.
Here, we identiﬁed a bidirectional sulfur-metabolizing bacterium,
Mycobacterium sp. MAG-M116, which can simultaneously perform
sulﬁde oxidation and ASR, from an autotrophic biodesulfurization
system operating at low pH, sulfate-rich, and H
2S-overloaded condi-
tions. Under aerobic conditions, it catalyzes sulﬁde oxidation arrested
at S8, diverting electrons into the quinone pool to simultaneously drive
forward electron transfer for ATP synthesis and reverse electron
transfer (RET) for NADPH generation, fueling the ASR. This bidirec-
tional pathway equips MAG-M116 with the capacity to rapidly detoxify
sulﬁde while efﬁciently preventing ROS overproduction. Collectively,
these ﬁndings not only rede ﬁne the intracellular microbial sulfur
metabolic network but also reveal a redox-buffering strategy driven by
simultaneous opposing redox reactions, establishing a framework for
the maintenance of microbial redox homeostasis in substrate-excess
environments.

The alternation of microorganisms in an autotrophic

The ﬂowchart of the autotrophic biodesulfurization system is shown in

Genome recovery from the biodesulfurization system
Metagenomic sequencing and genome-resolved binning were per-
formed on longitudinally collected samples to track temporal shifts in
the metabolic potential of the microbial community. Following dere-
plication and quality ﬁltering (completeness >50%, contamination
<10%), 120 non-redundant metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs)
were recovered (Supplementary Table S2). On day 118, Acid-
ithiobacillusMAGs A18 (87.7% completeness, 0% contamination), A36
(89.0% completeness, 2.0% contamination), and A117 (93.3% com-
pleteness, 0.6% contamination) accounted for 90.0% of the total
genome abundance (Fig. 2b). Strikingly, by day 235, Mycobacterium
MAGs M30 (89.3% completeness, 2.2% contamination), M104 (96.1%
completeness, 2.3% contamination), and M116 (95.7% completeness,
1.8% contamination) had become dominant, comprising 89.3% of the
total (Fig. 2b). Concurrently, Acidithiobacillus MAGs plummeted to
<0.01%. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis revealed that M30,
M104, and M116 share >98.4% ANI (exceeding the 95% species deli-
neation threshold
35; Supplementary Fig. S6), indicating that these
MAGs represent distinct strains within a single bacterial species. Given
the lower contamination level in MAG-M116, it was selected as the
representative of this species. Notably, the 16S rRNA of MAG-M116
exhibited ≥99.5% similarity to ASV1/2/3/5/6 (Supplementary Fig. S4b),
suggesting that both sets of markers characterize the same species.
Phylogenomic analysis shows that MAG-M116 has the closest afﬁliation
(90.7% ANI; Supplementary Fig. S6) to M. helveticum strain 17-773
(GCF_000195955.2; Fig. 2a). Importantly, MAG-M116 sharply con-
trasted with pathogenic members of the M. tuberculosis complex
(GCF_007714185.1, GCF_005156105.1, GCF_000253355.1, and
GCF_904810325.1), sharing only 8 0% ANI (Supplementary Fig. S6),
conﬁrming MAG-M116 as a non-tuberculousMycobacterium.

In sulfur autotrophic systems, sulﬁde oxidation donates electrons
to the quinone pool, driving RET to generate reduced electron carriers
with higher reducing potential (e.g., NADH). Electron leakage during
RET constitutes a major source of ROS generation 32,40–42, particularly
under conditions of accumulation of NADH46. On day 235, the NAD(H)
content assays revealed that at an H2Sl o a do f1 0 0g / ( m3·h), the NAD+/
NADH ratio in the system decreased from 4.2 in the presence of sulfate
to 2.2 in the absence of sulfate (Fig.5k), indicating that the ASR activity
markedly scavenged NADH within microorganisms. This occurs
because the electrons required for ASR need to be relayed via NADH,
thereby effectively preventing NADH accumulation. Electron ﬂux
analysis revealed that ASR, via RET pathway, dynamically captured
10.6–14.3% of the total electrons released from oxidizing S
2− to S 8
(Fig. 5e, f). Kim and Geoffrey 47 quantiﬁed that approximately 17%
of electrons from ammonia oxidation go through RET, while Milliken
et al.
48 indicated that environmental acidiﬁcation could further amplify
the proportion of RET electrons. This comparison underscores a
considerable share of RET electrons absorbed by ASR, thereby
attenuating RET’s inherent electron leakage propensity and inhibiting
ROS formation at the source (Supplementary Fig. S10b). Moreover,
ASR directly promotes Cys biosynthesis, which serves as the immedi-
ate precursor for the antioxidant glutathione, enhancing intracellular
glutathione-mediated peroxide scavenging capacity. In addition, Cys
and its downstream metabolites, such as reactive sulfur species, can
directly neutralize free radicals, providing a non-enzymatic redox-
buffering effect. Consequently, ASR functions as a critical cellular
redox homeostatic mechanism during sulﬁde oxidation.
Discussion
Mechanism of simultaneous ASR and sulﬁde oxidation at the
unicellular level
This study provides the ﬁrst evidence for the concurrent sul ﬁde oxi-
dation and ASR in a bidirectional sulfur-metabolizing microorganism,
as exempliﬁed by the species represented by MAG-M116. Supported by
genome-centric metatranscriptomics and metaproteomics evidence, a
plausible electron transport chain is proposed, which facilitates
mechanistic coupling between these pathways, mediating coordinated
energy and electron ﬂux within a uniﬁed cellular framework (Fig. 4b).
Speciﬁcally, SQR catalyzes the oxidation of sul ﬁde to S
8, donating
electrons to reduce quinone into QH 2. Subsequently, the QH 2 pool
channels electrons through two distinct routes: forward electron
transport and RET (Supplementary Fig. S10b). (1) Forward Electron
Transport Route: QH
2 transfers electrons either via the Complex III-IV
or directly through cytochrome bd oxidase to O 2. This electron ﬂow
generates a proton motive force that drives ATP synthesis, supplying
the energy required for ASR activation; (2) RET Route: QH
2 accumu-
lation promotes RET by Complex I, facilitating NAD + reduction to
NADH. NADH is then utilized by the proton motive force-energized
PNT to catalyze electron transfer from NADH to NADP ⁺, yielding the
critical reductant NADPH. Finally, the NADPH pool bifurcates, reducing
Trx
ox via PDR/TR and Fd ox via FNR to generate Trx red and Fdred
38,39.
Both reduced electron carriers drive the SIR-type ASR process.
The compartmentalized sulfur metabolism employed by bidirec-
tional sulfur-metabolizing microorganisms spatially segregates sulﬁde
oxidation and ASR into distinct subcellular niches to prevent meta-
bolic crosstalk. Structural analyses reveal SQR as a membrane-
anchored enzyme with its catalytic domain oriented toward the
periplasm
49, enabling periplasmic S 8 accumulation (Supplementary
Fig. S16). This periplasmic sul ﬁde oxidation effectively shields cyto-
plasmic components from exogenous sulﬁde toxicity. In contrast, the
ASR pathway is strictly conﬁned to the cytoplasm and operates via a
tightly coordinated enzymatic cascade, with constituent genes syn-
chronously regulated by shared transcription factors
50. This regulatory
architecture ensures intracellular sequestration of sul ﬁde inter-
mediates, channeling them toward sulfur-containing amino acid bio-
synthesis. Collectively, this compartmentalization strategy effectively
prevents exogenous sulﬁde interference with ASR processes within the
unicellular system.
Necessity of concurrent sulﬁde oxidation and ASR for uni-
cellular survival under sulﬁde overload
Mycobacterium derives energy from sul ﬁde oxidation to S 8 while
reducing sulfate to sulfur-containing amino acids via ASR, whereas
Acidithiobacillusoxidizes S
8/sulﬁde to sulfate for energy. This meta-
bolic complementarity suggests potential syntrophy. However, under
sulﬁde overload (>100 g-H
2S/m3/h), microbial systems face dual
metabolic challenges: (1) immediate detoxiﬁcation of cytotoxic sulﬁde
species30, and (2) mitigation of ROS overproduction during detox-
iﬁcation. Sul ﬁde oxidation to non-toxic S 8 provides an ef ﬁcient
detoxiﬁcation route. Effective ROS management requires not only
scavenging mechanisms—both enzymatic (e.g., SOD, catalase) and
non-enzymatic (e.g., Cys, glutathione)—but also stringent regulation of
electron ﬂux to minimize ROS formation at its source.Mycobacterium
employs a multidimensional adaptation strategy, in which ROS
scavenging is coupled with electron diversion via ASR, collectively
preventing excessive ROS accumulation and maintaining redox
homeostasis under high sul ﬁde stress (Fig. 4b and Supplementary
Fig. S10b). AlthoughAcidithiobacilluspossesses active ROS-scavenging
capacity, it fails to manage ROS at elevated sul ﬁde concentrations
(days 85–125; Fig.5h), contributing to progressive population collapse.
Ultimately, the syntrophy partner ship between them was disrupted
under high sul ﬁde loads due to Acidithiobacillus’si n a d e q u a t eR O S
management, necessitating sulfate supplementation to sustain reactor
functionality. Notably, despite sulﬁde load being the sole operational
variable, respiratory inhibition by sul ﬁde is unlikely to be Acid-
ithiobacillus’s primary mortality mechanism, as both genera utilize
sulﬁde-tolerant SQR
49 rather than sulﬁde-sensitive FccB (supported by
fccB absence in metagenomic raw data).
RET serves not only as an important source of ROS generation but
also as a core pathway for producing NADPH—the essential reductant
for biosynthetic processes in chemolithoautotrophs. Although ASR
mitigates ROS by diverting RET electrons, this process incurs an
energetic cost through partial loss of NADPH synthesis potential. This
explains Mycobacterium’s slower growth relative to Acidithiobacillus
during the initial low-sul ﬁde phase (Fig. 1c). Electron ﬂux analysis
reveals that ASR-type sulfate reduction (S
+6 to S−2 in sulfur-containing
amino acids) consumes 10.6–14.3% of total electrons (Fig.5e, f). While
energetically suboptimal compared to direct sulﬁde assimilation, this
expenditure remains sustainable due to system-wide energy surplus
during the sulﬁde-overload period, as evidenced by phosphocreatine
(PCr) accumulation (a cellular energy reserve) (Fig. 5c), thereby not
limiting Mycobacterium’s ﬂourish. Nevertheless, decreasing sul ﬁde
loads reduces the electron demand for sulfate reduction (from 14.3 to
10.6%; Fig. 5e, f), compelling Mycobacterium to reallocate energy
resources for survival. From a sulfur ﬂux perspective, ~47.5% of
reduced sulfate sulfur (6.1 mmol S-SO
4
2−; Fig. 5b) was released as
organic sulfur metabolites (1.9 mmol S-Cys and 1.0 mmol S-Met;
Fig. 5c), indicatingMycobacterium’s role as a potential nutrient source
for cross-feeding. However, the resulting low microbial diversity led to
the accumulation of these organic sulfur that were not utilized by
other microbes (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. S3).
Research signiﬁcance and application prospect
Redox homeostasis is a fundamental survival mechanism across all life
forms. This study reveals how, under sulﬁde-overloaded conditions, a
microorganism maintains intracellular redox balance amid rapid sul-
ﬁde detoxiﬁcation through the functional synergy of opposing sulfur
metabolic pathways within a single organism. Compared to scavenging

Methods
Bioreactor operation
This study utilized a biotrickling ﬁlter (BTF) system conﬁgured with a
cylindrical glass reactor (12 cm diameter × 75 cm height; Supplemen-
tary Fig. S2). The reactor was packed with randomly arranged poly-
urethane foam (PUF) cubes (2 × 2 × 2 cm, 95% porosity) to form a 45-cm
ﬁlter bed. Microbial inoculum derived from anaerobic-anoxic-oxic
process-activated sludge of a municipal wastewater treatment plant
(Beijing, China) was inoculated into the BTF. H
2S gas mixture (3% H2S,

calibrated massﬂow controller to ensure precise gas-phase regulation.
The raw gas stream was diluted with compressed air (carrier gas) at
controlled ratios to achieve target inlet concentrations. Following
homogeneous mixing in a gas equilibration chamber, the blended gas
entered the bottom of the BTF, traversed the bio ﬁlm-colonized ﬁlter
bed in countercurrent gas-liquid contact mode, and was discharged
from the top exhaust port. A mineral nutrient medium was con-
tinuously supplied via a peristaltic pump to the reactor head, trickled
downward through the ﬁlter bed, and collected in a temperature-
controlled (25–31 °C) recirculation reservoir at the base. The mineral
medium composition contained (g/L): KH

CoCl2·6H2O, 24; CuCl 2·2H2O, 17; ZnCl 2,6 8 ;N a 2MoO4·2H2O, 24; and
H3BO3, 62. A semi-continuous replacement protocol was imple-
mented, with ~50% of the medium being renewed at 10-day intervals to
achieve metabolite dilution and nutrient replenishment. During Phase
