Vision is the primary sensory modality for many animals, especially humans
and other primates. A fundamental constraint on visual resolution acuity is set
by the size and spacing of cone photoreceptors, notably in the fovea of the
retina where cones are the smallest. To optimally utilise this cone array, neu-
rons in the retina and lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) must have receptive
ﬁeld centres comprised of only one cone, yet such receptiveﬁelds have never
been observed directly. Here we comprehensively map parafoveal LGN
receptive ﬁelds in male macaques using an adaptive optics microstimulator
and align them to the underlying cone mosaic. The receptive ﬁeld centres of
parvocellular LGN neurons were most often deﬁned by signals originating
from a single cone photoreceptor, a ﬁnding conﬁrmed by biophysical light
capture modelling and spatial frequencytuning data. Our results demonstrate
that visual acuity originating at the fovea is mediated by LGN neurons working
at the limit of cone photoreceptor spacing. Revealing this physiological basis
of spatial resolution reconciles longstanding anatomicaland perceptual data
over what mechanism limits acuity prior to cortical processing, underscoring
the centrality of optical correction for attaining peak visual resolving power.
The visual system of humans and other primates evolved anatomical
specialisations in both the eye and the brain that emphasise the ver-
idical detection of high spatial frequencies, a capability that de ﬁnes
resolution acuity. In the eye, the hallmark is the fovea, a small locus in
the retina where cone photoreceptors have an exceptionally high
density
1,2, where other retinal cells are displaced to allow less optical
distortion and scatter for the photoreceptors3,4 and where the centre
of gaze is anchored for most eye movements5,6. To take advantage of
the extra visual information that comes from this dense array of foveal
photoreceptors, the cerebral cortex considerably expanded the
amount of cortical tissue devoted to these ocular adaptations, parti-
cularly in primary visual cortex (V1)
7– 10. The presence of all these fea-
tures underscores the importance of the fovea, but high acuity visual
information can only be utilised if the physiological signals from
individual cones are passed directly to V1.
There is ample evidence from retinal anatomy that a signal from
one cone photoreceptor can travel through a bipolar cell to a single
midget retinal ganglion cell, availing a one-to-one pathway from a
photoreceptor to an output neuron of the retina
11,12. This circuit is
considered responsible for transmitting the highest spatial-frequency
information available from the cone mosaic spacing and exists in two
functionally distinct forms— ON-centre and OFF-centre— mediated by
dedicated bipolar cells. While there is strong psychophysical evidence
that resolution acuity appears closely related to the distance between
cones in the fovea
13– 17, there is no direct evidence that the receptive
ﬁelds of any neurons prior to V1 are scaled to support perception at the
resolution afforded by the cone mosaic spacing. In contrast to reso-
lution acuity, there are visual hyperacuity capabilities — like vernier
acuity tasks— that enable spatial discrimination sensitivity of extended
objects to exceed that of the cone mosaic

Results
Single cone-sized receptiveﬁelds
We used the AOSLO to take cone-resolved images of the retina and to
identify the location of the fovea in the retina of all macaques studied
(n = 3). Our AOSLO system was also capable of simultaneously deli-
vering a movie stimulus containing red (710 ± 12 nm) and green
(543 ± 11 nm) wavelengths while imaging the retina in infrared
(842 ± 25 nm). The stimulus wavelengths and intensities were chosen
such that the green light equally drove M and L cones, while the red
light stimulated L cones preferentially by a factor of 19. The M and L
cones represent at least 90% of the cones in the macaque retina and are
much more likely to comprise single cone receptive ﬁelds than S
cones
27,28. To map the receptive ﬁelds of LGN neurons, we used
binarized uncorrelated noise movies in which the two colours were
modulated independently at 30 Hz. These stimulus movies subtended
from 0.18°– 0.32° on the retina and had a resolution of 32 × 32 stimulus
pixels (each stimulus pixel was composed of a 4 × 4 set of imaging
pixels) (Fig. 1b). The stimuli were thereby set to have spatial fre-
quencies ranging from 50– 88 cycles/°, with each stimulus pixel being
smaller than a cone at any eccentricity studied. When a stimulus pixel
had both red and green channels turned on, the photopic luminance
was ~280 cd/m
2, appearing as a vivid yellow and its contrast ratio
against the background light was greater than 250:1 (see ‘Methods’).
These movie stimuli were effective at driving neural responses during
each frame (Fig. 1c) and even more so for speciﬁc pixel conditions, as
exempliﬁed by the heightened spike activity in Neuron 1 for two ON
pixels (Fig.1d) where the ﬁring rate increased from 22 to 33 spikes/s at
the maximal response over baseline (Fig.1e). Spatial resolution of the
imaging and the stimuli could be varied depending on distance from
the fovea, to accommodate the fact that cone diameters increase
substantially within 5° of the foveal centre
1. AOSLO images were
512 × 512 pixels and ranged from 0.73°– 1.28° in width; consequently, to
accurately measure the eccentricity of the receptiveﬁeld locations on
the retina, we created montages for each eye (Fig. 2a, b).
We made extracellular recordings in the LGN of anesthetized
macaques undergoing neuromuscular blockade to quell eye move-
ments. To minimise any remaining motion artifacts that would enlarge
the receptiveﬁelds due to respiratory and cardiac pulsation, all stimuli
were delivered with real-time retinal stabilisation

post-mortem histology (Supplementary Fig. 1). During retinal imaging
and stimulation, continuous electrode recordings were made and the
corresponding AOSLO movies contained a ﬁducial cross to indicate
the location of each movie frame on the retina. Action potentials from
single neurons were identiﬁed by ofﬂine spike sorting (see‘Methods’).
We calculated chromatic spatiotemporal receptive ﬁelds by spike-
triggered averaging (STA) of movie frames occurring up to the time of
each spike. Example STAs from well-characterised parvocellular neu-
rons (n = 34, 18 ON and 16 OFF) highlight the typical features of the
receptiveﬁelds. Neuron 1 was an ON cell responding equally to red and
green stimuli, with a peak latency of two frames, a response duration
less than 150 ms and a receptive ﬁeld diameter of ~2.7 μmi nt h er e d
channel (Fig.2c). Neuron 2 was an OFF cell also driven by both colours,
but had shorter latency and duration (Fig.2d), while Neuron 3, an ON
cell, was solely activated by the green channel (Fig.2e). These STAs are
like those generated by midget retinal ganglion cells when stimulated
with uncorrelated noise, having similar temporal dynamics and no
prominent receptive ﬁeld surrounds
28,31. We used an elliptical 2D
Gaussian ﬁtting procedure to estimate receptiveﬁeld centre sizes for
the STAs (see ‘Methods’). Across our population of parvocellular
neurons, the receptive ﬁeld centre diameters were 4.2 ± 1.0 μm
(mean ± SD, equivalent visual angle: 1.06 ± 0.27 arcmin), representing
cells with ﬁelds located 0.42° – 4.11° from the foveal centre. In this
eccentricity range, these receptive ﬁeld diameters are anatomically
consistent in size with macaque cone inner segments 1, which are
considered the apertures for light capture by individual cones.
Receptive ﬁelds map to the cone mosaic
We next sought to determine if the activity patterns seen in the STAs
corresponded to cones in the retina as imaged by the AOSLO during
our recordings. Doing so would con ﬁrm that the LGN neurons were
receiving inputs primarily from one cone and validate that the spike
waveforms with high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) were from single
cells (Fig.3a). We superimposed the STAs from each LGN recording on
the cone mosaic, including multiunit and magnocellular recordings, so
that the number and location of the cones involved could be identiﬁed
(Fig. 3b). STAs having single isolated peaks (ON cells) or troughs (OFF
cells) were typical for high SNR recordings (n = 23), while a few low SNR
recordings had several clearly separated islands in their maps, occa-
sionally containing both ON and OFFﬁelds (Fig. 3b). These cases were
analysed as multiunit recordings from separate parvocellular neurons
(see ‘Methods’), providing additional receptiveﬁeld data (n = 11). When
magnocellular neurons were encountered, they were weakly driven by
our noise stimuli, but still yielded STA maps, though withﬁelds much
larger than parvocellular neurons (Fig.3b).
To complete the alignment of the STAs to the cone mosaic, we
had to account for chromatic aberrations introduced by the optics of
each animal’s eye. Transverse chromatic aberration (TCA) causes the
red and green wavelengths to land on the retina transversely offset
from the IR channel, which was used for stimulus targeting. TCA varies

Heightened spatial frequency tuning
If adaptive optics correction of ocular aberrations is indeed required
to reveal higher spatial frequency sensitivity in LGN neurons com-
pared to normal optics, then stimulation with traditionally employed
sinusoidal gratings should shift spatial frequency tuning curves to
higher frequencies. In a subset of neurons mapped with noise stimuli
(n = 5), we delivered drifting grating stimuli to probe their spatial
tuning characteristics. Gratings varied in spatial frequency from
6.3– 31.6 cycles/° (limited by aliasing at the stimulus pixel resolution)
and were delivered at different orientations spanning 180° at a drift
rate of 2 cycles/s using only the green channel. Raster plots of the
spike activity showed that LGN neurons were phase-locked to the
grating drift rate and that parvocellular neurons responded to spatial
frequencies exceeding 20 cycles/° (Supplementary Fig. 4). Tuning
curves were constructed from the amplitude of the Fourier transform
of neural responses and were ﬁt with a difference of Gaussians model
to quantify the characteristic spatial frequency (F
c) of the receptive
ﬁeld centres (see ‘Methods’). The 4 parvocellular neurons stimulated
with drifting gratings had a mean Fc of 22.1 cycles/° (Fig. 5a), a spatial
frequency which was ~4× higher than previous studies of parvocel-
lular neurons within 5° of the fovea conducted with normally cor-
rected optics
34,35. This result is consistent with the degree of
improved spatial frequency sensitivity found in humans when mea-
sured with and without adaptive optics correction of monochromatic
light
36,37. We also estimated F c from the elliptical Gaussian ﬁts of the
STA-mapped receptive ﬁelds (averaging the major and minor axes).
The distribution of F c values from our parvocellular neurons had a
median of 23.1 cycles/°, closely matching the F c found by ﬁtting
spatial tuning curves (Fig. 5b). These grating results con ﬁrm the
observations using noise stimuli that, in the absence of ocular aber-
rations, parvocellular LGN neurons near the fovea typically mediate
signals representing one cone receptive ﬁeld centres.

Discussion
In the parvocellular pathway of the visual system, which is thought to
transmit high-resolution visual information, anatomical and physiolo-
gical data have been at odds concerning the extent of convergence of
photoreceptor inputs onto these neurons
38. The anatomy of midget
retinal ganglion cells suggests that signals from individual cones
should be available
39,40, while receptive ﬁeld mapping experiments
have usually found theﬁeld centres to be several times larger than one
cone at parafoveal eccentricities41– 44.H e r ew eh a v er e c o n c i l e dt h e s e
two approaches by showing that the parvocellular LGN neurons most
often have receptive ﬁeld centres comprised of one cone, when the
optics are suitably corrected and uncorrelated spatial noise stimuli
are used.
Our results have several implications about the early visual sys-
tem. One is that midget retinal ganglion cells — o u tt oa tl e a s t4 °
eccentricity— are likely to transmit signals predominantly devoted to
one cone, given their dendritic ﬁeld size and dedicated bipolar
cells
39– 41,43,45– 47. Such an arrangement will enable these neurons to sig-
nal cone-opponency at high spatial frequencies. A second point is how
dependent the outcome of receptive ﬁeld mapping is on the type of
stimuli used. In two earlier studies where optical imperfections were
essentially mitigated, it was still found that receptiveﬁeld centres were
commonly larger than a single cone for cells near the fovea
23,48.I ti s
likely that their use of gratings and small spot stimuli more system-
atically engaged lateral contributions from neighbouring cones
through intraretinal circuitry, thereby extending the ﬁeld sizes
observed. The uncorrelated noise stimuli used in this current study
would not strongly drive receptiveﬁeld surrounds and thereby would
just produce ﬁelds with prominent centres, as evident in ex vivo
studies
28,31. Finally, the occasional presence (24%) of receptive ﬁelds
with 2 or 3 cone inputs is consistent with the existence of gap junc-
tional coupling between cone pedicles in the macaque retina, allowing
some degree of current to ﬂow among neighbouring cones when sti-
muli larger than a cone are used
33,49,50. The existence of multicone
receptive ﬁelds is also consistent with some intraretinal convergence
of signals onto single midget retinal ganglion cells via amacrine cells
and to a small degree by nearby midget bipolar cells
11.T h i sﬁnding
gives a more comprehensive account of foveal cone signals, indicating
there are exceptions to the commonly held idea that they have an
exclusively private line for relaying visual information to downstream
thalamic and cortical neurons.
Our study shows that the images falling on the retina are captured
at the sampling resolution of the photoreceptor mosaic, thereby
providing the highest ﬁdelity visual sensory input to the brain. These
LGN signals not only transmit optimal spatial sampling of the retinal
image, enabling resolution acuity that shows a strong correlation with
cone density
51, they also provide the strongest chromatic signals if one
assumes random wiring between receptiveﬁeld centres and opposing
surrounds for L and M cones28,52. For higher-level visual functions such
as hyperacuity or processing of dynamic signals in the presence of eye
movements, the single cone receptive ﬁelds again provide the best
possible sensory input to inform the brain about the object viewed.
Vernier acuity, for instance, requires sampled inputs to be slightly
offset from another, but now we know that those tasks more certainly
arise from pooling at the cortical level rather than any pre-cortical level
with lower sampling resolution. Finally, for normal vision, the blur

Knowing that most parvocellular LGN receptive ﬁelds have cen-

photoreceptor signal convergence occurring between the foveal retina
and V1. Because foveal signals are responsible for what humans con-
sider to be everyday vision, our results showcase the importance of
providing the best optical correction available, given that this pathway
always lies waiting to transmit visual information with a resolution
limited only by cone spacing. Neural models of vision and the design of
visual prosthetics can now more accurately consider the behaviour of
the parvocellular system that constitutes the single largest visual
processing stream from the retina to the brain.

Spike sorting and STA generation
To identify action potentials, we used off-line waveform templating

recordings and extracted a ~2 ms waveform surrounding each peak.

with k-means clustering. Clustering often revealed two or more
waveforms. One cluster usually had waveforms with larger peak
amplitudes than the oth ers, indicating better separation from back-
ground activity and commonly interpreted as arising from an isolated
single neuron. To con ﬁrm this idea, we checked interspike interval
histograms for the presence of an absolute refractory period. We also
computed the SNR of the average spike waveform in each cluster, as an

Receptive ﬁeld identiﬁcation and quantiﬁcation
From the spike sorting step, our data included 101 distinct waveforms
from the LGN recordings made in the 3 animals. An STA was computed
for each of these waveforms as described earlier. In some instances, an
STA showed no evidence of a receptive ﬁeld, likely due to it being
outside the stimulated area. If any STAs recorded at the same site
appeared similar, the STA drawn from the waveform with more spikes
had lower noise and was the one chosen for further analysis. Our
analysed data set then ended up including 56 STAs with unique can-
didate receptive ﬁelds.
Qualitatively, these STAs exhibited three general patterns that
drove neurons: a single small area, multiple small areas or a large area
spanning about half the STA width. The STAs with single areas were
considered to represent the activity of single LGN neurons (39 of 56
STAs), an interpretation supported by the high SNR of the source
spike waveforms. The remaining STAs with multiple activating areas
could be interpreted in two ways: either as a single neuron receiving
input from several spatially separated cones or as a multiunit record-
ing combining activity from several neurons. Three lines of evidence
suggest the latter explanation applies to these STAs: (1) some of the
STAs with multiple active areas had mixed ON and OFF behaviour,
which is unlikely from the known classiﬁcation of LGN receptive ﬁeld
centres being either ON or OFF exclusively, (2) the active areas were
distinctly separated on the cone mosaic, which implies input origi-
nating from signals transmitted by multiple non-adjacent retinal
ganglion cells and (3) the SNR of the average waveforms producing
these STAs was low, which is associated with the difﬁculty of isolating
multiple small-amplitude spikes. Consequently, we classiﬁed the STAs
with multiple active areas as multiunit recordings. Anatomically, the
STAs containing single or multiple small active areas were conﬁrmed
to be in the parvocellular LGN layers based on electrode recording
depth and on track reconstructions in the histological sections
(Supplementary Fig. 1). From the same criteria, the STAs with the single
large active areas were associated with the magnocellular LGN layers
and classiﬁed accordingly (n =1 0 ) .
From the multiunit recordings, based on the criteria above, we
considered each distinct active area to represent the receptiveﬁeld of
one LGN neuron. Because the AOSLO allowed us to map LGN receptive
ﬁelds with near diffraction-limited imaging, it provided a unique
opportunity to unveil how individual neural signals contribute to the
STA of a multiunit recording. There were 17 multiunit STAs that had 30
distinct active areas, thereby potentially adding 30 cells to our set of 39
single-unit LGN recordings. The cells were classi ﬁed as either ON or
OFF based on the receptive ﬁeld in the STAs.
The focus of this paper is on parvocellular neurons whose
receptive ﬁelds were mapped comprehensively. Therefore, we exclu-
ded cells with receptive ﬁelds that appeared truncated at the edge of
the stimulus frame, that had evidence of stimulus delivery failure (e.g.
poor stabilisation) or were likely to be magnocellular. Finally, we also
excluded some cells based on a low SNR of the receptive ﬁeld in
the STA.

population of 18 parvocellular ON cells and 16 parvocellular OFF cells.

Quantiﬁcation of cone spacing
Estimation of the cone photoreceptor inner segment diameters in our
data was derived from cone spacing measurements made from the
AOSLO images at the sites on the retina where receptive ﬁelds were
mapped (Supplementary Fig. 2). First, the centres of at least 50 cones
encompassing the receptive ﬁeld sites were identiﬁed by ﬁnding the
peak reﬂectivity of each cone. This was done by Fourier ﬁltering the
cone mosaic image of interest, followed by thresholding and peak
detection. The identiﬁed cone positions were then manually reviewed
and corrected when necessary. An average cone image was then
computed from the identiﬁed cones, which were no further than ~0.2°
from the receptiveﬁeld centre. Finally, to estimate the cone spacing, a
radial average was calculated from the centre of the average cone
image out to 25 imaging pixels using 2D linear interpolation along a
radius measured at 1° angular intervals. A smoothing spline was ﬁtt o
the interpolated radial average and cone spacing was de ﬁned as the
distance of the ﬁrst peak away from zero in the ﬁt. We validated the
radial averaging by measuring the spacing of synthetic cone mosaics
generated from Gaussian-convolved hexagonal grid patterns with
known spacing, which yielded spacing ~3% smaller than the pre-
determined spacing. Thus, the radial averages of the cone mosaics may
be marginally underestimated, though only when cones have a perfect
hexagonal arrangement.

First, we accounted for stimulus delivery jitter. Although stimuli
were stabilised on the retina, there were still small random positional
errors during the movie presentation. This error depends on stabili-

estimated it to have a SD of 1.5 imaging pixels (~0.83 µm, see Supple-

in a uniformly random direction with a magnitude determined by a
normal distribution with a 1.5 imaging pixel SD. This offset was applied
to the absorption maps by interpolation 100 times and an average was
taken, resulting in a slight blurring of the STA. Second, because the
cones driving the neural response were not known at the beginning of
each recording, it was unlikely that placement of the movies had any
stimulus pixel centred precisely on a cone. To incorporate this offset
into the model, the jittered absorption maps were shifted by up to 0.5
cone spacing, in a uniformly random magnitude and direction, using
interpolation. The resulting pattern of light capture with jitter and
displacement can be seen in the middle row in Fig.4b. Third, theﬁtting
