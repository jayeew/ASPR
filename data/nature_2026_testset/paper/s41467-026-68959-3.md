Detecting and utilizing salience information are essential for organisms to
prioritize behaviorally relevant stimuli. While the prefrontal cortex is critical
for cognition and learning, how it leverages salience information remains
unclear. We report that chandelier cells (ChCs) in the medial prefrontal cortex
(mPFC), a unique GABAergic interneuron, encode salience information and
inﬂuence learning. Using cell-type-speciﬁc calcium signal recording in male
mice, we demonstrate that ChCs respond robustly to stimuli across modalities,
with responses dependent on stimulus salience, as determined by novelty and
intensity. Circuit-speciﬁc manipulations reveal that ChC salience coding is
attributable to synaptic inputs from the anterior insular cortex and para-
ventricular thalamus. Furthermore, ChCs acquire salience-coding property for
behaviorally relevant stimuli through associative learning. Notably, bidirec-
tional manipulations of ChC-mediated salience detection correspondingly led
to improved or impaired associative learning. Together, theseﬁndings

Among the diverse interneuron subtypes, chandelier cells (ChCs)
stand out due to their unique morphology and connectivity. ChCs, also
known as axo-axonic cells, form highly speciﬁc “cartridges” of axonal
boutons that exclusively synapse onto the axon initial segments of
PNs
20,21, a key site for action potential initiation. Remarkably, a single
ChC can innervate hundreds of PNs 22–24, positioning them to exert a
powerful “veto” function over large populations of excitatory
neurons25. Notably, ChCs are enriched in the mPFC 26,27,a n dt h e i rv u l -

schizophrenia
28 and autism spectrum disorder 29,i m p l i c a t i n gt h e i r
dysfunction in disrupted cognitive processes and behavioral impair-
ments. Despite their hypothesized importance
25,30, the physiological
role of ChCs in the mPFC remains largely unexplored, particularly in
the context of salience processing.
Here, we report that ChCs in the mPFC play a critical role in
detecting and processing the salience of stimuli. Using advanced
genetic and viral tools for reliable in vivo labeling and manipulation, we
demonstrate that mPFC ChCs encode diverse forms of salience,
including novelty, intensity, and learned associations. Importantly, we
show that ChCs utilize salience information to modulate associative
learning. These ﬁndings identify mPFC ChCs as the integral compo-
nent of the salience processing circuitry, providing new insights into
the neural mechanisms underlying salience processing and high-
lighting their implications for cognitive function and neuropsychiatric
disorders.
Results
ChCs in the mPFC are robustly activated by multiple stimuli
across modalities regardless of valence
To selectively target and manipulate ChCs in the mPFC, we utilized
transgenic mouse strain Unc5b-2A-Cre ERT2 (hereafter referred to as
Unc5b-CreER mice)27,31,32, in combination with Cre-dependent adeno-
associated virus (AAVs). We injected AAV-DIO-GFP into mPFC of
Ucn5b-CreER mice and then administered tamoxifen (TMX) to induce
Cre-dependent GFP expression, enabling selective labelling of ChCs
(Fig. 1a, b). Consistent with prior descriptions of ChCs in Nkx2.1-CreER
mice
21,22, GFP-expressing cells were predominantly located at the layer
1-2 (L1-2) border, with dendrites extending into L1 and axon plexus
localized to L2/3. The characteristic axon“cartridges” of ChCs—strings
of boutons terminating on the axon initial segments of PNs —were
clearly observed in GFP-labeled cells (Fig. 1b). In sparsely labeled
samples, ChCs were unambiguously identi ﬁed based on their axon
“cartridge” morphology (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Additionally, elec-
trophysiological recordings conﬁrmed that the labeled cells exhibited
typical fast-spiking properties (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Together,
these results demonstrate that our approach using Unc5b-CreER mice
and viral tools enables efﬁcient and speciﬁc labeling of mPFC ChCs.
To monitor the activities of ChCs in the mPFC, we expressed the
genetically encoded Ca
2+ sensor GCaMP7s using viral vectors and
performedﬁber photometry to record neuronal activity in real time. In
freely moving animals, ChCs exhibited a high level of spontaneous
activity (Fig. 1c). To investigate the factors driving these activities, we
exposed the animals to a range of stimuli across modalities with
varying emotional valences, including a pure tone, electric foot shocks,
and water consumption (Fig. 1c). Interestingly, mPFC ChCs were
robustly activated by all tested stimuli, regardless of their valence
(Fig. 1d–f). For comparison, we also examined the responses of par-
valbumin (PV)-positive cells and somatostatin (SST)-positive cells,
which mediate perisomatic and dendritic inhibition, respectively
16,17.
Although ChCs and PV basket cells have been considered as two major
subtypes of PV-expressing cells, our previous study32 has shown that
the majority of ChCs labeled in Ucn5b-CreER mice in this area are not
captured by the PV driver mouse line, indicating that ChCs recorded
here did not belong to a subset of PV-positive cells. PV and SST cells in
the mPFC exhibited valence-dependent activity: both cell types were
activated by negatively valenced foot shocks, inhibited during posi-
tively valenced water consumption, and unresponsive to neutral sti-
muli such as pure tones (Fig.1g–l), which were consistent with previous
reports
33,34.
We further tested the responses of ChCs to additional olfactory
and visual stimuli, including exposure to 2-methylthiazole (2-MT, a
predator odor analog), visual ﬂash presentations, and exploration of
novel objects. All these stimuli elicited strong activation of mPFC ChCs
(Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Taken together, these results indicate that
mPFC ChCs are highly responsive to external stimuli across modalities,
regardless of emotional valence.
ChCs in the mPFC detect stimulus salience based on their
novelty and intensity
Stimulus salience refers to both the physical distinctiveness and
behavioral relevance of a stimulus. Novelty and intensity are critical
properties that determine the salience of a stimulus
1,2.T oi n v e s t i g a t e
whether the activity of ChCs in mPFC is inﬂuenced by stimulus novelty,
we conducted experiments using repeated presentations of stimuli
with varied valence in head-ﬁxed mice. This approach enabled precise
recording of ChC activity and accurate delivery of stimuli, categorized
by valence as neutral and positive (e.g., tone and sugar water con-
sumption) or negative (e.g., air puff and electric shock), with 10
repetitive presentations of each type of the stimulus in an approxi-
mately 5-min window per stimulus (Fig. 2a, b).
Recordings primarily captured ChC activity in the prelimbic area
of the mPFC (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). We deﬁned novel stimuli as the
ﬁrst presentation of each type of stimulus, which robustly activated
ChCs. Our results consistently showed a rapid decline in ChC respon-
ses across four types of repeated stimuli regardless of valence,
although the habituation dynamics might vary slightly across stimuli
(Fig. 2c–f). The 10th exposure of each type of stimulus was referred as
familiar stimuli in this short-term habituation stage. These results
suggested that mPFC ChCs could detect the novelty-based salience of
the stimulus. Notably, no correlation was observed between licking
behavior during sugar water consumption and ChC responses (Sup-
plementary Fig. 4a, b), suggesting that the adaptation of ChC activity is
not simply a re ﬂection of changes in behavioral engagement. In con-
trast, PV and SST cells (Supplementary Fig. 3c–f), examined under the
same conditions, showed no adaptive response to repeated stimuli
(Fig. 2g–n and Supplementary Fig. 5a–h). Given the recent literatures
indicating that ChCs ’ activity in single-cell level may correlate with
movement speed in hippocampus and visual cortex

ChC, PV, and SST population in mPFC was performed in freely moving
animals (Supplementary Fig. 6). We found that the population activity

ChCs in the mPFC encode stimulus salience to facilitate asso-
ciative learning
Learned associations imbue the predictive cue with acquired
salience12,41. To investigate the role of ChCs in this process, the trace
fear conditioning (TFC) was chosen because it is known to engage the
mPFC
42, which is critical for higher-order cognitive processes such as
decision-making and associative learning 43. We examined ChC
response dynamics to conditioned stimuli (CS, predictive cues) and
unconditioned stimuli (US, reinforcing stimuli) within a TFC paradigm,
characterized by a trace interval between CS and US presentations
(Fig. 6a). In this paradigm, mice were trained to associate a tone (CS),
which had lost its novelty through habituation, with a foot shock (US),
and the ef ﬁcacy of learning was measured by the animals ’ freezing
behavior (Fig. 6b).
We ﬁrst presented the tone on Day 0 (D0) to assess the habitua-
tion of ChC responses to the auditory stimulus prior to any learning
(Fig. 6c, d). ChCs exhibited robust re sponses to the novel tone pre-
sentation, followed by a rapid reduction in activity as the tone became
familiar through repeated exposure, in line with ﬁndings showed in
Fig. 2. This habituation phase established a baseline of diminished ChC
responsiveness to a non-reinforced, familiar cue.
During subsequent TFC session (in D1), ChCs initially exhibited
subdued responses to the pre-exposed tone as the CS (Fig. 6e, f).
However, their responses to the CS signiﬁcantly increased following its
association with the US (Fig. 6e–g), suggesting that associative learn-
ing enhanced ChC responses to detect the acquired salience of the CS.
Notably, ChCs exhibited sustained responses to the US when it was
part of the CS-US pairing during TFC, whereas their responses to
unpaired US presentations diminished rapidly (Fig.6e, h, and i). These
ﬁndings indicate that learned associations enhance ChC responses to
both predictive cues and reinforcing stimuli, enabling ChCs in the
mPFC to encode acquired salience during associative learning.

inputs to ChC activity during associative learning, we combined ﬁber
photometry recordings of ChCs with site-speciﬁc optogenetic inhibi-
tion of AI or PVT projections in the mPFC using eJaws (Fig. 7a–ba n d
Supplementary Fig. 13). Mice underwent the same TFC paradigm as in
Fig. 6, with conditioning performed under optogenetic inhibition (on
D1) (Fig. 7b). During conditioning, red light (638 nm) was delivered
starting 3 s before CS onset, continuing throughout the 5-s CS period,
and resumed 3 s before US onset lasting through the 2-s US. When light-
induced inhibition was applied, suppression of AI or PVT projections
(eJaws groups) prevented the expected increase in ChC responses to
the CS observed in control groups (mRuby3), indicating impairment in
the acquisition of acquired salience by ChCs through learned associa-
tions (Fig. 7c–h). Additionally, US-evoked responses, which are modu-
lated by learned associations, were also signi ﬁcantly reduced in the
inhibited groups (Fig.7c–h). These results demonstrated that salience-
related inputs from both AI and PVT are critical for the acquisition of
acquired salience in ChCs during associative learning.

Discussion
In this study, we identiﬁed a physiological role for ChCs in the mPFC:
encoding the salience information of external stimuli, including their
novelty, intensity, and learned associations. Recent studies in awake
mice have begun to illuminate region-speciﬁc roles of ChCs in different
brain areas. For example, ChCs have been shown to contribute to the
remapping of principal cell place ﬁelds in the hippocampus
31,r e ﬁne
direction tuning of pyramidal neurons (PNs) during learning in the
premotor cortex36, and convey arousal-related signals in the primary
visual cortex35,45.T h e s eﬁndings suggest that the function role of ChCs
is tailored to the speciﬁc needs of the circuits in which they reside.
At the system level, previous neuroimaging studies have mapped
the salience network, which includes cortical regions such as anterior
insular cortex (AI) and the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, as well as
subcortical structures like the thalamus and striatum
3,13. However,
these studies have lacked cellular and circuit-level resolution needed
to identify how salience is processed. Recent rodent studies have
begun to address this gap, revealing that neurons in subcortical
regions, such as the paraventricular thalamus (PVT), encode the
diverse forms of salience, including reward, aversion, and novelty
12.
Similarly, neurons in the ventral pallidum respond strongly to salient
social and motivational stimuli
11. Neuromodulatory systems, including
noradrenergic and midbrain dopaminergic pathways, also play crucial
roles in salience processing by enhancing signal-to-noise ratios and
encoding reward, novelty, and prediction errors
46,47.O u rﬁndings add
an additional dimension to this framework by identifying ChCs in the
mPFC as a cortical element for salience detection.
Notably, the discrepancy between the TetTox lesion (Fig. 4)a n d
terminal inhibition (Fig. 5) experiments suggests that AI and PVT
contribute to novelty processing through both direct (monosynaptic)
and indirect (polysynaptic) pathways. TetTox lesions of AI and PVT
reduce mPFC ChC responses to novel stimuli, whereas terminal inhi-
bition in the mPFC does not, indicating that these regions impact
novelty detection not only via direct inputs to mPFC but also through
collateral and feedback circuits involving other salience hubs, such as
the ACC, BLA, and hippocampus
3,37. Disruption of these polysynaptic
pathways reduces ChC activation to the novel stimulus. In contrast,
terminal inhibition prevented the normal decline in ChC activity across
repeated exposures, preserving the initial ChC response (Fig. 5). This
suggests that AI and PVT inputs to mPFC do not solely relay novelty
information but actively regulate habituation —downregulating ChC
responses to repeated stimuli. Without this inhibition, ChCs exhibit
perseverative activation, reﬂecting an inability to attenuate responses
to familiar stimuli. Further, AI and PVT lesions or inhibition ampliﬁed
ChC responses to low-value rewards in the later exposure stage
(Figs. 4 and 5), indicating that these pathways normally suppress
responses to low-salience stimuli. This aligns with PVT’sk n o w nr o l ei n
suppressing behavior when rewards are omitted or during stress/
extinction conditions
48–50. In contrast to PVT lesions, which resulted in
stable and invariant Ca² ⁺ responses to varying volume of water
rewards across S1 and S3 (Fig. 4p), AI lesions produced overall large,
invariant Ca²⁺ responses in S3, emerging from previously undetectable
responses in S1 (Fig. 4l). This suggests that AI contributes to salience
processing in a manner distinct from PVT, potentially beyond merely
inhibiting mPFC ChC responses to low-salience stimuli in familiar
contexts. The precise mechanisms underlying the differential roles of
AI and PVT in regulating mPFC ChC activity and salience encoding
remain an open question, with differences in their synaptic input to
ChCs (Supplementary Fig. 12) potentially providing important clues.
Taken together, these ﬁndings suggest a model in which AI and PVT
inputs to mPFC ChCs serve as salience modulators, adjusting ChC
responses to both novel and familiar stimuli. These inputs help reg-
ulate ChC responses to low-value or non-reinforcing stimuli and
facilitate dynamic adaptation during learning (Fig. 7). This model
reconciles our lesion and inhibition data and suggests that ChCs
integrate direct excitatory drive and polysynaptic inhibitory gating to
process stimulus salience.

The brain ’s ability to detect and prioritize salient stimuli is
essential for survival and adaptive behavior. Disruptions in salience
detection and processing have been implicated in multiple neu-
ropsychiatric disorders. For instance, the salience dysfunction
hypothesis of schizophrenia posits that patients misallocate salience
to irrelevant stimuli, which contributes to delusions and
hallucinations
6,7. Similarly, deﬁcits in salience processing are thought
to underlie impairments in social attention and motivation observed in
autism spectrum disorder
8,9. Notably, structural and functional deﬁcits
in ChCs have been reported in both schizophrenia and autism 28,29,
suggesting that ChC dysfunction may underlie some of the core
symptoms associated with these disorders. However, the salience
processing of mPFC ChCs relies on the complex interplay between
local circuits and global networks, as mentioned above. There are
likely multiple sites where compensatory adaptations may occur in the
event of ChC dysfunction. For example, our previous work has shown
that axo-axonic synaptic input from ChCs drives homeostatic plasticity
by structurally and functionally tuning the axon initial segment
32,
which may help compensate for network plasticity in the absence of
ChC function. These mechanisms suggest that the behavioral rele-
vance of ChC-deﬁcient conditions remains to be fully elucidated, and
further research is needed to understand the compensatory processes
that might occur under such circumstances.
In conclusion, our study identiﬁes mPFC ChCs as a critical element
in the detection and processing of stimulus salience. By integrating
inputs from key salience network nodes, ChCs encode the novelty,
intensity, and learned relevance of external stimuli, enabling them to
inﬂuence associative learning. These ﬁndings provide a cellular and
circuit-level perspective on salience processing, offering insights into
how disruptions in this process may contribute to neuropsychiatric
conditions. Further exploration of ChC function in health and disease
may yield new therapeutic strategies targeting cortical salience pro-
cessing circuits.
The current study presents a critical caveat regarding the lack of
single-cell resolution for recording ChC activity. The Ca
2+ signals
obtained through ﬁber photometry represent bulk activity from the
ChC population, and as such, the observed signals may arise from
several potential sources. One possibility is that the bulk signal origi-
nates from synchronized activity among ChCs with similar dynamics at
the single-cell level, especially given the known high connectivity
between ChCs via gap junctions
21. Another possibility is that the bulk
signals observed for salience detection reﬂect the activity of distinct
subsets of ChCs, each processing salience information of different
stimulus distinguished by type, valence, or modality et al. This type of
feature-speciﬁc encoding has been demonstrated in serotoninergic
neurons in the dorsal raphe, which process salience information in a
valence-speciﬁc manner
53.
Additionally, it is plausible that the diverse populations of
ChCs in the mPFC have unique encoding dynamics that are tuned
to speci ﬁc stimulus features (e.g., aversion, reward, novelty). In
this case, the bulk signal might re ﬂect the amalgamation of dis-
tinct signals from various ChC subpopulations, each responsible
for encoding different aspects of salience. This phenomenon has
been observed in prefrontal PNs, where the encoding of different
stimuli is attributed to functionally distinct subpopulations
44,57.
Furthermore, mPFC PNs encode distinct stimuli in divergent ways
depending on task demands, emotional valence, and context
58–60,
and ChCs in the mPFC are selectively innervated by subgroups
of PNs with distinct synaptic preferences 22. Moreover, ChCs in
the mPFC receive speci ﬁc long-range inputs, as evidenced
by differential projection preferences, such as contralateral
corpus callosum vs. amygdala inputs
22, and monosynaptic input
from AI but not PVT (Supplementary Fig. 12). Given these com-
plexities, it is likely that the bulk signals we observe in ChCs
reﬂect a combination of responses from distinct ChC sub-
populations that are shaped by diverse local circuits and/or glo-
bal networks.
However, due to the limitations of ﬁber photometry, which lacks
the resolution to distinguish individual cells, it is challenging to
interpret the precise cellular and circuit mechanisms that underlie the
processing of salience information by ChCs. Future research using
single-cell resolution techniques, such as two-photon imaging, activity-

Mice were anesthetized with isoﬂurane and injected intraperitoneally

Fiber photometry
Fiber photometry was used to monitor real-time Ca 2+ dynamics from
GCaMP7s-expressing neurons in head- ﬁxed awake or freely moving
mice. Before conducting behavioral tests, mice were gently handled
by experimenters for 15 min and then accustomed to the ﬁber patch
cord or experimental apparatus for at least 15 min per day over
3 days. The GCaMP signals were acquired using a ﬁber photometry
system (Inper Ltd., China, or ThinkerTech., China). GCaMP signals
were excited with a 470 nm LED (20 –40 μW at the ﬁber tip), and a
410 nm LED at the same intensity was used to record Ca
2+-indepen-
dent reference signals. The LEDs operated in continuous-wave mode
without frequency modulation or lock-in demodulation. A video
camera (Logitech, C930c) was utilized for recording animal beha-
viors, coupled with a screen recorder to synchronize behavioral
events with analog voltage signals of ﬂuorescence for the further
veriﬁcation. The ﬂuorescence signals and behaviors were recorded
throughout the test session.
Photometry data processing and analysis
The raw data were ﬁrst processed using the instrument ’ss o f t w a r e
(Inper or ThinkerTech) for photobleaching and motion correction,
followed by further analysis in MATLAB using custom scripts. (1)

applied to correct slow baseline drifts caused by photobleaching. (2)
Motion Correction: The 410 nm signal was linearlyﬁtted to the 470 nm
signal, and the ﬁtted reference was subtracted to minimize motion-
related ﬂuctuations. (3)ΔF/F Calculation: The corrected 470 nm signal
was normalized to a pre-stimulus baseline using the formula:ΔF/F = (Ft
-F 0 ) / F 0,w h e r eFt is the ﬂuorescence at time t, and F0 is the baseline
ﬂuorescence, deﬁned as the median value during a pre-de ﬁned win-

were calculated using the formula (F - μ)/σ,w h e r eμ represents the

Trace fear conditioning
All trace fear conditioning (TFC) and retention tests were conducted
using the Ugo fear conditioning system. The delivery of tones and foot
shocks was automated using ANY-maze software (version 6.31,
Stoelting Co., USA).
Two different contexts were used in TFC. Context A featured
black-and-white checkered wallpapers, and the chambers were
cleaned with 75% ethanol. Context B included dark gray wallpaper and
a non-shocking plexiglass ﬂoor of gray coloration, and the chambers
were cleaned with a 4% acetic acid solution. In theﬁrst day (D1), the test
animal was placed in Context A, and after 3 min of habituation, they
received six tones (conditioned stimulus, CS)—shock (unconditioned
stimulus, US) pairings, distributed at variable intervals of 90 ± 30 s. The
electrical foot shock (0.6 mA, 2 s) was delivered 13 s after the end of the
pure tone (60 dB, 4 kHz, 5 s). The empty trace interval (13 s) interposed
in each CS-US pairing. In the second day (D2), mice were placed in
Context B for 3 min and subsequently given four pure tone presenta-
tions (as the same as those in D1) without any shocks.
For ﬁber photometry, there was a separate day (D0), CS habi-
tuation stage, where the mice were placed in Context A for 3 min and
subsequently given six tone presentations without any shocks. To
assess the speciﬁcity of ChC responses to different associative learning
components, three independent groups of mice were used, each
receiving only one of the following paradigms on Day 1: (1) CS-US
paired, in which a 5-s tone (CS) was followed by a footshock (US) after a
13-s trace interval; (2) CS only, in which the tone was presented without
any footshock; and (3) US only, in which mice received unpredicted
footshocks without any preceding tone. Each animal was assigned to
only one paradigm. The order of stimuli within each group was pseu-
dorandomized to avoid temporal expectation effects.
In TFC, animals exhibited conditional responses to the CS offset
(trace interval) in addition to the CS itself. A fear response was oper-
ationally deﬁned as measurable behavioral freezing (more than 1-s
cessation of movement), which was automatically scored and analyzed
by ANY-maze software. In our experiments, animals connected to an
optical ﬁber to the head, light stimuli during test sessions might
interfere the program ’s freezing detection. To further validate the
automatic software detection, the freezing behavior was scored inde-
pendently for each video by an experienced experimenter in a double-
blind manner. The time spent freezing during the CS and trace was
measured for each trial.
Reward learning paradigm
Mice were trained in a Pavlovian appetitive conditioning task over 8
daily sessions. Each session included 100 trials: 50 CS+ trials (auditory
tone paired with a 5μL drop of 10% sucrose solution delivered 1 s after
