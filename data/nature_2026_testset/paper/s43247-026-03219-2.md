The Indo-Paciﬁc Convergence Zone is a key carbon sink via silicate weathering, yet its glacial-
interglacial carbon dioxide consumption remains unclear. Here we reconstruct silicate weathering
ﬂuxes over the past 700,000 years by combining the global geochemical numerical model GEOCLIM
with machine learning algorithms to constrain silicate weathering history in the Indo-Paci ﬁc
Convergence Zone. Our results show that during glacial periods, weathering ﬂuxes peaked at
approximately 1.86 × 10
12 moles per year, drawing down about 9.3 ppmv of carbon dioxide. Exposure
of continental shelves during low sea levels raised glacial weathering by 33%, but high-frequency
positive rainfall anomalies ampliﬁed this increase by 54– 130% (approximately 9.2– 13.7 ppmv carbon
dioxide). This underscores how sea-level change and hydrology control tropical shelf weathering. Our
study provides critical insights into past climatic transitions, emphasizing the Indo-Paci ﬁc
Convergence Zone’s role in regulating the global carbon cycle and offering constraints for future
climate projections.
In the context of global warming, quantifying changes of carbon dioxide
(CO2) and constraining underlying mechanisms over geological time scales
are crucial for enhancing our capacity to comprehend, anticipate, and adapt
to future global shifts
1,2. Notably, during the Quaternary glacial-interglacial
cycles, atmospheric CO2 levels have undergone substantialﬂuctuations, for
which a variety of processes have been postulated as explanatory factors,
including the iron hypothesis
3, the coral reef hypothesis4, the silicate leakage
hypothesis5, the respired carbon deepening hypothesis6, and the reminer-
alized carbon hypothesis7.
Silicate weathering acts as anot her carbon sink by effectively
removing CO 2 from the atmosphere, which is ultimately sequestered
in the ocean as carbonate precipitation 8. Considerable research efforts
have been devoted to investigating the carbon consumption asso-
ciated with modern global terrestrial silicate weathering. Using
quantitative simulations, previous research
9 has constrained the
substantial carbon sink effect of global silicate weathering under
contemporary conditions (1950 –2014), estimating a total carbon
consumption ﬂux (referred to as silicate weathering ﬂux) of
approximately 12.98 × 10
12 mol/yr. However, there has been limited
research, and there are large discrepancies in the studies focusing on
the role of weathering in glacial carbon consumption during past
glacial-interglacial cycles. Some rec onstruction studies suggested that
silicate weathering intensity was weaker and ﬂuxes were lower during
colder glacial periods compared to warmer interglacial stages, and
thus the carbon effect of weathering on the glacial cycle could be
neglected
10–12. A recent study of model simulation argued that the
global silicate weathering has a stable atmospheric CO 2 consumption
during the glacial stages 13. By contrast, calculations in the South
China Sea and Western Paci ﬁcW a r mP o o l14,15 suggested that shelf
weathering during the glacial low s ea level stages could lead to a
reduction of approximately 15 ppmv in CO 2.

Discussion
The maximum chemically weighted weatheringﬂux in our machine learning
calculations occurs at 30 kyr BP, reaching approximately 1.86 × 1012 mol/yr
(9.3 ppmv), while the minimumﬂux is observed at 217 kyr BP, amounting
to approximately 1.50 × 1012 mol/yr. At 30 kyr BP, the global silicate che-
mical weathering ﬂux is calculated as 8.37 × 10 12 mol/yr, with IPCZ con-
tributing around 22% of this total ﬂux. Comparing this to the previously
observed Holocene silicate chemical weatheringﬂux (1.17 × 1013 mol/yr)28,29,
the contribution from the IPCZ accounts for approximately 13–16% of the
total silicate chemical weatheringﬂux during that period.
We performed wavelet analysis 30 on the weighted results, and the
wavelet energy spectrum is shown in Fig.4a. The machine learning modeled
chemical weathering ﬂux of the IPCZ exhibits a very strong glacial-
interglacial cycle (100 kyr), while its obliquity cycle (41 kyr) and precession
cycle (23 kyr) also remain signiﬁcant31. Additionally, we conducted corre-
lation analysis, cross-wavelet transform (XWT), and wavelet coherence
(WTC) analysis
30 between our simulated results and previously recon-
structed sea-level data32.A ss h o w ni nF i g .4b, the machine learning modeled
chemical weathering ﬂux is signi ﬁcantly correlated with sea level
(R = −0.78), and this negative correlation is predominantly concentrated at
a 100 kyr cycle. This ﬁnding aligns well with our ﬁltered results (Fig. 3f).
These observations clearly demonstra te that, on the glacial-interglacial
timescale, sea-level variation is the dominant controlling factor of chemical
weathering ﬂux. Speciﬁcally, during Quaternary glaciations, vast areas of
loose sediments on the southern continental shelf of the South China Sea
were exposed due to the sea level drop, thereby intensifying silicate
weathering processes. These characteristics further corroborate previous
research ﬁndings: sea-level ﬂuctuations during Quaternary glacial-
interglacial cycles are critical factors in ﬂuencing the silicate weathering
ﬂux in the study area
14,15,33. Another interesting observation from Fig.4bi s
that when the sea level is above−80 m, it shows a strong negative correlation
with machine learning modeled chemical weatheringﬂux, whereas below
−80 m, a positive correlation emerges in this region. We propose that a sea
level of −80 m may serve as a threshold for the combined inﬂuence of shelf
exposure and temperature in controlling the weathering ﬂux. Above this
threshold, the increase in weatheringﬂux due to shelf exposure outweighs
the reduction caused by falling temperatures. Below it, the temperature-
driven decrease in weathering ﬂux becomes more signi ﬁcant than the
increase from shelf exposure. As a result,−80 m of sea level represents the
condition under which chemical weatheringﬂux is maximized.
An additional noteworthy observation in Fig. 4 is the relationship
between sea level and the Mg-Ca weathering ﬂux. The XWT analysis
(Fig. 4c) reveals signiﬁcant coherence between the two signals at precession
(23 kyr), obliquity (41 kyr), and eccentricity (100 kyr) periodicities. How-
ever, the WTC analysis (Fig. 4d) indicates strong correlations primarily at
the obliquity and eccentricity bands, while lacking consistent and signiﬁcant
coherence at the precessional band. This suggests that although sea level
exerts a dominant control on silicate chemical weathering in the IPCZ over
longer orbital time scales, it may not be the primary driver of weathering
processes at the precessional timescale. We proposed that precipitation
variation may account for this precessional signal of weatheringﬂux.

Climatic inputs and data sources
The climatic inputs for the GEOCLIM model primarily consist of global
surface temperature and surface runoff. In this analysis, we utilized global
surface temperature data sourced from the HadCM3 dataset25. Considering
that the HadCM3 dataset does not inc lude runoff but only precipitation
data, we calculated a runoff coef ﬁcient k by dividing the annual mean
precipitation in the youngest time slice (0 kyr BP) of the HadCM3 dataset by
the average runoff from the ERA5 dataset 43, additionally, our analysis
incorporates ﬁndings from previous simulation studies 34,r e s u l t i n gi n
k ≈ 0.07 and suggesting potential ≤20% ﬂux underestimations within the
most probable k range (detail computational results fork and their sensi-
tivity analyses are provided in the Supplementary Information and Sup-
plementary Fig. 3). This coefﬁcient was used to convert precipitation values
from the HadCM3 dataset into runoff, ensuring consistency with the cli-
matic inputs required for the model. The climate data used in this study were
derived from the HadCM3 dataset, including mean annual temperature and
precipitation at a spatial resolution of 0.5° × 0.5°.Digital elevation models
(DEMs) were obtained from GEBCO at an original resolution of 15 arc-
seconds, with mean slope values aggregated to 0.5° × 0.5° grid cells. Litho-
logical data were similarly processed at a consistent 0.5° × 0.5° resolution.
This uniﬁed spatial scaling minimizes potential errors arising from reso-
lution mismatches among input datasets.
In addition to climatic conditions, the input parameters for the
GEOCLIM model include land fraction, slope, lithology, and atmospheric
CO
2 concentration. The land fraction was calculated using the DEM
(https://www.gebco.net/) in conjunction with historical sea level data from
previous studies32, allowing for the land fraction to be treated as a function of
sea level height. Slope data were derived from earlier simulation results44.
Since these simulations did not consider the slope of exposed continental
shelves during the Quaternary glacia l periods, we performed additional
calculations utilizing the DEM. The lithology and atmospheric CO2 con-
centration input data were based on simulations from earlier research20,45.
Recognizing that regolith thickness and mineral composition may
require substantial time to reach new equilibria following environmental
change, we employed a simpliﬁed ﬁrst-order response model— af o r m u l a -
tion widely adopted in systems science and Earth system modeling46–48— to
assess potential biases introduced by steady-state assumptions in our
simulations. The results from this response model deviate by less than an
average of 1.87% (maximum 17.43%) from our reference simulation (see
Supplementary Information and Supplementary Fig. 4 for details), indi-
cating that the steady-state assumption introduces only minor bias and does
not alter our primary conclusions.
