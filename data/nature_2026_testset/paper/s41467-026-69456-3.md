Antibodies targeting TGF-β and PD-L1 initially showed promise as second-
generation PD-L1 agents. However, consecutive trial failures have limited their
clinical success. Our study reveals that the efﬁcacy of the TGF-β×PD-L1 bis-
peciﬁc antibody (BsAb) is compromised by insufﬁcient activation of innate
immune responses. To address this, we combine STING agonists with the
BsAb, signiﬁcantly enhancing tumor suppression beyond that achieved with
standard STING agonist plus anti-PD-L1combinations in preclinical models.
Unexpectedly, even STING agonist monotherapy is improved by TGF-β
blockade, suggesting that TGF-β suppresses STING-driven immune activation.
We ﬁnd that this synergy is mediated by the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis, where STING
activation and TGF-β blockade promote CXCL16 expression in macrophages
and dendritic cells, recruiting and sustaining cytotoxic CXCR6+ T cells. Addi-
tionally, PD-L1 blockade further enhances their antitumor activity. To optimize
this strategy, we develop Y101S, an antibody-drug conjugate targeting TGF-β,
PD-L1, and STING, which demonstrates superior tumor control and immune
modulation in preclinical models. Theseﬁndings highlight the therapeutic
potential of this triple-targeting approach.
The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly those
targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, has revolutionized cancer therapy.
Despite their success, many patients do not respond to ICI therapy due
to resistance
1. In these cases, PD-1/PD-L1 signaling may not be the
primary mechanism driving tumor immune evasion, suggesting that
other immune pathways are also compromised
2. Addressing these
imbalances within the tumor microenvironment (TME) is essential for
restoring effective antitumor immunity3.
Combining ICIs with other therapeutic strategies has emerged as a
promising approach to enhance their ef ﬁcacy and overcome
resistance4–7.N u m e r o u ss t u d i e sh a v ei d e n t iﬁed agents that exhibit
synergistic effects when combined with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors 8–12.

Our preliminary studies revealed a strong synergistic effect

models, adding a STING agonist markedly inhibited tumor growth and
extended survival in YM101-treated mice. Logically, STING agonists
initiate antitumor immune responses early in the cancer-immunity
cycle, enhancing innate immunity. However, the mechanisms under-
lying the interaction among these three signaling pathways remain
unclear. Investigating how these pathways cooperate to regulate
antitumor immunity is essential.
In this work, we clarify how STING activation enhances the anti-
tumor activity of TGF-β and PD-L1 dual blockade. We show that STING
agonists synergize with the anti-TGF-β×PD-L1 BsAb to produce stron-

blockade increases CXCL16 expression in myeloid cells and sustains
cytotoxic CXCR6
+ T cells, which are further potentiated by PD-L1
blockade. Building on these insights, we develop Y101S, an immunos-
timulatory antibody conjugate (ISAC) that integrates the anti-TGF-
β×PD-L1 BsAb with a cleavable STING agonist payload. This ISAC
simultaneously targets all three pathways, offering strategic advan-
tages over conventional multi-drug combinations and potentially
improving therapeutic outcomes in cancer.

Enhancing antitumor efﬁcacy with STING agonist and YM101
As we previously reported, YM101 demonstrated considerable anti-
tumor ef ﬁcacy in the EMT-6 model, with most tumors responding
positively to treatment (Fig. 1A)23. However, a subset of tumors
exhibited limited response, de ﬁned by a tumor growth inhibition
(TGI) value below 50% compared with the control group (Fig. 1B). To
investigate the molecular basis underlying this heterogeneous
response, we performed bulk RNA-seq analysis on YM101-sensitive
and YM101-resistant tumors. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)
revealed that responder tumors were markedly enriched in innate
immune response-related pathways (Fig.1C). To further delineate the
speciﬁc molecular mechanisms driving innate activation, we exam-
ined all signiﬁcantly enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms and focused
on those directly associated with immune signaling and cytokine
responses. According to normalized enrichment scores (NES), the
top ﬁve pathways were response to IFN- β, response to IFN-II, IL-1 β
production, TNF production, and IL-6 production, all with false dis-
covery rate (FDR) values below 0.05 (Fig. 1D). Except for the IFN-II
response, these immune and cytokine pathways represent major
downstream programs of STING signaling, suggesting that activation
of the STING pathway is a de ﬁning feature of YM101 responder
tumors.
We next validated these ﬁndings in another previously estab-
lished YM101-sensitive model CT26
23. Consistent with the EMT-6
results, a subset of CT26 tumors displayed limited response to
YM101, while GSEA of responder versus nonresponder tumors con-
ﬁrmed signi ﬁcant enrichment of innate immune response and
response to IFN-β pathways (Fig. 1E–H). The overlapping upregulated
genes between the two responder models included canonical STING
downstream effectors, such as Ifnb1 and Tnf, as well as the tissue-
resident T cell-associated gene Cxcr6 (Fig. 1I). Functional enrichment
of these shared genes con ﬁrmed the activation of innate immune
response, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, response to IFN-β,
IL-6 production, and the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway (Fig. 1J).
To further validate these transcriptomic ﬁndings across models
with distinct responsiveness to YM101, we compared the gene
expression proﬁles of YM101-sensitive EMT-6 and CT26 tumors with
the resistant B16 model. As we previously observed, B16 tumors
showed poor response to YM101, with no signi ﬁcant reduction in
tumor growth compared to the isotype control
24.I nc o n t r a s t ,E M T - 6
and CT26 models exhibited substantial tumor growth inhibition and
improved survival upon YM101 treatment, conﬁrming their sensitivity.
Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed that EMT-6 and CT26
tumors exhibited stronger enrichment of innate immune activation
and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways relative to B16 (Supplementary
Fig. 1A–D). The overlapping upregulated genes from the EMT-6 vs B16
and CT26 vs B16 comparisons were also signiﬁcantly enriched in innate
immune response-related pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F). At the
protein level, cytokine proﬁling in EMT-6 tumors demonstrated that
responder tumors expressed higher levels of STING downstream
cytokines, including IFN- β,T N F -α, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CXCL9, and
CXCL10 (Fig. 1K). Collectively, these results suggest that robust innate
immune activation and elevated STING pathway activity are hallmarks
of YM101-sensitive tumors.
Given this evidence, we hypothesized that YM101 resistance may
result from insuf ﬁcient innate immune activation, which could be
overcome through pharmacologic stimulation of the STING pathway.
To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the combination of the STING
agonist MSA-2 with YM101 (M + Y) in EMT-6, CT26, and B16 models.
The combination therapy signi ﬁcantly inhibited tumor growth and
prolonged survival in multiple models (Fig. 1L–Q and Supplementary
Fig. 1G, H). In addition, tumor rechallenge experiments showed that
mice which had achieved complete regression of the primary tumors
after M + Y treatment were protected from subsequent tumor out-
growth, indicating the induction of durable systemic antitumor
immunity (Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, triple-targeted therapy
produced superior tumor control and survival bene ﬁts compared
with the commonly tested STING agonist plus anti-PD-L1 antibody
combination (M + α-P). Moreover, MSA-2 plus anti-TGF- β (M + α-T)
also outperformed MSA-2 monotherapy, indicating that TGF- β may
act as a limiting factor in STING-mediated antitumor immunity.
Together, these ﬁndings extend beyond overcoming resistance to
dual TGF-β/PD-L1 blockade. They highlight the broader therapeutic
potential of integrating TGF- β inhibition into STING agonist-based
immunotherapy, suggesting that simultaneous targeting of STING,
TGF-β, and PD-L1 may offer a powerful strategy to achieve superior
clinical outcomes.

To investigate how the combination therapy increases Cxcr6+ Tc e l l s ,
we analyzed scRNA-seq data, focusing on chemokine signaling. Since
CXCR6 is involved in classical chemokine signaling, we examined cell-
to-cell communication in the TME (Supplementary Fig. 5). Our analysis
showed that the combination therapy signi ﬁcantly enhanced CXCL
signaling to T cells compared with other treatments (Fig. 3Aa n d
Supplementary Fig. 6A). Speciﬁcally, macrophages and cDCs were the
main sources of CXCL signaling to T cells, with the strongest signals
observed in the combination therapy group (Fig.3B–D). Among CXCL
family members, CXCL16 –CXCR6 signaling was notably enhanced,
aligning with the expansion of Cxcr6
+ T cells (Fig. 3E and Supplemen-
tary Fig. 6B, C). Macrophages and cDCs were the primary sources of
CXCL16, with their interactions with T cells playing a dominant role in
driving CXCL16–CXCR6 signaling (Fig. 3F–H).

TGF-β blockade enhances STING-driven CXCL16 expression
The canonical STING pathway induces IFN-I, which exerts its down-
stream biological effects via the IFNAR–JAK1/2–STAT1 axis. In human
macrophages and DCs, si-STAT1 or IFNAR1 blockade abrogated STING
agonist-induced CXCL16 expression, indicating that IFN-
I–STAT1 signaling is required for CXCL16 induction. The results con-
ﬁrmed that STING agonists drive CXCL16 expression through IFN-I
signaling and subsequent STAT1 activation (Fig.7G). TGF-β1 is a known
inhibitor of STING–IFN-I signaling. In vitro, we validated that TGF- β1
downregulates IFN-I expression (Fig.7H). Western blot analysis further
demonstrated that exogenous TGF-β1 suppresses IRF3 phosphoryla-
tion within the STING signaling cascade (Fig. 7I). Previous studies
suggest this inhibition may involve reactive oxygen species (ROS)-
mediated translocation of HDAC4 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm
30.
Consistent with these ﬁndings, we observed marked redistribution of
HDAC4 in human macrophages following TGF- β1 treatment, accom-
panied by reduced p-IRF3 levels, and both changes were reversed by
ROS inhibitor (ROSi) (Supplementary Fig. 12D). Accordingly, treatment
with either ROSi or si-HDAC4 effectively reversed TGF- β1-mediated
suppression of IFN-β and CXCL16 expression (Fig. 7J).
Our scRNA-seq data indicated that myeloid cells, particularly
macrophages and DCs, are the primary sources of CXCL16 production

minimal CXCL16 upregulation in murine tumor cells, whereas BMDMs
and BMDCs showed robust CXCL16 induction (Supplementary
Fig. 12E). Given that many tumor cells harbor defects in IFN-I signaling,
we hypothesized that myeloid cells are key mediators of STING acti-
vation and TGF-β blockade, and that the synergistic antitumor effects

abolished, demonstrating that the enhanced antitumor activity of the
triple-targeted therapy depends on intact STAT1 signaling in myeloid
cells (Fig. 7M–O). This loss of CXCL16 expression in tumor-inﬁltrating
macrophages and DCs was further con ﬁrmed by ﬂow cytometry
(Supplementary Fig. 12F).
Development and antitumor efﬁcacy of the TGF-β×PD-L1–STING
ISAC Y101S
Building on the above ﬁndings, we aimed to develop a therapeutic
agent capable of simultaneously targeting three key signaling path-
ways. To achieve this, we synthesized a lysosome-cleavable linker using
Mal-PEG4-NHS and Fmoc-Val-Cit-PAB, and then conjugated YM101 to
the STING agonist MSA-2 via this linker to construct the ISAC Y101S
(Fig. 8A). In principle, Y101S is designed to inhibit TGF- β and PD-L1
signaling while promoting CXCL16 expression in myeloid cells through
antibody-mediated internalization and intracellular release of MSA-2.
In both CT26 and 4T1 tumor models, Y101S demonstrated
superior antitumor efﬁcacy compared with YM101, signi ﬁcantly sup-
pressing tumor growth and prolonging survival in tumor-bearing mice
(Fig. 8B–G). Notably, its antitumor activity was comparable to that of
the free combination of YM101 with standard-dose MSA-2 (50 mg/kg),
while containing only 0.05 mg/kg of conjugated MSA-2 payload per
9 mg/kg Y101S dose. The potent ef ﬁcacy of Y101S was further con-
ﬁrmed in the EMT-6 model (Fig.8H–J). Consistent with our mechanistic
hypothesis that the CXCL16–CXCR6 axis is a key mediator of response,
the enhanced antitumor effects of Y101S were dependent on CXCL16
signaling (Fig. 8H–J and Supplementary Fig. 13). Moreover, tumor
rechallenge experiments demonstrated that Y101S induced durable
immunological memory, effectively preventing the outgrowth of
recurrent tumors (Supplementary Fig. 14). Flow cytometry analysis of
EMT-6 tumors after Y101S treatment revealed a marked increase in
CXCL16⁺ macrophages and DCs, accompanied by an elevated fre-
quency of CXCR6 ⁺CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 8K). In line with these ﬁndings,
multiplex IF staining of 4T1 tumors further con ﬁrmed that Y101S
treatment markedly increased the inﬁltration of CXCR6⁺CD8⁺ Tc e l l s
(Supplementary Fig. 15).

To further characterize the pharmacological properties of Y101S,
we next evaluated its stability and payload release proﬁle. In vitro, only
a small fraction of the MSA-2 payload was released over 72 h, with <10%
cumulative release in mouse serum and approximately 5% in
phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Supplementary Fig. 17A), indicating
that Y101S is suf ﬁciently stable under physiological conditions. Con-
sistently, LC–MS/MS analysis of EMT-6 tumor tissues showed that free
MSA-2 was readily detectable after Y101S treatment, whereas YM101-
treated tumors contained undetectable amounts of MSA-2 (Fig.8La n d
Supplementary Fig. 17B), con ﬁrming ef ﬁcient linker cleavage and
intratumoral release of the STING agonist. These data indicate that
Y101S remains stable in circulation but undergoes effective payload
release within the TME. These ﬁndings prompted us to hypothesize
that ISAC-mediated targeted delivery of MSA-2 has the potential to
outperform free STING agonist plus YM101 regimens by enhancing
intratumoral immune activation while limiting off-tumor effects.
To address this, we compared 9 mg/kg Y101S with two free-
combination regimens containing 9 mg/kg YM101: (i) 9 mg/kg YM101
plus low-dose MSA-2 (0.05 mg/kg, equimolar to the MSA-2 payload
delivered by Y101S), and (ii) 9 mg/kg YM101 plus standard-dose MSA-2
(50 mg/kg). Although Y101S did not outperform the standard-dose
free combination in terms of tumor growth control and immune
activation, it achieved signi ﬁcantly stronger antitumor effects and
intratumoral immune activation than the low-dose free combination
(Supplementary Fig. 18A –C). Flow cytometric analysis of tumor-
inﬁltrating immune cells further indicated that, compared with the
equimolar low-dose MSA-2 plus YM101 regimen, Y101S more effec-
tively remodeled the TME. Y101S treatment resulted in higher fre-
quencies of CXCR6 ⁺CD8⁺ T cells, Ki67 ⁺CD8⁺ T cells, CD69 ⁺CD8⁺
T cells, granzyme-B⁺CD8⁺ Tc e l l s ,a n dp e r f o r i n⁺CD8⁺ T cells, as well as
increased proportions of CXCL16 ⁺ DCs and macrophages within
tumors (Supplementary Fig. 18D). I mportantly, at 24 h after dosing,
Y101S induced higher transcript levels of Ifnb, Tnf, Il6,a n d Cxcl16 in
tumors than in non-tumor organs, indicating a preferential activation
of STING signaling within the TME (Supplementary Fig. 18E). Com-
pared with the low-dose free combination, Y101S induced more robust
immune activation within tumors. In contrast, when compared with
the standard-dose combination, Y101S achieved comparable tumor
control while showing reduced inﬂammatory signaling in non-tumor
organs. These data support the notion that Y101S can deliver an
effective STING agonist dose to the tumor while limiting off-tumor
inﬂammatory risk.
To evaluate the in vivo safety of Y101S, we monitored body weight
and found no signi ﬁcant weight loss in treated mice (Supplementary
Fig. 19A). At the end of the in vivo study, biochemical analysis of per-
ipheral blood revealed no signiﬁcant increases in markers of hepatic,
renal, or cardiac injury (Supplementary Fig. 19B). Furthermore, histo-
pathological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver,
spleen, lungs, and kidneys, showed no apparent pathological changes
(Supplementary Fig. 19C). Collectively, these results indicate that
Y101S is well tolerated and exhibits a favorable safety proﬁle in vivo.
Discussion
TGF-β plays a pivotal role in immune suppression and resistance to
immunotherapy across various cancers. Dual blockade targeting TGF-
β and PD-L1 has shown considerable promise for enhancing immune
responses, with bifunctional antibodies, such as M7824, demonstrat-
ing success in preclinical and early-phase clinical trials
33. However,
results from larger-scale studies have revealed unforeseen challenges,
suggesting that TGF-β×PD-L1 blockade alone may not be sufﬁcient to
provide durable clinical bene ﬁts in many cases. These ﬁndings
underscore the need to re ﬁne this therapeutic strategy and to eluci-
date the mechanisms underlying resistance to dual blockade.
Despite being conducted in genetically identical inbred mouse
models, our study revealed heterogeneous therapeutic responses to
YM101, with a subset of tumors showing limited regression. Such intra-
model heterogeneity has also been documented in other immu-
notherapy settings, including GL261 glioma
34, CT26 colon carcinoma35,
and E0771 breast tumors 36, and is now recognized as a biologically
meaningful phenomenon rather than technical variability. Compara-
tive analyses between responders and nonresponders within the same
model consistently revealed substantial differences in the TME, parti-
cularly in the abundance and function of inﬁltrating immune cells and
the intensity of chemokine and cytokine signaling. Key immune
pathways, most prominently IFN-I signaling, varied markedly among
tumors within the same model and closely correlated with treatment
efﬁcacy
37. Given that antitumor immunity involves multiple regulatory
thresholds, feedback, and feed-forward loops, stochastic activation of
immune signaling is likely inevitable, resulting in divergent therapeutic
outcomes even under identical genetic and experimental conditions.
Moreover, recent genomic analyses have shown that even commonly
used inbred mouse strains may harbor vendor-speciﬁc genetic varia-
tions that subtly in ﬂuence immune signaling and gene regulation,
further contributing to response variability
38.
In line with these observations, the combination of MSA-2 and
YM101 in our models also failed to induce complete tumor rejection in
all animals, with complete responses plateauing at approximately half
of the treated mice. We consider this pattern to be biologically plau-
sible rather than merely technical noise. Under strong immune pres-
sure from STING activation and dual TGF- β/PD-L1 blockade, tumor
subclones with reduced antigenicity, impaired interferon responsive-
ness, or compensatory upregulation of additional inhibitory pathways
are likely to gain a selective advantage. Such immunoediting and the
emergence of alternative escape routes, together with intrinsic varia-
bility in the engagement of the STING –IFN-I–STAT1–CXCL16 axis
across tumors, probably contribute to the persistence of residual
disease despite overall robust tumor regression. A more detailed dis-
section of these resistance mechanisms will require longitudinal and

Murine tumor models for evaluating YM101 antitumor efﬁcacy
Female BALB/c mice (strain code: 211) and C57BL/6N mice (strain code:
213) (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Labora-
tories and housed under speci ﬁc pathogen-free (SPF) conditions.
Experimental and control animals were housed in separate cages but
kept under identical conditions. Mice were maintained under a 12-h
light/dark cycle (lights on from 06:00 to 18:00). The animal room
temperature was set to 21 ± 2 °C with ~50% relative humidity. Mice had
free access to food and water.
To investigate mechanisms of response and resistance to YM101,
we used EMT-6 mammary carcinoma and CT26 colorectal carcinoma
models in BALB/c mice. For the EMT-6 model, 2 × 10
5 EMT-6 cells were
orthotopically inoculated into the right mammary fat pad. The CT26
model was generated by subcutaneously inoculating 1 × 10
6 CT26 cells
into the right inguinal region of BALB/c mice. When tumors reached
approximately 100 mm³ in volume, mice were randomized to receive
intravenous injections of YM101 (9 mg/kg) or isotype control IgG (CTL,
6.6 mg/kg) every other day for a total of three doses. Treatment
response was deﬁned using the TGI metric, which is widely applied in
preclinical efﬁcacy studies
51,52. TGI (%) was calculated as 100 × [1−(ΔT/
ΔC)], where ΔTa n d ΔC represent the change in tumor volume from
treatment initiation to the analysis time point in the YM101-treated and
CTL groups, respectively. Tumors with TGI < 50% were classi ﬁed as
nonresponders, whereas those with TGI ≥ 50% were classi ﬁed as
responders. Tumors from both responders and nonresponders were
collected for bulk RNA-seq analysis on day 19 (EMT-6) and day 18
(CT26) after tumor inoculation. Moreover, the EMT-6 model was
independently repeated for intratumoral cytokine analysis, and
tumors were harvested 6 days after the last dose. Total protein con-
centrations in tumor tissue homogenates were measured using a BCA
protein assay kit, and cytokine levels were quanti ﬁed using an IFN- β
ELISA kit (MIFNB0, R&D Systems) and multiplexﬂuorescence-encoded

The combined antitumor efﬁcacy of MSA-2 and YM101 was eval-
uated using EMT-6, CT26, and B16 murine tumor models. The EMT-6
and CT26 models were established as described above. B16 tumors
were generated by subcutaneous implantation of 2 × 10
5 B16 cells into
the right groin of C57BL/6N mice. Mice were randomized to receive
three intravenous injections via the tail vein of one of the following
regimens: isotype control IgG (CTL, 6.6 mg/kg), anti-TGF- β (6.6 mg/
kg), anti-PD-L1 (6.6 mg/kg), or YM101 (9 mg/kg). These antibody doses
were selected to be equimolar, based on their respective molecular
weights. MSA-2 was administered as a single oral dose (50 mg/kg) at
the initiation of antibody therapy.
Tumor size was measured using calipers, and tumor volume was
calculated as (length × width²) × 0.5. Mice were monitored for tumor
burden and general health status every 2 days. The maximum tumor
size permitted by the institutional ethical board was tumor volume ≤
2000 mm³ and/or a maximal diameter ≤ 20 mm, and all experiments
adhered to these limits. Humane endpoint criteria for early termina-
tion included tumor burden exceeding the approved limit, tumor
ulceration/necrosis, >20% body weight loss, inability to access food or
water, severe respiratory distress, impaired mobility, or other signs of
severe distress. Tumor-bearing mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhala-
tion followed by cervical dislocation when humane endpoints were
reached or at the predeﬁned experimental endpoint.
This experiment was repeated in independent cohorts to evaluate
overall survival in tumor-bearing mice. In separate mechanistic cohorts
designed to obtain adequate tumor tissue for bulk RNA-seq and ﬂow
cytometry, treatment was initiated at a larger tumor volume
(200–250 mm³), and tumors were collected 24 h after completion of
the dosing schedule. Unless otherwise speciﬁed, tumor samples used
for TME characterization were randomly selected within each treat-
ment group without pre-stratiﬁcation based on tumor regression.

remained essentially unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 22). This
~2.0 kDa increase in light chain mass is consistent with conjugation
of approximately two linker-payload molecules per light chain.

to an average DAR of approximately 4 for Y101S, in line with the
design speciﬁcation.
Antitumor activity and safety evaluation of Y101S
The antitumor activity of Y101S was evaluated in CT26, 4T1, and EMT-6
tumor models established as described above. When tumor volumes
reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomized into treat-
ment groups. Because Y101S and YM101 have similar molecular
weights, both antibodies were administered at 9 mg/kg by intravenous
(tail vein) injection every other day for a total of three doses. In the 4T1
model, Y101S was further compared with two free-combination regi-
mens containing 9 mg/kg YM101 plus MSA-2. Mice received 9 mg/kg
YM101 combined with either low-dose MSA-2 (0.05 mg/kg, equimolar
to the MSA-2 payload delivered by 9 mg/kg Y101S) or standard-dose
MSA-2 (50 mg/kg). For the low-dose regimen, MSA-2 was administered
orally every other day for three doses, concurrent with each YM101
injection. For the standard-dose regimen, MSA-2 was administered as a
single oral dose (50 mg/kg) at the initiation of antibody treatment. To
assess whether the antitumor ef ﬁcacy of Y101S was dependent on
CXCL16 signaling, CXCL16 neutralization was also performed in Y101S-
treated mice. In EMT-6, CT26, 4T1, and B16 tumor models, Y101S was
combined with intratumoral injections of a CXCL16-neutralizing anti-
body or isotype control, using the same intratumoral dosing schedule
as for CXCL16 blockade experiments.
After completion of treatment, major organs and peripheral
blood samples were collected to assess the safety proﬁle of Y101S. The
heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested, ﬁxed in 4%
paraformaldehyde, embedded in parafﬁn, sectioned, and stained with
hematoxylin and eosin for histopathological evaluation of potential
treatment-induced changes. Tis sue sections were independently
evaluated by two experienced pathologists who were blinded to group
allocation. To assess systemic toxicity, a panel of biochemical markers
commonly used to evaluate liver, kidney, and cardiac function was
measured in peripheral blood. These markers included alanine ami-
notransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen, crea-
tinine, uric acid, creatine kinase, and creatine kinase isoenzyme.
Biochemical analyses were conducted using an automatic biochemical
analyzer (LW C400, Landwind).
In a separate cohort of tumor-bearing mice, mRNA levels ofCxcl16

Statistical analyses and reproducibility
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and R
software. For comparisons between two groups with normally dis-
tributed data, Student ’s t test was used, with Welch ’s correction
applied when variances were unequal. For non-normally distributed
data, the Mann –Whitney U-test was used. For comparisons among
multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was performed, followed by
multiple-comparisons test. Survival analysis of tumor-bearing mice
was assessed using the log-rank test. Data are expressed as mean ±
standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. All statistical tests
were two-sided, with a P < 0.05 considered statistically signiﬁcant. In
vivo studies were performed in at least two independent models,
yielding consistent results. In vitro assays were repeated in at least
three independent experiments with similar outcomes.
