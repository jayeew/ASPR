Acinetobacter baumannii, a robust Gram-negative bacterium known for
causing nosocomial infections, exhibiting multidrug resistance, and lacking
antimicrobial peptides that target it,remains hard to treat. Here, we report a
few-shot learning pipeline integrating classiﬁcation, ranking, and regression
modules. Each module is trained via a few-shot learning strategy involving pre-
training and multipleﬁne-tuning steps, incorporating similar and true data for
ﬁne-tuning, to identify potent AMPs against Acinetobacter baumannii. This
pipeline effectively scans complete libraries of hexapeptides, heptapeptides,
and octapeptides (encompassing tensof billions of candidates) despite the
extreme scarcity of training data. Results show it discovers AMPs active against
Acinetobacter baumannii and Candida albicans, with low off-target toxicity
and negligible drug resistance susceptibility. Additionally, EME7(7) controls
Acinetobacter baumannii pneumonia in mice without kidney injury, a contrast
to the observed effects of polymyxin B. This work provides a paradigm for
addressing challenges of limited data availability.

Results
Overview of the FSLSMEP
Within the vast search space of hexapeptides, we present our data-
driven approach aimed at identifying optimal peptide candidates.
Our recent study con ﬁrms the effectiveness of a machine learning
pipeline consisting of classiﬁcation, ranking, and regression models.
Building upon this concept, we propose a plug-and-play deep
learning pipeline with the same modules. FSLSMEP takes peptide
sequences and physicochemical descriptors as input and generates
predictions regarding their classiﬁcation as an antimicrobial peptide
(AMP), antimicrobial ranking, and minimum inhibitory concentration
(MIC) score. However, owing to the limited availability of AMP data
speciﬁcally targeting A. baumannii [only 148 sequences in GRAMPA
dataset, (dataset 1)], training models with high predictive perfor-
mance is challenging.
To address this data deﬁciency problem, we employed a few-shot
learning strategy of pre-training and multiple ﬁne-tuning (Fig. 1a,b).
Notably, we selected ESM as the pre-training model due to its out-
standing performance in predicting the biological properties of pep-
tides after ﬁne-tuning

targeting A. baumannii, we considered utilizing similar data. As thou-

with singularﬁne-tuning. The fourth approach wasﬁnalized to perform
the classiﬁcation task. As a result, 122,673 samples were retained from
this initial module and proceeded to the subsequent stages.
Moving along the pipeline, we implemented a ranking module to
identify potent AMP candidates from the selected positive samples.
This module focused on ranking peptides based on their MIC values,
providing robustness against dataset bias and noise. Particularly, we
removed data of anti-all_strains AMPs from multipleﬁne-tuning steps
as their adverse impact on model performance was evidenced in the
classiﬁcation module. Instead, we used the parameters from the clas-
siﬁcation model ﬁne-tuned by anti- P. aeruginosa AMPs to initialize
parameters for the pre-training model and followed the multipleﬁne-
tuning steps. Additionally, given the superior performance of the
ranking model trained with physicochemical descriptors in our pre-
vious work
25, we employed the same set of 676 descriptors as features
to train a multilayer perceptron (MLP) using anti- P. aeruginosa and
anti-A. baumannii AMPs, in sequence. Fig. 2b and Supplementary
Table 4 presented the predictive performance of different approaches
following the FSLSMEP. The ESM(P.a.+A.b.)* and MLP(P.a.+A.b.)
approaches outperformed the others, warranting their selection for
the ranking task to identify the top-500 peptides in the pool.
In the ﬁnal stage, we incorporated a regression module to predict
the MIC value of a peptide, effectively concluding the pipeline. The
regression model demonstrated heightened sensitivity to subtle dis-
tinctions within the optimized candidate pool, and the positive train-

Exploring AMPs against C. albicans in the hexapeptide Library
using FSLSMEP process
In addition to A. baumannii , we retrained and deployed the EME
pipeline to discover AMPs targeting a kind of fungi, C. albicans (657
samples), to show the generalization ability of the approach. The
selection of C. albicans was due to three reasons: (i) clinical relevance
in therapeutic applications, (ii) limited availability of existing AMP

Lysing bacterial membrane has long been considered the primary
mechanism of action for most cationic AMPs
13. Bacterial membranes,

teria) or teichoic acids (in Gram-positive bacteria), exhibit higher
electronegativity compared to those of plants and animals, rendering
them more susceptible to the effects of cationic compounds. Through
electrostatic interactions, cationic peptides with amphiphilic nature
can bind to the bacterial membrane, resulting in membrane pore
formation, leakage of cellular contents, and ultimately, bacterial cell
death. Notably, the empirical selection process implemented a pre-
ﬁltering step to retain cationic and amphiphilic peptides. Thus, we
speculated that the leading compounds followed the membrane lysis
mechanism. To validate our hypothesis, we conducted scanning elec-
tron microscope (SEM) and transmission electron microscope (TEM)
imaging of peptide-treated A. baumannii cells. The images revealed
distinct membrane damage on the bacterial cell surface, providing
strong evidence of the membrane lysis mechanism induced by the
leading AMPs (Fig. 5d–i). In addition, Supplementary Fig. 11 showed
that upregulated genes exhibited transcriptional features of bacterial
cell membrane damage, including processes related to cell membrane
repair (e.g., fatty acidβ-oxidation via acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, protein
folding chaperone activity, and chaperone-mediated protein folding)

via protein phosphorylation, mo-molybdenum-molybdopterin cofac-

tion). These observations support the notion that the leading peptides
exert their antimicrobial effects by targeting and disrupting the bac-
terial membrane, underscoring their potential as effective anti-
microbial agents againstA. baumannii.

Discussion
In the domain of drug or peptide discovery, properly leveraging a
machine learning model and letting it generalize is a long-standing
challenge. Oftentimes, depending on the quantity and quality of the
available dataset, a model may perform well during training but fail at
the unseen samples. Earlier, we developed SMEP which integrated
classiﬁcation, ranking, and regression models, leading to a remarkable
success rate of 98.5% in the discovery of AMPs. To the best of our
knowledge, this represents the highest score achieved for such a task.
The foundation of each robust model lies in the availability of an
abundant dataset comprising high-quality positive samples (1762).
However, when we tried to apply such technology to the speci ﬁc
context of this study, we encountered a great hurdle due to the limited
positive data available against A. baumannii, consisting of only 148
sequences. Directly conducting the pipeline with such a scarce dataset
inevitably leads to over ﬁtting and unreliable predictions. Conse-
quently, the main challenge of this study is to devise a strategy to train
robust models capable of leveraging the potential of this limited data
while avoiding over ﬁtting and ensuring reliable compound
predictions.
In that regard, we developed FSLSMEP, which has been proven
effective in addressing few-shot learning challenges
26. We believe that
the key to resolve this challenge is to obtain a benign representation. If
applying the conventional training paradigm in machine learning in
this scenario, the limited size of the training data can by no means
provide a proper latent representation. By contrast, in FSLSMEP, we
propose to leverage a pre-training large model as the backbone ---
which is trained in a self-supervised scheme by a tremendous amount
of unlabeled peptide and protein data --- and proactively integrate it
into the architecture of the pipeline. To the best of our knowledge, this
is the ﬁrst instance of discovery of anti-A. baumannii peptides within
such a limited data scenario. Further, we devise multiple ﬁne-tuning
stages that consist of different objective functions catered accordingly
using anti- P. aeruginosa AMPs and subsequently anti- A. baumannii
AMPs. Put another way, the pre-training step is aimed to enhance the
model learning representations of peptide sequences, while the mul-
tiple ﬁne-tuning steps adequately adapt the pre-trained model and
ground it onto this very speci ﬁc task we study in this article. By
extensive experiments, this work ﬂow has effectively mitigated the
overﬁtting and eventually led to decent results.
As a point of comparison, we also trained the model ﬁne-tuned
with anti-all_strains, anti-P. aeruginosa and anti-A. baumannii AMPs.
However, its predictive performance sharply declined due to the
inclusion of anti-Gram-positive AMPs, which possess different
sequence characteristics from the anti-Gram-negative AMPs (Supple-
mentary Fig. 1). Given that our objective was to discover anti- A. bau-
mannii (Gram-negative bacteria) AMPs, incorporating anti-Gram-
positive AMPs could introduce data noise and decrease the model
predictability. Moreover, we constructed 4 distinct pipelines, with MIC
evaluation of the top-10 peptides selected by each pipeline from 64
million sequences. The EME pipeline demonstrated superior AMP
screening capability. The superior performance of the EME archi-
tecture was hypothesized to originate from its dual-path information
processing framework integrating orthogonal feature modalities. (1)
The ESM classi ﬁcation model demonstrated sequence semantic sen-
sitivity; (2) The MLP ranking model introduced physicochemical
structural representations, which mitigated the potential error accu-
mulation of homogeneous models in sequence and generated a more
diverse candidate pool for further screening; (3) Final regression ESM
model leveraged its sequence sensitivity to identify optimal

holds remarkable promise for accelerating the discovery of effec-

antibiotic-resistant pathogens. Furthermore, the versatility of
FSLSMEP makes it easily adaptable for various functional-peptide

module could be incorporated prior to FSLSMEP. This module
generates manageable-sized candidate libraries of AMPs — such as
one-billion-scale peptides — followed by whole-library screening. By
this approach, similar screening methods enable exploration of
larger peptide libraries.

that protein pre-training models have shown excellent performance in
various downstream tasks, we believe that the hidden information
learned by pre-training models about spatial structure in vast
sequences can be very effective in guiding the model to recognize
effective antibiotic peptide sequences. This information can also be

In the context of model selection for the ranking task, we leverage
protein feature descriptors embodying the physicochemical attributes
of peptides as the model input. The structural framework of the
ranking model is established through the adoption of a multi-layer

utility of traditional structured data modeling in facilitating the dis-

given the computational intricacies associated with ranking models,
the employment of a comparatively parsimonious model structure is
conducive to heightened inference speed, thereby facilitating the
handling of voluminous datasets.
Given the presence of a limited sample size in our overall task,
employing traditional machine learning models is not conducive to
multi-step ﬁne-tuning. Therefore, we opted for a straightforward yet
efﬁcient neural network model as the foundational structure for the
ranking model. The ultimate results from our wet lab experiments also
indicate that employing such a trained ranking model in the pipeline
leads to antimicrobial peptides with overall superior antibacterial
performance against Acinetobacter baumannii.

Antimicrobial activity assays
Brieﬂy, bacteria were cultured overnight in LB at 37 °C, 0.8 g to log
phase. Cells were then collected, washed twice, and suspended in the
assay medium at a density of 5×10
5 CFU/mL. The peptides were dis-

lowed by 2-fold serial dilutions. Then, 50 μL of each peptide solution
was added to 50μL of the bacteria suspension in a 96-well plate. Plates
were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. The MIC was determined as the
minimal peptide concentration at which no visible bacterial growth
was observed.

In vivo experiments
(i) 15 mice were divided randomly into 3 groups. To establish the acute
pneumonia model, mice were injected intraperitoneally with cyclo-
phosphamide 3-d and 1-d before the bacterial administration at the
concentrations of 150 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg, respectively, to induce
neutropenia. Mice were anesthetized by isoﬂurane and infected withA.
baumannii 17978(1 × 10
5 CFU in 100 μL PBS) by tracheal instillation via
a catheter. For the treatments, mice were administered with EME7(7),
polymyxin B, and PBS control, at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL in PBS
via a vibrating mesh nebulizer connected to a whole-body chamber.
The dosage was 2.5 mg peptides for a group of 5 animals per chamber.
Mice were sacriﬁced 24-h post-treatment, the lung, kidney tissues and
serum were collected for analysis. The CFU of A. baumannii per lung
was calculated by plating dilutions (0.25 g tissue in 10 mL PBS) of lung
homogenates on agar plates and counting colonies. The serum was
used for BUN measurement.
(ii) 15 mice were divided randomly into three groups. To establish
the in vivo toxicity study, mice were injected intraperitoneally with
EME7(7), polymyxin B, and PBS at concentrations of 10 mg/kg twice a
day for three days. They were sacri ﬁced 24 h after the last drug
administration. Kidney tissues were collected for further analysis. The
serum was collected for BUN and Cr measurements.
(iii) 15 mice were divided randomly into 3 groups. To establish the
pneumonia model, mice were anesthetized by isoﬂurane and infected
with A. baumannii 17978 (1 × 10
5 CFU in 100 μL PBS) on day 1 by tra-
cheal instillation via a catheter. On days 4, 5, and 6, mice were admi-
nistered EME7(7), polymyxin B, and PBS once daily, at a concentration
of 0.5 mg/mL in PBS via a vibrating mesh nebulizer connected to a
whole-body chamber. The dosage was 2.5 mg peptides for a group of
ﬁve animals per chamber. Mice were sacriﬁced on day 7, and the lungs
were collected for analysis. The CFU of A. baumannii per lung was
calculated by plating dilutions (0.25 g tissue in 10 mL PBS) of lung
homogenates on agar plates and counting colonies. The lungs were
collected for RNA-seq and western blotting analysis.
