climate impacts and impedes strategic carbon sequestration planning.

emissions, presenting a strategic conﬂict with carbon neutrality goals.

a comprehensive, full value-chain assessment of the emission reduc-
tion potential across distinct shale gas reservoir types. Building on this
analysis, an integrated value-chain management model is developed.
By incorporating key technologies such as CO
2 fracturing, CO2 huff-n-
puff, and geological sequestration in depleted wells, this model fun-

The integrated analysis framework reveals a fundamental tension
between maximizing environmental bene ﬁts and maintaining eco-
nomic viability across different emission reduction strategies. Our
analysis identiﬁes a clear trade-off: the HW (handled depleted wells)
and CS (combined strategies) scenarios achieve the most ambitious
environmental outcomes, transforming the shale gas value chain into a
geological carbon sink with negative emissions, but impose substantial
economic penalties due to high CO
2 procurement costs, transporta-
tion logistics, and energy-intensive injection operations. Conversely,
the OM and OP scenarios deliver modest but cost-effective carbon
reductions with favorable economic returns across most reservoir
types, yet fall short of achieving carbon neutrality. This trade-off
exhibits pronounced spatial heterogeneity driven by geological and
infrastructure factors. For marine shale formations in Sichuan Basin,
characterized by superior reservoir quality, high CO
2 adsorption
capacity, and established industrial infrastructure, the HW/CS path-
ways remain economically viable even without carbon pricing support.
However, for terrestrial and transitional facies in basins such as Ordos
and Songliao, the economic feasibility of negative-emission pathways
is critically contingent on robust carbon pricing mechanisms or policy
incentives that internalize the climate externalities (Supplementary
Table 18).
This study, underpinned by its developed multi-dimensional
assessment framework, provides a scientiﬁc methodology for global
shale gas resource holders to evaluate reservoir carbon sink potential
and design commercially viable negative emission pathways. The
proposed integrated strategy for carbon sequestration and enhanced
recovery in shale reservoirs holds potential for global applicability;
however, its successful implementation largely depends on the geo-
logical suitability of target regions, the availability of established CO
2
source-sink matching infrastructure, and a supportive policy land-
scape. While formation-speciﬁc challenges vary across reservoir types,
several fundamental barriers constrain all CO
2 sequestration opera-
tions. During CO 2 sequestration, the injection of CO 2 induces pore
pressure perturbations, altering the mechanical equilibrium of the
reservoir through the effective stress principle. This can potentially
activate critically stressed faults or natural fracture systems, leading to
microseismic events
11,14. Moreover, elevated pore pressure may trigger
the reactivation of caprock fractures or shear failure of wellbores,
forming leakage pathways that result in small-scale leakage
events
10,11,14. However, current monitoring techniques face challenges
in achieving precise early-stage detection and quantitative character-
ization of such small-scale leakage occurring beyond wellbore inter-
faces. Additionally, the challenge to economic feasibility is particularly
severe; the full-chain costs—encompassing CO
2 capture, compression,
and the deployment of extensive corrosion-resistant pipeline networks
to satisfy source-sink matching —pose signi ﬁcant challenges to
achieving proﬁtability in the absence of structural subsidies and car-
bon credit revenue mechanisms8,9. Furthermore, spatial heterogeneity
in geological conditions, infrastructure requirements, and operational
challenges creates uncertainties in both environmental and economic

Techno-economic analysis of emission reduction scenarios
The con ﬁguration of multiple scenarios delineates technological
pathways for decarbonizing the shale gas value chain. Crucially, sys-
tematic quantiﬁcation of the dynamic interactions between regional
resource endowments and technological costs remains imperative.
Through techno-economic analysis, this methodology reveals region-
ally heterogeneous economic viability of emission-reduction technol-
ogies. Consequently, it mitigates potential resource misallocation
arising from uniform policy implementation and establishes spatially
differentiated cost-optimized deployment pathways.
A typical shale gas development project encompasses a 21-year
lifecycle, comprising one year of construction followed by 20 years of
production. Under the baseline scenario, the cash outﬂows during the
initial year primarily arise from construction costs, including expen-
ditures for drilling, hydraulic fracturing, and surface facility engi-
neering. Throughout the production phase, cash in ﬂows originate
from shale gas sales revenue and government subsidies, while outﬂows
consist of continuously incurred business costs and tax costs.
Relative to the baseline scenario, emission reduction scenarios
necessitate distinct cash ﬂow considerations. The OM scenario
employs LiDAR and Optical Gas Imaging technology for LDAR, incur-
ring an incremental cost of approximately US$0.098 m
−362.T h i s
investment, however, generates compensatory revenue through
recovered methane sales by mitigating fugitive emissions. Con-
currently, the OP scenario implements CO
2 fracturing and CO2 huff-n-
puff during Year 1 and Years 13–18, while the HW scenario involves CO2
sequestration in abandoned wells, requiring expenditures for CO 2
procurement and ancillary energy inputs. The CS scenario, integrating
all aforementioned technologies, consequently demands holistic cash
ﬂow modeling that internalizes these interdependent cost-bene ﬁt
structures. To evaluate economic viability across different shale gas
reservoirs, we conduct policy sensitivity analyses incorporating vari-
able carbon credit revenues.
