interannual variability in global weather and climate. Over recent decades, the
amplitude of the lower-stratospheric QBO has weakened, and in recent years
the QBO has experienced unprecedented disruptions. However, the longer-

uncertain. Here, based on CMIP6 models with extended projections beyond
2100 and targeted sensitivity experiments, we show that under ongoing global
warming, the QBO exhibits a progressively weaker amplitude and shorter
period, eventually risking complete disappearance. This is projected to reduce

The quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO), features alternating westerly and
easterly zonal winds in the tropical stratosphere, propagating down-
ward with a ~ 28-month period, which primarily driven by a mix of
Rossby gravity waves, Kelvin waves, and other gravity waves of varying
scales
1–3. Although it originates in the tropical stratosphere, the QBO
signiﬁcantly in ﬂuences both tropical and extratropical atmospheric
circulation, thereby impacting tropospheric weather and climate4–20.
However, of late, the lower stratospheric QBO has become less
stable. In recent decades, while the amplitude of the QBO in the middle
stratosphere (10–30 hPa) has increased, the amplitude in the lower
stratosphere (50–70 hPa) has weakened
21. Furthermore, the disruption

Although previous studies suggest that global warming may
inﬂuence the amplitude of the QBO, the longer-term evolution of the
QBO under sustained warming remains unknown. Understanding the
evolution of the QBO and its impact on the troposphere is critical for
improving longer-term forecasting.
Here, we show that under sustained global warming, the QBO
undergoes a progressive reduction in amplitude and a gradual short-
ening of its period, driven by intensi ﬁed tropical upwelling and
enhanced wave activity, and is likely to ultimately disappear. These
alterations dampen the tropospheric QBO signal and consequently
reduce the predictability of tropospheric climate forecasts on 2–3-year
timescales.

Results
The disappearance of the QBO signal in the lower stratosphere
To examine the longer-term evolution of the QBO, we ﬁrst analyze
extended projections of the lower-stratospheric QBO using outputs
from four selected CMIP6 models (CESM2-WACCM, EC-Earth3-Veg,
IPSL-CM6A-LR and MRI-ESM2-0; See Methods). Under the extended
SSP5-8.5 scenario, weﬁnd that both the westerly and easterly at 50 hPa
simulated by the four models gradually weaken, and eventually stabi-
lize (Fig. 1a). Similarly, these models project a pronounced weakening
of the QBO amplitude under extended SSP5-8.5, followed by a plateau,
albeit with slight differences in the timing of the transition (Fig. 1b).
Notably, CESM2-WACCM shows a comparatively modest reduction in
amplitude relative to the other models, with values remaining within
4–7ms ⁻¹. This suggests a signi ﬁcant shift in the tropical lower stra-
tospheric circulation and the state of the QBO.
Modulations of the tropical lower stratospheric circulation
would alter the periodicity of the QBO. Wavelet analysis of the QBO
index at 50 hPa (see Methods) reveals that the QBO signal
(16–36 months) in all four models progressively shortens and
eventually disappears (Fig. 1c–f). The QBO disappearance occurs
around 2075, 2150, and 2100 in CESM2-WACCM, EC-Earth3-Veg, and
IPSL-CM6A-LR, respectively, while the MRI-ESM2-0 model exhibits
intermittent disappearance after 2200. The timing of the QBO signal
disappearance closely matches the in ﬂection point of the QBO
amplitude (Fig. 1b). After the disappearance of the QBO, wavelet
analysis still reveals annual and semiannual signals at 50 hPa
(Fig. 1c–f), and its disappearance manifests as a stepwise shortening
of the periodic signals.
As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2a–c, for the CESM2-WACCM, EC-
Earth3-Veg, and IPSL-CM6A-LR models, although the QBO disappears
after the year 2150, the entire stratospheric zonal wind continues to
exhibit alternating easterly and westerly regimes, albeit with a sig-
niﬁcantly shortened period compared to the pre-disappearance phase.
In contrast, the MRI-ESM2-0 model shows little change in the upper
stratospheric zonal wind (10–30 hPa), but the strength of easterly and
westerly in the lower stratosphere (50 hPa) markedly weakens (Sup-
plementary Fig. 2d).
It is found that the upper-level QBO disappears in CESM2-
WACCM, EC-Earth3-Veg, and IPSL-CM6A-LR; however, signals at
annual and semiannual periods persist after the QBO disappears
(Supplementary Fig. 3a –c, 3e –g). This indicates that following the
disappearance of the QBO, the tropical stratospheric zonal wind con-
tinues to exhibit alternating easterly and westerly regimes, but with a
shortened period of one year or half a year. Although the MRI-ESM2-0
model maintains the quasi-biennial signal at high levels, the power of
this signal gradually weakens after approximately 2200, suggesting
that the upper-level QBO in MRI-ESM2-0 may also disappear over
longer timescales (Supplementary Fig. 3d, 3h). The persistence of the
upper-level QBO in MRI-ESM2-0 during the current simulation period
may be attributed to its relatively weaker increase in wave forcing
under global warming compared to other models. We will discuss this
aspect in detail later.
The carbon dioxide (CO₂) increasing sensitivity experiments (see
Methods) show that the QBO begins to disappear when CO ₂ con-
centration reaches 6 or 8 times the pre-industrial level (Supplementary
Fig. 4a –h). Supplementary Fig. 4i –j further show that the QBO also
disappears when atmospheric CO₂ is held at the pre-industrial level,
while sea-surface temperatures are warmed to those induced under six
times CO₂. Together, these results suggest that QBO disappearance
under global warming is not driven by a direct CO₂ effect on the QBO,
but instead is associated with a cascade of responses to CO₂-induced
sea-surface warming.
In contrast, simulations under the extended SSP1-2.6 scenario
show no signi ﬁcant trends in either zonal wind variability or QBO
amplitude within the tropical lower stratosphere, with the quasi-
biennial signal maintaining coherence throughout the simulation
period (Supplementary Fig. 3i –p, Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supple-
mentary Fig. 6).

It is worth noting that, under the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario, the
simulated enhancements of the resolved wave (Fig. 2d), gravity wave
forcing (Fig. 2e) and residual vertical velocity (Fig. 2a), are much
weaker in the MRI-ESM2-0 model than in CESM2-WACCM. This dif-
ference likely explains why the upper-level QBO persists longer and the
lower-level QBO disappears more gradually in the MRI-ESM2-0.
Sensitivity experiments with increasing CO ₂ concentrations
similarly indicate little change in meridional advection forcing,
whereas vertical advection westerly forcing and both resolved wave
forcing and gravity wave forcing —including their respective easterly
and westerly components —increase with rising CO ₂ levels (Supple-
mentary Fig. 7b–e). The net effect of the momentum budget indicates
that the downward propagation of both the westerly and easterly
phases of the QBO is accelerated, thereby shortening the QBO period.
Using a one-dimensional QBO model (see Methods), we further
demonstrate the role of enhanced wave forcing in the disappearance
of the QBO. In simulations with gradually intensiﬁed wave forcing, the
QBO period shortens in a stepwise manner
37, exhibiting phase locking
at 24-month and 12-month periods before ultimately converging to
~6 months (Fig. 2f). This behavior provides a mechanistic explanation
for how warming-induced strengthening of wave forcing would leads
to a progressive shortening of the QBO period and, once the QBO
disappears, to a regime in which annual and semiannual periodicities
dominate the zonal wind variability (Fig. 1c–f). Moreover, the phase-
locking behavior becomes more pronounced under stronger tropical
upwelling (red triangles vs. yellow circles in Fig. 2f), explaining the
persistent 12-month locking of the QBO period under enhanced tro-
pical upwelling in the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 1c–f). These
results suggest that under the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario, enhanced
forcing by resolved and gravity waves may contribute to the dis-
appearance of the QBO cycle.
Enhanced stratospheric wave activity is often linked to intensiﬁed
tropospheric convection driven by sea surface temperature increases.
Diagnostics of convection under the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario reveal
ac o n t i n u o u si n t e n s iﬁcation of convective activity in the tropical
central-eastern Paciﬁc which is indicative of enhanced wave generation
(Supplementary Fig. 9a –d). It is also worth noting that changes in
lower-stratospheric winds under the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario may
modify the ﬁltering of waves propagating upward from the tropo-
sphere into the stratosphere, ther eby further contributing to the
apparent increase in stratospheric wave activity.
Using wavenumber–frequency spectra to diagnose changes in
individual equatorial wave types (see Methods), weﬁnd that under the
extended SSP5-8.5 scenario the strengthened resolved waves are pri-
marily associated with the symmetric components —Kelvin waves
38,
inertio-gravity waves, and equatorial Rossby waves (Supplementary
Fig. 10; Supplementary Fig. 11). In contrast, the antisymmetric com-
ponents, including mixed Rossby –gravity waves and eastward-
propagating inertio-gravity waves, exhibit a clear weakening (Supple-
mentary Fig. 12; Supplementary Fig. 13). The contrasting behavior
between symmetric and antisymmetric wave components may reﬂect
the tendency for tropical convection to become more spatially con-
centrated under global warming, thereby modifying the excitation and
structure of different equatorial wave modes
38–40.
In contrast to the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario, the extended SSP1-
2.6 scenario shows no discernible long-term trend in the residual
vertical velocity in the lower stratosphere (Supplementary Fig. 14a).
Consistent with this, the momentum budget also exhibits no sig-
niﬁcant long-term trend (Supplementary Fig. 14b–e). This is associated
with weaker changes in tropical convection (Supplementary Fig. 9e–h),
leading to relatively stable wave activity (Supplementary Figs. 15–18).
These features correspond to the persistent presence of the QBO and
its sustained amplitude throughout the extended SSP1-2.6 scenario.

forcing (see Methods), show that the 16–36 month zonal wind signal in
three key regions (M1, M2, and M3; see Methods) is stronger with QBO
forcing than without (Fig.4a–c). The SNR of the zonal-mean zonal wind
is consistently higher across all periods in these regions with QBO
forcing than without (Fig. 4d–f). Note that, the weak trend-like ﬂuc-
tuations in SNR are most plausibly attributable to internal variability
rather than to a forced response. In addition, in the sensitivity
experiment with QBO forcing, the ﬁrst EOF mode differs markedly
from that without QBO forcing (Fig. 4g, h). The ﬁrst EOF mode of
CESM2-WACCM simulations under the extended SSP5-8.5 scenario
during the ﬁrst 50 years with QBO exist (Fig. 4i) closely resembles the
corresponding sensitivity experiment results (Fig.4g). During the last
50 years with QBO disappearance (Fig.4j), it also closely resembles the
corresponding sensitivity experiment results (Fig. 4h). When strato-
spheric signals are removed before calculating the EOF, this ﬁrst EOF
mode shift remains (Supplementary Fig. 25), indicating a signi ﬁcant
phase change in the tropospheric 16–36 months zonal wind mode.
Here we show that, under a high-emission scenario, the QBO may
vanish between 2075 and 2200, with potentially profound implications
for short-term climate predictability in the troposphere. It calls for
global mitigation efforts toward a development that is compatible with
the 2 °C target of Paris Agreement. In addition, this raises an open
question of whether the disappearance of the QBO under extended
SSP5-8.5 scenario constitutes a climate tipping point, an issue that
merits further investigation.

a given dataset. A higher SNR indicates a clearer signal, which makes it
easier to detect and predict. In this study, the 16–36 months periodic
signal in the regional zonal wind is considered the signal, while the
other frequency bands are treated as noise. The sliding 50-year SNR is
calculated with a sliding step of 1 month. The value at the midpoint of a
50-year sliding window is calculated based on data within that 50-year
window.
