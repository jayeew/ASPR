Archean ocean marine primary productivity may have been limited by biologically available nitrogen.
Due to low molybdenum abundances, early biological nitrogen ﬁxation is thought to have relied on
alternative nitrogenases that incorporate vanadium or iron instead of molybdenum. Here, we examine
nitrogen ﬁxation in a Cyanobacteria-dominated, ferruginous, low-sulfate, low-molybdenum lake,
which replicates biological and chemical conditions relevant to early marine primary productivity.
Nitrogen ﬁxation occurs even when molybdenum is <1 nM, 100x less than the abundance in modern
oceans. Molybdenum additions did not increase nitrogen ﬁxation rates, indicating that diazotrophs
were not molybdenum limited. Only the molybdenum-iron nitrogenase was detected in metagenomes
and metatranscriptomes, indicating that the alternative nitrogenases were not required. We suggest
that low sulfate (<1 μM) and/or ef ﬁcient uptake mitigated molybdenum limitation. These results
indicate that molybdenum bioavailability may be strongly controlled by sulfate and that alternative
nitrogenases are not essential for nitrogen ﬁxation at low molybdenum.
Nitrogen (N) is a biologically essential element because it is a key component
of proteins and nucleic acids. Biologically available (“ﬁxed”) inorganic forms
of N, such as ammonium and nitrate, are often scarce in terrestrial and
aquatic systems, making N a limiting nutrient for primary productivity
1.
During the Archean Eon (4 − 2.5 billion years ago; Gyr), the ﬁxed N ﬂux
from meteoroid impacts (>2 ×107 molecules cm−2 s−1) and lightning strikes
(2.4 ×105− 6.5 ×108 molecules cm−2 s−1)2,3 was likely insufﬁcient to support
more than a small early biosphere4. Therefore, it is often suggested that N2
ﬁxation was important in sustainingearly marine primary production5.
Biological N 2 ﬁxation in extant diazotrophs is catalyzed by the
metallocluster protein complex ‘nitrogenase’. Nitrogenase enzyme metal-
loclusters contain iron (Fe) and either molybdenum (Mo), vanadium (V), or
only Fe, to form the Mo-Fe, V-Fe, or Fe-Fe nitrogenases6.T h enifHDK genes
that produce the Mo-Fe metallocluster likely evolvedﬁrst7, and are present
in extant methanogens that appear to be the earliest diazotrophs8.

genated oceans (~100 nM), where it is predominantly present as the
molybdate ion (MoO

Conclusions
Our discovery that there is no activit y of alternative nitrogenases in the
dominant cyanobacterium diazotrophic community in Deming Lake even
when N
2 ﬁxation proceeds at Mo <1 nM and sulfate≤ 1 μM, is critical for
our understanding of the evolution of metal use by nitrogenase. If Mo-
Fe nitrogenase can dominate N2 ﬁxation even when Mo <1 nM, then Mo-
free alternative nitrogenases would have conferred no advantage to early
diazotrophs. This conclusion is consistent with the absence of N isotope
evidence for alternative nitrogenases in Archean sedimentary rocks
5 and
data indicating that the Mo nitrogenase is ancestral to all nitrogenases 7.
Inferences from geobiological, molecular, and ﬁeld evidence converge to
form a picture that despite low Mo in the Archean, the alternative Fe-Fe or
V-Fe nitrogenases were not present or utilized.
The hypothesis that sulfate availability plays a key role in modulating
Mo limitation has additional evolutionary implications for metal use in
nitrogenase. Archean ocean Mo is estimated to have been <5 nM (Fig. 4)
increasing above 5 nM in the Proterozoic
10. In the late Archean, seawater
sulfate was between 50 to 200 µM18 on average but could have been as low as
1–5 µM locally with high spatial and temporal variability 19,p o t e n t i a l l y
spiking to mM values in the late Archean41. During the Proterozoic, seawater
sulfate concentrations are estimated at 100 to 400μM18 but could have been
as high as 1–2m M42.I nt h i sc o n t e x t ,r i s i n gs u l f a t el e v e l sc o u l dh a v ei n d u c e d

help account for an emerging picture that the alternative nitrogenases

also regulate microbial N uptake44, suggesting a larger role for rising sulfate

parameter (1e-5). MEGAN software was utilized to apply Lowest Common
Ancestor to determine species annotation for assembled transcripts
