The Willis coupling vectors are recently discovered material properties that enable acousticﬂuids with
broken time reversal symmetry, reciprocity, and energy conservation. The time modulation of material
properties has been identi ﬁed as another approach to break similar taboos in wave dynamics.
Combining both mechanisms promises unparalleled new freedom to manipulate waves, but the
signiﬁcant challenge of physically realizing bulk materials with time-modulated Willis vectors has left
the opportunity unexplored. Here, we introduce an active metamaterial of two-dimensional geometry
that can be programmed for desired Willis vector magnitude and direction, and modulated very quickly
in time. To highlight the newfound possibilities for wave manipulation, we experimentally demonstrate
a nonreciprocal directional ﬁlter and a steerable beamforming shell. Through time modulation of the
Willis vectors, the operating direction of these devices is changed at sonic speeds. The results
illustrate a small fraction of the potential applications for time-varying bulk Willis media.
Willis coupling of the momentum and strain in composite metamaterials
arises from unit cell asymmetry and phase change across the cell width1–4,
typically with a small effect on the acoustic response. The unit cell design can
be optimized for signiﬁcant asymmetric reﬂection and wavefront shaping5–8,
but the values that the Willis parameters may take are fundamentally
constrained by passivity and reciprocity (transmission between two points
must be symmetric). Active metamateri als with sensor-driver pairs that
produce a programmed acoustic response to the local ﬁeld are a far more
ﬂexible strategy for realizing Willis media. They are founded in a polarized
source model of acoustic metamaterials in which the monopole and dipole
scattering of a unit cell is proportio nal to the local acoustic pressure and
particle velocity
2,9–11.
Active metamaterials have been e mployed to showcase a variety of
applications of Willis coupling, including broadband sound isolation12,l a r g e
nonreciprocity13, wave-steering 14, anomalous re ﬂection15,o n e - w a y
ampliﬁcation16, and localized ﬁeld ampliﬁcation17. However, the demon-
strated metamaterials were limited to the form factor of either a row of unit
cells or a metasurface, with the direction of the Willis vectorﬁxed normal to
the geometry. These constraints greatly simplify both the necessary meta-
material architecture and the analysis of the acoustic behavior, but they
signiﬁcantly limit the potential to manipulate sound.
Here, we design a bulk active metamaterial that can be easily pro-
grammed for desired 2D Willis vector s and employ it to experimentally
demonstrate novel nonreciprocal devices. We additionally modulate the
Willis vector orientation and magnitude in time at frequencies up to twice
the incident wave frequency through the active metamaterial digital pro-
gramming. We primarily leverage th is capability to rapidly change the
direction of the steady-state behavior, but the approach is also suitable for
enacting wave phenomena of recent interest that arise from the time
modulation itself
18–23.

The results demonstrate the effectiveness of the active metamaterial
implementation strategy and motivate the development of more rigorous

ease with which the acoustic parameters can be varied in time and space

wave physics.
Methods
Unit cell design
The unit cells are composed of two custom PCBs and a glued 3D printed
frame with embedded magnets for mounting in the waveguide. Their
dimension in a square lattice is 5.2 cm. The upper PCB that interfaces with
the waveguide interior supports four bottom port I2S MEMS microphones
(Knowles, SPH0645LM4H-B) and four speakers (Soberton, SP-1704). The
lower PCB supports a microcontroller (PJRC, Teensy 4.0) and four I2S
ampliﬁers (Sparkfun Electronics, MAX98357A). The unit cells are indivi-
dually powered and programmed t hrough USB connection with the
microcontroller. The digital signal processing occurs at a sampling rate of 96
kHz. Variations in the speakers from the manufacturer and inconsistencies
in their mounting on the PCBs can result in a distribution of cell responses.
With a metamaterial of many cells, these errors should mostly average out,
but the cells can be individually calibrated to adjust the gains. Further details
concerning the signal processing can be found in previous work
24.
Experimental ﬁeld measurement
The 2D waveguide consists of square meter parallel polycarbonate and glass
plates spaced 6 cm apart. Cardboard tube quarters af ﬁxed to the edges
decrease re ﬂections from the change in impedance at the outlets. A
microphone within the waveguide is magnetically mounted to a stage on an
orthogonal pair of motorized rails. A single external speaker was used for the
slab demonstrations, while two back-to-back speakers of opposite phase
were used for the shell demonstrations. A National Instruments data
acquisition system was used to command the external speakers, send an
electrical trigger signal to set the metamaterial as active or inactive, and read
the microphone measurements. When the cells are active, and there is no
acoustic wave to excite them, they are nearly silent. The speaker commands
were trapezoidal pulses with two periods of ramping. The microphone was
scanned across a square 60 cm area withstep size of 2 cm. At each location, 5
samples were taken and averaged with the metamaterial inactive (back-
ground ﬁeld) and 5 with the metamaterial active (totalﬁeld). The scattered
ﬁeld was calculated as the difference between these two sets of measure-
ments. As a result, the scatteredﬁelds are purely the active response of the
metamaterial, and any passive effects of the structure, such as leakage from
the mounting, are included in the backgroundﬁeld. For the slab 2D plots,
the ﬁelds are shown at the frequencyf
0 using a time windowed FFT. For the
experimental shell 2D plots, theﬁelds are shown at an instant in time with a
low-pass ﬁlter applied to remove noise. The square grids of measurements
were interpolated in a 15 cm radius circle around the metamaterial for the
time-dependent plots. The frequency spectra were computed with the FFT
windowed to the time span of nonzero background ﬁeld at the circle. All
plots were normalized by the backgroundﬁeld pressure at the center of the
ﬁeld for the slab and on the dotted circle for the shell.
Simulation
Simulated frequency domain results were computed using a polarized
source method10 extended for Willis media (see Supplementary Note 1).
These simulations are an ideal model of the active metamaterial steady-state
behavior. The ﬁelds were validated through ﬁnite element method (FEM)
simulations of the equivalent continuous media with macroscopic acoustic
parameters using COMSOL Multiphysics11.
