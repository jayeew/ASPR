The shelf areas of the Arctic Ocean host some of the world’s most productive marine ecosystems, yet
their under-ice early-season phenology and inter-trophic dynamics remain poorly understood amid
rapid climate change. Addressing these knowledge gaps, we assembled a data-driven biophysical
model integrating high-resolution ocean physics with biological dynamics across ice algae ( Nitzschia
frigida), Arctic copepods ( Calanus glacialis), and ﬁsh larvae ( Boreogadus saida, reliant on C. glacialis
nauplii). The model accurately recreated observed spatio-temporal production and recruitment
patterns and revealed how the northern Barents Sea’s unique geography and ocean-climate facilitate
key habitat and phenological synchrony, yielding large-scale biological export across the bio-region.
Consequently, these geographically linked multi-trophic adaptations appear highly vulnerable to
climate-forced shifts— e.g., proportion of C. glacialis in zooplankton is reduced up to 26% per 1 °C
increase— as open-water area rises and ice-associated production and habitat degrades. Ourﬁndings
thus provide timely insights for anticipating ecosystem disruptions from ongoing ice loss.

Discussion
Here we summarize the emergent properties of our data-driven simulation
system and discuss the spatiotemporal correlations/relationships among the
trophic levels of the Arctic shelf pelagic production system in the transition
from winter dormancy to Arctic dawn. Qualitatively, we can pinpoint the
start of the ice algae growth season to early March, with exponentially
increasing growth rates towards end of May, and fully“saturated” growth
rates under the ice towards late June. Thus given the best available physical
forcings, we were able to recreate key observations of the under ice species
assemblage in the northern Barents Sea, namely with relatively high
abundance of C. glacialis eggs, pre-feeding, and grazing nauplii already
spawned in March, and elevated growth rates of algae underneath the ice in
May
9,11,34. The modelled temporal succession ofC. glacialis egg and nauplii
stages in early spring also aligned to a high degree with the requirements of
the ﬁrst feeding stages of the polar cod; although the long-tailed egg pro-
duction season of C. glacialis into late summer must be interpreted with
caution due to the lack of dynamical predation pressure on adult females in

Although the maximum ice extent was relatively static across the study
period, a longer term trend in north wards displacement of Arctic water
masses is evident both in the northernBarents Sea and the Arctic Ocean in
general23,25,43. This displacement of ecologically important nursery habitat
characterized by a sub-freezing surface layer and a seasonal ice cover have
already most likely caused a signiﬁcant shift in C. glacialis and polar cod
distributions in the Arctic Ocean16,44. How the phenology of the Arctic shelf
production system will be effected byfurther habitat degradation and dis-

scenarios have predicted relatively warmer and more ice free areas in the
northern Barents Sea with respect to wide range of warming scenarios (2.6,

The ﬁrst layer of the model was the physical drivers such as solar

1280 × 580 polar stereographic grid, with 32 vertical sigma layers and an

In the individual-based model for polar cod, the duration of egg incu-

the hydrodynamic model 74. The vertical distribution of eggs and non-

the polar cod IBM python codebase in “Code availability ”). Post-
hatching larval growth was temperature-dependent, starting from an
initial length of 7 mm and continuing through to juvenile metamor-
phosis, with parameters derived from
76. For feeding larval stages, vertical
positioning was primarily driven by turbulent mixing, but individuals
actively swam at approximately 0.3 body lengths per second either
toward or away from the surface depending on whether they were above
or below the 1 W m
−2 light isolume. The positive buoyancy of eggs and
pre-feeding larvae resulted in a pronounced near-surface concentration
throughout much of the winter/spring, with only the larger larvae of the
distribution occasionally crossing the pycnocline — typically not until
the post- ﬂexion stage (>25 mm), when enhanced swimming strength
allowed larvae to counter turbulent forces. This deeper positioning down

dominance of polar cod larvae in the upper 0 –30 m layer during BESS
sampling

To select the most realistic drift/lifehistory trajectories contrived by the
polar cod model we compared modeled drift end points to YOY polar cod
observations in the BESS. This was achieved by releasing particles (polar cod
eggs) at all ocean grid points on the Barents Sea shelf shallower than 400 m
that had a modelled ice cover above 0.15 in the time period between
December 1st to September 9th. Then, when approaching the drift end
point, each individual ’s “success” was evaluated as a function of: (1)
proximity to YOY observation in the BESS; (2) count of YOY in the sample;
and (3) similarity between modelled individual size and BESS sampled mean
size. The release points and corresponding “success factor” were then
aggregated across years to visualize the most probable spawning area.
