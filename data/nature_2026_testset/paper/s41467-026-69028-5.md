Evidence shows that local functional trait composition and diversity along the
fast– slow leaf economics spectrum can predict the temporal stability of
community productivity in response to environmental changes. However, it
remains unclear whether these relationships persist at larger spatial scales.
Combining a ﬁeld survey of plant diversity with remote sensing estimates of
primary productivity across a large environmental gradient in 235 grasslands
across the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau andt h eI n n e rM o n g o l i aP l a t e a u ,w eﬁnd that
species richness contributes to stabilizing productivity, while functional
diversity contributes to destabilizing productivity at the local scale. In con-
trast, we ﬁnd no evidence that variation in species richness and functional
diversity among local communities contributes to stabilizing productivity
across larger spatial scales. While the relationships between environmental
conditions and local diversity and stability differ between the two regions, the
overall positive relationships between diversity and stability are consistent at
both the local and larger spatial scales. Our study offers insights into how
functional traits along the fast-slow leaf economics spectrum mediate the
effects of environmental factors (e.g., precipitation) on ecosystem stability at
contrasting spatial scales.
Biodiversity loss, driven by climate change and human activities, is
altering ecosystem functioning and diminishing the stable provision-
ing of essential ecosystem services1,2. There is a general consensus in
grassland ecosystems that local species richness beneﬁts community
stability— the temporal stability of community biomass productivity3– 9
(hereafter ‘community stability’). Beyond species richness, the trade-
offs in functional traits along the fast – slow leaf economics spectrum
have been shown to predict community stability10,11 through two pri-
mary mechanisms12. First, resource acquisition strategies13,14 determine
species’ responses to environmental changes, with fast species exhi-
biting faster recovery but lower resistance and slow species having
higher resistance but slower recovery
10,13,15.I nt h i sc o n t e x t ,w h i l e
communities dominated by fast species can exhibit high community
stability due to high resilience, communities dominated by slow spe-
cies can also exhibit high community stability due to high resistance10,11
(Fig. 1a, c). A second mechanism is based on compensatory dynamics
that result from trait (di)similarity: species can either respond asyn-
chronously to environmental ﬂuctuations when they differ in their
functional traits (Fig. 1c), or display negative covariance due to com-
petition if they have similar traits (Fig. 1a). Therefore, communities
with high functional diversity in fast – slow traits are likely to show
greater stability due to compensatory dynamics, while communities
with low functional diversity may also be stabilized due to negative
covariance
10,12,15.

Pathways through which environmental factors affect stability
across scales and regions
Our multi-group SEM further con ﬁrmed that alpha diversity (both
alpha richness and functional diversity) consistently contributed to
alpha stability in both regions, while beta diversity (both beta richness
and functional diversity) did not contribute to spatial asynchrony
(Fig. 4). Speci ﬁcally, the relationships between alpha diversity and
alpha stability were robust across regions: alpha stability was positively
related to alpha richness, negatively related to local functional diver-
sity, and unrelated to the dominance of fast-growing species, as sum-
marized by CWM PC1. Despite the negative relationships between
alpha functional diversity and alpha stability, the overall relationships
between alpha diversity and alpha stability remain positive. In contrast,
the pathways through which environmental factors inﬂuenced stability
at the two spatial scales differed between the two regions. At the local
scale, environmental factors in ﬂuenced alpha stability mostly indir-
ectly via alpha richness and functional diversity, but they in ﬂuenced
spatial asynchrony directly at the larger spatial scale.
Speciﬁcally, at the IMP, mean annual precipitation (MAP) and soil
fertility inﬂuenced alpha stability indirectly by increasing alpha richness
and alpha functional diversity, while MAP also had a direct effect on
alpha stability (Fig.4a and Table S1). These combined direct and indirect
pathways resulted in an overall positive relationship between both MAP
and soil fertility with alpha stability (Fig.4c). At the larger spatial scale,
higher MAP directly promoted spatial asynchrony without inﬂuencing
beta richness or beta RaoQ. Although a higher mean annual tempera-
ture (MAT) increased beta RaoQ, it did not further in ﬂuence spatial
asynchrony. Overall, MAP emerged as the strongest driver of both alpha
stability and spatial asynchrony, mainly through a direct positive effect
(Fig. 4c). Neither precipitation nor temperature variability contributed
to alpha stability or spatial asynchrony (Fig. S1a).
Unlike the IMP, the direct positive contribution of MAP on alpha
stability was not signi ﬁcant at the QTP (Fig. 4b). The previously
reported positive effect of MAT on beta RaoQ was not observed, while
a new negative effect on beta richness emerged. Alpha richness was the
strongest driver of alpha stability, while MAP had the greatest in ﬂu-
ence on spatial asynchrony; both primarily through direct, positive
effects (Fig. 4c). Additionally, increased precipitation variability
reduced both alpha richness and alpha RaoQ, resulting in lower overall
alpha stability (Fig. S1b).

At the larger spatial scale, no relationship was detected between
beta richness or functional diversity and spatial asynchrony in either
region. Although spatial asynchrony varied more at the QTP than at the
IMP (Fig. 3d, e), no relationship was found even when analyses were
restricted to the same ranges of temperature and precipitation in both
regions (Fig. S4). There are a few factors that may have inﬂuenced the
observed patterns. First, the lack of a relationship between beta
diversity and spatial asynchrony could be due to the relatively homo-
geneous environments
28,31,49. In contrast, environments with larger
abiotic heterogeneity have shown positive relationships between beta
richness and spatial asynchrony17,45, as well as between beta functional
diversity and spatial asynchrony 33,45. Hautier et al. 27 found that beta
diversity was positively associated with spatial asynchrony in a globally
distributed network of natural grassland communities, but this rela-
tionship was not observed following fertilization, which reduced
abiotic heterogeneity. Second, unmeasured functional traits may also
help explain the neutral relationship between beta diversity and spatial
asynchrony. For instance, belowground traits, particularly those rela-
ted to ﬁne roots, may have disproportionate effects compared to
aboveground traits, as species with similar ﬁne-root syndromes can
exhibit contrasting aboveground traits
50. In precipitation-driven sys-
tems, belowground traits (e.g., root depth) can respond to drought at
rates and magnitudes that differ markedly from those of aboveground
traits such as SLA. This response diversity across heterogeneous
environments can enhance spatial asynchrony among communities,
thereby strengthening the relationship between beta functional
diversity and ecosystem stability. However, such belowground traits
were not included in this study and remain underrepresented in large-
scale assessments of ecosystem stability.
Third, the relatively short distance between our subplots could
have led to high similarity in species composition, potentially limiting
the detection of strong beta diversity and spatial asynchrony patterns.
Synthesis studies have shown that seed dispersal distances for herbs
and graminoids can reach up to several hundred meters

steep decline in dispersal likelihood probability as distance increases51.
Given that the centers of our subplots were 50 m apart, this may
represent a relatively small spatial scale compared with the dispersal
capabilities of many species. Finally, the scale differences between
ﬁeld and remote sensing data may also in ﬂuence the observed rela-
tionships between beta diversity and spatial asynchrony. Although
previous studies have demonstrated that such mismatches do not
necessarily compromise results

smaller (0.5 m × 0.5 m) than the remote-sensing pixels (30 m × 30 m),
which may amplify scale mismatch because ﬁner-scale ﬁeld measure-

Higher MAP increased alpha stability in both regions, but through
contrasting pathways. At the IMP, precipitation increased alpha sta-
bility both directly and indirectly by increasing alpha diversity. In
contrast, its direct effect was not observed in the QTP, weakening the
positive relationship with alpha stability (Fig.4c). This again highlights
precipitation as a dominant factor in the IMP, whereas at the QTP, the
direct effect of precipitation may have been masked by the inﬂuence of
alpha diversity. Although higher MAP and temperature favored the
dominance of fast-growing species, this did not translate into higher
alpha stability, in contrast to the partial regression plots, where fast-
growing species dominance was positively linked to alpha stability
(Fig. 3b). Instead, greater precipitation led to reduced alpha stability,
mediated by increased alpha functional diversity. This suggests that
while dominant species’traits may contribute to stability, their effect
was overshadowed by the stronger, negative inﬂuence of alpha func-
tional diversity. This negative relationship may stem from low func-
tional diversity promoting stability through negative covariance
among similar, competing species, whereas high functional diversity
may reduce stability due to a lack of functional redundancy.

and functional diversity contribute to alpha stability, they show no
relationship with spatial asynchrony. The inﬂuence of environmental
conditions on diversity and alpha stability differs between regions, yet
the relationship between alpha diversity and stability is universally
consistent. This suggests that management strategies aimed at

Methods
Study sites and sampling
We surveyed 43 grassland sites in 2021 and 201 sites in 2022 across the
QTP (93 sites) and the IMP (151 sites), China, during the peak growing
season (July and August). Our sites span a latitudinal gradient from
26.98°N to 50.15°N and a longitudinal gradient from 79.03°E to
121.50°E (235 sites in total after removing 9 sites; See below for details.
Fig. 2a), with elevation ranging from 406.8 to 4957.0 m, and MAT and
MAP ranging from −4.6 to 10.3 °C and from 2.7 to 1338.5 mm in the
period between 2013 and 2022 (Fig. 2b). All sampling sites were loca-
ted away from settlements, roads, and showed no signs of livestock
activity to minimize the impact of human disturbance and ongoing
grazing. At each site, a 50 m × 50 m plot was established, and 4 sub-
plots of 0.5 m × 0.5 m in size were selected at the four corners of the
plot (Fig. 2c). We recorded the latitude, longitude, and elevation of
each subplot, and sampled species richness and aboveground biomass
of the 4 subplots. Aboveground biomass was collected by harvesting
all vegetation at ground level within each of the four systematically
placed 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats per site. The harvested biomass was
then sorted by species, dried at 65 °C for 72 h, and the species-level
biomass was recorded.
Our study shows that 10.2% of the total species pool is shared
between the two regions. We measured SLA for plant species whose
accumulative biomass accounted for an average of 89.8% of the total
biomass across all sites at the QTP and the IMP (Fig. S5a), ensuring that
our functional traits re ﬂect the dominant biomass-contributing spe-
cies. We sampledﬁve healthy, mature leaves from up toﬁve randomly
chosen individuals per species and site. Leaves were not detached
from the plants in the ﬁeld until just before scanning. Before that,
individual plants were temporarily stored in moist paper envelopes,
which were then placed in sealed plastic bags to retain humidity. All
samples were kept in a cooler with ice packs immediately after col-
lection and transported to the lab
53. We scanned the leaves on the day
of collection (within ~12 h) and measured leaf area from the scanned
images using the software Digimizer (v.4.6.1; MedCalc Software Ltd.,
Ostend, Belgium), then dried the leaves at 65 °C for 72 h and measured
the dry mass to a resolution of 0.0001 g. SLA was calculated as leaf area
divided by its dry mass (m
2 kg−1)53. We also measured leaf N and leaf P at
each site using the same leaves from SLA measurement in 72% of sites,
subject to sample availability and species selection feasibility (the
species for which we measured leaf nitrogen and phosphorus content
accounted for an average of 81.5% of the total aboveground biomass
across all sites; Fig. S5b). Leaf nitrogen content was measured by an
elemental analyzer (Elementar vario MACRO cube, Germany). Leaf
phosphorus content was measured using molybdenum-antimony
resistance colorimetry with acid digestion on a Chemistry Analyzer
(Smart Chem 450 Automatic Discrete Chemistry Analyzer, Smart
Chem Technologies Ltd, China).
At each site, we collected six soil cores (3.8 cm in diameter and
15 cm in depth), combined them, and analyzed at the laboratory for
their soil total carbon (STC; mg kg
-1), total nitrogen (STN; mg kg-1), and
total phosphorus (STP; mg kg-1). STN and STC were measured using an
elemental analyzer (Elementar vario MACRO cube, Germany). STP was
determined by acid digestion (molybdenum-antimony resistance
colorimetry) using Smart Chem 450 Automatic Discrete Chemistry
Analyzer (SmartChem Technologies Ltd, China).
Satellite-based temporal stability of productivity and climate
We used the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) as a
proxy for plant aboveground productivity. We also conducted the
same analyses using NIRvP (Near-Infrared Re ﬂectance of vegeta-
tion × photosynthetically active radiation), which yielded qualita-
tively similar results (Supplementary Methods 1). Therefore, we

metacommunity level. The community-level alpha richness for each
site was the average species richness across the four subplots. We
used the total number of species found in the four subplots as the
gamma richness of each site, and beta richness was calculated by
multiplicative partitioning
12,59.

Functional trait composition and diversity, and soil fertility
For the calculation of functional diversity, we calculated the mean
trait value of each species across all sites and retained only species
with complete SLA, leaf N, and leaf P data. After this procedure, the
selected species accounted for an average of 94.3% of the total
aboveground biomass per site (Fig. S5c). To quantify the effects of
dominant species ’ traits, we calculated the community weighted
mean (CWM; calculated as the average of species trait values
weighted by their relative biomass in the community) of SLA, leaf N,
and leaf P in each subplot using the dbFD function in the FD
package
61. We used the ﬁrst axis of a PCA of CWM of SLA, leaf N, and
leaf P to represent the fast – slow leaf economics spectrum 10,13.P C A
was performed using the prcomp function in the factoextra
package62. The ﬁrst principal component of the PCA based on
CWM (CWM PC1) captured 57.6% of the total variation among com-
munities, with higher values indicating that communities are domi-
nated by fast-growing species (Fig. S9). We calculated the average
CWM PC1 across the four subplots in a site as CWM values at the
community level. To quantify compensatory dynamics, we calculated
functional diversity as Rao ’s quadratic entropy
63 (RaoQ) at alpha,
beta, and gamma levels of the aggregated SLA, leaf N, and leaf P by
the multiplicative partitioning method using the RaoRel function in
the cati package
64,65. We applied the Euclidean distance to calculate
species dissimilarities with these three traits. We used “unweighted”
species relative abundance, where each species is given equal weight,
and the Jost correction proposed by de Bello et al.
66.
We also used a PCA based on STC, STN, and STP, and took theﬁrst
principal component to represent soil fertility. The PC1 captured 83.2%
of the total variation in soil nutrient concentrations, with a higher value
of PC1 indicating higher soil fertility (Fig. S10).
Statistical analyses
We examined Pearson correlations among environmental variables
(temperature, precipitation, and their variability, and soil fertility),
diversity metrics, and stability indices to assess potential multi-
collinearity. No strong correlations were detected among the candi-
date variables (Pearson correlation coef ﬁcient | r| > 0.7; Fig. S11). We
then constructed SEM with generalized least squares (GLS) regression
models to assess the pathways by which MAT and MAP (or variability of
temperature and precipitation) and soil fertility impact alpha stability
and spatial asynchrony via both alpha (including alpha richness, CWM
PC1, and alpha RaoQ) and beta diversity (including beta richness and
beta RaoQ). First, we built a priori theoretical SEM re ﬂecting hypo-
thesized relationships among all variables (Fig. 1e; Supplementary
Methods 2). Speci ﬁcally, we related alpha richness, CWM PC1, and
alpha RaoQ to alpha stability at the community level, and related beta
richness and beta RaoQ to spatial asynchrony at the metacommunity
level. To account for the potential effect of spatial autocorrelation (the
Moran’s I value for alpha stability and spatial asynchrony was 10.63
(p < 0.001) and 7.35 ( p < 0.001), respectively), we used GLS models
including a rational quadratic spatial correlation structure (corRatio)
after comparing different autocorrelation structures by Akaike ’s
information criterion (AIC) values (Table S2). We included the strongly
correlated errors (e.g., X % ~ ~ % Y) based on the directed separation (d-
sep) approach implied by the initial model to improve model ﬁt. The
goodness-of-ﬁt test of the models was assessed by Fisher ’s C and p
value (p > 0.05).
