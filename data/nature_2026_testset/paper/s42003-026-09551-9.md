The turnover number (kcat) is a key parameter in enzyme kinetics that quantiﬁes catalytic efﬁciency and
underpins mechanistic understanding of enzyme activity. However, existing computational
approaches for k
cat prediction often suffer from limited accuracy and generalization, largely because
most models rely only on substrate information and neglect the role of the full reaction context.
Although TurNuP partially addresses this limitation by encoding entire reactions using binary
ﬁngerprints, its two-stage design separates enzyme and reaction modeling, limiting both predictive
performance and interpretability. Here, we present PMAK, a deep learning framework that integrates
pre-trained representations of enzyme sequences and reaction SMILES with a residue-aware
attention mechanism. By jointly modeling enzymes and reactions, PMAK captures their interactions
and highlights key residues contributing to catalytic activity. Comprehensive evaluations demonstrate
that PMAK consistently outperforms existing methods, achieving average R
2 improvements of
approximately 16.9% and 10.0% over TurNuP under new-reaction and new-enzyme settings,
respectively, in ﬁve-fold cross-validation. Beyond improved accuracy, PMAK provides interpretable
insights into residues associated with enzyme catalysis, offering a robust and informative tool for
enzyme kinetic prediction and related applications.
T h et u r n o v e rn u m b e r(kcat)q u a n t iﬁes the maximum rate at which a single
enzyme molecule converts substrate, reﬂecting its catalytic efﬁciency under
saturating conditions. This parameter is crucial for understanding enzyme
kinetics, stability, and speciﬁcity, enabling comparisons between enzymes
and forming applications inﬁelds such as drug development and industrial
biocatalysis1. However, accurately measuringkcat is challenging and requires
strict control over variables such as substrate concentration, temperature,
and pH, making the process complex, resource intensive, and sensitive to
environmentalﬂuctuations
2. Consequently, obtaining large-scalekcat data
experimentally is difﬁcult, especially in high-throughput settings where time
and resources are limited3.
Given the challenges associated with experimental determination of
kcat, developing efﬁcient and accurate computational prediction methods
has become an important and pressing need 4,5. The early kcat prediction
methods were based primarily on experimental data and metabolic models.
For example, Davidi et al. estimated the values of k
cat for enzymes in
Escherichia coliby utilizingﬂux data and proteomics data under 31 different
growth conditions6. Similarly, Heckmann et al. constructed well-designed
input features based on enzyme active site characteristics, substrate con-
centrations, experimental conditions, and reactionﬂuxes calculated through
ﬂux balance analysis (FBA), using machine learning models to predictk
cat
values for E. coli enzymes 7. However, such methods are limited by their
reliance on extensive experimental data , restricting their applicability in
complex and diverse biological systems, and fail to meet the demands of
large-scale studies on enzymatic reactions. To address this issue, various
high-throughput prediction models based on deep learning and machine
learning have emerged, enabli ng effective prediction of k
cat values for
unknown enzymes without relying on large-scale experimental data. These
models can be broadly classiﬁed into two categories based on the type of
input features: Theﬁrst category uses features from the enzyme and a single
substrate, whereas the second category uses features from both the enzyme
and the reaction it catalyzes.

Discussion
In this paper, we have introduced PMAK, a deep learning model designed to
predict the turnover number (kcat) of enzymes with enhanced accuracy and
generalization. PMAK integrates pre-trained representations and a residue-
aware attention mechanism. The model leverages ProT5-XL-Uniref50 to
encode enzyme sequences and RXNFP to encode reaction SMILES strings,
generating robust representations that mitigate issues associated with lim-
ited training data. The residue-aware attention mechanism further reﬁnes
these representations by identifying key residue features relevant to k
cat
prediction, thereby improving the model’s ability to discern patterns asso-
ciated with catalytic ef ﬁciency. Comprehensive evaluations, including
warm-start and cold-start exp eriments, demonstrate PMAK ’ss u p e r i o r
performance over existing models, with signiﬁcant improvements in pre-
dictive accuracy and generalization to unseen enzymes and reactions.
Despite the advancements offered by PMAK, several limitations must
be acknowledged. Firstly, the model’s performance is contingent upon the
quality and diversity of the pre-trained models used for generating enzyme
and reaction representations. While ProT5-XL-Uniref50 and RXNFP have
been shown to be effective, their training data may not fully capture the
complexity and variability of all enzymatic reactions, potentially limiting the
model’s applicability to certain types o f enzymes or reactions not well-
represented in the training datasets. Additionally, the residue-aware atten-
tion mechanism, while effective in highlighting important residue features,
may not fully account for the intricate interplay between enzyme dynamics
and substrate interactions, which can in ﬂuence catalytic ef ﬁciency. The
model currently focuses on static structural features and may beneﬁtf r o m
incorporating dynamic information, such as molecular dynamics simula-
tions, to better understand the temporal aspects of enzyme catalysis. Lastly,
while the model demonstrates robustness in predictingk
cat values for a wide
range of enzymes and reactions, its performance may still be affected by the
presence of outliers or anomalies in the data, which can skew the predictive
outcomes. Future research could ex plore the integration of anomaly
detection techniques to enhance the model ’s resilience against such data
irregularities.
It is also worth noting that some existingk
cat prediction models, such as
EITLEM15, adopt transfer-learning strategies that pre-train the model on
related kinetic parameters likeKm and kcat/Km. Such approaches can indeed
improve predictive accuracy by leveraging auxiliary biochemical informa-
tion; however, they may also introduce potential sequence overlap between
pretraining and test datasets, leading to arti ﬁcially inﬂated performance
when evaluated on overlapping or hi ghly similar enzymes. In contrast,
PMAK was trained exclusively on experimentally measured k
cat data
without any transfer-learning from other kinetic tasks, thereby ensuring a
fairer assessment of model generalization. Future work could systematically
investigate the effect of incorporating cross-task transfer learning (e.g., from
K
m or kcat/Km datasets) while controlling for sequence redundancy, to better
quantify the true contribution of such strategies to enzyme kinetic modeling.

enhancing the capabilities of PM AK. One promising direction is the

Selection of suitable embedding methods
To determine the best embedding methods for our downstream prediction
tasks, we evaluated different prot ein embedding methods. For enzyme
representations, we compared thre e pre-trained models: ProT5-XL-

are commonly applied in protein-related prediction tasks, whileESM1bts is
an adaptation of the ESM1b model speciﬁcally optimized by Kroll et al. for
enzyme-substrate association prediction25. The amino acid sequences of
proteins are encoded by these pre-trained models, resulting in embedding
matrices with dimensions of 1024 for ProT5 and 1280 for ESM. Further-
more, to highlight the superiority of embeddings obtained through pre-
trained representation learning overthose derived from traditional hand-
crafted methods, we constructed a set of handcrafted features for each
enzyme in the dataset. These features include amino acid composition
(AAC), dipeptide composition (DPC), conjoint triad (CTriad), grouped
amino acid composition (GAAC), grouped dipeptide composition (GDPC),
grouped tripeptide composition (GTPC), composition-based features

ultimately generating a 256-dimensional embedding vector. RXNFP has
been shown to effectively encode complex chemical transformations with-

reagents. The remaining three reactionﬁngerprints are binary representa-

(DRFP), 4096 (str.FP), and 2048 (diff.FP), respectively. Ultimately, based on

Experimental settings of baseline methods
To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison, all baseline methods were
retrained using the original source codes provided by their authors under a
uniﬁed hardware and software environment. All experiments were con-
ducted on a Linux server equippedwith an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. Unless
otherwise stated, each model was trained following the con ﬁgurations
reported in the corresponding original studies. For methods that only
support a single substrate representation (UniKP, CataPro,a n dEITLEM),
we adopted the substrate selection strategy described in CatPred
13:e n t r i e s
containing cofactors were processed by excluding all molecules from a
curated cofactor list, and the remaining non-cofactor molecule was selected
as the substrate. In the rare case where all associated molecules were iden-
tiﬁed as cofactors, the ﬁrst molecule was selected as the substrate.
The speci ﬁct r a i n i n gc o nﬁgurations for each baseline model are
summarized below. UniKP: implemented using an ExtraTrees regressor
with the same hyperparameters as in the original study, with the number of
estimators set to 1000. EITLEM: trained with a batch size of 64 for 100
epochs using a learning rate of 0.001. We faithfully reproduced the core
architectural components of EITLEM, including the ProMolAtt module for
joint enzyme-ligand representation learning and the AttentionAGG mod-
ule for feature aggregation, following the same optimizer and hyperpara-
meter settings described in the original work. However, unlike the original
EITLEM training pipeline, we did not include the transfer learning pre-
training stage based onK
m or kcat/Km data. Therefore, the EITLEM results
reported in this study correspond to a simpliﬁed version trained solely on
kcat data, and should be interpreted as an architectural comparison rather
than a full reproduction of the original EITLEM performance. CatPred:
trained with a batch size of 32 for 30 epochs and a learning rate of 0.0001.
CataPro: trained with a batch size of 8 for 100 epochs and a learning rate of
0.00001. TurNuP: implemented using XGBoost with exactly the same
hyperparameter settings as provided in the ofﬁcial source code.
By retraining all baseline methods under the same computational
environment and dataset preprocessing procedures, we aimed to ensure
consistency in evaluation and to isolate the impact of model architectures
from differences in training data composition or optimization strategies. In
particular, for experiments involving mutant enzyme prediction, this uni-
ﬁed training setup allows us to focus on architectural generalization beha-
vior rather than advantages introduced by heterogeneous pre-training or
transfer learning schemes.
Statistics and reproducibility
When comparing predictive performance between different models across
the same test splits, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. For analyses
involving attention-based residue selection, including comparisons between
attention-derived residues and random or pooled baselines, permutation
tests were applied. It is important to note that the experiments conducted in
this study were not randomized.
