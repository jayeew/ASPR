The development of bioactive protein-based materials hinges on scalable expression systems and
robust and mild processing methods. Here we report the successful bacterial overexpression and
puriﬁcation of a fusion protein comprising the mini-spidroin (A
3I)3-A14 and the Immunoglobulin G (IgG)-
degrading enzyme IdeS. High-density fed-batch bioreactor cultivation resulted in expression levels of
7.2 g/L, demonstrating the scalability of the system. Structural and functional characterization
conﬁrmed that the IdeS moeity was folded and retained enzymatic activity. Importantly, the high
solubility of the fusion protein ( > 200 mg/mL), combined with the mini-spidroin ’s ability to assemble
into gels and ﬁbers, allowed us to develop self-assembled hydrogels and wet-spun ﬁbers that
maintained enzymatic function. Notably, enzymatic activity was preserved in ﬁbers stored for
prolonged times under both wet and dry conditions. These ﬁndings illustrate the potential of spidroin
fusion proteins as a modular platform for producing robust bioactive materials in a scalable process,
offering new avenues for biomedical applications.
The design of advanced biomaterials increasingly relies on protein engi-
neering strategies that integrate structural, self-assembling domains with
functional moieties within a single construct
1–5. This approach entails that
the self-assembling domain providesan inherent immobilization platform
for the bioactive fusion partner, ensuring its stable incorporation into the
material. A key advantage of this methodis that both the structural scaffold
and the bioactive component are produced simultaneously, making mate-
rial fabrication more ef ﬁcient and cost-effective. Moreover, the covalent
peptide bond linking the compone nts guarantees a homogeneous dis-
tribution of the functional moiety and eliminates the need for crosslinking
steps
4. Importantly, because the preserva tion of protein native states is
essential, the process inherently must avoid extreme temperatures, harsh pH
conditions, and toxic solvents, thereby offering a more sustainable and
environmentally friendly alternative for biomaterial production
6,7.
Among structural protein-based materials, spider silk has attracted
signiﬁcant attention for its exceptional mechanical properties and bio-
compatibility. Previous attempts to fabricate bioactive materials with silk
fusion proteins have proven their versatility and paved the way for further
developments

proteins (spidroins), provide a promising platform for biofabrication owing
to their high recombinant yields, compatibility with native puri ﬁcation
conditions, and ability to assemble into ﬁbers under mild, physiologically

The fusion protein (A3I-A-IdeS) was successfully overexpressed inE. coli
BL21 using both a standard LB media shakeﬂask-based protocol and a fed-
batch cultivation bioreactor setup. The expression level for the bioreactor
culture was 7.2 g/L (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary
Figs. 1 and 2). This is an impressive yield, comparable to industrially pro-
duced biopharmaceutical proteins such as human insulin (7.0 g/L)
36 and
interleukin-6 (7.5 g/L)37, but below the yields previously reported for a few
recombinant mini-spidroins (20.9 g/L, for NT2RepCT and 13.0 g/L for
(A3I)3-A14)35,38. When compared, both shake ﬂask and bioreactor cultures
yielded comparable amounts of protein per gram wet cell pellet (approx.
30 mg/g). Thus, the bioreactor cultivation protocols allowed for an increase
in cell density by a factor of 50, without hampering protein production. This
highlights the scalability of the expression of A
3I-A-IdeS in E. coli.
To purify the A3I-A-IdeS, the wet cell pellet was resuspended in 20 mM
Tris-HCl buffer before subsequent cell disruption and recovery of the
soluble proteins. Interestingly, with buffer to wet cell pellet ratio of 20:1,
almost all of the fusion protein was found in the insoluble fraction after cell
lysis. Increasing this ratio to at lea st 40:1 helps to solubilize ~60% of the
fusion protein (Supplementary Fig . 3). We speculate that the increased
recovery of soluble proteins is due to the release of protein from condensates
formed at high protein concentrations in the cytoplasm
39.
Puriﬁcation of the target protein using IMAC was performed under
native conditions. (A 3I)3-A14 exhibited excellent binding to the column

tary Figs. 4B and 5). In line with this, size exclusion chromatography

assemblies (Supplementary Fig. 6) in which the His-tag could be more
shielded. Despite the loss of protein during puri ﬁcation, 8.20 mg/g cell
pellet could be recovered after IMAC (Supplementary Fig. 7 and

A3I-A-IdeS forms bioactive hydrogels
Inspired by previous reports on the ability of recombinant spidroins to form
hydrogels when incubated at 37 °C13 we next investigated if hydrogels could
be produced from the A 3I-A-IdeS. Indeed, from s olutions with a con-
centration of >50 g/L gels could be formed at 37 °C. The gelation time
decreased with increasing protein concentration, whereas the gel stability
directly correlated with protein concentration (Supplementary Fig. 15A). To
probe the robustness of the gels after self-assembly, they were challenged
with a washing step using 1 × PBS (pH 7.4). The gel droplets that remained
intact without signs of disruption upon visual inspection were considered as
stable. SDS-PAGE of the washing solutions recovered from the 50 and
100 mg/mL gels revealed that they are stable after incubation for at least 4 h,
with insigniﬁcant amounts of soluble A
3I-A-IdeS in the washing solution
(Supplementary Fig. 15B). The 25 mg/mL gels, on the other hand, have only
partially polymerized, even after incubation for 16 h, and disentangled
during the washing step. This is further conﬁrmed in the SDS-PAGE gel,
showing abundant presence of A
3I-A-IdeS in washing solutions for all
incubation times. Vial inversion tests further conﬁrm the gelation of 50 and
100 mg/mL solutions within 16 h (Supplementary Fig. 16).
