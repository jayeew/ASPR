Marginal seas with pronounced stratiﬁcation are susceptible to low dissolved oxygen concentrations,
which can lead to hypoxia or anoxia below the pycnocline. In the Baltic Sea, the Major Baltic In ﬂows
ventilate the deep basins while further strengthening strati ﬁcation. It was suggested that the largest-
ever measured Major Baltic Inﬂow, which occurred in 1951, triggered the deoxygenation of the central
Baltic Sea because it signi ﬁcantly strengthened strati ﬁcation. With the help of a coupled
hydrodynamical-ecosystem numerical model, we conducted a couple of numerical experiments and
statistically analyzed the hypoxic volume and strati ﬁcation strength variability on multidecadal
timescales. We demonstrate that, although it had some short-term inﬂuence, the Major Baltic Inﬂow in
1951 did not signiﬁcantly impact the stratiﬁcation or hypoxia in the Baltic Sea on longer timescales. We
provide quantitative estimates of the Major Baltic In ﬂows’inﬂuence on multidecadal variability of
stratiﬁcation and hypoxia in the central Baltic Sea basins.
Marginal seas worldwide are experiencing increasing anthropogenic
pressure in different dimensions 1,2. One such dimension is the elevated
nutrient input from land, which has in ﬂuenced the state of the world ’s
most researched coastal systems, such as the Baltic Sea, the Chesapeake
Bay, and the Gulf of Mexico
3– 6. Eutrophication is a direct consequence of
elevated nutrient input from land, which, in turn, leads to hypoxic
(dissolved oxygen or DO concentrations less than a certain threshold,
typically 2 mg O
2 L−1 or 2 mL O 2 L−1, as in this study) and anoxic
(absence of DO) conditions. No higher trophic life is possible under such
low DO concentrations, which makes them very disruptive to local
ecosystems
7. In recent decades, an increasing number of coastal systems
have been affected by hypoxia and anoxia 8,9.
Elevated nutrient load is not the only driver responsible for hypoxia in
marginal seas. Some coastal system s are naturally prone to low-oxygen
conditions due to a pronounced pycnocline, which limits the vertical supply
of DO to deep waters, where it is primarily consumed. The circulation
pattern associated with such stratiﬁcation is often an estuarine circulation,
i.e., a two-layer circulation with lighter waters outﬂowing at the surface and
denser waters in ﬂowing in the deep layers. This type of circulation is
common for coastal seas with positive water balance. This, together with
long residence times, makes some marine systems around the world, with
very different shapes and sizes, prone to hypoxia
10.
The Baltic Sea is a prominent example of such a marine system. It is one
of the most extensively researched and monitored marginal seas worldwide,

This study utilizes a coupled hydrodynamic-biogeochemical model
(see methods for more details) to investigate the in ﬂuence of the parti-
cular MBI in 1951 and the general impact of MBIs on strati ﬁcation and
hypoxia in the four deep subbasins of the central Baltic Sea (Fig. 1). In
total, 13 model simulations were performed, including a reference run
(REF) and 12 shorter sensitivity experiments with modi ﬁed wind,
atmospheric pressure, and open boundary sea level forcing. REF follows
the actual temporal evolution of the atmospheric parameters and sea
level at the open boundary. A ﬁrst sensitivity simulation (NO_MBI1951)
completely suppresses the MBI in 1951, while the second (LIGHT_-
MBI1951) shifts the smaller MBI in 1993 to 1951. The other 10 random-
forcing experiments (RandForcExps) are driven by randomly selected
5-year snippets of atmospheric surface ﬁelds of sea-level pressure and
wind, as well as sea level at the open boundary, sampled from years with
low in ﬂow activity (see Supplementary Fig. 5 for exact years). They
generally inhibit the strong in ﬂows (including MBIs) during the transi-
tion from an oxic to a hypoxic state. We performed 10 random-forcing
experiments to ensure the results are statistically robust. This may still be
a relatively small number, but it is a good compromise between statistical
robustness and necessary computing time. For more information on
model experiments, refer to Supplementary Table 1 and the Methods
section. For the graphical description of the strategy for the sensitivity
experiments, see Supplementary Fig. 5. The model was validated against
observations and demonstrated overall good performance. For details on
model validation, see Supplementary Figs. 1 – 4.
Our results suggest that MBIs generally affect the multidecadal varia-
bility of strati ﬁcation and hypoxic volume in the deep subbasins of the
central Baltic Sea. By comparing REFwith RandForExps, we demonstrated
signiﬁcantly weaker stratiﬁcation in all subbasins of the central Baltic Sea,
except the Bornholm Basin, without MBIs. At the same time, hypoxic
volume was signiﬁcantly reduced without MBIs only in the wGB and BB.
We found that no single MBI, even the largest measured MBI in 1951, can
signiﬁcantly impact the long-term evolution of hypoxia and anoxia in the
deep central Baltic Sea subbasins. This leaves the elevated nutrient load as
the primary driver of the 20th-century expansion of hypoxia in the deep
basins of the central Baltic Sea.
Additionally, we showed that MBIs ’ inﬂuence on strati ﬁcation is
inhomogeneous across the deep central Baltic Sea basins. More precisely, we
identiﬁed two distinct patterns of MBIs inﬂuencing the deep central Baltic
Sea subbasins. Theﬁrst pattern is visible mainly in the Bornholm Basin and
is characterized by a signiﬁcant advective DO supply via inﬂows from the
North Sea. The second pattern is mostly visible in the wGB. This subbasin
shows hypoxia reduction under RandForExps, as does the Bornholm Basin,
but also signiﬁcantly smaller stratiﬁcation strength and a weaker relation-
ship between stratiﬁcation strength and hypoxic volume. This highlights the
reduced advective DO supply by MBIs to this subbasin, yet they still
transport salt and intensify stratiﬁcation, leading to greater hypoxic volume
if they are present. The eGB and nGB are transition subbasins, with the
former closer to the BB and the latter to the wGB.

Discussion
This study analyzed the effects of t he largest individual MBI in 1951 on
hypoxic volume and strati ﬁcation strength, as well as their relationship
across the deep central Baltic Sea subbasins. We did not observe a signiﬁcant
inﬂuence of the MBI in 1951 on either hypoxic volume or strati ﬁcation
strength in the deep central Baltic Sea on a multidecadal timescale. By
analyzing the results of RandForExps, we conﬁrmed the general relationship
between inﬂows from the North Sea and stratiﬁcation in the deep central
Baltic Sea basins, which is especially evident during the stagnation period
from 1983 to 1992

experiments, that a single MBI, even the largest measured one, cannot cause
signiﬁcant changes in hypoxia in the deep central subbasins of the Baltic Sea
on a multidecadal timescale. In addition, hypoxic volumes in both
LIGHT_MBI1951 and NO_MBI1951 model experiments are indis-

The model approach utilized in this study has some uncertainties. First,
the model system used in this study does not resolve any DO dynamics
induced by relatively short-lived submesoscale processes, such as intrusions
beneath the pycnocline
13 or submesoscale eddies41. While both mechanisms
mentioned above are certainly important, we assume their contribution to
the multidecadal variability of DO in the deep central Baltic Sea is minor.
Second, the model clearly demonstrated the positive bias in DO con-
centrations at the BY5 station in the Bornholm Basin, which is most likely
related to the negative bias in strati ﬁcation strength also revealed during
model validation. This bias is well-known in Baltic Sea models and was
already shown by e.g., Naumov et al.
37 for a similar model setup. The origin
of the bias in strati ﬁcation strength can be attributed to the complex
dynamics of the Bornholm Basin42,43 and the model’s resolution. While the
model biases introduce additional uncertainties in the conclusions related to
the Bornholm Basin, there are some factors that still make them valid, at
least qualitatively. Despite biases, the trends in hypoxia are similar in model
data and observations, suggesting that the model correctly reproduces the
temporal dynamics of drivers shaping hypoxia in the Bornholm Basin. In
addition, the inverse relationship between hypoxia and stratiﬁcation in the
Bornholm Basin was also reported by Krapf et al.
21, which gives additional
conﬁdence in the results.
There is still a not fully answered question regarding the further
development of hypoxia in the deep central Baltic Sea basins: the role of
climate change. It is evident that climate change promotes development of
hypoxia in the deep central Baltic Sea basins
44. However, it is thought that
the successful implementation of the n utrient mitigation strategies will
effectively mitigate the climate change impact32,45.O u rﬁndings suggest that
the natural variability, regardless of its source, plays a second role in the
development of hypoxia in the deep central Baltic Sea throughout the
20th century. This means that the most effective course of action to reduce
hypoxia in the deep central Baltic Sea subbasins in the warmer world
would still be to further reduce nutrient inputs from land, as Conley
46 stated
earlier.
Methods
Model setup
In this study, we used a coupled 3-dimensional hydrodynamical-biogeo-
chemical ocean model of the entire Baltic Sea (see Supplementary Fig. 7 for
model domain). The ocean component was represented by the Modular
Ocean Model version 6 (MOM6), whichwas developed by the Geophysical
Fluid Dynamics Laboratory (GFDL)
47. The main novelties of the MOM6
compared to its predecessor, the MOM548, are the Arbitrary Lagrangian-
Eulerian (ALE) method for ha ndling vertical advection 49 and various
improvements in mixing parametrizations 50. In the actual simulations,
vertical mixing is parameterized using the K-pro ﬁle parametrization
(KPP)51, and the vertical coordinate is set to z-star48.

The biogeochemical part is governed by the Ecological Regional Ocean
Model (ERGOM), which models the cycles of main nutrients in the Baltic
Sea (phosphorus and nitrogen) and carbon, oxygen, and hydrogen sulﬁde.
Nutrients are split into inorganic (PO 4
3-,N O 3
-,a n dN H4
+) and organic
(DON, DOP, and PON, POP) compounds. The same is true for carbon. The
primary producers are represented via four classes of phytoplankton (large-
cell PP, small-cell PP, cyanobacteria, and limnic PP). Zooplankton state
variable grazes on phytoplankton. The ERGOM maintains a non-Redﬁeld
nutrient ratio in organic matter via extracellular release under unfavorable
environmental conditions (when on e or more nutrients are missing).
However, the detritus state variable is always in the Red ﬁeld ratio. The
oxygen cycle is fully represented in the ERGOM. It is produced through air-
sea exchange and photosynthesis and consumed through the respiration of
plankton, the mineralization of organic matter in the sediments and the

Sensitivity experiments
To design sensitivity studies with different MBIs’dynamics, the V17 metric
for REF wasﬁrst calculated. V17 represents the volume of water in the central
Baltic Sea basins to the east from the Arkona transect (see Fig.1) with salinity
higher than 17 g kg −1, an established threshold for the water masses
inﬂowing from the North Sea. Based on this metric, the years with low
inﬂow activity were selected. For the NO_MBI1951 experiment, the wind,
atmospheric pressure, and sea-level at the open boundary from 1937 to 1959
were replaced with those from 1926 to 1948 (orange-highlighted area in
Supplementary Fig. 5), during which inﬂow activity was low. This led to the
absence of the MBI in the NO_MBI1951 experiment in 1953. After 1959, the
same forcing as in REF was applied. The transition between the different
forcing datasets always occurred in June to avoid disturbances in MBIs, since
they mostly occur in winter and autumn
19. The same strategy was applied to
generate forcing for the LIGHT_M BI1951 experiment, with the wind,
atmospheric pressure, and sea-level at the open boundary from 1940 to 1954
replaced by those from 1982 to 1996 (green-highlighted area in Supple-
mentary Fig. 5). After 1954, the forcing in LIGHT_MBI1954 matches REF
exactly. The transition between different forcing datasets happens in June, as
in NO_MBI1951. The main difference between the NO_MBI1951 and
LIGHT_MBI1951 experiments is that the replaced forcing does not contain
any MBIs in NO_MBI1951, but it does include the weaker MBI 1993, which
in LIGHT_MBI1951 arrives in 1951. It should be noted that despite the
MBIs’ absence, the background in ﬂow activity is higher in the
NO_MBI1951 than in the LIGHT_MBI1951, which is visible in Supple-
mentary Fig. 5 when comparing green and orange areas. The forcing for
each of the 10 random forcing experiments (wind, air pressure, and sea-level
at the open boundary) was construct ed by randomly combining 5-year
periods with low V
17 in REF (gray highlighted areas in Supplementary
Fig. 5), which means that each 85-yearrandom forcing experiment consists
of combined 17 randomly selected 5-year periods with low V17 (each 5-year
period can be chosen more than once). The exact 5-year periods, from which
was randomly chosen, are 1892– 1897, 1928– 1933, 1943– 1948, 1954– 1959,
1963– 1968, and 1982 – 1987. In all RandForExps, the transition between
different forcing periods always occurred in June, as in LIGHT_MBI1951
and NO_MBI1951. The following designof sensitivity experiments allows
us to investigate the individual impact of the largest measured MBI in 1951
(NO_MBI1951) and a hypothetical, less-intensive MBI that could have
occurred in that year (LIGHT_MBI1951) on the long-term evolution of
hypoxia in the deep central Baltic S ea subbasins. With the help of the
RandForExps, we can simulate the system with way less salt input from the
North Sea than in reality and demonstrate the system’s response to these
changes. The other factors that could affect stratiﬁcation in the deep central
Baltic Sea basins, such as global warming orﬂuctuations in runoff, are not
changed in sensitivity experiments.
