Unlike humans, small mammals dedicate considerable energy expenditure (EE) to maintaining core
body temperature (Tb). We investigate the in ﬂuence of ambient temperature (Ta) on energy
homeostasis in single housed male mice and rats studied by indirect calorimetry and continuous Tb
measurement at Ta ranging from 22 °C to 35 °C. With Tb increases at higher Ta, food intake and body
weight decrease, demonstrating that the higher Tb signi ﬁes heat stress and not a benign adaptive
response. Rats at 30 °C were at the edge of their thermal tolerance. Neither rats nor mice have a broad
thermoneutral zone (TNZ). Rather, both have thermoneutral points (TNP), below which EE increases
and above which Tb increases. In mice, the EE increase above the TNP is due to the higher Tb (van ’t
Hoff Q
10 effect) and increased physical activity. Interestingly, the remaining EE is lower, demonstrating
acute thermal adaptation with a reduced basal metabolic rate. In conclusion, rats are less cold-
sensitive and more prone to overheating than mice. Despite their larger size, rats, like mice, have a TNP
and not a broad TNZ. Studying rats and mice at a Ta of 28 –29 °C is a sensible compromise that safely
minimizes cold-induced thermogenesis while reducing the risk of heat stress.
Mice and rats are used to model human physiology and pathophysiology
and are typically the ﬁrst species chosen for in vivo studies in drug
development1,2. Yet, they differ from humans in several ways, with size
(≈3000-fold smaller for mice and ≈300-fold smaller for rats) being a very
important factor3. The geometric consequence of a smaller body is a larger
surface area to weight ratio (≈14-fold greater for mice and ≈7-fold greater
for rats)4 which increases sensitivity to the ambient temperature (Ta) due to
greater heat loss5. To defend body temperature (Tb), small mammals use
non-shivering thermogenesis, largely by activation of brown-adipose tissue
(BAT), to increase heat production 6.W h e nk e p ta tr o o mt e m p e r a t u r e
(~22 °C), single-housed mice devote ~1/3 of their total energy expenditure
(TEE) to maintaining Tb7,8. If challenged (e.g., by fasting or cold exposure),
mice can thermoregulate to a slightly reduced Tb8 or more profoundly with
torpor9, conserving energy and reducing nutritional needs. Rat temperature
sensitivity has also been studied10. As expected from their greater body size
they are less sensitive to a cool environment than mice, with a wide range of
required EE reported to maintain Tb at room temperature
10–12.

Results
Both rats and mice have TNPs, and not broad TNZs
We compared the effect of increasing Ta (24 h at each Ta) using housing
conditions that include nesting and e nrichment materials. For reference,
housing male mice without nesting and enrichment increases energy use,
with the magnitude depending on circadian phase and arousal state (Sup-
plementary Fig. 1A–I). Previously we reported (with 1 h at each Ta) that
mice have a thermoneutral point (TNP), a discrete Ta below which TEE
increases and above which Tb increases, and not a broad TNZ
19. The current
chow and DIO mouse cohorts demonstrate the EE and Tb vs Ta changes
characteristic of a TNP, with TNP values in approximately the 30 °C to 32 °C
range (Fig. 1A, B, E, F). These mouse results demonstrate that the TNP
concept applies when using different assay durations and when nesting
materials are present.
Examining the rat data, the resting EE increased as the Ta was reduced
below 32 °C, while the Tb increased above 30 °C (Fig.1C, D, G, H). These
observations in both the chow and DIO cohorts demonstrate that rats have a

The Tb increases at higher Ta indicate heat stress, in both rats
and mice
In mice, Tb increased gradually with increasing Ta, starting at ≈31 °C to
≈33 °C (Fig.1A, B). In contrast, the increase in rats was more abrupt at a Ta
of 31 °C and some of the DIO rats did not tolerate a Ta of 32 °C (Fig.1C, D).
In all four cohorts the Tb increase was accompanied by reductions in food
intake, RER, and body weight, withou t consistent changes in physical
activity (Fig.1I–W). The lower food intake going from 22 °C to 30 °C reﬂects
the tight coupling between energy expenditure and energy intake. At Ta
30 °C, the need for cold-indued thermogenesis is minimal, causing TEE to
be lower and thus food intake also to be lower. The fact that body weight
does not change shows that energy in take and expenditure are indeed
matched before heat stress occurs at Ta >30 °C. Above Ta 30 °C there is a
reduction in food intake below steady state needs, causing use of stored fat
and weight loss. Thus, the Tb increase in both mice and rats signiﬁes heat
stress and is not simply a benign adaptive response.
Mice, but not rats, tolerate treatment with a β3-adrenergic ago-
nist at Ta 30 °C
Selective β3-adrenergic agonists stimulate lipolysis in white adipose tissue
and thermogenesis in BAT and have been used to investigate thermogenic
capacity and heat dissipation in mice
35–37. In rats at 22 °C, CL-316243
treatment was reported to increase Tb by 0.6 °C to 0.9 °C38.W ef o u n dt h a t
CL-316243 treatment of chow rats generated the expected increase in Tb
and TEE and reduction of RER and food intake (Fig. 2A–E). DIO rats
behaved similarly at 22 °C but when exposed to 30 °C after CL-316243

large Tb increase (Fig. 2F–L). These results conﬁrm that rats at 30 °C are
near their thermal tolerance limit, with DIO rats unable to compensate for

no clear difference between chow-fed and DIO mice after treatment with

Discussion
Ta effects on energy physiology
Increasing housing temperature to minimize cold-induced thermogenesis is
postulated to better align mouse and human thermal biology22,23,42.W h e t h e r
improved translatability can be obtained by increasing Ta in rats is
unknown. Here we compared rats to mice and obtained results consistent
with the principles of heat transfer, namely that smaller bodies (mice) lose
heat more readily and are relatively cold sensitive, while larger bodies (rats)
dissipate heat less well and tend more towards heat intolerance. Our studies
used housing conditions that include nesting and enrichment materials, as
recommended for research animals
43. In mice, there was a ≈100% increase
in resting TEE at 22 °C compared to 30 °C while the increase in rats was only
≈35%. Thus, below thermoneutrality, the rat is much less sensitive to
reductions in Ta than is the mouse.
At warm Ta, mice gradually increased their Tb. In contrast, the increase
in rats was more abrupt, steeper, and less well tolerated, especially in DIO
rats. Rats showed heat stress starting at 31 °C, and BAT activation with aβ3-
adrenergic agonist was not tolerated in DIO rats at Ta 30 °C.
Rats and mice have a thermoneutral point and not a broad
thermoneutral zone
The mouse results are an independent conﬁrmation of the TNP concept.
We show here that rats also have a TNP and not a broad TNZ. These results

Choosing a Ta for rat and mouse studies
The optimal Ta for studying mice is debated, particularly for studying
weight loss pharmacotherapies. One common strategy suggests 30 °C as
thermoneutrality
25. Another proposed a few degrees below
thermoneutrality24. However, mice do not have a broad TNZ, but rather a
TNP that changes diurnally19 and probably also between the rest/sleep and
active/awake states29. Thus, housing strictly at thermoneutrality is simply
not feasible. When trying to minimize cold-induced thermogenesis, a Ta
just below thermoneutrality has multiple attributes in its favor: (1) it
minimizes the energy spent on cold-induced thermogenesis and the food
intake needed to fuel it, mimicking the physiology of larger homeotherms
8,
(2) it avoids increasing Tb, which stimulates in ﬂammation and immune
responses44, worsens metabolic ﬁtness45, and may contribute to reduced
lifespan46, and (3) it provides a safety margin for variation in vivarium
temperature. For mice, a Ta of≈28–29 °C fulﬁlls these criteria19.
An optimal Ta for minimizing cold-induced thermogenesis in rats has
not been reported. Applying the criteria above to the data reported herein
leads to suggesting a Ta of ≈28–2 9 ° Cf o rr a t sa l s o .I ti sp a r t i c u l a r l y
important to avoid Ta≥30 °C for rats to reduce the risk of heat stress, which
is why we suggest 29 °C as an upper bound.
A fundamental consideration whenchoosing the Ta is the purpose of
the experiment, particularly for thermal physiology endpoints. For example,
acutely transferring a mouse to 30 °C followed by maximal β3-adrenergic
agonist stimulation allows quanti ﬁcation of the animal ’st h e r m o g e n i c
capacity, allowing detection of subtle differences
37,47. However, to com-
pletely remove cold-induced thermogenesis for measurement of the cost of
physical activity, a Ta of 35 °C was required
33. Generally, group housing48–51
and nesting materials52,53, both of which reduce the thermal demand, are
avoided. However, huddling and nesting allow animals to manipulate their
thermal environment, thereby reducing the importance of Ta choice by the
experimenter. Sometimes studies at two Ta, such as≈22 °C and ≈28–29 °C
will prove informative.
Since the TEE vs Ta slope is shallower in rats, the choice of Ta is
probably less crucial and ≈22 °C could be a reasonable selection for most
experiments. An argument can also be made for allowing group housing
with nesting materials at≈22 °C, even when studying obesity drug efﬁcacy.
The bottom line is that the Ta should be chosen based on the
experimental goals.

Fat does not provide major thermal insulation in rats or mice
In larger mammals such as humans, incr easing adiposity contributes to
thermal insulation54,55, although it is at best, a minor effect in mice7,8 where
f u ri st h em a i ni n s u l a t i n gf a c t o rp r o v i d i n g3 0–50% of the total insulation7.
Factors contributing to the minimal insulation by adipose tissue in mice
include: (1) fat distribution — the subcutaneous adipose tissue mainly in
shoulder and inguinal regions and not particularly surrounding the body
core
39, (2) the low absolute amount of fat — Newton’s law of heat transfer
states that insulation is proportional to the layer’s thickness, and mouse
adipose tissue is only millimeters thick40, and (3) mice are so small that little
heat is conserved via redistribution of bloodﬂow away from the paws and
tail56 — the body core is simply too close to the body surface. We show here
that adiposity, while more importantin rats than in mice due to the greater
fat thickness and larger body size, is still not a major contributor to
insulation.
Acute and chronic adaptation to environmental temperatures
Physiologic adaptation to temperature extremes can mean the difference
between life and death. In humans and mice, chronic adaptation to a hot
environment occurs over days to weeks57,58. Here we report a BMR reduction
of 18% in mice exposed to a hot Ta over a span of minutes, demonstrating
that mice can acutely adapt to the heat. The BMR reduction counters the
TEE increase caused by the hyperthermic Tb/Q
10 effect but the underlying
mechanisms for the BMR reduction are not known. Possible contributors
include reduced sympathetic tone and heart rate and decreased perfusion of
and metabolism by the gastrointestinal tract.
Whether there is a TNP or a broad TNZ likely depends on the body size
of the animals/species studied and not the duration of exposure to a Ta.
Indeed, when TEE is measured at Ta = 22 °C and Ta = 30 °C, the absolutes
levels and the 22 °C:30 °C ratios are similar whether the mice have been at
these Ta for 4 weeks
26,o n eh o u r19, or one day, as in the current study.
Study limitations
We studied male rats and mice, one strain each, under single-housing
conditions for 24 h at each Ta. The results may be inﬂuenced by environ-
ment considerations including the type and amount of bedding, nesting,
and enrichment materials and by animal characteristic including age, strain,
coat condition, and fur density. There are known sex differences in thermal
biology for single housed mice
59,60 and particularly with group housing, since
females huddle more than males48–51,61. While single housing may be more
stressful than group housing, it is required for using a Scholander analysis59.
With prolonged exposure to hot or cold temperatures, mice and rats initiate
adaptation mechanisms, including increasing fur density to improve insu-
lation and behavioral changes such spreading of saliva to increase eva-
porative heat loss
62,63.S u c ha d a p t i o n sa r em o r ei m p o r t a n tt h ef a r t h e ro n ei s
from the thermoneutrality. Since our study was performed within a relative
narrow range near the TNP, we expect t hat the Ta:TEE relationships we
report will be generally applicable, including in chronic studies. We have
studied only mice and rats, but since body size drives the physiology, we
expect that the principles reported herein apply generally to other similarly
small mammals.
Conclusions
The results align with the principles of heat transfer, namely that due to their
smaller body size, mice lose heat readily and tend towards cold sensitivity.
Despite their larger size, rats do not differ considerably from mice in thermal
biology and have a TNP instead of a broad TNZ as humans. However,
compared to mice, rats tend towards heat intolerance. To minimize the
inter-species gap in thermal biology between humans and mice and rats, we
suggest safely minimizing cold-induced thermogenesis by using a Ta
of ≈28–29 °C.
