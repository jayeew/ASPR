Unravelling the nucleation–elongation
mechanism of one-pot catenation

The formation of catenanes through dynamic covalent reaction of self-
assembling precursors in one pot offers a powerful route to complex inter-
locked architectures, yet the mechanistic insight remains underexplored.
Here, we elucidate the nucleation–elongation mechanism in the one-pot
synthesis of topologically distinct catenanes, using dimeric (DCC)a n dt r i m e r i c
cage-catenanes (TCC) as model systems. Unlike conventional supramolecular
polymerization, catenation exhibits topology-dependent pathways:DCC for-
mation is driven by the strong templating effect of its monomeric unit MC-1,
while TCC assembly proceeds with several pathways in parallel, via coopera-
tive binding of partially catenated intermediates. Kinetic proﬁling of the
catenation reveals sigmoidal growth with distinct induction periods, where
longer induction period correlates with more challenging nucleation. Seeded
growth experiments demonstrate that preformed nuclei (e.g.,MC-1 for DCC)
bypass kinetic barriers, remarkably reducing its induction period, akin to
seeded supramolecular polymerization. Our work bridges the gap between
supramolecular polymerization and mechanical bonding for interlocked
structures, offering a hint for the rational synthesis of structures with
sophisticated topology.
The elucidation of self-assembly processes is essential for under-
standing and functionalizing both biological and synthetic systems.
Taking the formation of proteinﬁbrils, for instance, it is thought to be
related to degenerative diseases. Although the kinetics of ﬁbril for-
mation are complex, their growth is somewhat associated with the
nucleated polymerization mechanism, also known as
nucleation–elongation (Fig. 1A)
1–5. This mechanism relies on non-
covalent interactions, which drive the self-assembly of monomers into
well-deﬁned higher-order structures. The process begins with nuclea-
tion, where monomers overcome an energy barrier to form a stable
nucleus, which acts as a template for further chain elongation. Once
the nucleus reaches a critical size, the elongation occurs rapidly as
monomers attach to it, leading to the formation of well-ordered
polymeric structures until equilibrium is achieved
4–7.
The nucleation–elongation mechanism has been extensively stu-
died in supramolecular polymerization (Fig. 1A)8–15. For example,
Meijer and co-workers systematically elucidated the complexity of self-
assembly pathways, emphasizing the critical role of
nucleation–elongation process in regulating the assembly process
and, ultimately, the resulting supramolecular structures
8,16,17.B y
designing metastable intermediates and precisely controlling
nucleation–elongation dynamics, the Aida laboratory has advanced
the ﬁeld of supramolecular polymerization, transitioning from step-
growth to chain-growth, and ultimately achieving living supramole-
cular polymerization
9,18. The Würthner group demonstrated a seeding
strategy19,20, where preassembled nuclei (seeds) suppress spontaneous
nucleation and promote elongation, enabling the formation of supra-
molecular polymers with narrow dispersity.
Catenanes, a classic type of interlocked molecules, have recently
gained considerable interest due to their distinctive mechanical bonds,
dynamic behavior, and diverse applications in nanotechnology and
molecular machines
21–27.

Traditional synthesis of catenanes relies on metal–ligand coordi-
nation, which facilitates preorganization of reactive components fol-
lowed by ring-closing reactions to form interlocked
architectures
21,22,28–30. Following Fujita ’sw o r ko n “magic rings”31 and
Leigh’s single-step catenation in 20% yield 32, one-pot strategies fea-
turing concise synthetic routes and high efﬁciency have emerged as a
promising frontier33–45. However, a signi ﬁcant gap persists in the lit-
erature: while diverse catenated structures are accessible by one-pot
synthesis, mechanistic insights into their formation, including reaction
pathways, reactive intermediates, and kinetic control, remain
underexplored
42,44,46,47.
We herein report a mechanistic study on one-pot catenation using
dimeric and trimeric cage-catenanes (denoted as DCC and TCC,
respectively) as case study models (Fig. 1B), synthesized via dynamic
imine condensation between stoichiometrically balanced trialdehyde
panels—designed to promote templating effect via their ef ﬁcient
stacking—and diamine linkers of appropriate length. Through com-
prehensive thermodynamic and kinetic analyses, we reveal that cate-
nation follows a nucleation–elongation mechanism that is reminiscent
of supramolecular polymerization. Leveraging this insight, we show
that the catenane formation can be accelerated by introducing pre-
assembled nuclei (seeding), underscoring the critical role of pre-
organization in efﬁcient interlocking.

Spontaneous formation and seeded growth of cage-catenanes
Extensive kinetic simulations and experiments studies on supramole-
cular polymerization have provided profound insights into the
mechanism with the feature of nucleation–elongation growth

the initial stages of the process

mechanism of the catenated structure in one pot, we examined the
kinetics of spontaneous formation of DCC and TCC (i.e., reactions

similar induction period was indeed observed during the formation of
both DCC and TCC.
After plotting the evolution of concentration over time in Fig.4B,
two parameters were employed to quantitatively describe the behavior
of a sigmoidal-like growth (Fig. S11)

as the time required for half of the target catenane to form; (ii) Length
of the induction period (tinduction), determined by the intersection of
the tangent line (at t

designated as τ, is given by:
τ = tinduction=thalf ð1Þ
τ is the dimensionless time ranging from 0 to 1. A decrease in τ
indicates that nucleation is more facile, as the induction period
becomes shorter. Conversely, an increase in τ indicates a more kine-

lenging to initiate the catenation.

cooperativity in Fig. 10.

This study provides a comprehensive mechanistic insight into the
one-pot synthesis of catenanes through dynamic covalent chemistry.
By investigating critical concentration, reaction pathways, sponta-

strated that the assembly of DCC and TCC followed a
nucleation–elongation mechanism, yet their pathways and nucleation
stage diverged due to the differences in binding capability. The strong
binding of MC-1 to the reactive precursor drives ef ﬁcient DCC for-
mation, while fast elongation of TCC relies on partially catenated
species rather than MC-2. Kinetic studies introduced the dimension-

These insights not only advance the mechanistic understanding of
catenane formation but also offer practical strategies for synthesizing
higher-order interlocked structures. Future work could explore the
extension of these principles to more complex topologies, such as [n]
catenanes and polycatenanes. By bridging supramolecular and
dynamic covalent chemistry, this study might provide some guidelines
for the rational design of mechanically interlocked molecules with
tailored properties and functions.
