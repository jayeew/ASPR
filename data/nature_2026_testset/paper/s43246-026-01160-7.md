During nanoindentation of layered materials, a plethora of deformation mechanisms manifest in the
load-displacement data as discrete displacement bursts, whose physical origins are often dif ﬁcult to
isolate from indentation data alone. This study develops a mechanistic framework to classify
nanoindentation displacement bursts, also known as pop-ins, in layered materials and structures,
enabling attribution of distinct pop-in populations to the underlying deformation mechanisms. Using
muscovite (a mica-group phyllosilicate) as a model system, we conducted nanoindentation
experiments normal to the basal plane over temperatures from ambient to 300 °C and extracted
features from the load-displacement data, including pop-in velocities and energies. Here, we report
that fracture and plastic deformation events are reliably distinguished using a statistically robust
threshold in pop-in width. This new procedure revealed that with increasing temperature, the onset
load for fracture-induced pop-ins decreased – consistent with declining fracture toughness – whereas
the onset load for plasticity-induced pop-ins remained unchanged. This work provides a systematic
approach to identify and isolate deformation mechanisms in layered materials during nanoindentation.
Layered materials possess intrinsic structural features that make them
attractive for applications beyond the reach of conventional bulk solids.
Their architecture consists of atomic layers with strong chemical bonds
that are stacked and held together by weak van der Waals forces and/or
electrostatic interactions, depending on whether the layers are charged.
This bonding hierarchy gives rise to high anisotropy and large surface
area-to-volume ratios, properties that underpin their functionality
1.
Layered materials are ubiquitous in the earth ’s crust, comprising a sub-
stantial fraction of soils and rocks. Their mechanical behavior therefore
governs processes central to geophysics and geomechanics, from seismic
response monitoring to underground waste storage 2–4. For example,
predicting how layered rocks deform under stress is essential for
hydraulic fracturing, geological CO
2 storage, and other subsurface
engineering operations3. Beyond geological settings, layered structures
inspire synthetic analogs such as graphene, MXenes, and transition metal
dichalcogenides, which leverage their unique structure for a wide range of
technologies
5,6. Their large surface area enhances reactivity for carbon
sequestration, nuclear waste containment, catalysis, and energy storage5,7.
In their two-dimensional forms, layered materials have exceptional
mechanical stability which makes them promising candidates for ﬂexible
electronics, including ﬂexible displays, ﬂexible computational circuits,
and wearable healthcare devices
5,6.
The same layered architecture that makes these materials desirable for
various technologies also makes them susceptible to distinct deformation/
degradation mechanisms, such as delamination and kink band formation.
Furthermore, the expansive surface area of layered materials intensi ﬁes
interactions with the environment, which can alter their response to
mechanical stress
7,8. A fundamental understanding of how deformation
mechanisms initiate and evolve under external and environmental stimuli is
therefore critical for the reliable integration of these materials into advanced
technologies. In lithium/sodium-ion batteries, for instance, layered metal
oxides such as lithium cobalt oxide an d nickel-cobalt-manganese ternary
compounds serve as common cathode materials because their layered
architecture enables ion intercalation and deintercalation, yielding high
speciﬁc capacities
9. However, the stability of the structure during the charge/
discharge cycle is challenged by stresses generated within the lattice and
interactions at the electrode-electrolyte interface. These stresses drive
interfacial delamination, plasticity or fracture— processes further modu-
lated by the surrounding chemical and electrical environments— and limit
device performance and longevity
9–11. Comparable challenges arise in

Effect of temperature on modulus and hardness
The temperature dependence of muscovite ’s mechanical properties –
hardness and modulus– was evaluated to understand its thermomechanical
response. Figure 3a shows a decline in reduced modulus with increasing
temperature, showing a reduction of 20% from the value at 25 °C when the
temperature reached 300 °C. The reduction in modulus with increasing
temperature reversed upon cooling (Fig. 3b), with a one-way ANOVA
analysis ( α = 0.05) con ﬁrming that changes in reduced modulus after
cooling were not statistically signiﬁcant. Furthermore, all reduced modulus
values obtained at 25 °C were comparable to those obtained in other
studies
7,25. The reversible reduction of modulus with increasing temperature
is consistent with observations of reversible lattice expansion and contrac-
tion from the Raman analysis (Fig. 2c), further supporting the absence of
thermal history contributions to the evolution of the reduced modulus.

Framework for distinguishing pop-ins
A framework was developed to deﬁne a threshold pop-in width, based on
quantitative features of theﬁrst pop-in event, to distinguish fracture from
non-fracture deformation mechanisms. Previous studies have shown that
muscovite deforms linear elastically until theﬁrst pop-in develops; theﬁrst
pop-in event has therefore been typically associated with the onset of non-
fracture deformation mechanisms1,37. Thus, by restricting analysis to theﬁrst
pop-in event, we minimize overlap with other event types. We analyzed the
widths, energies, and velocities of the ﬁrst pop-in event across all tem-
peratures and display these in Fig.7a-c. The data for the energies, velocities
and widths were not normally distributed but were skewed toward higher
values. This skew motivated the use of boxplots rather than mean and
standard deviation to represent the data. Figure7a shows that the median
energy ofﬁrst pop-ins at all temperatures was less than 2 pJ, with maximum
values below 5 pJ even at elevated temperatures. Median velocities were less
than 400 nm/s, with maximum values typically under 600 nm/s (Fig.7b). In
a prior nanoindentation study, fracture-related pop-ins in muscovite were
generally associated with energies more than 7 pJ
7. In a separate study, where
a wedge was driven laterally through mica sheets to propagate a stable crack,
crack propagation velocities of ~1000 nm/s were reported under ambient
conditions38. Both studies offer useful reference points for benchmarking
deformation behavior. The increased rate of indenter penetration at
fracture-induced pop-ins is known to be associatedwith the initiation of the
cracks
39,40. We therefore assume that the velocity of a propagating crack is at
least the same order of magnitude as indenter tip velocity and is ultimately
constrained only by the instrument’s time constant in capturing the fracture
event. The fact that the energies and velocities of theﬁrst pop-ins were less
than 7 pJ and 1000 nm/s, respectively, indicates that these pop-ins were
associated with non-fracture events. While these values could serve as
empirical thresholds for distinguishing fracture from non-fracture events,
there are limitations to such an approach for pop-ins occurring at loads

the pop-in width criterion to distinguish populationsto make population-
level assessments, but do not assert that every pop-in with a width >5 nm
could only come from fracture and that every pop-in with a width <5 nm
comes from a non-fracture deformation mechanism. Later pop-ins (those
happening deeper in the sample with the advancement of the nanoindenter
probe) may include contributions from both fracture and non-fracture
deformation mechanisms within a single event.

Conclusions
We performed elevated temperature nanoindentation on muscovite to
investigate deformation mechanisms in layered materials. By analyzing
discontinuities in load-depth data, we developed a robust framework to
classify pop-ins based on their magnitude and associated mechanical sig-
natures. This framework was validate d through fracture toughness esti-
mates and statistical analysis and identiﬁed a ~5 nm threshold above which
pop-ins represent fracture. While small pop-ins showed no temperature
dependence, likely due to spatially dominated statistics introduced by the
large indenter tip, large pop-ins associated with fracture occurred at lower
loads with increasing temperature, re ﬂective of a decline in fracture
toughness. Our work offers a transferrable methodology for distinguishing

Materials and methods
High-quality muscovite single crystal (V1) obtained from Electron
Microscopy Sciences was used in this study. The sample had a 15 mm
diameter and 200 µm thickness, with a (001) basal plane surface for
testing. To ascertain the underlying chemical structure of the sample,
Raman spectra were collected using an XploRA Plus Raman microscope
(HORIBA Scientiﬁc) with a cooled CCD detector (Jobin Yvon ’s Synapse
camera, Syncerity OE). An unpolarized laser with 532 nm emission
wavelength was used for excitation with the radiation power ~100 mW at
100% power in conjunction with 1200 (750 nm) grating. A combination
of 100 µm slit and 300 µm aperture was used. An objective with 10×
magniﬁcation and a numerical aperture of 0.25 was used for excitation,
with the resulting laser spot diameter of 2.6 µm. Raman spectrometer
calibration was performed every 24 h, using a polished silicon wafer. The
sample was equilibrated at target temperature for at least 20 min. Each
Raman spectrum was collected with 2 s exposure and averaged over 100
scans. Three spectra were collected at each temperature. These three
spectra were combined via summation to produce the ﬁnal spectrum
used in analysis. Background subtraction was performed using linear
segments under the main peaks.
To determine the roughness of t he muscovite surface, AFM was
conducted with a resolution of 512 points per line and a scan rate of
1 Hz using a Digital Instruments A FM. For nanoindentation, the
muscovite sample was mounted to an (001)-oriented sapphire sub-
strate from University Wafers, using high temperature cement
obtained from Omega Engineering . The cement ensured that the
bond between muscovite and sapphire was maintained throughout
the elevated temperature nanoinde ntation tests. A thermocouple was
also mounted on the sample using the cement to monitor the tem-
perature of the sample before and during nanoindentation tests. For
all tests, the muscovite-sapphire mount was clamped to the stage with
clamps pressed ﬁrmly on the sapphire substrate.
