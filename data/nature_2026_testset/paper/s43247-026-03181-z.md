In the Arctic Ocean, a substantial portion of primary production occurs under sea ice and around the
subsurface chlorophyll maximum. In these low-light environments, primary production will depend on
photoacclimation, which increases phytoplankton’s chlorophyll content per unit of carbon biomass to
enhance photon capture. Here, we incorporated a photoacclimation theory based on resource
allocation optimization into a global ecosystem model, showing that remarkably high chlorophyll
content responding to low-light environments is a primary driver of under-ice production. The
distribution of primary production is more strongly correlated with phytoplankton carbon biomass than
with chlorophyll concentration. We estimated that approximately half of the Arctic Ocean ’s primary
production occurs in regions where satellite observations are challenging (i.e., where ice concentration
exceeds 10%). This mechanistic framework enhances our ability to interpret observed distributions of
chlorophyll and primary production and predict ongoing changes in the Arctic Ocean primary
production under shifting environmental conditions.
The Arctic Ocean has experienced a substantial decline in sea ice extent over
the past three decades1,2. During this period, sea ice thickness also underwent
a notable shift, transitioning from p redominantly thicker to thinner ice3.
These changes in sea ice profoundly affect light availability, temperature,
and nutrient conditions, which are key factors that regulate oceanic primary
production. Studies estimating primary production based on the satellite-
observed surface chlorophyll a concentration indicate that the total primary
production in the Arctic Ocean increases as sea ice retreats
4– 6. However,
such satellite-based estimations e xclude production occurring in ice-
covered regions7– 9. Additionally, estimating p rimary production in the
ocean interior from surface data in troduces inherent uncertainties10.A
comprehensive understanding of th e mechanisms driving primary pro-
duction under sea ice and within the oceanic interior is crucial for accurately
quantifying primary production in the Arctic Ocean.
Under-ice production, a substantial component of Arctic Ocean pri-
mary production, occurs universally beneath sea ice, as revealed by recent in
situ observations
11,12. This production persists even under ice with a thick-
ness of 3 m12– 14. Under-ice chlorophyll a concentrations frequently exceed a
few μgL −1 and occasionally surpass 10.0μgL −1 11,12. In compiled observa-
tion data, vertically integrated under-ice production averaged over obser-
vation periods ranges from 0.012 to 1.4 g C m−2 day−1
, with maximum daily
production reaching 3.7 g C m−2 day−1 12,15. In situ observation indicates that
melt ponds on the ice, which enhance li ght penetration by reducing light
reﬂectivity, are a key driver of high under-ice production 15. For primary
production occurring in the Arctic Oce an interior, the subsurface chlor-
ophyll a maximum (SCM) plays a pivotal role10,16– 18.T h eS C M ,ar e g i o no f
peak chlorophyll a concentration below the surface19, is a common feature in
the global ocean20. In the Arctic Ocean, SCM typically occurs below 75 m
depth17 and is notably shallower under sea ice compared to open water21.I t
is often situated well beneath the pycnocline, closely aligned with nitracline,
and has been observed to coincide with the maximum phytoplankton
carbon biomass in the Arctic waters of BafﬁnB a y

limitation is relaxed 27. Depending on light and nutrient environments,
chlorophyll content varies more than tenfold in compiled datasets from
incubation experiments28. Under sea ice and around SCM, light intensity is
lower, and nutrient concentration is higher than in open-water regions,
which likely contributes to heightenedphytoplankton chlorophyll content.
However, the precise physiological mechanism and the extent to which
chlorophyll content varies in the Arctic Ocean remain unknown.
In this paper, we estimate the variation in chlorophyll content in the
Arctic Ocean and its impact on primary production. We achieve this using a
global ocean ecosystem model based on a phytoplankton acclimation theory
proposed by Pahlow
29 and others30,31. The theory assumes a resource allo-
cation trade-off between light and nutrient acquisition capacity, with opti-
mized allocation aimed at maximizing phytoplankton growth rates. Under
low-light and nutrient-replete cond itions, phytoplankton allocate more
resources to light acquisition and less to nutrient acquisition, resulting in a
substantial increase in chlorophyll content per unit of carbon biomass. The
theory aligns with results from incubation experiments
29,32 and is consistent
with the empirical cell quotamodel introduced by Droop33,34.A d d i t i o n a l l y ,
an ocean ecosystem model based on this theory successfully simulated the
global distribution of SCM depth
35.

Discussion
A limitation of the study is that our model implicitly represents the melt
pond effect on albedo, based on ice and snow temperature and thickness43.
Thus, the model may underestimate p rimary production in regions with
m e l tp o n d so ns e ai c e .A n o t h e rl i m i t a t i o ni st h a to u rm o d e ld o e sn o ti n c l u d e
ice algae inhabiting sea ice. Total Arctic primary production by the ice algae
is estimated to be 2.8 – 21 TgC year
−1 by multi-model comparison44.T h i s
amount is underestimated in our model. We employ a single general algae
whose parameters are tuned to reproduce global distributions, and there-
fore, special species adapted to A rctic conditions may not be fully
represented.
Model bias for surface chlorophyll a concentration is discussed below
(Supplementary Fig. 8). In the Arctic open-ocean areas where data exists for
both model and satellite estimate
45, excluding the Russian seas, the temporal
average of simulated chlorophyll a concentration from April to September is
0.79 μg L
−1, which is close to the satellite-estimated value of 0.78 μg L −1
(Supplementary Fig. 9). The correlation coefﬁcient between simulated and
satellite-estimated seasonal chlorophyll a variation is 0.96, although the
model tends to overestimate seasonal variation. The model overestimates ice
cover, and the reproducibility of grid- scale spatiotemporal variability is
insufﬁcient (Supplementary Fig. 8). It is dif ﬁcult to say whether the dis-
crepancy in chlorophyll a concentration in the Russian seas between the
model and satellite estimate indicates model bias. This is because chlor-
ophyll a concentrations in the Russian seas, particularly at the mouths of
major rivers, vary several times over, depending on the algorithms used to
retrieve them
45. The algorithms use different methods to distinguish
chlorophyll a from colored dissolvedorganic and inorganic matter, which
are abundant around river mouths. Observed nitrate concentrations in the
Russian seas from July to September fall below 2.0μmolN L−1 east of 110° E
(Supplementary Fig. 5b), and it is debatable whether strong phytoplankton
blooms exceeding a chlorophyll a concentration of 1.0μgL ⁻¹ can persist for
more than a few months, as in satelliteestimates, under such low-nutrient
conditions.
Our study shows that photoacclimation drives spatial variations in
chlorophyll content and thereby shapes the distribution of primary pro-
duction under sea ice and around SCM. Under low-light and high-nutrient
conditions, photoacclimation driveshigher chlorophyll content, which is
indispensable to simulate observed high chlorophyll concentration under
ice and around SCM. Since an increase in chlorophyll content increases the
phytoplankton growth rate (Eq. 1), higher chlorophyll content underpins
primary production under ice and around SCM. If a model does not ade-
quately reproduce observed SCM dep ths, it will not accurately simulate
chlorophyll content and resultant under-ice production. Through photo-
acclimation, we can understand the observed distributions of chlorophyll

The following procedure calculates the biological variables shown in
Fig. 1. 1. At each grid point, acclimation responding to daily-averaged solar
irradiance (in sunshine duration), nutrient concentration, and temperature
determines phytoplankton CN ratio and chlorophyll content per unit of
carbon biomass. This is a diagnostic calculation because the instant accli-
mation assumption
49 is applied. 2. Uptake of inorganic nitrogen by phyto-
plankton is derived from the product of the growth rate (photosynthetic rate
minus respiration rate) and phytoplankton nitrogen biomass at the previous
time step. Net primary production is the product of nitrogen uptake and the
phytoplankton CN ratio. 3. Phytopl ankton nitrogen biomass at the new
time step is calculated from nitrogen uptake. Phytoplankton carbon biomass
at the new time step is the product of phytoplankton nitrogen biomass and
the CN ratio. Chlorophyll a concentration is obtained by multiplying
phytoplankton carbon biomass and chlorophyll content per unit of carbon
biomass.
