UFMylation, a ubiquitin-like modiﬁcation, is crucial for cellular processes and
is linked to human diseases, including cancer. However, its role in cancer
remains unclear. Here, we report thatUFL1 promotes breast tumor growth by
remodeling lipid metabolism. Mechanistically, UFL1 interacts with and
UFMylates AKT, enhancing its localization at the endoplasmic reticulum and
phosphorylation by PDK1 and mTORC2, thereby increasing AKT-mediated
lipid synthesis. Moreover, AKT phosphorylates UFL1, boosting its activity.
Thus, UFL1 and AKT form a positive feedback loop, accelerating lipid synthesis
and breast tumor growth. Clinically, UFL1levels are increased in human breast
tumors and are associated with poor clinical outcomes in breast cancer
patients. Importantly, UFMylation inhibitors sensitize breast cancer cells to
AKT inhibitors and anticancer drugs. Our ﬁndings reveal a critical role for
UFMylation in lipid metabolism and identify the UFL1-AKT axis as a potential
therapeutic target in breast cancer.
UFMylation, a ubiquitin-like modiﬁcation, is sequentially catalyzed by
E1 (UBA5, ubiquitin-like modi ﬁer activating enzyme 5), E2 (UFC1,
ubiquitin-fold modiﬁer-conjugating enzyme 1), and E3 (UFL1, UFM1-
speciﬁc ligase 1), leading to the covalent conjugation of ubiquitin-fold
modiﬁer 1 (UFM1) to the lysine residues of substrates 1–3. Currently,
UFL1 is the sole E3 ligase for UFMylation; however, by itself, UFL1 is
unstable and inactive
4,5. UFBP1 (UFM1 binding protein 1) is essential for
UFL1 stability and activity1,6,7. UFMylation is a highly dynamic process,
and UFM1 can be removed from UFMylated substrates by UFSP1/2
(UFM1-speciﬁc proteases 1/2) to prevent excessive UFMylation
8–10.A
growing body of evidence highlights the functional importance of
UFMylation in various biological processes, such as DNA damage
repair
11–14 and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response 15,16.
Although UFMylation dysregulation has been implicated in cancers,
only a few UFMylation substrates have been identiﬁed in cancer cells,
and the functions and molecular mechanisms of UFMylation in cancer
remain largely uncharted. Moreover, UFMylation exhibits different, or
even opposing, functions depending on cancer types or subtypes
17.
Therefore, identifying novel UFMylation substrates and deciphering
their functions, as well as the underlying mechanisms, will advance our
understanding of the fundamental roles of UFMylation in cancer and
may potentially beneﬁt cancer treatment. Recent research shows that

Increased de novo lipid synthesis contributes to the unlimited
proliferation and survival of cancer cells and is therefore considered a
hallmark of cancer
20,21. The serine/threonine protein kinase AKT (also
known as protein kinase B, PKB) plays a central role in de novo lipo-
genesis by directly regulating the activity of ACLY (ATP citrate lyase) or
indirectly modulating the expression of ACC1 (acetyl-CoA carboxylase
1), FASN (fatty acid synthase), ACSS2 (acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain
family member 2), SCD1 (stearoyl-CoA desaturase), and HMGCR (3-
hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase), which are rate-limiting
enzymes in de novo lipid synthesis
22,23. In mammalian cells, there are
three AKT isoforms (AKT1, AKT2, and AKT3), which possess redundant
as well as unique functions 23. AKT1 is an oncogenic kinase that is
hyperphosphorylated and overactive in most human cancers 24,w i t h
elevated AKT1 activity observed in approximately 40% of breast
cancers
25. The recruitment of AKT1 to the plasma membrane or intra-
cellular membrane through binding of its PH (pleckstrin homology)
domain to PIP3 (phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate) is a pre-
requisite for AKT1 phosphorylation at threonine (T) 308 by PDK1 and

activity remain poorly understood.
In the present study, we report that a positive feedback loop
between UFL1 and AKT1 promotes breast tumor growth by repro-
gramming lipid metabolism. Mechanistically, UFL1 UFMylates AKT1
at lysines (K) 189, K276, and K297, disrupting the interaction between
the PH domain and KD (kinase domain) of AKT1, thereby facilitating
AKT1 localization at the ER and subsequent phosphorylation at T308
and S473 by PDK1 and mTORC2, respectively. Interestingly, AKT1
phosphorylation stimulates UFSP2-mediated AKT1 deUFMylation. On
the other hand, AKT1 phosphorylates UFL1 at T426, enhancing UFL1
activity and AKT1 UFMylation by UFL1. Activated AKT1 sustains
breast tumor growth by phosphorylating ACLY and inducing the
expression of several lipogenic genes to reinforce lipid synthesis.
Clinically, the mRNA and protein levels of UFL1 are both elevated in
breast cancer tissues and negatively correlate with the overall sur-
vival of breast cancer patients. Intriguingly, the levels of phos-
phorylated UFL1 and phosphorylated AKT1 display a positive
correlation in human breast tumors. Importantly, targeting UFMy-
lation and AKT1 exhibits a synergistic anticancer effect in vitro and
in vivo. Our ﬁndings delineate a mechanism in lipid metabolism via
the UFL1-AKT1 positive feedback loop and uncover this regulatory
loop as a potential therapeutic target in breast cancer.
Results
UFL1 possesses oncogenic potential in breast cancer
UFL1 is the only E3 ligase in the UFMylation system, but its expression
and function in cancers are highly context-dependent. Previous
studies on UFL1 in breast cancer, even within the same breast cancer
subtype, have led to controversial conclusions
32,33. To comprehen-
sively evaluate the clinical relevance of UFL1, we ﬁrst assessed its
expression levels in human breast tumors. Analysis of the Gene
Expression Proﬁling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) breast cancer data-
set revealed that UFL1 mRNA levels were signi ﬁcantly increased in
human breast tumors compared with normal breast tissues (Fig. 1A).
This elevation was observed in all breast cancer subtypes, including
Luminal A, Luminal B, human epidermal growth factor receptor
2-positive (HER2
+), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) (Sup-
plementary Fig. 1A). The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)
dataset also indicated the highest levels of UFL1 mRNA in stage III
breast cancers (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Importantly, GEPIA (Fig. 1B)
and GEO (Supplementary Fig. 1C) datasets both showed that high
levels of UFL1 mRNA were associated with decreased overall survival
in breast cancer patients. Furthermore, we performed immunohis-
tochemistry (IHC) assays and found that UFL1 protein levels were
markedly upregulated in breast tumors (Fig. 1C, D), and all breast
cancer subtypes showed elevated UFL1 protein levels (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 1D). Consistently, moderate and high levels of UFL1 protein
were associated with poor clinical outcomes in breast cancer patients
(Fig. 1E). A similar negative correlation was found in the
Kaplan–Meier Plotter dataset (Fig. 1F). Collectively, UFL1 expression
is increased in breast tumors and negatively correlates with clinical
outcomes in breast cancer patients.
The aforementioned ﬁndings prompted us to hypothesize that
UFL1 is an oncogenic protein in breast cancer. To con ﬁrm this
hypothesis, UFL1 was depleted in MCF-7 (Luminal A), BT474 (Luminal
B), SK-BR-3 (HER2
+), MDA-MB-231 (TNBC), and MDA-MB-468 (TNBC)
breast cancer cells using two independent shRNAs targeting UFL1
(shUFL1#1 and shUFL1#2) for cell growth assays. Compared with
scrambled shRNA (shSC), shUFL1#1 and shUFL1#2 dramatically sup-
pressed breast cancer cell growth (Fig. 1G, H; Supplementary
Fig. 1E–G). Due to the lack of effective therapeutic targets, TNBC is
much more aggressive than other breast cancer subtypes. Therefore,
we mainly focused on TNBC cells. Cell growth is coordinately con-
trolled by cell proliferation and cell death. Immunostaining assays
showed that UFL1 depletion dramatically downregulated Ki-67 (a cell
proliferation marker) protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 1H, I), while
increasing the protein levels of cleaved caspase-3 (CC3, a cell death
marker) (Supplementary Fig. 1J, K). Consistently, the intensity of
Annexin-V (a marker of early apoptotic cells) and propidium iodide (PI,
a marker of necrotic or late apoptotic cells) staining was signiﬁcantly
increased by UFL1 ablation, as evidenced by ﬂow cytometry assays
(Supplementary Fig. 1L, M). All these results suggested a critical role for
UFL1 in promoting proliferation and suppressing cell death. Next, we
conducted xenograft mouse assays. shSC, shUFL1#1, and shUFL1#2
MDA-MB-468 cells were implanted into the mammary fat pads of
female NOD-SCID mice. As expected, UFL1 depletion signi ﬁcantly
inhibited MDA-MB-468 tumor growth (Fig. 1I, J; Supplementary
Fig. 1N). Consistently, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining revealed
that UFL1 knockdown markedly enhanced cell death and impeded cell
proliferation, as evidenced by increased CC3-positive cell numbers and
decreased Ki-67-positive cell numbers, respectively (Fig.1K–N). Taken
together, these ﬁndings indicate that UFL1 promotes breast tumor
growth by increasing cell proliferation and repressing cell death.

UFL1 interacts with and UFMylates AKT
To elucidate the mechanism of UFL1-mediated lipid accumulation, we
performed liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-
MS/MS) assays using MDA-MB-231 cells stably expressing Flag-UFL1 to
identify potential substrates of UFL1. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that
AKT1 is a high-con ﬁdence potential UFL1-binding protein (Supple-
mentary Fig. 3A). Notably, several key components of the UFMylation
system, such as DDRGK1 (UFBP1), CDK5RAP3, UFM1 and UFC1, as well
as more than 10 well-known UFL1 substrates, including PARP1
14,
RPL2616,M R E 1 112,13 and p5341,w e r ea l s oi d e n t iﬁed in the list of putative
UFL1-interacting proteins (Supplementary Fig. 3A), con ﬁrming the
feasibility of our approach. Moreover, GSEA analysis via the BEST
database indicated that UFL1 may regulate the PI3K-AKT-mTOR sig-
naling pathway, which is critical for lipid metabolism (Supplementary
Fig. 3B). Consistent with the LC-MS/MS analysis, exogenous UFL1-
showed a robust interaction with ectopically expressed AKT1 but
weakly associated with AKT1 ’s paralogs, AKT2 or AKT3, in
HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Hence, we focused on AKT1.
The endogenous interaction between UFL1 and AKT1 was also
observed in MDA-MB-231 (Fig. 3A, B) and MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 3C, D)
cells. To further determine whether UFL1 directly binds to AKT1, we
performed in vitro GST pull-down assays using puriﬁed GST-AKT1 and
immunoprecipitated UFL1 from breast cancer cells. UFL1 was pulled
down by GST-AKT1 but not by GST alone (Fig. 3E), suggesting a direct
interaction between UFL1 and AKT1. Given that UFL1 predominantly
localizes to the perinuclear ER
6,7, we performed immunoﬂuorescence
staining and found that UFL1 and AKT1 mainly colocalized at the
perinuclear region of MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 3F; Supplementary
Fig. 3D), suggesting potential ER colocalization of UFL1 and AKT1 in
breast cancer cells. Next, we sought to identify the domains that
mediate the UFL1-AKT1 interaction. Domain-mapping assays indicated
that UFL1 associates with AKT1 via its N-terminus (Fig. 3G, H; Supple-
mentary Fig. 3E, F). Additionally, AKT1 associated with UFBP1 and UFC1
(Supplementary Fig. 3G, H), suggesting that UFL1 may modulate AKT1
in a UFMylation-dependent manner.
Indeed, AKT1 was UFMylated in breast cancer cells, and this
modiﬁcation was signiﬁcantly decreased by UFL1 ablation in MDA-MB-
231 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig.3I, J). In contrast, ectopic expression of
UFL1 in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells led to upregulation of
AKT1 UFMylation (Supplementary Fig. 3I, J). UFL1 is inactive on its own
and is activated by forming a complex with UFBP1
5,42,43. Mutation of
leucine (L) 45 to arginine (R) in UFL1 (L45R) disrupts the UFL1-UFBP1
interaction
5,42,43. To further assess the impact of UFL1 activation on
AKT1 UFMylation, we compared the effect of wild-type (WT) and L45R
mutant UFL1 and found that only WT UFL1, not L45R UFL1, was capable
of UFMylating AKT1 (Supplementary Fig. 3K). These data conﬁrm that
UFL1 UFMylates AKT1 in breast cancer cells. To identify potential
UFMylation sites in AKT1, we assessed UFMylation within AKT1’sc o n -
served domains, including the PH domain, KD, and CD (C-terminal
domain). Our results showed that UFMylation levels were signiﬁcantly
higher in the KD compared to the PH domain and CD (Fig. 3K), sug-
gesting that the primary UFMylation sites are located within the KD.
The KD contains six conserved lysine (K) residues (Supplementary
Fig. 3L), and we constructed Flag-AKT1 variants with single mutations
at each of these K residues to assess UFMylation in HEK293T cells.
Notably, single mutations of K to R at positions K189, K276, or
K297 signiﬁcantly reduced AKT1 UFMylation (Supplementary Fig. 3M),
indicating that these sites are key for AKT1 UFMylation. Indeed, a triple
mutation of these residues (referred to as 3 K/R) nearly abolished AKT1
UFMylation (Supplementary Fig. 3M). To further conﬁrm that AKT1 is
UFMylated at K189, K276, and K297 in TNBC cells, we expressed both
WT and 3 K/R mutant Flag-AKT1 at comparable levels in MDA-MB-231
and MDA-MB-468 cells. Compared with Flag-AKT1-WT, the UFMylation
levels of Flag-AKT1-3K/R were signiﬁcantly lower in these TNBC cells
(Fig. 3L, M). Similar results were obtained from MCF-7, BT474, and SK-
BR-3 breast cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. 3N –P). These ﬁndings
demonstrate that K189, K276, and K297 are key UFMylation sites in
AKT1 in breast cancer cells. Although K189, K276, and K297 are con-
served in AKT2 and AKT3, their UFMylation levels were lower than
those in AKT1 (Supplementary Fig. 3Q) potentially due to their weak
association with UFL1 (Supplementary Fig. 3 C), suggesting that AKT1 is
the primary substrate of UFL1. Intriguingly, other lipid synthetic
enzymes, such as FASN and Rictor (Supplementary Fig. 3A), were also
identiﬁed as potential UFL1-interacting proteins in LC-MS/MS analysis,
prompting us to test their UFMylation. Indeed, UFL1 associated with
and UFMylated FASN and Rictor in breast cancer cells (Supplementary
Fig. 3R–W). Compared with FASN and Rictor, AKT1 possesses a much
more comprehensive and profound impact on lipid synthesis, Hence,
we focused on AKT1. Collectively, our data demonstrate that UFL1
directly associates with AKT1 and UFMylates AKT1 at K189, K276, and
K297 in breast cancer cells.

UFMylation inhibitors synergize with AKT inhibitors in breast
cancer treatment
Although several ongoing clinical trials are investigating AKT inhi-
bitors in advanced solid tumors, the clinical bene ﬁts of these AKT
inhibitors have been limited due to their low efﬁcacy, non-selectivity,
and the development of resistance 57,58, necessitating a combination
of AKT inhibitors with other chemotherapies. UFMylation inhibitor
DKM 2-93 and AKT inhibitor MK-2206 both signi ﬁcantly decreased
AKT1 UFMylation and phosphorylation (Fig. 7A–D). Co-treatment
with DKM 2-93 and MK-2206 displayed a synergistic inhibitory effect
on AKT1 UFMylation (Fig. 7A, B), AKT1 phosphorylation (Fig. 7C, D;
Supplementary Fig. 7A), and lipid accumulation (Fig. 7E–H; Supple-
mentary Fig. 7B –E) in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells. Con-
sistently, DKM 2-93 and MK-2206 synergistically inhibited the
viability of these breast cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. 7F, G).
Activated AKT1 has been reported to alleviate the sensitivity of breast
cancer cells to clinical drugs, including cisplatin and etoposide
59.
Since DKM 2-93 markedly suppresses AKT1 activation, we examined
whether DKM 2-93 could sensitize breast cancer cells to these che-
motherapeutic agents. As expected, DKM 2-93 sensitized MDA-MB-
231 and MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells to both cisplatin and eto-

signiﬁcant reduction in tumor volume and lipid abundance com-

remained identical (Supplementary Fig. 7M). Similarly, the combi-

(S473) (Fig. 7L, N), p-UFL1(T426) (Fig. 7L, O), and cell proliferation

AKT or UFL1 (Supplementary Fig. 7N, Q). In summary, targeting AKT1
UFMylation represents a promising strategy for sensitizing breast
cancer cells to AKT inhibitors or chemotherapeutic agents.
Discussion
In this study, we uncover a positive feedback loop between UFL1
and AKT that drives breast tumor growth by reprogramming lipid
metabolism (Fig. 8). While UFMylation is increasingly recognized
for its critical roles in tumorigenesis, its substrates and functions in
cancer remain largely uncharted. Our work signi ﬁcantly expands
this understanding by identifying the central kinase AKT1 as a key
substrate of UFL1, the unique E3 ligase in the UFMylation system.
We demonstrate that UFL1-mediated UFMylation of AKT1 at K189,
K276, and K297 is a critical prerequisite for its phosphorylation,
full activation, and subsequent promotion of de novo lipid synth-
esis. Although these three lysine residues in the kinase domain are
the primary sites, our data suggest that additional sites in the PH
and C-terminal domains may also be identi ﬁed, because weak
UFMylation signals were observed in these domains. Importantly,
we demonstrate that AKT1 phosphorylation stimulates its deUF-
Mylation by UFSP2. These ﬁndings shed light on the mechanism by
which posttranslational modi ﬁcations, including ubiquitination
60,61
and SUMOylation 31, have no impact on the molecular weight of
phosphorylated AKT1. While AKT1 is a key substrate in this context,
other components of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR and lipid synthesis sig-
naling pathways (such as Rictor and FASN) are also identi ﬁed as
UFL1 substrates. This suggests that UFL1 modulate lipid synthesis
through multiple layers of mechanisms. A comprehensive analysis
of how UFL1 in ﬂuences these signaling pathways and their com-
ponents could further elucidate its role in cellular metabolism and
tumor progression.
The canonical model of AKT1 activation posits that PIP3 binding
via its PH domain promotes membrane localization, facilitates proxi-
mity to its activating kinases PDK1 and mTORC2, and allosterically
disrupts the auto-inhibitory intramolecular interaction between the PH
domain and KD
49–51,54. However, this model cannot fully explain the
observed desynchrony between transient membrane accumulation
and sustained phosphorylation
26. Moreover, other studies have shown
that PIP3 binding to the PH domain per se does not necessarily disrupt
the PH-KD interaction 44–48.O u r ﬁndings introduce UFMylation as a
crucial regulatory layer that directly addresses this gap. We show that
UFL1-mediated UFMylation triggers a conformational change in AKT1,
disrupting the intramolecular PH-KD interaction and thereby relieving
autoinhibition. Furthermore, UFL1, itself an ER-resident protein,
enhances the recruitment and retention of AKT1 to the endoplasmic
reticulum. This colocalization facilitates its phosphorylation by PDK1
and mTORC2 and strengthens its association with ER-derived PIP3.
Thus, our work provides a mechanistic explanation for how sustained,
localized AKT1 activation is achieved. Notably, posttranslational
modiﬁcations such as methylation
29,30,59, ubiquitination 60,61,
SUMOylation31, and acetylation 62 activate AKT1 through a similar
mechanism. A critical question for future research is how cancer cells
coordinate AKT1’s UFMylation and other posttranslational modi ﬁca-
tions to achieve and maintain the desired levels of AKT1 activation.
A major question in UFMylation research concerns the upstream
regulation of UFL1 itself. Previ ous studies have shown that UFL1 ’s
activity is modulated by phosphorylation in a context-dependent
manner
11,32. ATM phosphorylates UFL1 at S462 to enhance its ligase
activity and promote ATM activation in a positive feedback loop,
thereby facilitating DNA damage repair
11. AMPK phosphorylates UFL1
at T536, disrupting the UFL1-PD1 interaction and PD1 UFMylation to
trigger its degradation in T cells
32. Our study adds a new dimension to
this regulatory landscape by demonstrating that AKT1 phosphorylates
UFL1 at T426. This modi ﬁcation enhances UFL1 ’s E3 ligase activity
towards AKT1, creating a potent positive feedback loop that mutually
ampliﬁes the activities of both UFL1 and AKT1 to accelerate lipid
synthesis and tumor growth. The strong positive correlation between
p-UFL1(T426) and p-AKT1(S473) levels in human breast tumors
underscores the clinical relevance of this regulatory axis. Notably,
these ﬁndings also demonstrate that UFL1 phosphorylation at distinct
amino acids may determine substrate speci ﬁcity, ultimately control-
ling its diverse biological functions.
The expression and function of UFL1 in cancers are highly
heterogeneous
6,7. Previous studies in breast cancers, even within the
TNBC subtype, have been conﬂicting32,33. Our comprehensive analysis
clariﬁes this by demonstrating that UFL1 mRNA and protein levels are
consistently elevated across all major breast cancer subtypes, includ-
ing Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2
+, and TNBC. Importantly, UFL1-T426A
and AKT1-3K/R mutations diminish the promoting effect of UFL1 and
AKT1, respectively, in the lipid synthesis and growth of cell lines
representing these diverse subtypes. These data indicate that the UFL1-
AKT1 positive feedback loop is a ubiquitous driver of breast cancer, not
limited to a speciﬁc molecular subtype. Therefore, targeting the UFL1-
AKT1 axis could be a promising therapeutic strategy across different
breast cancer subtypes. Whether this axis functions in other cancer
types needs further investigation.
The evolutionarily conserved AKT serves as a central regulatory
hub in tumorigenesis and cancer progression. As such, AKT has been
considered an attractive therapeutic target in cancer treatment.
However, the clinical ef ﬁcacy of AKT inhibitors has been limited by
low potency, a lack of isoform selectivity, and acquired resistance
26.

synergistically suppresses AKT1 activation, lipid synthesis, and breast

(integrated intensity/area) of AKT1, p-AKT1 (S473), anti-p-AKT1 (T308),
UFL1, and p-UFL1 (T624) in xenograft tumors was determined using
Image-Pro Plus (Media Cybernetics). The human breast tumor tissue
microarrays (TMAs) consisting of 120 human breast cancer tissues and
46 adjacent normal breast tissues were purchased from the Tissue
Resource at The First Af ﬁliated Hospital of Jinan University, which
provided only the status of ER (estrogen receptor), HER2 (human
epidermal growth factor receptor 2), and PR (progesterone receptor)
of breast cancer tissues. Ethical approval (JNUKY-2023-0062, 2023/01/
01-2026/12/31) and informed consent have been obtained regarding
the use of patient tissues. The IHC staining of TMAs was quantitatively
scored by Drs. Yan Chen and Junjie Liang on a four-grade scale (0 –3)
based on the percentage of positive cells and the overall staining
intensity, as previously described
67. The sections were assigned by the
following proportion scores: 0 if 0% of the tumor cells displayed
positive staining, 1 for 0–1%, 2 for 2–10%, 3 for 11–30%, 4 for 31–70%, 5
for 71–100%. Additionally, the intensity of staining was assigned a four-
grated scale (0 –3). Grades 0 –3 represent: no expression (0), weak
expression (1), moderate expression (2), and strong expression (3). A
total score (range, 0 –8) was obtained by combining the proportion
and intensity scores. GraphPad Prism 8.0 was employed to perform the
Pearson correlation analysis (two-tailed). Note that the scores for some
samples overlap.
Statistical analysis
At least 3 samples prepared from independent experiments were used
to ensure adequate power for all studies. Statistical analysis was car-
ried out by one-way or two-way ANOVA with multiple testing correla-
tion within multiple groups, two-tailed t-test within two groups, two-
tailed Pearson correlation test, or log-rank test. Data were presented as
mean ± SD. Signiﬁcance was established at p < 0.05.
