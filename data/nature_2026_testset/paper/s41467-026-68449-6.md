Genome-wide association studies have identiﬁed 1p36.23 as a schizophrenia
risk locus. However, the functional variants and genes driving the association
remain unknown. Here, we identiﬁed two functional variants (i.e., rs159961 and
rs301792) at the 1p36.23 risk locus. Both variants reside introns of RERE and
exhibit allele-speciﬁc enhancer activity. Risk alleles of rs159961 and rs301792
increase enhancer activity by altering REST and POLR2A binding, leading to
RERE upregulation. Consistently,RERE was signiﬁcantly elevated in brains of
schizophrenia cases. Functionally,RERE-overexpression impaired neurogen-
esis, altered dendritic spine density and dendritic complexity, and altered
genes related to dendrite development and glutamatergic synapses. Through
interacting with RARB and RXRA at the Grin2a promoter, RERE regulates the
well-known schizophrenia risk geneGrin2a (encodes an NMDAR subunit), and
RERE-overexpression impairs excitatory synaptic transmission. Our study
indicates that functional variants rs159961 and rs301792 confer schizophrenia
risk by upregulating RERE, which affects neuronal development and synaptic
function.
Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a severe and heterogeneous psychiatric
disorder1 that affects approximately 1% of the global population. Due
to its high prevalence, mortality, and morbidity, SCZ imposes a sub-
stantial economic burden and ranks among the top ten causes of dis-
ability worldwide 2. Unfortunately, the etiology of SCZ remains
incompletely elucidated. Accumulating evidence indicates that
genetic factors play a major role in the development of the disease
3–5.
The heritability of SCZ is about 81% 3, underscoring the major con-
tribution of genetic factors. So far, genome-wide association studies
(GWASs) have identi ﬁed more than 300 SCZ risk loci
6–11. However,
translating these genetic ﬁndings into insights regarding the under-
lying pathomechanisms remains a major challenge. Identifying
potential causal variants and genes (or variants and genes with func-
tional consequences) from risk loci is the ﬁrst step toward a mechan-
istic understanding of disease biology. However, this step is hampered
by the linkage disequilibrium (LD) among risk variants and the com-
plex gene regulation process. As each risk locus usually contains
hundreds or thousands of risk variants that show high-level LD,
prioritizing causal variants from risk loci is a daunting task. Besides,
even if the causal variant has been pinpointed, identifying the target

To prioritize potential functional or causal variants from SCZ risk

functional variants as likely causal variants at the 1p36.23 risk locus, the
mechanisms by which they confer SCZ risk remain unknown. Speciﬁ-
cally, the molecular mechanisms of their regulatory effects, the iden-
tity of their target gene, and the role of this gene in SCZ pathogenesis
remain to be clariﬁed.
In this study, weﬁrst demonstrated the regulatory mechanisms of
the two variants with dual-luciferase reporter gene assays and elec-
trophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA). We then identi ﬁed RERE as
the target gene regulated by these variants through expression
quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-
out assays, and TF knockdown assays, among others. Finally, we
showed that RERE plays a critical role in regulating neurogenesis,
dendritic spine morphogenesis, dendritic complexity, the expression
of N-methyl-

and genes at the 1p36.23 risk locus and elucidated their underlying
mechanisms in SCZ. Theseﬁndings translate GWAS results into disease
biology and provide important insights into the pathogenesis of SCZ.
Results
rs159961 and rs301792 are functional SNPs at the 1p36.23
risk locus
GWASs have shown that 1p36.23 is a robust risk locus for SCZ6–8,10.T o
pinpoint the likely causal variants from this locus, we previously used a
functional genomics approach to identify functional (i.e., TF binding-
affecting) SNPs. Our functional genomics approach showed that
rs159961 and rs301792 are functional variants at the 1p36.23 risk
locus
12. To further explore whether rs159961 and rs301792 are poten-
tial causal variants, we performed ﬁne-mapping using SuSiE 13,
CARMA14, and FINEMAP 15. Genetic associations from populations of
European-ancestry11 were used as input, and the results indicated the
presence of an independent causal signal at this locus. SuSiE analysis
yielded a 95% credible set that includes 55 SNPs. CARMAﬁne-mapping
identiﬁed 102 SNPs, which comprise the 99% credible set. The 92%
credible set comprised 50 variants in FINEMAP analysis. Notably, both
rs301792 and rs159961 were included in the credible sets in all three
analyses, with low posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) (rs301792
(PIP of SuSiE: 0.0073, CARMA: 0.0052, FINEMAP: 0.0066) and
rs159961 (PIP of SuSiE: 0.0066, CARMA: 0.0046, FINEMAP: 0.0058)).
The full results of the ﬁne-mapping are provided in supplementary
material (Supplementary Data 1–4).
SNPs rs159961 and rs301792 showed strong associations with SCZ
in European populations
10. These associations were conﬁrmed in both
East Asian populations (29,519 cases, 44,392 controls) and cross-
ancestry populations (96,806 cases and 492,818 controls) in our
replication study
11, with the same risk alleles as in Europeans (Fig. 1a
and Supplementary Fig. 1). These results indicate that these two var-
iants are credible risk variants for SCZ. Both rs159961 and rs301792 are
located in the intronic regions of RERE (Fig. 1b), and the intronic
sequences containing rs159961 and rs301792 show enrichment for
REST and POLR2A binding motifs (Fig. 1c–f). To validate the
functionality of these two SNPs, we examined whether these two SNPs
are located in active regulatory elements. Epigenomic data indicated
that rs159961 and rs301792 are located in open chromatin regions (as
detected by DNase-seq) that were marked with enhancer-associated
histone modiﬁcations (H3K27ac, H3K4me1, and H3K9ac) in human
neuronal cells and human brain tissues (Fig.1c, d). To further assess the
functional consequences of rs159961 and rs301792, we conducted
dual-luciferase reporter gene assays. Both risk alleles (rs159961-T and
rs301792-G) exhibited increased enhancer activity in SH-SY5Y cells (a
human neuroblastoma cell line) compared with protective alleles
(rs159961-C and rs301792-A) (Fig. 1g). In U251 cells (a human glio-
blastoma cell line), the rs159961-T allele also exhibited higher enhancer
activity than the C allele, whereas rs301792 alleles showed no sig-
niﬁcant difference (Fig. 1h). Notably, the allele-speci ﬁc enhancer
effects were absent in HEK-293T cells (Fig.1i), indicating the regulatory
effects of these variants are neuronal-dependent. Collectively, these
results support the regulatory effects of these two functional variants.
Molecular regulatory mechanisms of rs159961 and rs301792
Our reporter gene assays revealed the neuronal-dependent regulatory
effects of rs159961 and rs301792. To dissect the molecular mechan-
isms underlying these neuronal-dependent regulatory effects, we
conducted EMSA. The EMSA showed that different alleles of rs159961
and rs301792 affect the binding afﬁnity of TFs (Fig. 1j–m). The super-
shift assay indicated that rs159961 binds to REST, as a super-shift band
was observed when REST antibody was added (Fig. 1l). The risk allele
(T allele) of rs159961 attenuated binding to REST compared with the
protective allele (C allele) (Fig. 1j, l). Different alleles of rs301792 also
exhibited differential binding to POLR2A (band 1 in Fig. 1k, m), as the
binding intensity was decreased when POLR2A antibody was added
(Fig. 1m). The risk allele (G allele) of rs301792 demonstrated stronger
binding ability to POLR2A compared with the A allele (Fig. 1k, m).
Taken together, these results indicate that functional genetic variants
rs159961 and rs301792 regulate the enhancer activity by affecting the
binding afﬁnity of REST and POLR2A, respectively.
Risk alleles of rs159961 and rs301792 confer SCZ risk by upre-
gulating RERE expression
To further identify the target gene (s) regulated by these two func-
tional variants, we examined their associations with gene expression in
the human brain. The largest brain eQTL dataset, BrainMeta (v2), which
was generated using human brain tissues, was explored
16.B o t h
rs159961 and rs301792 showed signi ﬁcant associations with the
expression of RERE (P =1 . 3 9×1 0⁻⁴⁸ and P =1 . 9 8×1 0⁻⁴⁹), RERE-AS1
(P =4 . 5 6×1 0⁻⁸ and P =4 . 7 2×1 0⁻⁸), and SLC45A1 (P =1 . 5 1×1 0⁻⁴ and
P =1 . 6 4×1 0⁻⁴)( F i g .2a). The strongest associations were observed for
RERE, suggesting that these two functional genetic variants may confer
SCZ risk by regulating RERE expression. In addition, eQTL analysis
using postmortem adult brain tissues also showed that the risk alleles
of rs159961 and rs301792 were associated with higher expression of
RERE (Fig. 2b–e), consistent with the results of reporter gene assays
(Fig. 1g). To test the colocalization posterior probability (PP) between
the 1p36.23 locus and the eQTLs forRERE, we conducted colocalization
analysis. The results showed that the colocalization PP of the 1p36.23
locus with the eQTLs for RERE was high (0.854), supporting that
genetic variants in this locus confer SCZ risk by regulating RERE
expression. However, we noticed that the colocalization PP for SNP
rs159961 and rs301792 was relatively low, SNP.PP.H4 = 0.09 for
rs301792, and SNP.PP.H4 = 0.013 for rs159961 (Supplementary Data 5).
These consistent results suggest that risk alleles of these two func-
tional variants contribute to SCZ risk by upregulatingRERE expression.

rs159961 or rs301792 sites (Fig. 2f–h), followed by quanti ﬁcation of

RERE affects the proliferation and differentiation of mNSCs
Our consistent and convergent results suggest that risk alleles of
rs159961 and rs301792 upregulate RERE expression, which in turn
contributes to SCZ susceptibility. To explore the potential role ofRERE
in SCZ pathophysiology, we examined its function in neurodevelop-
ment using the NSC model, which is widely used in delineating SCZ
pathogenesis19.W e ﬁrst examined RERE expression in major cell types
in the human and mouse brain using RNA-seq data from Zhang et al.20,21
and Yao et al.22. The results showed that RERE was broadly expressed,
with relatively higher expression levels in neurons and astrocytes
(Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). Further spatiotemporal expression showed
that RERE was highly expressed in the prenatal human brain 23 (Sup-
plementary Fig. 6d), implying that this gene may have a role in neu-
rodevelopment. The proliferation and differentiation of NSCs are
crucial for brain development, and previous studies have shown that
SCZ risk genes regulate these processes
24–29. We obtained mNSCs from
the subventricular zone (SVZ) of mouse brains at E14.5 and validated
their identity using three well-characterized markers, SOX2, PAX6, and
NESTIN (Fig. 3a). To mimic the effect of RERE upregulation on neuro-
development, we overexpressedRERE in mNSCs (Fig.3b). Proliferation
assays showed that RERE overexpression inhibited the proliferation
capacity of mNSCs (Fig. 3c–e). Furthermore, cell-cycle analysis
revealed a signiﬁcant increase in the proportion of cells in the G2/M-
phase in the RERE-overexpression group compared with the controls,
indicating that RERE-overexpression induced cell-cycle arrest at the
G2/M-phase (Fig. 3f–h and Supplementary Fig. 7).
We also investigated the effect of RERE-overexpression on the
differentiation of mNSCs. We used markers for astrocytes (GFAP),
mature neurons (MAP2), oligodendrocytes (CLDN11), and immature
neurons (TUBB3). After three days ofspontaneous differentiation, the
protein levels of GFAP and MAP2 were measured using IF (Fig. 4a–d),
and the expression levels of Gfap, Map2, Cldn11,a n d Tubb3 were
assessed using qPCR (Fig.4e–h). The proportion of GFAP-positive cells
did not differ signi ﬁcantly between the RERE-overexpression and
control groups (Fig.4a, c), indicating that overexpression ofRERE did
not affect astrocytic differentiation of mNSCs. However, the propor-
tion of MAP2-positive cells was signi ﬁcantly reduced in the RERE-
overexpression group compared with controls (Fig.4b, d), indicating
that RERE-overexpression inhibited neurogenesis. Consistent with IF
results, qPCR analysis also revealed that Map2 and Tubb3 expression
were signiﬁcantly downregulated in the RERE-overexpression group
compared with controls (Fig.4f, h). By contrast, the expression ofGfap
and Cldn11 was unchanged (Fig. 4e, g). Western blot results further
conﬁrmed that MAP2 protein levels were signi
ﬁcantly reduced,
whereas GFAP levels did not differ between the RERE-overexpression
and control groups (Fig. 4i–l). Taken together, these results indicate
that RERE-overexpression impairs neural development by inhibiting
the proliferation and differentiation of mNSCs.

RERE-RXRA/RARB complex regulatesGrin2a via promoter
binding
To investigate how RERE-overexpression in neurons leads to the
downregulation of Grin2a,w e ﬁrst performed TF binding predictions
to explore if the promoter region (~2000 bp upstream of the tran-
scription start site, TSS) of Grin2a contains binding sites for RERE-
related TFs. This analysis revealed the presence of motifs for RARB and
RXRA (Fig. 8a, b and Supplementary Fig. 10). Within this region, we
identiﬁed two fragments, designated F1 and F2, both of which con-
tained putative binding motifs f or RARB and RXRA (Supplementary
Fig. 10). RERE has been reported to function as a nuclear receptor
modulator that interacts with the RAR/RXR heterodimer to regulate
gene expression through retinoic acid response elements (RAREs)
32.
We therefore hypothesized that RERE repressesGrin2a expression via
this pathway. To test this, weﬁrst performed a dual-luciferase reporter
assay and found that both F1 and F2 were transcriptionally active
(Fig. 8c). However, their regulatory activity was attenuated uponRERE-
overexpression (Fig. 8d and Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting that
RERE acts as a repressor. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments
further demonstrated that RERE interacted with RXRA and RARB in rat
neurons (Fig. 8e). To conﬁrm the direct binding of RARB and RXRA to
these promoter fragments, we performed Chromatin immunopreci-
pitation (ChIP)-qPCR, which veriﬁed their enrichment at fragments F1
and F2 (Fig. 8f, g). Finally, knocking down Rxra or Rarb in rat neurons
resulted in a signi ﬁcant downregulation of Grin2a expression
(Fig. 8h–k). Together, these ﬁndings indicate that RERE interacts with
RARB and RXRA at the promoter region of Grin2a to repress Grin2a
expression (Fig. 8l).
Discussion
SCZ is a complex polygenic disorder. The neurodevelopmental
hypothesis of SCZ posits that SCZ is caused by a combination of
environmental and genetic factors, which impact normal brain
development
45. Genetic factors have a dominant role in the patho-
physiology of SCZ3. Over the past two decades, genetic studies of SCZ
have advanced rapidly. To date, GWASs have identiﬁed over 300 risk
loci for SCZ6–11. Although theseﬁndings provide a pivotal starting point
for delineating the pathogenesis of SCZ, major obstacles still exist.
Identifying potential causal variants and genes from the reported risk
loci is the ﬁrst step in translating GWAS ﬁndings into disease biology.
However, this step is hampered by complex LD between risk variants
and the complicated regulatory mechanisms of risk variants. In this
study, we systematically delineate the molecular mechanisms of the
1p36.23 risk locus in SCZ.
Accumulating evidence indicates that 1p36.23 is an important risk
locus for SCZ. In 2014, the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC)
identiﬁed 108 risk loci, including the locus at 1p36.23
6. The association
between the 1p36.23 locus and SCZ was replicated in subsequent
studies by Li et al. 7 and Pardinas et al. 8, respectively. In 2022, an
expanded PGC GWAS (PGC-SCZ3) again conﬁrmed the important role
of the 1p36.23 locus in SCZ 10. Furthermore, ﬁne-mapping combined
with summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) analysis
nominatedRERE at 1p36.23 as one of 120 candidate genes10. Consistent
with these reports, our study con ﬁrmed the association of rs159961
and rs301792 with SCZ in an East Asian population, with the same risk
alleles11. These consistent genetic ﬁndings strongly suggest that
1p36.23 is a pivotal risk locus for SCZ.
We ﬁrst characterized the genetic regulatory mechanisms of the
1p36.23 risk locus. We identiﬁed two functional variants (rs159961 and
rs301792) and demonstrated their molecular regulatory mechanisms.
The risk and protective alleles of these variants exhibited differential
binding afﬁnities to REST and POLR2A, leading to differential enhancer
activity. The risk allele rs159961-T attenuated the binding of the tran-
scriptional repressor REST, whereas the risk allele rs301792-G
enhanced the binding of POLR2A. Consistent with these effects, both
rs159961-T and rs301792-G exhibit increased enhancer activity.
Reporter gene assays demonstrated that the two variants exhibit
cell–type–speciﬁc enhancer activity in SY5Y cells (Fig. 1g). Analysis of
single-cell eQTL data from Fujita et al.
46 and Bryois et al. 47 further
showed signiﬁcant associations between these two variants and RERE
expression in astrocytes (Supplementary Table 3), supporting cell-
type-speciﬁc regulatory effect of rs159961 and rs301792. These results
indicate that the two variants are functional and their regulatory
effects are cell-type-speciﬁc. Interestingly, both variants are located in
intronic regions of RERE and show robust associations with RERE
expression in the human brain, suggesting thatRERE is the target gene
through which these variants inﬂuence SCZ risk. Consistent with this,
knockout of the genomic elements containing these variants provided
further evidence for their role in regulating RERE. These consistent
results indicate that rs159961 and rs301792 contribute to SCZ risk by
regulating RERE expression. In line with these ﬁndings, TWAS also
supports RERE as an SCZ risk gene, with genetically predicted upre-
gulation associated with increased SCZ risk. Moreover, our lines of
evidence not only demonstrate the regulatory mechanisms of rs159961
and rs301792 but also prioritize RERE as the likely causal gene at
1p36.23 risk locus. Of note, rs159961 is in high LD with rs301792
(r
2 > 0.8), indicating the presence of two highly linked functional var-
iants at the 1p36.23 risk locus. Indeed, a previous study has shown that
a proportion of risk loci identi ﬁed by GWAS contain more than one
causal variant48.
In addition to elucidating the molecular regulatory mechanisms
of the 1p36.23 risk locus, we also investigated the role of RERE in the
pathophysiology of SCZ. Both reporter gene assays and eQTL data
suggested that RERE upregulation is associated with increased risk of
SCZ. We therefore mimickedRERE elevation by overexpression assays.
We found that RERE-overexpression led to abnormal neurodevelop-
ment, including impaired proliferation of mNSCs and neurogenesis.
Furthermore,RERE-overexpression affected dendritic complexity and
dendritic spine density, indicating its pivotal role in dendrite mor-
phogenesis. Our results indicate that RERE may confer SCZ risk by
affecting neurodevelopment and dendritic spine density. Though the
pathophysiology of SCZ remains unclear, multiple lines of evidence
support the neurodevelopmental hypothesis of SCZ

previous studies have investigated the function ofRERE using loss-of-
function strategies, which do not model the upregulation of RERE
observed in SCZ cases. However, previous studies have provided evi-
dence associating RERE-overexpression with an increased suscept-
ibility to SCZ
18,57. Therefore, we used an overexpression strategy in this
study to explore the role ofRERE in SCZ. Our results indicate thatRERE
may confer SCZ risk by disrupting neurodevelopment.
An intriguing ﬁnding of our study is that RERE regulates the
expression of the NMDAR subunit GRIN2A. Both common and rare
variants in GRIN2A have been signi ﬁcantly associated with SCZ,
underscoring the pivotal role of GRIN2A in SCZ 10,39. The Schizo-
phrenia Exome Sequencing Meta-Analysis (SCHEMA) 39 identiﬁed
GRIN2A as one of ten high-con ﬁdence risk genes for SCZ in a large-
scale exome sequencing study. In mice, reduced NMDAR expression
causes SCZ-like behavior that can be ameliorated with antipsychotics
such as haloperidol or clozapine
58. Research shows that individuals
with SCZ exhibit speci ﬁc changes in gene expression related to
NMDAR subunits in the prefrontal cortex, including down-regulation
of NR2A (GRIN2A) expression
59. NMDARs are crucial for synaptic
transmission and plasticity 60. Furthermore, phosphorylation of the
NR1 subunit serine(S)897 of NMDAR is notably reduced in patients
with SCZ, impacting synaptic transmission and plasticity
61. Accumu-
lating evidence indicates that dysfunction of the glutamatergic sys-
tem plays an important role in SCZ. Our study shows that RERE
contributes to SCZ risk through regulating the expression and
function of GluN2A.
There are several limitations of this study. First, our ﬁne-
mapping and colocalization analyses did not provide strong causal
evidence for rs301792 and rs159961. Nevertheless, it should be noted
that ﬁne-mapping primarily relies on statistical inference; hence,ﬁne-
mapping strategies inherently remain correlative in nature. In con-
sequence, it remains unclear which of the ﬁne-mapped variants are
indeed functional and which are merely statistical bystanders. In fact,
recent massively parallel reporter assays (MPRA) studies
62,63 showed
that many variants inferred by ﬁne-mapping were not supported by
functional experimental validation. MPRA ﬁndings showed that
posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) showed no signi ﬁcant differ-
ence between functional and non-functional variants, and statistical
ﬁne-mapping alone cannot effectively discriminate MPRA-positive
(i.e., functional) and MPRA-negative (i.e., non-functional) variants.
These ﬁndings suggest that statistical ﬁne-mapping may help to
identify potential causal variants for loci with small credible sets and
simple LD patterns, but may be less effective for loci characterized by
complex LD structures and larger credible sets
62,63. Thus, variants
with low statistical ﬁne-mapping posterior inclusion probabilities are
not necessarily non-causal functional variants. These observations
are consistent with our comprehensive functional analyses and
highlight the limitations of relying solely on statistical ﬁne-mapping
or colocalization analyses to identify causal variants. These data also
underscore the importance of integrating functional genomics and
experimental validation to elucidate the biological mechanisms
underlying genetic associations. However, we acknowledge that
other functional variants within this region may contribute to SCZ
risk and warrant further investigation. Second, while we have pro-
vided lines of evidence supporting that rs159961 and rs301792 confer
SCZ risk by modulating RERE expression, our genome editing assays
involved deleting about 300 bp regions containing rs159961 or
rs301792. Single-base deletion or editing is needed to con ﬁrm whe-
ther RERE is the only target of these variants. Third, our conditional
eQTL analyses suggest that rs159961 and rs301792 do not act inde-
pendently in regulating RERE expression. Haplotype analysis indi-
cates that only the diplotype AC/GT at rs159961 and rs301792 is
associated with increased RERE expression, implying a potential
synergistic effect. Further experimental validation is needed to
investigate whether rs159961 and rs301792 act independently or
synergistically to regulate RERE. Fourth, neuronal physiological
analyses in this study were performed in rat primary neurons rather
than mouse NPC-derived neurons. RERE is conserved across human
and rodent species, and rat primary neurons reliably achieve func-
tional maturation in vitro
25,26, allowing reproducible assessment of
neuronal morphology and synaptic function following RERE over-
expression. Although mouse NPC-derived neurons are valuable for
modeling early neurodevelopment, their increased heterogeneity
and incomplete maturation can complicate physiological
analyses
64,65. Several limitations should be considered: species-
speciﬁc differences between rat and mouse may in ﬂuence pheno-
typic outcomes; mature primary neurons may preferentially re ﬂect
later-stage neuronal functions rather than early developmental pro-
cesses; overexpression relies on exogenous constructs with potential
variability in transcript usage and expression ef ﬁciency; and precise
control of gene dosage remains challenging, with supraphysiological
expression potentially deviating from endogenous regulation.
Despite these limitations, this validated primary neuron model pro-
vides a controlled system to investigate the impact of RERE on neu-
ronal structure and synaptic function. Finally, while we used in vitro
models (neural stem cells and primary neurons), future studies
employing genetically modiﬁed animal models are necessary to fully
understand the role of RERE in SCZ pathophysiology in vivo.
In summary, our study provides consistent and convergent lines
of evidence that rs159961 and rs301792 confer SCZ risk by regulating
RERE, a gene that plays an important role in neurodevelopment and
dendrite morphogenesis (Fig. 9). Our study not only elucidates the
biological mechanisms of the 1p36.23 risk locus in SCZ but also pro-
vides a framework for translating GWASﬁndings into disease biology.
