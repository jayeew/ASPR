Elevated sugar intake has been linked to poor cardiovascular health, but the
effects of early-life sugar restriction on the lifelong risk of heart failure remain
unclear. Using the end of sugar rationing in the United Kingdom in 1953 as a
natural experiment, we classify participants in the UK Biobank by birth timing
to deﬁne early-life exposure to sugar rationing. Here we show that individuals
exposed to early-life sugar rationing exhibit an approximately 14% lower risk of
heart failure and develop the condition about 2.6 years later than those
unexposed. Longer exposure is associated with stronger effects, and
population-level estimates suggest that around 4– 5% of heart failure cases may
be attributable to the absence of early-life sugar restriction. Genetic suscept-
ibility does not modify these associations but acts additively. These ﬁndings
underscore the long-term importance of early-life sugar exposure for heart
failure, and further studies are warranted.
Heart failure (HF) is characterised as a syndrome comprising symp-
toms or signs resulting from cardiac abnormalities and is conﬁrmed by
elevated natriuretic peptides or evidence of congestion 1.E s t i m a t e s
from the Global Burden of Disease Study indicate that approximately
55.5 million individuals globally were affected by HF in 2021
2,3. Notably,
HF is associated with considerable health challenges, including
reduced quality of life, recurrent hospitalisations, rising healthcare
expenses and an increased risk of premature death
4.
High sugar intake is a potential risk factor for HF. Previous pro-
spective studies in adults have demonstrated a strong correlation
between increased consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs)
or artiﬁcially sweetened beverages (ASBs) and the incidence of HF
5– 7.
Although restricting sugar intake during childhood or early life had
been considered to mitigate cardiometabolic risk factors such as
hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), and hypercholesterolemia 8– 11,
evidence inadequate to determine whether restricting sugar intake
during pregnancy or early life reduced the risk of HF later in life.

Table 2 illustrates that participants exposed to sugar rationing
exhibited a signiﬁcantly lower risk of developing HF than those who
were not exposed (Cox regression, hazard ratio [HR] = 0.87, 95% con-

tions (PAF) analysis indicated that undergoing sugar rationing in the
initial 1000 days of life may have averted roughly 4%– 5% of HF events.
Compared to those who were never rationed + born 21 months after
rationing, individuals exposed to sugar rationing for a longer duration
had a lower risk of developing HF ( P for trend <0.05) (Table 2).
Additional and sensitivity analysis
We further subdivided the duration of rationing into six-month inter-
vals, yielding similar conclusions (Supplementary Table 1). Upon con-
trolling for covariates, participants exposed to sugar rationing
exhibited a delay of 2.57 years in the onset age of HF relative to those
not exposed. The delay increased with the length of sugar rationing,
reaching up to 4.50 years (Supplementary Table 2).
No signi ﬁcant multiplicative interaction was found between
genetic susceptibility and sugar rationing, indicating that genetic
susceptibility did not signiﬁcantly alter the association between sugar
rationing and HF. A notable additive interaction was observed
(Table 3), exhibiting a positive synergistic effect (the relative excess
risk due to interaction [RERI] or the attributable proportion due to
interaction [AP] > 0, P < 0.05). Individuals with high genetic suscept-
ibility who were not exposed to sugar rationing exhibited an increased
risk of developing HF.

Discussion
We employed a natural experiment in the UK, revealing that sugar
rationing during the initial 1000 days of life reduced the lifelong risk of
HF and delayed its onset, especially in middle age. The effects of sugar
rationing ampliﬁed with prolonged periods of restricted sugar intake.
A notable positive synergistic effect was observed between genetic
susceptibility and early-life sugar rationing.
Prior research has emphasised the potential risk of high sugar
intake for HF. Zhang et al. conducted a study involving 209,829 adults
from the UKB and discovered that compared with nonconsumers,
individuals who consumed over 2 L per week of SSBs or ASBs exhibited a
22% and 30% heightened risk of developing HF, respectively, during a
median follow-up of 9.9 years
7. Rahman et al. conducted a study
involving 42,400 males aged 45– 79 years, revealing that individuals who
consumed two or more servings of SSBs daily exhibited a 23% increased
risk of developing HF compared with nonconsumers
5.Y a n ge ta l .
examined 109,034 females involved in the Women ’sH e a l t hI n i t i a t i v e
and discovered that females who consumed one or more servings of
SSBs daily exhibited a 35% higher risk of developing HF compared with
those who consumed fewer than one serving of SSB weekly
6.
Evidence demonstrating how elevated sugar consumption in early
life contributes to the onset of HF is scarce. Nevertheless, numerous
studies have investigated intermediate outcomes
17. Gracner et al.
observed the protective effects of early-life sugar restriction against
type 2 DM and hypertension, utilising a study design akin to that of the
current research
8. Leermakers et al. conducted a study involving 1316
Dutch children and discovered that increased consumption of SSBs at
13 months correlated with elevated cardiometabolic risk factor scores
at 6 years of age. This score is a continuous measure derived from body
fat percentage, blood pressure and insulin, high-density lipoprotein
(HDL) cholesterol and triglyceride levels
9. Numerous additional stu-
dies have recognised the beneﬁts of sugar restriction for cardiometa-
bolic health in children and adolescents, correlating with elevated HDL
levels, diminished obesity rates, decreased triglycerides, total choles-
terol, insulin resistance and systolic blood pressure
10,11,18– 20. Additional

should be exercised in extrapolating theseﬁndings to practical contexts.

Methods
Background
The UK National Food Survey (NFS), initiated in 194029, offers historical
data on population dietary patterns.The survey initially targeted urban
working-class households to assess wartime food policies. Beginning
in 1950, it was broadened to encompass a nationally representative
sample that included urban and rural regions, providing a more thor-
ough insight into dietary habits. A strati ﬁed sampling design cate-
gorised households by residential area, social class and household
composition to ensure representativeness. Surveys were administered
quarterly, encompassing the initial two months of each quarter. Par-
ticipating households documented all food purchases and consump-
tion, excluding sweets, mineral water and alcoholic beverages. Food
was quantiﬁed by weight or volume (e.g., grams and liters) and con-
verted into per capita intake, energy content and essential nutrients
(including protein, fat, carbohydrates and cereals) by using standar-
dised food composition tables. Additional information can be found at
https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/family-food-historic-
reports.
According to the UK NFS, per capita daily consumption of foods and
nutrients, including protein, fat and produce, remained relatively stable
before and after the sugar rationing period (1950 Q1 – 1953 Q2), while
sugar intake signiﬁcantly increased (Fig.1a and Supplementary Fig. 2).
Signiﬁcantly, absolute variations in average individual energy intake were
negligible, with relative changes of only 3%, approximately three-
quarters of which were due to heightened sugar consumption. This
proportion may be underestimated because the NFS omitted sweets and
implemented a 10% adjustment to caloric intake for potential wastage
and food acquired outside the home (Supplementary Figs. 3– 5). Conse-
quently, concentrating on a limited temporal framework with largely
analogous cohort attributes and negligible dietary alterations aside from
sugar consumption facilitates the discernment of the independent
effects of sugar rationing on health outcomes.

comprising over 500,000 participants from England, Scotland, and
Wales, recruited between 2006 and 2010

Follow-up of outcome
In the UKB, the initial occurrences of health-related outcomes for
participants were ascertained by amalgamating self-reported data,
primary care records, hospital admissions and death registry infor-

the date of initial diagnosis. Follow-up outcomes were delineated as
the earliest manifestation of HF, loss to follow-up or the end of the
follow-up period (December 2022).
Covariates
This study primarily utilised a birth cohort design, focusing on the
management of early-life risk factors. The exposure period to sugar
rationing was conﬁned to the initial 1000 days of life, encompassing
both infancy and the prenatal stage. Currently, conventional risk fac-
tors for HF, such as smoking, physical activity, BMI, hypertension and
diabetes history, are either unquantiﬁable or lack clinical signiﬁcance
and comparability. Consequently, drawing from prior research
8,t h e
primary analysis considered the following time-invariant covariates:
birthplace, sex, race, parental CVD, parental hypertension, parental
DM, calendar month of birth, and PRS for HF.
The birthplace was classiﬁed as England, Wales or Scotland. Sex
was classiﬁed as male and female. Race was categorised as White and
other classi ﬁcations. Parental CVD was de ﬁned as the participant
indicating that either their father or mother had experienced heart
disease or stroke. Parental hypertension and DM were also self-
reported. Calendar month of birth indicators (with January as the
reference category) were incorporated to account for seasonality. The
PRS for HF was computed using summary statistics from the genome-
wide association study conducted by Shah et al.
31 and PRSice-2, which
calculates PRS across a range of thresholds to determine the optimal
one for explaining phenotypic variance (R

Two models were used to assess the association between the status
and duration of sugar rationing and the risk of HF over the life course.
The initial method employed was traditional Cox regression, which
posits that the inﬂuence of exposure variables on the outcome remains
constant over time, referred to as the proportional hazards assump-

sure factors to ﬂuctuate over time. These models employ penalised
splines to reﬁne the hazard function, adeptly encapsulating intricate,
nonlinear associations

of birth, birthplace, sex, race, parental CVD, parental hypertension,
parental DM, and PRS for HF. Furthermore, PAFs were calculated using
Miettinen’s formula
37,38, where PAF = P c (R − 1)/R, P c represents the
proportion of HF cases unexposed to sugar rationing, and R is the
estimated HR of HF for the unexposed versus the exposed group. The
95% CIs were calculated using the delta method
39.
We further subdivided the duration of rationing into six-month
intervals and used linear regression to examine the association
between the status and duration of sugar rationing and the age of HF
onset. Additionally, the interaction between genetic susceptibility and
sugar rationing on HF risk was evaluated. To assess the multiplicative
interaction, we incorporated an interaction term between the two
factors in regression model and computed the RERI and AP to evaluate
the additive interaction
40,41.
The robustness of our ﬁndings was assessed using two sensitivity
analyses. First, we reexamined the association between sugar rationing
and HF with a parametric proportional hazards model based on the
Gompertz distribution, which assumes that the hazard increases
exponentially over time. Second, we discarded the initial birth cohort
design, which commenced follow-up at birth, and opted to utilise the

analysis. Four categories of potential confounders were considered: (1)

hypertension, and CVD; (3) lifestyle factors, including physical activity,
smoking, alcohol consumption, and dietary patterns; and (4) early-life
growth and developmental factors. Socioeconomic status was asses-

or stroke. Physical activity was categorised into walking, moderate and
