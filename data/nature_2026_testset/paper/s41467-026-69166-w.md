Active galactic nuclei (AGN) with broad emission line regions (BLRs) contain
rapidly growing supermassive black holes. Their masses are typically derived
from BLR gas motions inferred from spectra and BLR sizes estimated from the
AGN luminosity. The breathing BLR modelpredicts that the spectral line width
should change as luminosity varies, keeping mass estimates constant. Here, we
present observations of an AGN sample at redshift <0.1 with two epochs
separated by 20 years. On average, weﬁnd that luminosity and mass estimates
vary by a factor of 2, showing a long-term mean-reversion pattern, while the
line widths remain constant. A constant line width suggests that full BLRs do
not change size during continuum or line ﬂux variability, naturally explaining
why scaling relations depend on instantaneously measured Eddington ratios.
As a result, weﬁnd that single-epoch masses withthe best repeatability after 20
years are obtained when sizes are estimated from the narrow [OIII] λ5007Å
emission line luminosity.
The origin of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) and the history of
their growth in the Universe is one of the most highly debated ques-
tions in cosmology
1,2. Robust measurements of black hole masses are
crucial to this topic and almost always rely on diagnosing the motion of
material in orbit around the SMBH. In practice, nearly all known SMBHs
are so distant that mass measurements rely on estimates of the orbital
sizes of the broad emission line region (BLR) located light-days from
the SMBH, and on the velocities of the gas emitting in the BLR, while
assuming approximately Keplerian motion
3–5. The gas motions are
probed through the projected line-of-sight velocity that is reﬂected in
the width of the broad emission lines in active galactic nuclei (AGN)
spectra and likely depends on viewing angle
6.
The traditional method to estimate the size of the BLR, RBLR,i s
reverberation mapping (RM), which measures a time delay between a
variable continuum band (used as a proxy for the ionising continuum)
and its response in theﬂux of a spectral emission line emitted from the
BLR
7,8. The RM method relies on long-term monitoring, which is
resource-intensive or even impractical in the large BLRs of rapidly
growing SMBHs observed at high redshift. Hence, a much simpler
method, known as the virial single-epoch (SE) method
9–15, has become
the standard and has been applied to several hundred thousand
objects accounting for over 99% of all measured SMBHs
16–18.W h i l e
observationally efﬁcient, requiring only a single spectrum, it is less
precise, since RBLR is inferred from AGN luminosity such as the con-
tinuum at 5100Å, L5100, or broad H βλ 4861Å emission, LHβ,u s i n g
empirical relations calibrated against low-redshift RM measurements.

help but rely on RBLR − L relations calibrated using RM observations.

Discussion
The diversity in the Hβ/[OIII] ratio in AGN has previously been attrib-
uted to dust extinction47 and to changes in accretion state, particularly
in the context of changing-look and changing-state AGN48,49.T ow h a t
extent this diversity is due to long-lived AGN properties versus short-
term variability is only slowly revealed by new data. In our 20-year
comparison, we observe that changes in the line ratio span 0.5 dex (16-
84th percentile range). The overall diversity of instantaneous line
ratios within the AGN population is not much larger; in our sample the
central 68th percentile range spans 0.8 dex for the WiFeS epoch. Thus,
it is not inconceivable that the line ratio or Seyfert subtype of any AGN
at the luminosities in our sample mightﬂuctuate over the entire range
of observed diversity on time scales that are not too much longer than
20 years, perhaps just a few orbital timescales at the BLR radius. In the
case of changing-look AGN, the variations in line ratios are extreme,
with the broad H β emission disappearing or reappearing on much
shorter timescales, ranging from a few years to even months

The breathing of a broad emission line’s characteristic size reﬂects
the radial distribution of responsivity within the BLR. When line
responsivity varies with radius, the responsivity-weighted radius shifts
with changes in the ionising continuum
29,38. The expectation that this
radius follows the RBLR − L relation holds only for emission lines with
near-unity responsivity, i.e. those that respond linearly across most of
the BLR. This condition is not met by broad H β, whose responsivity
varies non-linearly with radius 38,53. Because the expected breathing
slope assumes linear responsivity, and changes in ionising luminosity
shift only the responsivity-weighted radius rather than the physical
BLR size, the absence of a breathing signature in Fig.3 is unsurprising.
This distinction between responsivity- and emission-weighted
measures is central to interpreting our results. While the response-
weighted radius may breathe for certain lines, the emission-weighted
centroid of the BLR has not been robustly observed to do so.
Accordingly, weﬁnd that the full BLR shows substantial size inertia, as
inferred from line widths in response to continuum variations, and
therefore requires an R
BLR − L relation tied to a more slowly varying
luminosity. By contrast, standardRBLR − L relations are calibrated using
RM lags, which probe the response-weighted centroid andﬂuctuate on
the timescales of continuum variations. This likely explains the larger
intrinsic scatter reported in previousL
[OIII]-based RBLR − L relations25,46.

In summary, from a large, representative sample of nearby AGN,
we observe that the long-term variability in AGN luminosity, which
already shows a mean reversion pattern on decadal timescales, is
mostly not accompanied by changes in the inferred orbital velocity of
the BLR, which is derived from the width of the observed total emis-
sion line. This is because the emissivity-weighted centroid of the BLR
ionised by the central source remains largely unchanged. Variability on
a 20 year timescale already appears to account for a fraction of the
scatter observed in theR

sample, when the luminosity L is a short-term variable probe such as
the optical continuum luminosity L5100 or the broad-line luminosity
LHβ. Adjustments in the width of the overall emission line and overall
BLR size are not rapid enough to make an object move parallel to the
R
BLR − L relation. Instead, an object’s offset from theRBLR − L relation is
primarily driven by its ﬂuctuations in the luminosity axis, i.e., inferred
ﬂuctuations in the Eddington ratio over decadal timescales, which then
shift the relation by an inevitable factor of L BEL/L[OIII] and produce a
trivial dependence of the relation onREdd. This variability contributes a
level of scatter comparable to that introduced by orientation effects
and responsivity-induced delays. However, such variability-induced
offsets and the associatedR

power is traced over long timescales, as effectively achieved by the
narrow-lineL

This result is shown to apply in a volume-complete sample of low-

this behaviour extends to high-luminosity quasars remains uncertain,
as variability studies on such objects involve much longer variability
timescales. After decades of work on measuring BLR sizes and black
hole masses with reverberation mapping and virial single-epoch esti-

snapshot measurements of BLR size and black hole mass

hence the most luminous quasars. It is thus a largely complementary

develop our understanding of BLR evolution regarding the diversity in

Sample and observations
The parent sample in our study is the 6-degree Field Galaxy Survey
(6dFGS
39,63), which includes spectra of 125,071 galaxies at a median
redshift of 0.053. These galaxies were selected from the Two Micron
All Sky Survey (2MASS) Extended Source Catalogue 64, complete to
K < 12.65 mag, and includes miscellaneous auxiliary targets. The survey
covered the Southern sky at Galactic latitude∣b∣≥10∘, with observations
dating between 2001 and 2009. From the spectra of this ﬂux-limited
galaxy sample, a sample of broad emission line (BEL) AGN was iden-
tiﬁed by spectralﬁtting and visual inspection (6dFGS AGN
40). Here, we
focus on 574 objects at z < 0.1, which appear to be a near-complete
sample of AGN in large galaxies at K < 12.65 mag in the Southern Sky,
and use that as our historic reference sample. The objects not in our
sample include AGN in dwarf galaxies below the K-bandﬂux limit, weak
AGN with BELs undetectable with 6dFGS sensitivity, or high-luminosity
AGN that appear as point sources in 2MASS.
We re-observed 560 of the AGN from the 6dFGS sample with the
Australian National University (ANU) 2.3m Telescope at Siding Spring
Observatory (SSO) near Coonabarabran in Australia, mostly between
2022 and 2024. These 560 objects include 137 type 1.9 Seyferts, which
feature a broad H α line without a broad H β line. Observations were
conducted with the Wide Field Spectrograph (WiFeS
65,66), an integral
ﬁeld spectrograph with a 38 × 25 arcsec ﬁeld of view and 1 arcsec 2
spaxels. The B3000 and R3000 diffraction gratings provided coverage
over 3200 − 9800Å. Data reduction was performed using PyWiFeS67,a
Python-based pipeline designed for WiFeS. The WiFeS spectra of the
6dFGS AGN will be separately published as a legacy resource, once
observations of missing objects are complete. In addition to the 6dFGS
AGN, we also observed a sample of 13 AGN with WiFeS, which have
been previously reverberation-mapped to get a black hole mass esti-
mate; references for mass estimates from the reverberation mapping
studies are listed in Table 2.
The 6dFGS spectra were obtained through ﬁbres with a 6.7
arcsec aperture, corresponding to 12.4 kpc at z = 0.1, and include
narrow line emission from star formation in the host galaxy,
although the [OIII] emission is usually dominated by ionisation from
the AGN ( > 90%)
68. These spectra were observed at a spectral
resolution of R ~ 1000. We match the WiFeS observations to 6dFGS
and avoid instrumental biases in the two-epoch comparison by
integrating light within a 6.7 arcsec diameter region in the ﬁeld of
view of the WiFeS integral ﬁeld unit and smoothing the spectra to
the 6dFGS wavelength resolution.
