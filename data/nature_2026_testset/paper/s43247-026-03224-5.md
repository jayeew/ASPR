A lack of historical land-use data hinders the appropriate assessment of ecosystem restoration
potential. Here we integrate pollen and phytolith analysis, isotopic dating, historical records, and high-
resolution satellite imagery to reconstructﬁve centuries of land-use and vegetation change in the karst
landscapes of Southwest China. We show that prior to the 18th century, dense forests dominated
the region under minimal human in ﬂuence. However, the introduction of maize cultivation, rapid
population growth, and extensive deforestation triggered a persistent shift toward open landscapes.
This transformation is evidenced by a marked rise in pioneer fern spores (~11%) and an increase in
herb pollen by 3.7 –18.8%. In areas with severe historical human disturbance, ecological recovery
remains difﬁcult, and tree numbers have remained nearly unchanged over the past two decades,
despite the absence of human intervention. Overlooking these historical land-use legacies in
restoration efforts may possibly lead to unrealistic expectations, misguided interventions, and wasted
resources.
Ecosystem restoration has become a gl obal priority in efforts to mitigate
biodiversity loss, enhance carbon se questration, and rebuild degraded
landscapes1–4. However, the potential for successful restoration is not solely
determined by present-day environmental conditions—it is also shaped by
the long-term legacy of past land use 5,6. Centuries of deforestation, agri-
culture, and other human activities h ave left lasting ecological imprints,
altering soil properties, hydrology, and species composition in ways that
may limit the capacity of ecosystems to recover7–10. Without accounting for
these historical constraints, restoration efforts risk setting unrealistic goals,
misallocating resources, or overlooking areas where natural regeneration
could thrive
11,12.

Results and discussion
A history of forest loss and agricultural expansion
Isotopic dating results (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. S4,
S5), pollen and phytolith analyses reveal a major shift in vegetation com-
position over the past 500 years, largely driven by human activities (Fig.2
and Supplementary Figs. S6–S8). Pollen records indicate that before the 18th
century, dense forests dominated the region. However, the introduction of
maize cultivation initiated a profound tree-to-grass regime shift27,28.T h i s
transition is marked by a sharp increase inDicranopterisspores—a pioneer
species that thrives in degraded,deforested environments (Fig.2a–c)29,30.I n
all study sites, Dicranopteris spores rose signi ﬁcantly following the ﬁrst
appearance of maize pollen and phytoliths, increasing from ~5% to over
11%. Across the entire stratigraphic sequence, Dicranopteris spores were
strongly correlated with both maize pollen and maize phytoliths, particu-
larly in the Beisu depression, where the correlation coef ﬁcient between
Dicranopterisspores and maize phytoliths reached 0.92 (N = 33, p < 0.001)
(Supplementary Table S3). Simultaneously, herbaceous pollen expanded,
while tree pollen, particularly from typical climax-community oak trees
(Quercus), declined (Fig. 2de and Supplementary Fig. S9).

extent of rocky desertiﬁcation (Supplementary Fig. S9): In areas with light to

Long-term impacts on forest recovery
Despite recent ecological restoration efforts, tree recovery remains uneven
across the region. While NDVI values have increased in all three depressions
—from 0–0.2 in the 1980s to approximately 0.4 in the 2010s–2020s (Sup-
plementary Fig. S10)—tree recovery patterns differ depending on historical
vegetation changes and the severity of rocky desertiﬁcation. Satellite-based
tree mapping from 2004 to 2022/2023 (Fig.3) reveals that areas with rela-
tively mild historical disturbance have shown increases in tree numbers and
canopy cover. However, in regions tha t experienced severe deforestation
and complete tree-to-grass transiti ons, tree populations have remained
stagnant, even after two decades of passive recovery. In sites with light to
moderate degradation (Beisu and Jiale Depression), tree numbers increased
by over 6%, with canopy cover expanding by ~70%. In severely degraded
areas (Jiayu Depression), tree numbers remained unchanged, and canopy
cover expanded by only 42%, suggesting limited natural regeneration and
only existing trees expanded in size (Fig.3de). These ﬁndings indicate that
the extent of past deforestation and subsequent rocky desertiﬁcation has set
ecological thresholds that constrain spontaneous forest recovery, consistent
with numerous studies showing that even after more than two decades of
natural regeneration, vegetation in severely degraded karst landscapes rarely
returns to a closed-forest state
31,32.

The role of human activity in long-term landscape change
By integrating paleoecological data, historical records and recent tree
mapping, we identiﬁed four distinct phases of vegetation change, shaped by
shifting patterns of human activity (Fig.4):
Phase 1: pre-16th century — a pristine forest landscape . Before sub-
stantial human settlement, this region was covered by dense forests.
Historical records describe remote mountainous areas as inhospitable
due to miasma (toxic gases from decomposing organic matter)
33. The
population of Guangxi Province was estimated at only one to two million,
with forest cover ranging from 140,000 to 160,000 km
2 (Supplementary
Fig. S11). The remote, miasma-prone mountainous areas of north-
western Guangxi remained largely uninhabited
24,34.
Phase 2: 16th –18th century — initial human settlement and low-
impact agriculture. Ethnic minority communities practiced small-scale,
slash-and-burn agriculture with minimal impact on forest cover. How-
ever, as populations grew, land clearance began to expand gradually.
Guangxi’s population began to grow moderately, and forest area started
to decline, recorded at approximately 92,400 km
2 in both 1661 and 1700
(Supplementary Fig. S11) 24,34.
Phase 3: 18th century — maize introduction and accelerated defor-
estation. The widespread adoption of maize cultivation marked a
turning point, as forests were cleared at an unprecedented scale. Unlike
the traditional staple crop rice, which requires ﬂat, well-irrigated paddy
ﬁelds, maize can thrive on steep, rain-fed hillslopes, making it especially
attractive to farmers with limited arable land
27. This expansion of slope
farming greatly accelerated soil loss, promoted bedrock exposure, and in
many cases triggered or intensiﬁed rocky desertiﬁcation in karst regions.
At the same time, population growth and Han migration into the region
further intensi ﬁed deforestation, with agricultural expansion replacing
large areas of forest with grassland.
Similar patterns of agricultural expansion leading to landscape mod-
iﬁcation have been observed globally, including in the Amazon Basin
5,35,36
and the High Andes37. However, maize was introduced to China much later
than in the Americas, its place of origin 27. The earliest records of maize
cultivation in Guangxi date back to the Yongzheng era (1723 –1735), as
documented in historical texts such as Guangxi Tongzhi (General Records
of Guangxi), Pingle Fu Zhi (Records of Pingle Prefecture), and Taiping Fu
Zhi (Records of Taiping Prefecture). This timeline closely aligns with the
appearance of maize in our palynological and phytolith records. Notably,
maize pollen ﬁrst appeared in the northern Beisu depression (~1710s),
preceding its occurrence in the southern Jiayu depression (1760s–1780s),
likely due to Han migration patterns moving southward
38.
We ruled out climate as the dominant driver of vegetation changes. The
three sampling sites are located at comparable elevations (approximately
400–700 m a.s.l.) and share broadly similar climatic conditions, as indicated
by GPS measurements (SupplementaryTable S1), Copernicus DEM data,
and 50-year temperature and precipitation records from seven nearby
meteorological stations (Supplementary Fig. S12). Over the past ﬁve cen-
turies, regional climatic conditions have generally shifted toward warmer
and wetter trends, which should have favored forest expansion
39–42.T h i s
i n c l u d e sa ni n t e n s iﬁcation of the Asian monsoon and increasing
precipitation42,43 as well as a post-Little Ice Age warming trend 40 (Supple-
mentary Fig. S13). Therefore, we conc lude that the forest-to-grassland
transition in this region was primarily driven by human activities.
Phase 4: post-2000s — ecological restoration and challenges . Since
the early 2000s, both natural regrowth and afforestation efforts have
contributed to a signi ﬁcant increase in NDVI values (Supplementary
Fig. S10), aligning with broader trends in regional greening and carbon
sequestration
17,18. While some areas have shown promising tree regrowth
(Beisu and Jiale Depression), sites with extensive historical degradation
(such as Jiayu Depression) struggle to recover. Here, intense human
activity may have pushed the ecosystem into an alternative biome state,
making it difﬁcult to restore its original forest structure
44. The lack of tree
population growth (Fig. 3) suggests that reforestation remains challen-
ging, and passive recovery may be insuf ﬁcient without active
intervention.
Implications for sustainable restoration
Our study underscores the long-termecological consequences of historical
land-use change and highlights the challenges of restoring landscapes that
have undergone centuries of deforest ation and soil degradation. While
natural recovery is possible and low-cost in areas with moderate historical
disturbance
45, heavily degraded sites with extensive historical tree loss,
particularly those situated on intri nsically harsh substrates, may require
more active restoration interventions beyond passive regeneration46.T h e
ﬁnding warns of the particularly severe impact of deforestation on fragile
environments in karst regions. For example, ongoing deforestation in karst
areas of Southeast Asia
47 highlights the urgent need to recognize its long-
term consequences.
The long history of human activity has a profound imprint on vege-
tation dynamics, inﬂuencing the potential for ecological restoration. Our
ﬁndings have broad implications for global reforestation initiatives,
emphasizing the need to consider historical land-use legacies when

Methods
Paleoecology methods
Regional study area . We selected northwestern Guangxi Province,
southwest China, as the study area, one of the regions most severely
affected by rocky desertiﬁcation, according to 2021 statistics (Fig. 1). The
area experiences a typical subtropical monsoon climate, with an annual
average temperature of 18–22 °C, annual rainfall of 1000–1900 mm, and
monthly relative humidity consistently above 70%.
Site selection and core collection . Within this region, we selected
three depressions with varying degrees of degradation: Beisu, Jiale, and
Jiayu (Supplementary Table S1). Beisu Depression exhibits light to
moderate rocky deserti ﬁcation, with broadleaf trees and relatively thick
soil layers on slopes (10 –100 cm). In contrast, Jiale and Jiayu Depres-
sions suffer from moderate to severe and severe to extreme rocky
desertiﬁcation, respectively, with limited vegetation dominated by
shrubs and pine trees and extensive rock exposure (Fig. 1). In 2022,
sediment cores (diameter 5 cm) were sampled at every depression ’s
center using drills.
Isotopic dating techniques . To date sediment layers, we employed
three commonly used isotopic dating techniques: 137Cs, 210Pbex, and 14C21.
The earliest appearance of highest 137Cs activity can identiﬁes the layer of
1963 CE, while a210Pbex level of zero in the upper sediment layer indicates
approximately 100 years before sampling, corresponding to 1921 CE in
our study
48. The 14C method was applied to organic remains such as
charcoal and bone. Measurements of 137Cs and 210Pbex were conducted
using gamma spectrometry with a high-resolution coaxial germanium
detector (GMX40P4, ORTEC) at the Key Laboratory of Mountain
Hazards and Surface Processes, Institute of Mountain Hazards and
Environment, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The activities of
137Cs, 210Pb,
and 226Ra were determined based on gamma-ray peak areas at 662, 46.5,
and 351.9 keV, respectively. 210Pbex was derived by subtracting 210Pb from
226Ra, with measurement uncertainties typically below 10%. For 14C
analysis, we extracted four to seven charcoal samples per depression,
which were analyzed at BETA Laboratory, Miami, USA. The calibrated
ages provided by the BETA Laboratory formed the basis of our primary
analysis.
Pollen analysis techniques . Before chemical treatment, all sediment
samples were pulverized. Pollen analysis followed the standard palyno-
logical protocol (e.g., ref. 49). The procedure included: (1) removing
calcareous material using cold 10% HCl, (2) eliminating silica content
with 40% HF, (3) ﬁltering through a 7- μm nylon sieve to remove ﬁne
debris, and (4) embedding samples in glycerin jelly. To determine
absolute pollen concentrations (grains per unit sediment weight), we
added pre-counted Lycopodium spore tablets (10,315 ± 846 grains/
tablet)
50. Pollen percentages were calculated from total terrestrial pollen
counts, while spore percentages were based on the combined total of
pollen and spores
22. The identi ﬁcation of key palynomorphs (pollen,
spores, freshwater algae) followed 51,52. Notably, maize pollen
(50–100 μm) is signiﬁcantly larger than typical Poaceae pollen, enabling
clear differentiation 53. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates pollen identi ﬁ-
cation from the sediment. The pollen analysis for Jiayu Depression
(2.04 m depth) was previously reported by Yue et al., with a high-
resolution sampling interval of 2 cm. Recognizing the rapid depositional
environment, we analyzed pollen every 10 cm for Beisu (3.4 m total
depth) and Jiale (4.0 m total depth) Depressions to optimize data
resolution.
Phytolith identi ﬁcation. Phytolith extraction followed the wet ashing
technique54. Approximately 2 g of air-dried soil was processed to remove
organic matter using 30% hydrogen peroxide. To facilitate counting, a
Lycopodium spore tablet (10,315 ± 846 spores/tablet) was added to each
sample. Calcium carbonates were eliminated using 15% hydrochloric
acid, and samples were rinsed to achieve a neutral pH. Phytoliths were
extracted using a heavy liquid (ZnBr
2, speciﬁc gravity of 2.35). The
supernatant containing phytoliths was transferred, washed with absolute
ethanol, and mounted on slides using Canadian neutral balsam. Micro-
scopic examination was performed under an Olympus BX51 microscope
at ×400 magniﬁcation, with at least 100 phytoliths counted per sample for
accuracy. We identiﬁed Variant 1 of cross-shaped phytoliths (>20μm) as
maize (Supplementary Fig. S2) based on refs. 55–57.
Remote-sensing methods
Forty-year vegetation growth condition . To assess vegetation growth
over the past four decades (1986–2024), we analyzed the longest available
Landsat time-series imagery (30 m resolution), including (1) Landsat-5
Thematic Mapper (TM, 1986 –1998), (2) Landsat-7 Enhanced Thematic
Mapper Plus (ETM+, 1999–2012), and (3) Landsat-8 Operational Land
Imager (OLI, 2013 –2024). We derived the Normalized Difference
Vegetation Index (NDVI) for the three depressions from atmospherically
corrected surface re ﬂectance data, using the Google Earth Engine plat-
form (https://code.earthengine.google.com/).
Twenty-year individual tree mapping by high-resolution remote
sensing images. To evaluate forest regeneration under natural recovery,
we analyzed high-resolution satellite images covering the past 20 years
(2004–2023). Data were obtained from the China Platform of Earth
Observation System ( https://www.cpeos.org.cn/dataSearch3D), includ-
ing (1) QuickBird imagery (0.61 m panchromatic, 2.44 m multispectral),
and (2) GF-2 imagery (0.8 m panchromatic, 3.2 m multispectral). We
applied Gram-Schmidt pan-sharpening fusion processing to enhance
multispectral imagery, achieving effective resolutions of 0.61 m and
0.8 m, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). To complement the
remote sensing analysis, we conducted ﬁeld surveys (January 11 –13,
2025) using three approaches: (1) Laser hypsometers to measure the
height of 39 representative trees, (2) Differential GPS to georeference 30
ground control points (GCPs), and (3) Drone aerial imaging to delineate
depression boundaries. To distinguish trees from shrubs, we incorpo-
rated high-resolution airborne LiDAR-derived canopy height data (2 m
resolution).
A deep learning U-Net neural network was implemented for tree
crown segmentation
25,26.T h ew o r kﬂow included: (1) Geo-registration of
satellite images using GCPs from differential GPS surveys, (2) Matching
ﬁeld-measured tree coordinates and manually delineated UAV tree crowns
(N = 39) with 2022/2023 satellite imagery, and 146 canopies were selected as
an independent evaluation dataset on the depression drone images within a
1.5 × 1.5 km area centered on the three sampling sites, and (3) Training a
convolutional neural network (CNN)with (a) Learning rate = 0.0001, (b)
Training epochs = 2500 (stabilized at ~2000 epochs), (c) 64 × 64 pixel pat-
ches for training and validation, and (d) Batch size = 4. The trained model
achieved 89.8% accuracy (F1-score = 0.91) on an independent evaluation
dataset.
To reﬁne canopy segmentation and distinguish trees from shrubs, we
applied a 4-m height threshold, based on ﬁeld-measured canopy height
distributions (4.8–26.1 m for trees). This threshold effectively excluded
shrubs. The ﬁnal tree canopy segmentation maps for the three depressions
enabled precise quantiﬁcation of mean canopy coverage. Supplementary
Fig. S3 presents the workﬂow for tree identiﬁcation via remote sensing.
