Robotic jumping research advances engineering and biomimicry frontiers,
prioritizing range, precision, and predictability to navigate unstructured
environments. Earth’s gravity necessitates powerful actuators and lightweight
bodies in robotic designs for maximal jump height. While many robots excel in
statical environments, precise, predictable jumps in dynamic settings remain
challenging. We realized this with a bipedal robot leveraging thrust-induced
hypogravity, alongside dual regulation of aerial attitude and parabolic trajec-
tory via thrust vectoring. Hypogravity multiplies leap range (max: 6.9 m)
despite leg force saturation, enablingthe robot to clear multi-level stairs, a
2.35-m-high wall, and 3-m-wide stream.Parabolic trajectory regulation allows
leap distance precision/consistency surpassing existing thrust-assisted
hybrids and leg-only jumpers. It enables pre-jump prediction of aerial/landing
positions and timing, facilitating leaps in dynamic scenarios: through fast-
moving windows (3.8 m/s), onto shifting, conﬁned targets, and against wind
disturbance. This research establishes extended range, precise, and pre-
dictable jumping through self-generatedhypogravity and parabolic trajectory
regulation.
Jumping locomotion, a biomechanically ef ﬁcient strategy for sur-
mounting large obstacles and enabling rapid traversal across
unstructured terrains, is extensively adopted in nature. Jumping spe-
cialists in animal kingdom can achieve superior jumping ranges, cope
with dynamic environmental uncertainties via predictive jumping, and
stably land onto con ﬁned target areas. However, for robotics, the
pursuit of extended leap range also leads to an inherent compromise
of leap precision, predictability, landing stability and safety. Con-
quering this trade-off to reach animal-level jumping performance with
trajectory predictability, landing precision and stability, and ultra-
broad range remains a key challenge. Paradigms for maximizing
jumping elevation frequently involve enhancing the power-to-weight
ratio, entailing advancements in jumping actuation systems. A pre-
valent approach is the use of catapult mechanisms, such as loading
springs with cam mechanisms
1–5, winching mechanisms6–15, and mini-
scale jumpers with bistable structures adopting torque reversal
mechanisms16–21(also commonly adopted by robotic grippers22–24), soft
buckling beam25,26,o rs h e l l27–30 structures. Nonetheless, catapult-based
jumping’s energy-storing phases, often much longer than the leap
aerial phases, hinder agility and continuity. Its reliance on elastic
energy poses difﬁculties for precise feedback control of trajectories or
landing positions, most such jumpers are manually, or open loop
controlled. In contrast, actuator direct-drive methods (e.g., motor
31–33,
electrohydraulic actuation34,35) enable agile, continuous jumping with
better controllability. Still, their limited instantaneous power restricts
ultra-high leaps (e.g., 3 m high), with the highest record (1.25 m)
achieved by Salto-1P
36.

elongated aerial phases7,37–40. While these systems cover enhanced leap

and vertical air drag, impairing jumping elevation. Meanwhile, the

In literature, the precise control of parabolic leap trajectory and
landing position is a challenging issue. For leap trajectory control,
current state-of-the-art robotic jumpers mainly utilize the delicate
control of launch speed and angle (e.g., Salto-1P

parabolic trajectory regulation during mid-ﬂight. In real-world appli-

launch or touchdown angle, leading to large trajectory errors (e.g., In a
chair-desk jumping task, Salto-1P

could distort a trajectory’s ballistic pattern, rendering such methods
insufﬁcient. For robustness and predictability of leap patterns, we
investigate a previously underexplored locomotion pattern: parabolic
trajectory regulation under thrust-induced hypogravity condition. It
involves studying the discrete-point errors in every timestamp of a full
leap parabola, under the guidance from a reference parabolic target
with coupled time and positions (time-coupled parabolic trajectories,
TCPT). Previous ﬁxed-thrust-based jumping hybrids (thrust direction
ﬁxed to airframe) employed active thrust propulsion for touchdown
attitude and height regulation
49,50, non-parabolic position control51,o r
horizontal velocity and height control 52, to initiate rebound-based
motions. These systems exhibit limited precision in regulating indivi-
dual parabolic trajectories, not only because of their control objectives
but the inherent ﬁxed-thrust conﬁguration limitations. Speci ﬁcally,
attitude adjustments require airframe rotation that induces extra
translations, while positional control rigidly requires airframe rotation,
restricting the control ﬂexibility of inclination. Another technical
contribution of this work lies in decoupling the rigidity between the
regulation of inclination and parabolic trajectory and enables simul-
taneous control of the two components.
Following the mid- ﬂight regulation of leap trajectory, balanced
landing without secondary bounces is critical for tasks involving con-
ﬁned targets. Many jumpers show imbalance upon landing, hence
using self-righting mechanism to recover from instability
5,6,8–10,53–55.
Soft-bodied jumpers27,29,30,34,56–61 can partly reduce sary bounces with
inherent compliance. Yet, balanced and bounce-free landing (stop at
the ﬁrst land point) is insufﬁcient in the literature.
To tackle the aforementioned challenges, we devise a 370 g
bipedal robot called GravOff (Gravity offset) robot (Fig. 1A) that ﬁrst
investigates robotic jumping under thrust-induced hypogravity while
regulating the aerial-phase parabolic trajectories. The component of
hypogravity endows the robot with range-tunable projectile motions,
extending leap ranges multifold despite the saturation of leg joint
torque. This enables the robot to achieve a height jump up to 46 times
body height (6.9 m), and facilitates large, rapid spatial transitions in
ﬁeld tests: leaping over multi-level stairs, a 2.35 m-high wall, a 3 m-wide
stream, etc. An energy comparison study between hypogravity jump-
ing and trajectory-controlledﬂight modes reveals that the total energy
cost of hypogravity jumping modes is less than 10 % of that of theﬂight
mode when overcoming the same medium-scale obstacle of ~5 body
heights. The legs cooperate with thrusters to control directional
jumping motions, enabling both longitudinal and lateral leaps. During
aerial descent, the legs act as an active deceleration mechanism, dis-
sipating 50% of the impact momentum compared to direct landings,
thereby facilitating bounce-free landings within conﬁned targets. Legs’
propulsion and active deceleration are coordinated with vector-thrust
and attitude control to enable seamless consecutive leaps in challen-
ging scenarios, including jumps across inclination-varied targets and
across area-conﬁned, dynamic targets. With directed takeoff, the robot
leaps under thrust-induced hypogravity while regulating the projectile
motion for aerial-phase and landing-point precision and predictability.
We use synchronous thrust vectoring to enable dual regulation of
inclination and parabolic trajectory. The system reveals effectiveness
in targeted jumping and shows an order of jumping distance (JD)
precision and consistency improvement than our ﬁxed-thrust-based
regulation case and previous precise leg-only jumpers, which could
achieve landings within a robot-projection-sized target region (0.25 m
by 0.25 m) with sub-centimeter distance error and standard deviation.
Under interference, such as large takeoff angle deviations (up to 15°)
and wind disturbance (3.5 m/s), the robot can remain targeted landing
with small variations. Owing to the time-coupled feature of the refer-
ence parabola (i.e., the TCPT), the timing for all positions in the sub-
sequent parabolic curve, including the landing point, can be estimated.
This allows the robot to execute well-timed leaps in response to
dynamic environmental changes. The robot demonstrates this cap-
ability by conquering rapid-moving obstacles, such as leaping through
a pendular window at 3.8 m/s and a rotating window at 100 deg/s.

Prototyping the GravOff bipedal robot
Bush crickets (Tettigoniidae) have long and thin legs, but can jump in
tens of milliseconds. Their elongated limbs facilitate an extended
acceleration phase while allowing for reduced muscle force demand to
achieve high take-off velocities. They power their jumps by muscle
direct actuation that exert forces on long levers and showcase superior
quickness than spring-actuated jumpers (such as locust) for escape
movements
62. The GravOff robot also utilizes direct actuation method,
with a pair of 3-Degree-of-Freedom (DOF) long legs (leg extension ratio
reaches 340%) to initiate jumps resembling bush crickets (Fig. 1B, C),
experiencing balanced crouching state, synchronous leg extension,
and the airborne phase. For the proposed hypogravity effect, the four
thrusters are controlled at a constant rotational speed while balancing
the robot simultaneously (by a cascaded Proportional-Integral-
Derivative (PID) attitude controller, see Fig. S2 and Table S2). The
thrusters are arranged with a relatively wide span (220 mm by 190 mm)
for long moment arms to facilitate balancing stability and aerial atti-
tude control.
The 2-DOF jumping unit (Fig. 1D(i)) features a parallelogram
mechanism and is reinforced with two additional bars for increased
lateral stiffness. A brushless DC motor (Fig.1D(ii)) serves as the primary
power source, driving the upper link of the mechanism. The second
DOF, controlled by servo 1, manages the overall rotation of the
jumping unit. We adopt this integrated design for the jumping unit to

Longitudinal jumping with the pre-tilt method
To achieve more consistent takeoff angles and velocities, the robot
employs the pre-tilt method for longitudinal jumps (see Fig. 4Aa n d
Fig. 1D(iv)). During the pre-tilt phase, a 0.6-s ramp-up angle command
ϕ0 is sent from the Teensy controller to the low-level PID attitude
controller while the tilt servos (Fig. 1D(iv)) simultaneously rotate to a
negative angle/C0 ϕ0. Once the pre-tilt stance is achieved and stabilized,
the robot applies synchronous extension commands to both legs. This
method aligns the ground reaction force closely with the leg extension
line, resulting in a consistently directed initial velocity. Owing to the
thrust vectoring unit, which eliminates horizontal thrust components,
the robot can maintain stability during the pre-tilt phase under oscil-
latory angle commands, as demonstrated in Supplementary Movie 5.
Beyond this pre-tilt-based longitudinal jumping method, the GravOff
robot can also initiate lateral jumps by creating phase differences
between its leg motors during extension, as shown in Supplementary
Section S6, Fig. S8, and Supplementary Movie 6.

Bounce-free landings
Following a well-controlled parabolic trajectory, a stable robotic
landing is the critical ﬁnal step. The GravOff bipedal robot employed
two landing methods according to targets ’ dimension and impact
energy: passive damping (PD) and active damping (AD). The PD
method utilizes the TPU shock absorber (see Fig. 1D) for jumps with
high impact momentum, while AD is adopted for missions requiring a
precise and bounce-free landing within conﬁned targets.
We compared the proposed landing methods in two vertical
jumping tasks, as illustrated in Fig. 6A, B. With AD, the robot main-
tained the legs extended in the aerial phase and employed the legs ’
synchronous contraction near the ground contact point as a soft
deceleration mechanism (see Fig. 6A(i) and Supplementary Movie 8).
In this process, the leg contraction speed was set at apex leap height
and timing for ﬂexion was based on a height threshold method (see
supplementary section S9). The contraction movement of AD
method extended ground contact duration (0.338 s) twofold com-
pared to PD method (0.17 s), while reducing the average

Leap against wind disturbances
Robustness to disturbances is a critical factor in tracking problems, yet
it is rarely explored in the context of jumping, especially during the
aerial phase when disturbances like wind can affect landing position. In
a targeted jumping task, we investigated our robot’sr e s p o n s et ow i n d
disturbances generated by an electric fan, measured at 3.5 m/s using an
anemometer (model RA110, Dongguan SANLIANG Electronics Co.,
Ltd., China) placed 1 meter away. Tests were conducted under three
scenarios: TV-based jumping with inclination control, FR-based
jumping, and jumping with open-loop control (attitude maintains
level), as shown in Fig.7A and Supplementary Movie 9. Results indicate
that wind disturbances signiﬁcantly affected all groups near the apex
of the trajectory. However, the controller-enabled groups compen-
sated for displacement loss in the y-direction by decreasing the incli-
nation angle ϕ (FR-based case) or the tilt angle ε
r (TV-based case), as
demonstrated in Fig. 7B. Despite imperfect tracking of the TCPT dur-
ing descent, both controllers allowed the robot to reach the 0.25 ×
0.25-meter target region (landing points correspond to trajectory
endpoints in Fig. 7C; data presented as mean ± SD; all landing posi-
tions are presented in Table S15). Although both methods enabled the
robot to reach the target region, FR-based MPC caused deviation
beyond the region after ﬁrst contact due to necessary forward incli-
nation demands compensating for trajectory errors; in contrast, TV-
based MPC maintained ﬁnal position proximity to target with proper
inclination control, as compared in Supplementary Movie 9.

Predictive leaps through static/dynamic aerial obstacles
Since the tracking of parabolic trajectories is rigidly time-coupled, it
indicates the timings for subsequent aerial positions ahead of a leap.
Utilizing this feature, our robot showcased predictive jumps to tackle
dynamic/static aerial obstacles. As shown in Fig.8 and Supplementary
Movie 11 (clip 1), we designed three scenarios where the robot was
required to jump through a stationary or shifting window (0.51 by

the task of crossing the stationary window, the robot was required to
follow the TCPT, adjust its leg pose to pass through the window
without contact, and realize a soft landing (see Fig. 8A). The robot ’s
legs experienced four phases: extension for jumping, contraction,
second extension for damping preparation, and active damping. Fig-
ure 8B shows the controlled leap curve aligns closely with the refer-
ence, regulating the cross-point error to 1.4 cm (lateral) and 2.6 cm
(vertical) for safe passage. The robot attained a 6.56 g peak accelera-
tion and experienced a 0.278g hypogravity, and softly landed with an
average acceleration belowg (Fig. 8C). The aerial phase attitude angle
and corresponding controller commands are presented in Fig.8D.

that hinders the robot from the targeted landing (see Fig. 8E, F and

Versatile locomotion inﬁeld tests
In addition to indoor TCPT tracking-based jumping, we evaluated the
GravOff bipedal robot in ﬁeld settings by integrating its three loco-
motion modes: air-propelled passive wheeling, synchronous legged
crawling, and jumping (Fig.10). Figure10A depicts transitions between
these modes. The legged crawling posture serves as an intermediate
state for jumping and wheeling (see supplementary section S12 for
mode transformation and crawling control) and proved effective as an
alternative to jumping or hopping on terrain with unpredictable sur-
face contact angles, such as grassland (Fig. 10B, Supplementary
Movie 13), sand (Fig. 10C), cobbles (Fig. 10D), and grooved roads
(Fig. 10E). The crawling motion enables minor turns and incremental
steps, facilitating precise launch positioning and orientation adjust-
ments during ﬁeld-based jumping tasks.
For speed and ef ﬁciency, the air-propelled passive wheeling was
adopted for locomotion on ﬂat terrain. A speed test in (Fig. 10F, Sup-
plementary Movie 14) revealed that the robot could attain a peak speed
of 4.8 m/s (around 24 body lengths per second), with a 50% throttle
command.
The hypogravity jumping strategy facilitates rapid traversal of
large outdoor barriers. As shown in Fig. 10G, the robot successfully
leaped over a 2.35 m-high wall under quasi-moon gravity, achieving a
3.3-meter apex height. To reach target locations, the robot executed
locomotion mode transformations to navigate different types of ter-
rains. This was demonstrated in scenarios involving targets above a
staircase (crawling-jumping transition in Fig. 10H; wheeling-jumping
transition in Fig. 10I), atop a 1.6 m wall (Fig. 10J, crawling-jumping
transition), and across a 3 m-wide stream (Fig. 10K, crawling-jumping
transition), as shown in Supplementary Movie 15.
Discussions
In this work, we present the GravOff robot, a system designed to leap
by parabolic trajectory regulation under thrust-induced hypogravity,
realizing ultra-range, precise, predictable and stable leap motions. The
proposed gravity offset strategy signiﬁcantly boosts leap elevation to
6.9 meters (46 times body height) under microgravity level, over-
coming the mechanical constraint of legged propulsion while reducing
to 25 % the landing momentum of the value under Earth gravity for the
same height. These qualities allow the robot to traverse large obstacles
in ﬁeld tests while preserving landing safety, such as leaping over a
2.35 m wall, multi-level stairs, a 3 m wide stream, etc. The robot can
execute both longitudinal and lateral jumps with the pretilt and the
actuation phase difference methods. With the pre-tilt takeoff, the
robot performed parabolic trajectory regulation via its inherent thrust
vectoring con ﬁguration. For targeted leaps, this method proved
robust, maintaining high leap consistency even when facing large
takeoff angle deviation ( ± 10°) and strong aerial-phase wind dis-
turbance (3.5 m/s). To ensure landing stability, we implemented active-
damping technique for bounce-free landings on conﬁned targets and
passive damping for high-impact landings. By leveraging the time-
coupled nature of the tracked trajectories, the robot had prior
knowledge of future positions (and the time) before executing jumps,
enabling predictive leaps in dynamic environments. These include
leaping through fast-moving (rotating, pendular) windows, onto a
shifting yet con ﬁned target platform, and seamlessly initiating con-
secutive jumps. During ﬁeld tests, GravOff robot also demonstrated
multimodal motions combining hypogravity jumping, legged crawl-
ing, and wheeling to tackle composite terrains.
The leg mechanism is crucial for thrust-induced hypogravity
jumping and versatile motions across outdoor composite terrains, in

lishment of takeoff speed compared to air thrust propulsion, enabling
agile crossing of dynamic targets (Fig.8); (iii) Landing stability: During

Thruster tilt angle calibration
To establish precise tilt angle control, we calibrated the relationship
between servo rotation angles and pulse-width modulation (PWM)
input signals. A cross-shaped calibration bar with infrared re ﬂective
markers (Fig. S30) was rigidly mounted to the servo rotor. Using
motion capture system (Swift 30 A, LUSTER LightTech Co., Ltd.,
China), we constructed the calibration bar as rigid body and tracked its
angle values. We assigned step a command between−15° and 15° to the
servo, and calibrated PWM value was determined when the measured
rotation matched the commanded angle within a tolerance of ±0.5°.

The leg actuator uses a high KV brushless DC motor (T Motor F1408

AG). A steel-gear servo motor (DS1906 B, GDW Digital Servo Manu-
facturer Co., Ltd, China) is mounted beside (in opposite direction) the
brushless motor by a 3D-printedﬁxer (see Fig.1D(ii)). The output shaft
of the servo and a coaxial steel shaft (a two-head threaded shaft in

actuator to a 3D-printed hip ﬁxer (polyethylene terephthalate glycol

Robot calibration
The robot’s setup requires the calibration of the leg motor, leg servos,
and the pitch turning servos. For the leg motor, once the radial magnet
is glued, its angular position is ﬁxed. We included a correction factor
and manually tuned it in our code to eliminate the remaining errors
from the calibration position (see Fig. S27A). Since the applied PWM
(Pulse Width Modulation) servos have no position readback, we ﬁrst
calibrated their rotation ranges to 180 degrees in the code and
obtained a mapping between PWM value and actual angle (measured
by an as5600 magnetic encoder module) and then set the zero-degree
position (Fig. S27A). The servo angles are then directly controlled by
setting the corresponding PWM values.

Procedures for leaping in dynamic environments
Infrared markers were attached to the robot (7 markers), the window (5
markers), and the rotational target platform (5 markers) to construct
rigid bodies. The robot’s CoM was calculated by SolidWorks (version
2019, Dassault Systèmes) and was set in the motion capture software
Moca 2.3.0.8. Their position and attitude data were streamed to Matlab
(2019b, MathWorks, Inc.) for real-time leap control. Before leaping, the
robot was placed at the launch point by referring to the initial point of
the tracked trajectory. The leap tasks calculated the exact trigger
points. During implementation, since the streamed data are discrete,
the experimental trigger timings were set to be the ﬁrst data points
surpassing the calculated timing (Figs. 7H, L, 8E). In the window-
crossing tasks, the contraction and extension of the legs in the aerial
phase were controlled according to the current elapsed time in TCPT,
which re ﬂects the robot ’s spatial position. For tasks in Fig. 7,c o n -
sidering the motor startup time and signal transfer (~200 ms) and
contraction process(~160 ms), the contraction signals were generated
400 ms before reaching the window to ensure safe passage. The leg
extension signal (only for task in Fig.7H) was generated 70 ms (~5 MPC
loops) after window crossing. For the task in Fig. 9,t h el e g s ﬁrst
adopted a slow contraction signal before reaching the height thresh-
old for damping to eliminate preparation time for motor start-up (see
supplementary section S9 for details). The leg command signals,
thrust, pitch and roll angle commands were all generated in Matlab and
transmitted wirelessly to the development board Teensy (Teensy® 4.0)
via two HC-12 modules (transmitter and receiver, Guangzhou Hui-
cheng Information Technology Co., Ltd, China) to control leg move-
ment and attitude.
