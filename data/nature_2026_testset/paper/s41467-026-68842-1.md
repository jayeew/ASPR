East dust emissions as a major external driver of IOD variability. Observational
evidence shows that dust over the 1980-2020 period account for ~36% of
interannual IOD variance, surpassing El Niño-Southern Oscillation as the main

The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) is the major mode of interannual cli-
mate variability in the Tropical Indian Ocean (TIO), marked by a seesaw
pattern of sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies between the
western and eastern TIO
1,2. This anomalous SST pattern leads to
changes in atmospheric circulation and rainfall patterns over the vast
areas surrounding TIO, including Indonesia, India, Australia, and East
Africa
3–5. Positive IOD (pIOD) events bring warming to the western
basin and cooling to the east, reshaping rainfall patterns across East
Africa, India, Indonesia, and Australia, while negative phases (nIOD)
have opposite effects. The extreme pIOD event in 2019 triggered
unprecedented Australian bushﬁres and historic ﬂooding along the
Yangtze River
6–8, underscoring the urgent need to understand the
mechanism underlying IOD events. Most studies attribute IOD varia-
bility to external forcing

Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) inﬂuences1,10,11,y e tt h er o l eo fa e r o -
sols remains largely unexplored. The TIO is located close to the world’s
second largest dust source— the Middle East deserts. Previous studies
have identiﬁed the far-reaching impacts of Middle East dust on the
South Asian monsoon, Arabian Sea SST, and Indian summer monsoon
rainfall
12–14. Around 2010, both IOD behavior and Middle East dust
emissions shifted markedly, with IOD transitioning from frequent
negative to positive phases and Middle East dust emissions reaching
historically low levels by 2019
15–18. This coincidence motivates our
investigation into whether dust forcing in ﬂuences IOD phases and
quantiﬁes its contribution to the variability of IOD.
Here, we show that variability in summertime Middle East dust
(SMED) is a critical driver of autumn IOD events. Using model experi-
ments, we establish a causal link where reduced SMED triggers a chain
of atmosphere-ocean interactions in the tropical Indian Ocean. These
interactions, primarily driven by wind-induced ocean dynamics and
reinforced by cloud feedbacks, robustly promote the development of
positive IOD conditions.

The coupled response is not limited to SST anomalies but also
involves coherent atmospheric and oceanic processes. Ocean proﬁles
reveal subsurface heat transport from the eastern to western TIO,
weakened downwelling in the east, and a deepened mixed layer in the

atmospheric anomalies, including a low-pressure center over the wes-
tern TIO (Fig. 1f). The resulting zonal pressure gradient drives a clock-

easterlies or weakened westerlies across the basin. Such circulation
anomalies are characteristic of pIOD events, reinforcing the notion that
dust reductions can set favorable conditions for their development.
Collectively, observational evidence and modeling analyses
demonstrate a robust negative SMED –IOD relationship. Reduced
Middle East dust emissions are statistically linked to atmospheric and
oceanic states resembling pIOD events, suggesting that decadal var-
iations in dust loading likely exert an important, previously under-
appreciated inﬂuence on tropical Indian Ocean climate.
Causal evidence from climate modeling
As i g n iﬁcant correlation between SMED and the boreal autumn DMI
does not necessarily imply a causal relationship, as dust emission and
transport are also modulated by SST variability 15,25. Additionally, rea-
nalysis and satellite products often integrate signals from multiple
climate modes, complicating efforts to isolate the dust in ﬂuence. To
rigorously evaluate the role of Middle East dust in shaping TIO varia-
bility, we performed coupled climate model simulations using the
Community Earth System Model (CESM), which explicitly incorporates
both atmospheric forcing (solar radiation, greenhouse gases, aerosols)
and ocean feedbacks. The model was nudged toward reality by pre-
scribing the observed evolution of Middle-East dust loading from
1980–2020. Speciﬁcally, the summertime-mean dust mass density in
CESM was scaled each year according to the corresponding summer-
time station-based dust-frequency observations over the Middle East,
yielding a transient dust-mass time series that faithfully mirrors the
observed variability (see Methods). The primary reason for focusing on
the summer season is that dust activity in the Middle East exhibits
strong seasonality, with emissions and long-range transport being
most intense and spatially coherent during summer (Supplementary
Fig. 1). This model has previously been validated for its capability to
capture key features of the IOD
26.
The CESM experiments reveal a robust dipole SST anomaly pat-
tern across the TIO in response to reduced dust forcing (see Method),
closely mirroring the observed regression between dust and SST
(Fig. 2a) despite some latitudinal displacements partly attributed to
the model bias. This pattern is characterized by signiﬁcant warming in
the western basin (up to +0.5 K) and cooling in the eastern basin (up to
−0.5 K), despite minor spatial mismatches. Strikingly, the SST response
to reduced SMED forcing translates into a DMI amplitude of roughly
1.0, comparable in magnitude to a moderate pIOD event. Both the
spatial conﬁguration and the intensity of these anomalies suggest that
SMED could act as a signiﬁcant external forcing capable of generating
pronounced interannual variability in the TIO. Moreover, when the
model is driven by observed SMED from 1980 to 2020, it reproduces a
statistically signiﬁcant fraction of the IOD variability in the CESM his-
torical run with all forcing (Supplementary Fig. 9a, R = 0.61, p ≤ 0.05).

This SST variability drives a substantial reorganization of regional
atmospheric and oceanic states. Ocean diagnostics reveal coherent
subsurface heat redistribution, including east-to-west heat transport,
reduced downwelling of warmed water in the eastern TIO, and a
westward-propagating thermocline deepening accompanied by
enhanced mixed layer depth (MLD) in the western basin (Fig.2b). It is
noteworthy that a weak increase in MLD is also simulated over the
eastern tropical Indian Ocean (Fig. 2b), which may be attributed to
internal climate variability rather than a direct response to dust for-
cing. These oceanic adjustments induce a robust atmospheric
response: enhanced convection over the warmed western TIO gen-
erates a prominent low-pressure anomaly (Fig.2c). The resulting zonal
pressure gradient establishes a thermally direct circulation, manifest-
ing as a clockwise vertical cell (Fig.2d) and surface easterly anomalies
that weaken the background westerlies across the basin (Fig. 2c).
Although slight discrepancies exist between simulated and observed
circulation features (Figs. 1f, g, 2c, d), these biases likely stem from
observational limitations, the omission of aerosol-cloud interactions in
the Community Atmosphere Model version 4 (CAM4) con ﬁguration,
and intrinsic uncertainties in representing coupled atmosphere–ocean
processes
29,30.
The TIO response described above is further validated through
sensitivity simulations under dust reduction scenarios (Supplementary
Fig. 10). The TIO response to reduced dust forcing is quanti ﬁed by
comparing SON meteorological ﬁelds between two simulations (see
Methods for details): (1) a baseline experiment using the 1980 –2020
climatological mean dust mixing ratio, and (2) a reduced-dust experi-
ment forced by a climatological dust perturbation, either reducing or
increasing dust levels based on internal variability (approximately 20%
change in dust levels, Supplementary Fig. 10; see Methods for details).
Both simulations were carried out using the CESM model with the
Parallel Ocean Program (POP2). This approach — contrasting a dust
climatology against a systematically perturbed scenario — ensures
temporal consistency and strengthens causal attribution compared to
conventional high-versus-low dust year comparisons, which could
conﬂate dust-driven signals with unrelated interannual variability.
These simulations reveal a robust dipole SST anomaly pattern over the
TIO that closely mirrors the regression pattern between dust and SST
observed in both observational data and model simulations (Supple-
mentary Fig. 11a). This pattern is characterized by signiﬁcant warming
in the western basin (up to +0.5 K) and cooling in the eastern region
(up to −0.5 K), similar to the previous simulation driven by observed
SMED from 1980 to 2020, despite minor spatial discrepancies. The
dust-increased scenario also results in a dipole SST pattern over the
TIO, albeit with an opposite signal (Supplementary Fig. 12a).
Mechanism: how dust shapes the IOD
As noted previously, the transport of dust aerosols from the Middle
East to the TIO reaches its highest intensity during boreal summer,
largely driven by the strong and persistent northwesterly Shammal
winds (Supplementary Fig. 1b)
15. These winds act as an ef ﬁcient con-
veyor, lifting and carrying vast quantities of mineral dust from the arid
regions of the Middle East across the Arabian Sea toward the western
TIO. Under this dynamic wind regime, the in ﬂux of dust aerosols is
substantial, inﬂuencing both radiative and cloud processes over the
region. Once transported, these aerosols exert a strong radiative for-
cing by absorbing solar radiation, which heats the upper troposphere,
while simultaneously reducing the amount of shortwave radiation
reaching the ocean surface. The dust-induced heating rate pro ﬁle
aligns with the simulated vertical distribution of dust extinction, which
is primarily conﬁned within the troposphere. This vertical coherence
conﬁrms that the radiative heating maxima correspond well with the
dust layer’s location, reinforcing the direct thermodynamic impact of
dust radiative absorption on the atmospheric column. (Supplementary
Fig. 13). This occurs through a combination of the direct radiative
effect of dust and its indirect effects via interactions with cloud
microphysics. As a result, the upper atmosphere experiences pro-
nounced warming, whereas the surface undergoes cooling
31,32.
The regression analysis demonstrates that reduced dust loading
shifts the radiative balance. Increased solar radiation penetrates to the
surface, as demonstrated by both coupled climate simulations (Fig.3a)
and atmosphere-only simulations (Supplementary Fig. 14a), leading to
enhanced warming near the surface and reduced heating aloft. The
translation of increased shortwave radiation into the initial SST
anomaly involves the gradual heat accumulation within the ocean ’s
mixed layer. The observed ~2-month lag re ﬂects the mixed-layer
adjustment timescale, which is approximately 2 to 2.5 months for
sustained thermal forcing in the TIO
33. Given the relatively shallow
mixed layer in the western Indian Ocean 34–36, this persistent radiative
forcing can ef ﬁciently warm the upper ocean within this timeframe,
overcoming damping from mixing and latent heat loss.
By employing prescribed SSTs, this atmosphere-only simulation
inherently excludes ocean feedbacks, thus allowing a clear attribution
of the atmospheric response to the dust forcing. This vertical redis-
tribution of energy alters atmospheric stability: the lower atmosphere
warms more strongly, while diminished warming in the upper atmo-
sphere results in a relative cooling there (Supplementary Fig. 15). These
changes signiﬁcantly affect cloud dynamics, driving a decrease in low-
level cloud cover (blue shades) and an increase in high-level clouds
(brown shades) over the western TIO (Fig.3c, d). The transformation of
cloud ﬁelds further modiﬁes the radiative budget by increasing both
longwave and shortwave radiation, thereby amplifying the warming
signal over the western TIO in synergy with the direct radiative effects
of reduced dust (Fig. 3a, b). Speci ﬁcally, the decrease in low clouds
reduces the albedo, allowing more shortwave radiation to penetrate
and warm the ocean surface
37,38. Concurrently, the increase in high
clouds enhances the atmospheric greenhouse effect by absorbing and
re-emitting longwave radiation, which inhibits radiative cooling at the
surface
37,39. These two effects act in synergy with the direct radiative
effect of reduced dust loading to amplify the warming signal over the
western TIO. This cloud feedback process, particularly the low-cloud
albedo feedback, is well-established as a key positive feedback
mechanism modulating TIO variability. Moreover, the initial warming
and enhanced evaporation might increase atmospheric water vapor
over western TIO, which strengthens downward LW radiation (Fig.3b).
Moreover, the dipole pattern of radiativeﬂux anomalies over the TIO
arises from the anomalous zonal circulation (Fig. 2d) and zonal
asymmetry in cloud vertical structure (Fig. 3c, d). Over the western

The resulting surface warming over the western TIO enhances
convective activity, triggering strong ascending air motions and
establishing a low sea level pressure anomaly in this region (Fig. 2c).
This anomaly creates a pressure gradient between the western and
eastern TIO, which in turn drives an anomalous clockwise vertical
circulation spanning the basin (Fig.2d). Near the surface, this circula-
tion anomaly is expressed as prevailing easterly wind anomalies
(Fig. 2c), opposite to the climatological westerlies over the TIO. These
anomalous easterlies suppress the usual eastward advection of warm
subsurface water from the western to the eastern TIO, leading to cold
anomalies in the mixed layer of the eastern TIO (Fig. 2b). Conse-
quently, the equatorial thermocline tilts upward toward the east and
downward toward the west, reinforcing the temperature gradient
across the basin (Fig. 2b). The intensiﬁed zonal SST gradient further
strengthens the easterly wind anomalies through the well-known
Bjerknes feedback
40. Additionally, the weakening of westerlies over the
western TIO reduces surface evaporation, which in turn enhances
warming there via the wind –evaporation–SST (WES) feedback
mechanism41,42. Cloud-induced radiative feedbacks also contribute to
this dipole pattern, as changes in cloud cover and type strengthen the
contrast between warm western and cool eastern SSTs through the
cloud–radiation–SST feedback
43,44.
The differences between atmospheric-only and coupled model
experiments further elucidate the mechanisms at play. The coupled
simulations show a stronger and more extensive signal in the eastern
TIO, with greater decreases in short- and longwave radiation (Fig. 3a,
b), larger reductions in high clouds (Fig.3c), and a notable increase in
low cloud cover (largely absent in the atmospheric-only model,
Fig. 3d). These differences underscore that changes in the eastern TIO
are predominantly governed by oceanic processes rather than purely
atmospheric responses. A decomposition of SST changes (see Meth-
ods) further conﬁrms this asymmetry: warming in the western TIO is
primarily driven by enhanced shortwave radiation absorption
(Supplementary Fig. 16a), whereas cooling in the eastern TIO is
dominated by increased ocean heat uptake (Supplementary Fig. 16b).
Moreover, the atmospheric-only simulation exhibits a radiative
response that is spatially coherent with the dust aerosol distribution
(Supplementary Fig. 14), while the coupled simulation displays addi-
tional spatial heterogeneity due to oceanic adjustments (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 11). This redistribution likely stems from ocean-atmosphere
coupling, wherein the initial radiative perturbation triggers atmo-
spheric circulation adjustments and subsequent wind-evaporation-
SST feedbacks, creating secondary radiative perturbations that par-
tially obscure the direct dust –radiation signal, which is ultimately
manifested as a teleconnected response. Notably, the spatial pattern of
the SW ﬂux response in the CAM simulations, with its maximum over
the northern and eastern Arabian Sea rather than uniformly across the
basin (Supplementary Fig. 17a, c), suggests that the radiative impact of
dust reduction initiates an atmospheric teleconnection. The initial
perturbation mainly over the land likely alters regional atmospheric
heating and circulation patterns
45–47, which in turn redistributes the
energy downstream, setting the stage for the more extensive and
evident ocean-atmosphere signals and feedbacks that propagating to
the TIO in the coupled model. Nevertheless, both experiments con-
sistently support the proposed physical framework linking Middle
Eastern dust forcing to IOD variability.
Atmosphere-only model and observation comparisons further
reinforce these ﬁndings. Changes in radiation ﬂuxes and cloud ﬁelds
simulated by the coupled model broadly agree with reanalysis datasets
(Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 14), despite some discrepancies in the
spatial distribution and magnitude of signals. Speci ﬁcally, simulated
radiation ﬂux responses are weaker than those observed (Fig. 3a, b;
Supplementary Fig. 14a–d), and the reduction in low cloud fraction
exhibits lower statistical signi ﬁcance (Fig. 3d; Supplementary
Fig. 14g –h). These mismatches likely re ﬂect uncertainties in cloud
parameterizations and their effects on radiative ﬂuxes, especially in
capturing the complex microphysical processes controlling cloud
droplet formation under varying aerosol conditions
48,49. Despite these
limitations, the combined evidence from atmospheric and coupled
simulations, supported by reanalysis, provides a coherent explanation
for the positive SST dipole pattern triggered by reduced Middle East-

We further extend the analysis to a parallel sensitivity experiment
that imposes a dust-increase scenario, while otherwise mirroring the
preceding experimental design. In contrast to the reference simula-
tion, this perturbation yields an approximately opposite atmospheric
response (Supplementary Fig. 12). Elevated dust loading systematically
reduces net surface shortwave radiation and suppresses high-level
cloud cover over the western TIO (Supplementary Fig. 12c, f). These
cloud-radiative adjustments jointly diminish both longwave and
shortwave ﬂuxes, amplifying the direct radiative cooling exerted by
the dust itself and generating a pronounced surface cooling anomaly
in the western TIO (Supplementary Fig. 12). The resulting cooling
establishes an anomalous westward surface pressure gradient across
the basin, driving persistent westerly wind anomalies at the surface of
the tropical Indian Ocean (Supplementary Fig. 12b). These westerlies
push subsurface warm water eastward, inducing positive temperature
anomalies within the oceanic mixed layer of the eastern TIO and tilting
the equatorial thermocline downward from west to east (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 12g). All above mentioned processes are favorable to the zonal
SST gradient over TIO with cooler western part and warmer eastern
part (Supplementary Fig. 12a).
The relative contribution of Middle East dust to IOD
Prior work with a parsimonious IOD model quanti ﬁed the roles of
Indian-Ocean internal dynamics and ENSO in modulating IOD intensity
and asymmetry. To quantify the relative roles of dust, ENSO and
intrinsic feedbacks in shaping the IOD, we construct a parsimonious
linear IOD model by applying least-squares regression to multi-source
observations (Methods)
50,51. The framework resolves the DMI varia-
bility into four additive terms: local ocean –atmosphere feedbacks,
remote ENSO forcing, Middle East dust forcing and a stochastic resi-
dual (Supplementary Fig. 18).
Across all dust proxies, the model attributes the largest share of
SON IOD variance to Middle East dust forcing (Supplementary Fig. 18,
the absolute values of parameter over 0.40), exceeding even ENSO
during the mature phase of the dipole. This dominance aligns with the
climatological peak of IOD amplitude in SON and validates our choice
of a time-lagged dust–IOD relationship. Conversely, ENSO emerges as
the primary driver during the preceding boreal winter and spring (not
shown here, the absolute values of parameter over 0.20), reﬂecting its
own peak in the boreal winter and the ﬁnite propagation time of the
ENSO–IOD teleconnection. Intrinsic ocean –atmosphere feedbacks
remain secondary throughout the annual cycle (the absolute values of
parameters over 0.08), accounting for a modest fraction of the
explained variance relative to the two external forcings. This decom-
position outcome closely aligns with the model-based ﬁndings dis-
cussed earlier, thereby substantiating the predominant in ﬂuence of
Middle East dust in modulating IOD variability, particularly during
boreal autumn.
Discussion
Using reanalysis datasets and coupled-model experiments, we
demonstrate that Summertime Middle East Dust (SMED) exerts an
important control on the following autumn Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD).
Dust transported by Shammal winds normally cools the western tro-
pical Indian Ocean (TIO) through upper-atmospheric absorption and
surface short-wave attenuation; when dust is diminished, the resulting
local warming generates an anomalous low that drives easterlies.
These easterlies advect warm surface water westward, suppress east-
ern ocean downwelling, shoal the thermocline in the east and deepen it
in the west, thereby sharpening the zonal SST gradient. Positive
Bjerknes feedback and accompanying low-cloud reduction and high-
cloud increase further sustain the SST gradient, strongly favoring
positive IOD (pIOD) development (Fig.4).
Over the 1980 –2020 hindcast period, the long-term ensemble
simulations driven by observed SMED from 1980 to 2020 indicate that
dust forcing generates a dipole SST trend that projects onto the pIOD
pattern and accounts for more than 36% of the long-term variance in
the Dipole Mode Index (DMI) variability. Meanwhile, at the interannual
time-scales, reduced dust anomalies elicit a basin-wide response
resembling a moderate pIOD event (DMI≈ +1.0), whereas dust deﬁcits
favor the opposite polarity.
Moreover, the decomposition of IOD growth using a linearized
framework further clariﬁes the relative roles of various forcings. Dur-
ing the critical September–October–November (SON) season, the dust
forcing in JJA emerges as the important driver of IOD variability,
exceeding even ENSO inﬂuence during the mature phase of the dipole.
In contrast, ENSO dominates during the preceding boreal winter and
spring, reﬂecting its own peak phase and the delayed adjustment of the

Our ﬁndings hold important implications for understanding and

Climate model projections suggest that in a warmer climate, the

extreme weather events across the Indian Ocean rim countries.
While this study demonstrates a robust statistical linkage between
SMED and IOD variability, reinforced by sensitivity experiments that
seek to isolate the dust in ﬂuence from other factors (e.g., ENSO and
PDO), we acknowledge that conclusively attributing this signal solely
to dust remains challenging. Despite our efforts, the potential for
complex, non-linear interactions with other aerosols or low-frequency
climate modes cannot be entirely ruled out. It should also be noted
that the magnitude of the dust radiative forcing is also dependent on
dust mineralogy, which is not speci ﬁcally constrained for the Middle
East region in our simulations, which may introduce some uncertainty
in the quantitative estimates. Therefore, we conclude that SMED likely
acts as a signi ﬁcant external forcing on the IOD, but its independent
contribution and the precise pathways involved should be further
validated through future ideali zed modeling studies designed to
deﬁnitively separate these intertwined inﬂuences. This study has lim-
itations regarding the model and station dust observations. Speci ﬁ-
cally, the model experiments prescribe dust and thus neglect
feedbacks of atmospheric circulation on dust emissions. Additionally,
the observational trends could be in ﬂuenced by the uneven spatial
distribution of meteorological stations over the analysis period.
Beyond the Middle East, other dust source regions, such as North
Africa and Central Asia, also contribute aerosols to the Indian Ocean
and may in ﬂuence its coupled climate system. Future studies should
explore how these additional dust sources interact with local and
remote drivers, including ENSO, the Madden –Julian Oscillation, and
greenhouse gas–induced warming, in modulating IOD dynamics. Fur-
thermore, improving the representation of aerosol –cloud–radiation
interactions in climate models is crucial for reducing uncertainties in
projections of IOD variability. Given the strong sensitivity of the IOD to
dust forcing demonstrated here, incorporating dust aerosol effects
into seasonal forecast systems could enhance the predictability of IOD
events and their associated climate impacts, including rainfall
anomalies in East Africa, droughts in Indonesia, and shifts in Australian
monsoon patterns.
In conclusion, our results reveal that Middle Eastern dust is not
merely a passive tracer but an active climate forcing that shapes the
evolution of the IOD through a complex interplay of radiative, oceanic,
and cloud-mediated processes. This previously underrecognized
mechanism underscores the need to integrate dust aerosols into pre-
dictive models to improve both seasonal forecasts and long-term cli-
mate projections of the IOD and its far-reaching impacts.

The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is a multivariate statistical

matrix

of climate data75,76. For standardized anomaly datasets X (representing
SMED with MISR non-spherical AOD, station dust frequency dataset,
and CESM dust AOD) and Y (representing TIO SST), both structured as
space-time matrices (m × n), the cross-covariance matrixC
XY = XY T is

orthogonal matrices whose columns correspond to the dominant
spatial patterns of SMED and TIO SST, respectively, while the diagonal
matrix Σ contains singular values (σ
k ) that quantify the magnitude of
explained cross-covariance for each coupled mode. The temporal
evolution of these modes, termed Principal Components (PCs), is
derived by projecting the original datasets onto their respective sin-
gular vectors ( PC
X = UT X , PC Y = V T Y ), which represent the normal-

loadings in U (Supplementary Fig. 6a, c, e) and V (Supplementary
Fig. 6b, d, f) highlight regions where dust activity and SST anomalies
exhibit the strongest co-variability, with the ﬁrst mode capturing the
maximum shared variability and subsequent modes accounting for
residual covariance under orthogonality constraints. To sum up, this
approach could successfully isolate coherent spatiotemporal linkages
between SMED and TIO SST.
