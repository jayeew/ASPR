Under conditions where obesity is negatively associated with
bone mass and strength 8,9, several potential interactions between
bone and adipose have been proposed. First, an obese phenotype is
frequently accompanied by the overproduction of in ﬂammatory
cytokines, like interleukin-1β and interleukin-6, and the activation of
immune cell populations
10,11 that can result in increased osteoclas-
togenesis and bone loss 12,13. Second, adipokines, such as leptin and
adiponectin, that can be altered in obesity have been shown to
impinge through both direct and indirect mechanisms on the bone
remodeling process that maintains skeletal homeostasis
14–16. Finally,
the dysregulation of hormone signaling networks related to insulin,
growth hormone, and vitamin D17,18 that can occur secondary to or in
concert with the development of obesity directly impact bone cell
performance
19–21.
Unfortunately, therapeutic strategies designed to treat the con-
sequences of obesity or to reduce body mass index also have negative
effects on the skeleton. As an example, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass sur-
gery, which induces substantial weight loss, is associated with both
short-term and long-term deterioration of bone architecture as a result
of increased bone turnover
22,23. Caloric restriction has also been linked
to bone loss and an increase in fracture risk24–26. Similarly, pharmaco-
logical agents intended to reduce insulin resistance, like thiazolidine-
diones, induce bone loss at least in part by diverting the differentiation
of osteoprogenitor cells present in bone marrow from bone-forming
osteoblast towards the adipocyte-lineage
27–29. These reports suggest
that novel, bone-sparing strategies are necessary to combat the obe-
sity epidemic.
In our previous work
30–32,w ei d e n t iﬁed bone-derived sclerostin, a
cysteine knot glycoprotein, as a regulator of adipose tissue accumu-
lation. Produced primarily by osteocytes embedded within the bone
matrix, sclerostin antagonizes Wnt/β-catenin signaling by interacting
with low-density lipoprotein receptor related protein-5 (LRP5) and
LRP6
33 and thereby inhibits osteoblastic bone formation. Loss-of-
function mutations in the SOST gene result in dramatic bone over-
growth in both humans and genetic mouse models 34,35. Besides the
skeletal effects, Sost -/- mice and mice treated with a sclerostin-
neutralizing antibody exhibit reduced fat mass, due to an altered
ratio of anabolic to catabolic metabolism and reduced adipopro-
genitor differentiation, as well as improved insulin sensitivity
30,31.
Ectopic overexpression of sclerostin produces the opposite metabolic
phenotype. Theseﬁndings suggest that in addition to functioning as a
local regulator of skeletal homeostasis sclerostin acts as a circulating
endocrine factor that contributes to the regulation of metabolic
homeostasis. This idea accords with the identiﬁcation of other bone-
derived proteins that affect systemic metabolism
36,37 and the associa-
tions between serum sclerostin and adipose tissue mass and indicators
of insulin sensitivity in humans
38–40. Moreover, the data are in line with
a growing body of work documenting actions of sclerostin on tissues
and cell types beyond the bone microenvironment, like the brain
41 and
cardiovascular system42,43.
In this study, we questioned whether sclerostin in ﬂuences the
emergence of beige adipocytes. Like brown adipocytes, beige adipo-
cytes have the potential to uncouple oxidative metabolism from ATP
synthesis and instead dissipate energy as heat. Increasing their abun-
dance, which occurs through the trans-differentiation of mature adi-
pocytes and the differentiation of resident progenitor cells
44–46,h a s
been proposed as an avenue for the treatment of obesity and related
metabolic disorders47,48.A s Sost gene deﬁciency and pharmacological
neutralization result in enhanced capacity for fatty acid oxidation and
increased expression of gene markers of beige adipocytes in white
adipose tissue depots
30, we reasoned that combining sclerostin loss-of-
function with stimuli that induce white adipose tissue beiging would
synergistically improve metabolic well-being while protecting bone
mass. Here, we report thatβ3-adrenergic and thermogenic signals that
induce white adipose tissue remodeling and beige adipogenesis
increase the expression of Sost in bone and the serum levels of scler-
ostin. Further and in support of our hypothesis, inhibition of sclerostin
function increased the abundance of beige adipocytes in response to
these stimuli. Together, these data establish a physiologic interaction
between bone and adipose that could be targeted for the treatment of
metabolic disease.

documented extra-skeletal roles for the cysteine knot protein 30,41,43.
With regards to metabolic function, Sost -/- mice exhibit reduced adi-
pose tissue mass and enhanced insulin sensitivity when compared to
littermate controls

-/- mice exhibited pockets of multi-locular adipo-

littermates (Fig. 1A). Consistent with this observation, the expression

which is in line with the lack of an effect of sclerostin deﬁciency on BAT
mass or gene expression

3.42 ± 0.81 fold increase (p = 0.002 by two-sided t test, Fig. 1F). Inter-
estingly, gene expression studies suggested that the increase in sen-
sitivity to CL316,243 was speci ﬁc to inguinal adipose (Fig. 1G–I).

Pparc1a and Ucp1 in the gonadal white adipose (gWAT) and BAT of
control and Sost

ampliﬁed in Sost-/- mice. Notably, the increased CL316,243 sensitivity in
Sost-/- mice was not due to a change in the expression level of β-

sufﬁcient to induce a detectable change in oxygen consumption

β3-adrenergic signals enhance sclerostin expression via an adi-
pose to bone relay
The consistent effect of chronic CL316,243 treatment or housing
temperature on Sost mRNA levels in bone and serum sclerostin levels
led us to question the mechanism by which thermogenic signals reg-
ulate sclerostin. Since both acute cold stimulation (4 °C) and
CL316,243 treatment stimulated rapid increases in Sost mRNA that
were translatable to an increase in serum sclerostin (Fig. 5A, B), the
effects ofβ3-agonists and temperature are likely to be due to a change
in Sost expression rather than to changes in bone cell numbers. How-
ever, cells of the osteoblast lineage, including osteocytes that produce
sclerostin, do not express the β3-adrenergic receptor
15 and treatment
of primary osteocytes with CL316,243 had no effect onSost expression
(Fig. 5C) which suggests the mechanism of action is indirect. The fact
that linoleic acid treatment induced a more than 3-fold increase inSost
mRNA in osteocyte cultures (Fig. 5C) led us to hypothesize that pro-
ducts of adipose tissue lipolysis are responsible for the control ofSost
expression after thermogenic stimulation. To test this idea, we treated
mice lacking Atgl in adipocytes (Pnpla2ﬂox/ﬂox;A d i p o Q - C r e ,A dΔPnpla2)

the lipolytic response in AdΔPnpla2 mice (Fig.5D), abolished both the
increase in Sost mRNA in the femur and the increase in serum scler-
ostin evident in control mice treated with CL316,243 (Fig.5E, F). Thus,
β3-adrenergic signals appear to be relayed to bone by adipose tissue
lipolysis.
Based on our prior work indicating that Pparγ contributes to the
regulation of sclerostin expression
52, we next reasoned that the nuclear
hormone receptor could be the link between lipolysis and Sost gene
regulation. Consistent with this idea, linoleic acid was unable to
enhance Sost expression in cultured osteocytes de ﬁcient for Pparg
(Fig.5C) and mice lacking Pparg in osteoblasts and osteocytes (ΔPparg,
Ppargﬂox/ﬂox; Bglap2-Cre) did not exhibit the increase in Sost mRNA in
the femur or serum sclerostin after 10 days of CL316,243 treatment
that was evident in control littermates (Pparg
ﬂox/ﬂox,F i g .5G, H). Similar

greater reductions in adipose tissue mass and greater abundance of
multilocular, beige adipocytes in iWAT than similarly treated control
mice (Fig. 5I–O). Together these data suggest β3-adrenergic signals
control sclerostin production in bone via adipose lipolysis and Pparγ
signaling, since osteoblasts and osteocytes cannot directly respond to
the stimuli.

Discussion
In these studies, we examined an interaction between the bone-
derived protein sclerostin and the effects of β3-adrenergic and ther-
mogenic signaling on adipose tissue and metabolism. The function of
sclerostin has primarily been studied in the context of bone acquisition
as the protein acts as a local antagonist of osteo-anabolic Wnt/ β-
catenin signaling in the bone microenvironment and loss-of-function
mutations in the SOST gene lead to dramatic bone overgrowth
33–35.
However, the presence of sclerostin in serum and the association of
serum sclerostin with parameters of body composition and metabolic
health in humans
38–40 prompted us to explore extra-skeletal functions
and whether sclerostin contributes to the regulation of metabolic
function
54. Consistent with this idea, our prior work demonstrated that
Sost-/- mice and mice in which the Sost gene has been deleted speciﬁ-
cally in osteoblasts and osteocytes exhibit a reduction in fat mass and
improved insulin sensitivity
30,31 in addition to dramatic increases in
bone mass. The emergence of beige adipocytes within the inguinal
white adipose tissue depot of Sost
-/- mice led us to question whether
the metabolic effects of sclerostin de ﬁciency were intertwined with
endogenous β3-adrenergic signals.
By examining the effect of CL316,243 and the physiologic
response to housing in the cold, we demonstrated here that these
stimuli rapidly increaseSost expression and that sclerostin deﬁciency
increases the sensitivity of the inguinal adipose tissue depot to β3-
adrenergic signals. Relative to control littermates, Sost
-/- mice exhib-
ited enhanced glucose uptake by inguinal adipocytes and an exag-
gerated lipolytic response after acute treatment with CL316,243. The
Sost
-/- mice also developed considerably more beige adipocytes in
inguinal adipose after chronic CL316,243 treatment and an identical

sensitivity evident under standard conditions when Sost

The lack of a corresponding boost in oxygen consumption or
energy expenditure in Sost-/- mice after acute CL316,243 treatment was
not entirely unexpected. In our previous work 30 and here during the
measurement of baseline VO 2 and energy expenditure prior to
CL316,243 administration, we did not observe a difference between
control and Sost

calorimetry and the relatively small contribution of white adipose tis-
sue to resting metabolic rate
55,56. Additionally, sclerostin de ﬁciency
does not in ﬂuence the catabolic activity of interscapular brown
adipocytes30 or the response of BAT to CL316,243 as shown here. This
could be due to the much lower expression of Lrp4, a receptor that
facilitates sclerostin action
57,58, in BAT than WAT 32. We would expect
that after chronic treatment with CL316,243 energy expenditure would
be higher in Sost-/- mice than controls, since this treatment and chronic
cold exposure led to larger increases in beige adipocyte abundance.
The effect of sclerostin deﬁciency on β3-adrenergic sensitivity is
driven at least in part by the increases in β-catenin signaling. Co-
stimulation of stromal vascular-derived adipocytes with Wnt10b and
CL316,243 enhanced gene expression associated with beige adipo-
cytes while postnatal expression of a constitutively active β-catenin
mutant in adipocytes produced an increase in beige adipogenesis that
was suppressible by housing at thermoneutrality just as we observed in
Sost
-/- mice. Likewise, ablating the expression of β-catenin in adipo-
cytes only in ﬂuenced body composition, insulin sensitivity, and β3-
adrenergic sensitivity when mice were also de ﬁcient for sclerostin.
These data are consistent with a study by Bagchi and colleagues59 that
demonstrated the ablation ofβ-catenin in mature adipocytes resulted
in a decrease in genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and fatty
acid metabolism, but did not affect the response to isoproterenol or
whole body metabolism due to enhanced adipoprogenitor cell
recruitment. The molecular mechanisms by which β-catenin directs
beiging in the context of sclerostin de ﬁciency will require further
investigation and the development of additional mouse models, but
one possible mechanism is through TLE3. Pearson and colleagues
60
demonstrated that conditional deletion of Tle3 in adipocytes pro-
motes a beige phenotype through the de-repression of early B-cell
factor-2. It is conceivable thatβ-catenin could exert a similar effect as
TLE3 belongs to a family of co-repressors that are displaced by β-
catenin
61 and the pair have been shown to have an antagonistic rela-
tionship in adipocytes62.
In the course of these studies, we observed that chronic treatment
with CL316,243 resulted in greater than a 25% decrease in trabecular
bone volume, which gene expression studies suggest was due to
reductions in osteoblast proliferation and an increase in signaling for
osteoclastogenesis. On the other hand, exposure to cold and treat-
ment with mirabegron did not produce signi ﬁcant effects on bone
architecture, despite the effect of all three stimuli on sclerostin
abundance. Since trabecular bone volume did trend downward in mice

sclerostin inhibition. Mirabegron-induced bone loss could also have

When placing these data in a physiologic context, the question
arises as to why bone should be involved in the response of adipose
tissue to β3-adrenergic signaling. One explanation for the interaction
relates to energy metabolism. We
70,71 and others 72–74 have
demonstrated that fatty acids liberated from adipocytes serve as an
energy source to fuel bone formation during periods of rapid bone
growth and in response to osteo-anabolic signals. In this context,
sclerostin may act as a rheostat. Since osteoblasts and osteocytes do
not directly respond toβ3-adrenergic signals, they may not be primed
to ef ﬁciently metabolize fatty acids as an energy source and would
signal back to adipocytes, via sclerostin, to limit lipolysis until it can be
coordinated with bone formation. When this negative feedback inhi-
bition is lost as in the case of sclerostin de ﬁciency, increased β3-
adrenergic signaling intensity or duration could lead to increased
lipolysis and ultimately the attainment of a beige phenotype. A second,
but related explanation, relates to the activation of transcriptional
regulators by products of lipolysis. Data we presented here suggests
that the actions ofβ3-adrenergic signals in adipose are relayed to bone
by fatty acids that activate PPAR γ signaling in osteoblasts and osteo-
cytes and perhaps other members of the PPAR family. Activation of
PPARγ, in particular, has a negative effect on osteoblast function and
impairs bone acquisition via both sclerostin-dependent and -inde-
pendent mechanisms
52,75,76. Sclerostin-mediated inhibition of lipolysis
could thereby prevent prolonged or a high level of PPARγ activation in
bone and limit bone loss.
In summary, these studies highlight an intriguing interaction
between bone and adipose tissue wherein the bone-derived protein
sclerostin in ﬂuences the process by which β3-adrenergic signals
induce white adipose tissue beiging. We expect these data and the co-
treatment study performed will be useful for the treatment of meta-
bolic disease and osteopenia/osteoporosis.
