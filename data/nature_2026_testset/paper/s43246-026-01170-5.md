Oxides with ABO 3-type perovskite structures are of great scienti ﬁc interest due to their diverse
technological applications. Here we demonstrate the phase formation pathways and local structural
evolution of two distinct perovskite oxides synthesized from hydrogen peroxide-derived peroxo
precursors. Utilizing advanced techniques, including electron real space pair distribution function
analysis, we monitored their thermal evolution. Our ﬁndings reveal that the initial disorder in the B-O
octahedron critically in ﬂuences the crystallization path. Highly distorted B-O octahedra lead to
amorphous precursors and a nucleation and growth pathway, whereas minimal distortions result in
crystalline precursors undergoing topotactic phase transformation to the ABO
3 phase. These
pathways culminate in varying disorder levels in the ﬁnal nanometric materials, providing insights
crucial for tailoring material properties for speci ﬁc applications.
In recent years, comprehensive studies on phase transformation kinetics
during the synthesis of ceramic powders have signiﬁcantly enhanced our
understanding of phase formation pathways in multi-component oxides.
These studies address the stability of metastable phases, topotactic phase
transformations, and crystallizationfrom amorphous precursors, leading to
more predictable synthetic routes
1–6. Understanding these transformation
processes not only aids in predicting desired ceramic phases but also enables
methods to control the degree of distortion in the crystal lattice
7,w h i c h
control is crucial for tailoring the electronic and chemical (catalytic) prop-
erties of the synthesized materials
7,8. This progress has been supported by
advancements in structural characterization techniques, particularly those
that analyze highly disordered or amorphous structures8–10.
Material structures can be described by their atomic arrangement. At
short distances, corresponding to the nearest atomic neighbors, the struc-
ture is governed by chemical bonds with well-deﬁned distances and angles,
forming coordination polyhedra (such as tetrahedra and octahedra) and
creating short-range order (SRO). Crystalline materials possess a periodic
atomic lattice and exhibit long-range order (LRO) over larger distances.
While the LRO in crystalline material s can be effectively determined by
diffraction techniques likeX-ray diffraction (XRD) and electron diffraction
(ED), analyzing the structure of glasses, amorphous materials, and liquids
remains a challenge since these materials often display an intermediate
atomic arrangement, termed middle-range order (MRO)
9,10.
Determining the structure of these materials in reciprocal space is
challenging due to the broadened of XRD peaks, diminished signal intensity,
and peak overlap
9–11. Accurately assessing the local atomic structure (SRO
and MRO) in amorphous, glassy, liquid, and nanocrystalline materials
requires methods that extend beyond identifying Bragg diffraction peaks. In
this sense, total scattering analysis, which includes both Bragg reﬂections
and diffuse scattering, is essential
9. The atomic pair distribution function
(PDF) technique is a speci alized approach for ana lyzing total scattering
diffraction data, providing insights into atomic distances and coordination
numbers. Among PDF techniques, electron PDF (ePDF), obtained from
ED, has emerged recently as an effective method for characterizing nano-
materials and amorphous substances
10.

Synthesis strategies for perovskiteoxides include solid-state reactions,
solvothermal processes, and sol-gel approaches, each with distinct advan-
tages and limitations

synthesis, the production of collo idal perovskite oxides remains a
challenge12–14,18. An innovative methodology for the low-temperature
synthesis of colloidally stable BaSnO₃ perovskite nanoparticles was repor-
ted by Shin et al.15,i nw h i c ha“Crystalline Superoxide–Molecular Cluster”
(CSMC) is obtained through the basic precipitation of Ba² ⁺ and Sn ⁴⁺ in
concentrated hydrogen peroxide solution. The crystalline cluster was
initially identiﬁed as BaSn(OO)₆ and subsequently converted to the desired
BaSnO₃ perovskite phase at 200 °C

et al. 19 q u e s t i o n e dt h ep r e s e n c eo fs u p e r o x i d eg r o u p si nt h ec r y s t a l l i n e
cluster, identifying it instead as BaSn(OH) ₃(OOH)(OO), an almost
superoxide-free cubic perovskite-structured compound in which the oxy-

along with peroxide bridges.
Despite the advantages of low-temperature phase formation through
CSMC, our understanding of the phase evolution during molecular cluster
decomposition toward ceramic phase formation remains limited. The
selection of the CSCM route for synthesizing perovskites was driven by the
potential to obtain both a cubic cry stalline precursor and an amorphous
precursor. This structural distinction in the precursor materials presents a
unique opportunity to deepen our understanding of the phase formation
pathways and how the structural cha racteristics of the solid precursor
inﬂuence the crystallization process.
The application of advanced structural analysis techniques, particularly
those based on Pair Distribution Function (PDF) analysis, combined with
traditional methods, can signiﬁcantly enhance our comprehension of the
ﬁnal phase formation pathway. This approach allows us to gain insights into
how the restructuring of short-range order (SRO) and medium-range order
(MRO) contributes to the emergence of long-range order (LRO). Such
understanding is crucial, as it reveals how the crystallization pathway affects
the degree of disorder within the perovskite phase.
In this work, we successfully synthesized peroxo precursors for BaSnO₃
and BaZrO ₃ perovskites through a straightforward modi ﬁcation of the
methodology proposed by Shin et al. Both precursors underwent thermal
treatment, and their phase evolutionwas rigorously evaluated using Raman
spectroscopy, thermogravimetric analysis, differential scanning calorimetry,
and X-ray powder diffraction. Furth ermore, advanced structural char-
acterization in real space was performed using extended PDF (ePDF)
analysis to investigate the structure of the precursors and the changes in their
short- and mid-range atomic environments that lead to the formation of the
perovskite phase.
Results
Chemistry of the process
Synthesis in hydrogen peroxide solutions often leads to the formation of
extremely small clusters or nanoparticles, which can be further processed for
technological applications20. We consider the methodology proposed by
Shin15 highly attractive for producing peroxo precursors for other perovskite
oxides and related devices, given its scalability, high yield, colloidal stability,
suitability for thin-ﬁlm processing, and the low temperatures required for
perovskite phase conversion. Howev er, some drawbacks related to the
stoichiometry of BaSnO₃ powders in Shin’s procedure have been reported,
addressed, and solved by Shepherd21.
A major limitation arises from the tendency of tin(IV) chloride to
hydrolyze within minutes of solubili zation, producing insoluble oxides/
hydroxides that deplete Sn⁴⁺ from the solution and result in Ba-rich powders.
This imbalance compromises the desired material properties 21.T h e
hydrolysis process can be suppressed by shifting the chemical equilibrium
through the addition of acid to the hydrogen peroxide solution21. In general,
elements in their highest oxidation states do not signiﬁcantly catalyze the
decomposition of hydrogen peroxide; instead, they interact with it under
basic conditions to form dioxygen-metal complexes with diverse bonding
modes and geometries
22,23. Zirconium is another element capable of forming
those complexes, but its chloride is highly moisture-sensitive, undergoing
rapid hydrolysis upon contact with water, air, or hydrogen peroxide solution
to yield ZrClO₂·8H₂Oa n dH C l
24,25.
In Shepherd’s21 procedure, hydrochloric acid is introduced only after
salt solubilization. However, we found this strategy insufﬁcient to suppress
the hydrolysis of moisture-sensitive ZrCl₄ i nt h eh y d r o g e np e r o x i d em e d -
ium. To overcome this limitation, we cooled the reactants in an ice bath and
added hydrochloric acid to the hydrogen peroxide solution prior to salt
dissolution. This approach successfully inhibited the hydrolysis reaction and
enabled complexation of Zr ⁴⁺, resulting in a non-colored, transparent
solution, analogous to the case of Sn⁴⁺.

Discussion
The results presented herein unequivocally demonstrate that the formation
pathways of the BSO and BZO phases are fundamentally distinct. For the
BSO phase, X-ray diffraction and ePDF analyses indicate that the decom-
position of the crystalline precursor,likely the CSMC with a stoichiometry
approximating BaSn(OH) ₃(OOH)(OO), proceeds via a diffusional
mechanism. In this process, Ba
2+ ions undergo short-range diffusion,
inducing a contraction of the precursor’s cubic lattice. This transformation
occurs at temperatures exceeding 200 °C, where partial decomposition of
peroxide groups has already taken place. The persistence of the regular
Sn–O octahedron within the CSMC structure during thermal treatment,
combined with the con traction of the Ba² ⁺ cuboctahedral cage, strongly
suggests a topotactic phasetransformation mechanism.
Conversely, the formation of the BaZrO ₃ phase from hydroperoxo
complexes follows an entirely differ ent route. The pronounced disorder
within the Zr–O octahedron, arising from multiple Zr–Ob o n d i n gc o nﬁg-
urations, leads to the generation of an amorphous precursor that crystallizes
only at elevated temperatures (T > 600 °C). Nucleation appears to be directly
associated with the elimination of octahedral disorder, which occurs sub-
sequent to the removal of peroxide a nd hydroperoxide groups, thereby
enabling structural ordering. Consequently, this phase formation involves
nucleation and growth process, acco mpanied by substantial structural
rearrangements, in stark contrast to the topotactic transformation observed
for BSO.
This divergence in formation pathways is expected to manifest in the
degree of disorder of the resulting particles. Crystalline disorder refers to
deviations from an ideal lattice, encompassing defects such as vacancies,
interstitials, dislocations, and chemical heterogeneities. Such disorder can
be evaluated by comparing the crystallite mean size (CMS) obtained from
XRD with the local structural coherence (LSC) estimated via ePDF
9,35.
Figure 6 illustrates the evolution of these parameters with increasing heat-
treatment temperature. Notably, BZO samples exhibit markedly reduced
LSC values up to 600 °C, consistent with their amorphous nature and
absence of LRO. Upon crystallization at 800 °C, BZO attains a crystallite
size of 23 nm and an LSC of 3.8 nm, indicating that the crystallite mean
size is approximately six times greater than the LSC, a clear indication of
signiﬁcant structural disorder. In contrast, BSO treated at 800 °C exhibits a
crystallite mean size of 9.5 nm and an LSC of 4 nm (a ratio of 2.3),
reﬂecting a comparatively lower degree of disorder. This reduced disorder
is plausibly linked to its topotactic transformation pathway, whereas BZO,
formed via nucleation and growth with extensive structural reorganiza-
tion, displays pronounced disorder.

Methods
All chemicals were used as received without further puriﬁcation. Moisture-
and air-sensitive reactants were stored in a glovebox.
Synthesis of BaSnO₃ and BaZrO₃ perovskite peroxo precursors
Peroxo precursors for BaSnO ₃ (BSO-RT) and BaZrO ₃ (BZO-RT) were
synthesized via a modiﬁed route based on Shin et al.15. In a typical procedure,
5 mmol of anhydrous Barium(II) ch loride (99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich),
5 mmol of citric acid (99.0%, Synth), and 5 mmol of either anhydrous
Tin(IV) chloride (99.995%, Sigma-Aldrich) or anhydrous Zirconium(IV)
chloride (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) were accurately weighed inside a glovebox.
After weighing, the beaker was sealed with paraﬁlm to allow removal from
the glovebox and placed in an ice bath. The para ﬁlm was then quickly
removed, and a cold solution cont aining 85 mL of hydrogen peroxide
(>35%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 6.5 mL of hydrochloric acid (36.5–38%, Sigma-
Aldrich) was added to the salts for solu bilization while maintaining the
mixture in the ice bath. The resulting colorless transparent solutions were
transferred to a round-bottomﬂask and placed in a 50 °C bath. After 15 min,
37.5 mL of cold ammonium hydroxide (28–30% NH₃,S i g m a - A l d r i c h )w a s
rapidly added under vigorous magnet ic stirring, forming a whitish sus-
pension. A Graham condenser was attached, and the suspensions were
allowed to age for 1 h. The peroxo precursors were collected by cen-
trifugation in six 50 mL tubes, washed multiple times with Milli-Q® water
until the supernatant reached pH≈7, and subsequently washed twice with
isopropyl alcohol (P.A., Synth). The washed precursor powders were dried
under vacuum at room temperature in a desiccator, then gently crushed in a
crucible (BSO-RT and BZO-RT). Powdered precursors were subjected to
thermal treatment in a tubular furnace under vacuum (≈ 0.1 Torr) with a
heating rate of 20 °C min⁻¹ for 1 h at 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C,
yielding BSO-200, BSO-400, BSO-600, BSO-800 and BZO-200, BZO-400,
BZO-600, BZO-800, respectively.

calorimetry (DSC)

under a synthetic air ﬂow of 20 mL min ⁻¹ and heating rates of 5

Raman spectroscopy
Raman spectra were acquired with a Horiba XploRA Plus spectrometer
using a 532 nm laser at 10% intensity, a slit of 50 μm, a hole of 100 μm, a
spectral step of 2 cm⁻¹, and a spectral range of 300–1200 cm⁻¹.
Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD)
XRD patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance ECO diffractometer
in Θ/2Θ geometry using Cu–Kα radiation, with a step size of 0.02° in the 2Θ
range of 20–90°. Phase identiﬁcation was carried out by comparison with
reference CIF ﬁles from the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD):
BaSn(OH)₃(OOH)(OO) (30141), BaSnO₃ (239582), BaZrO₃ (90049), and
BaCO₃ (144618). Average crystallite sizes were estimated using the Scherrer
equation, and Rietveld reﬁnements were performed with TOPAS software
using the same reference CIFﬁles.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nano-area electron
diffraction (NAED) and scanning transmission electron
microscopy (STEM)
TEM images were acquired using a double aberration–corrected FEI Titan
Cubed Themis operated at 300 kV and equipped with a 4k × 4k Ceta 16 M
CMOS camera. Since parallel beam conditions are essential for ePDF ana-
lysis, electron diffraction patterns were acquired in nanoarea electron dif-
fraction (NAED) mode. A 50 μm C2 aperture was used to establish quasi-
parallel illumination, resulting in an illuminated area of approximately
300 nm in diameter. Diffraction patterns were recorded using a camera with
a length of 360 mm.
Prior to data acquisition, beam damage was evaluated, and no obser-
vable damage was detected at a beam current of ≈250 pA at the sample.
Individual diffraction frames were recorded with an exposure time of 0.5 s,
corresponding to a dose rate of approximately 5.95 e⁻ Å⁻²s ⁻¹a n daﬂuence of
≈110.4 e⁻ Å⁻² per pattern. To improve counting statistics at higher scattering
vectors (q-range), a stack of sixteen diffraction patterns was recorded and
aligned to sub-pixel accuracy. The reported dose values correspond to
individual diffraction frames; ePDF analysis was performed by summing
sixteen frames.
Standard post-processing steps—including masking of the beam-stop
region, determination of the diffraction pattern center, and correction for
elliptical distortions—were applied prior to extraction of one-dimensional
intensity proﬁles for ePDF data processing. High-resolution STEM images
were acquired in bright-ﬁeld (BF) mode using a probe current of 125 pA.
Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses and
quantiﬁcations
