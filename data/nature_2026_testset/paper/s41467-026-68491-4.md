An acidic tumor microenvironment (TME), a hallmark of cancer progression,
promotes tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis. Detecting and targeting
tumor acidity have emerged as key frontiers in early cancer diagnosis and
treatment. However, current approaches lack sensitive and speciﬁcm e t h o d s
to visualize and quantify tumor acidityin vivo across different stages of tumor
development. Here we show that ATP6V0C, a subunit of the V-ATPase
responsible for proton transmembrane transport, is a critical mediator of TME
acidiﬁcation. High ATP6V0C expression correlates with hepatocellular carci-
noma (HCC) progression and metastasis. To enable real-time assessment of
tumor acidity, we develop a pH-responsive ratiometric photoacoustic sensor
(PPS) that dynamically monitors TME acidity throughout HCC initiation, pro-
gression, and metastasis. Using PPS-assisted photoacoustic molecular ima-
ging, we visualize the spatiotemporal evolution of TME acidity from early
tumor foci to solid tumors. PPS exhibits enhanced photothermal effects under
acidic conditions, and its combination with the proton pump inhibitor
esomeprazole synergistically suppresses tumor growth. Overall, PPS-assisted
photoacoustic molecular imaging provides a sensitive and speciﬁc approach
to characterize tumor initiation and progression, supporting its potential
application in early diagnosis and therapeutic strategies that target the
acidic TME.
The initiation and progression of cancer are closely associated with
alterations in the tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly loca-
lized acidiﬁcation arising from metabolic reprogramming and vascular
perfusion abnormalities
1. A major contributor to this acidi ﬁcation is
enhanced glycolysis in tumor cells, commonly known as the“Warburg
effect”, wherein cells preferentially rely on glycolysis for adenosine
triphosphate production even under normoxic conditions2,r e s u l t i n g
in the accumulation of lactic acid and protons. These acidic metabo-
lites are exported via proton pumps such as V-ATPases and lactate
transporters, thereby lowering extracellular pH 3,4. In parallel, the
structurally abnormal and poorly perfused tumor vasculator impairs
proton (H+) clearance, producing a TME pH typically between 6.5 and
6.85. This acidic milieu is not only a hallmark of cancer progression but
also actively promotes tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis 4,6,
making it an attractive target for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
However, TME acidity is highly heterogeneous and dynamically
evolves in early-stage tumors and metastatic lesions
6,7. In these stages,
low-level acidiﬁcation and the complex structure of the extracellular
matrix complicate both detection and therapeutic targeting8,9.T h u s ,
there is a pressing need for highly sensitive, speciﬁc, and noninvasive

Biomedical imaging technologies are indispensable for tumor
diagnosis and therapeutic evaluation 10–12. Conventional modalities
such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound, and computed
tomography are widely used to detect primary and metastatic
tumors
13–15, but often lack the spatial resolution or molecular sensitivity
required to capture subtle changes within the TME, particularly in
early-stage or small tumors. Molecular imaging provides greater sen-
sitivity: ﬂuorescence imaging enables responsive detection of acidity
using acid-responsive probes
16,17, yet limited optical penetration
restricts its applicability in deeper tissues. MRI-based extracellular pH
(pHe) measurement offers further biological insights
18,19,b u tt y p i c a l l y
faces inherent trade-offs between spatial and temporal resolution that
constrain real-time and high-resolution assessment of dynamic acidity.
Therefore, beyond sensitivity and speci ﬁcity, the ability to con-
tinuously monitor and image the spatial-temporal evolution of TME
acidity remains crucial for advancing early cancer diagnosis and
improving therapeutic outcomes.
Photoacoustic (PA) molecular imaging has emerged as a powerful
modality that combines the advantages of PA imaging with molecular
labeling
20–23. Unlike conventional optical methods, which generally
penetrate less than 1 mm, PA imaging, based on the “light-in, sound-
out” principle, enables acoustic-resolution (~100 µm) imaging at
centimeter-scale depths, while maintaining diffraction-limited optical
resolution (~3µm) imaging for superﬁcial tissues (~1–2m m )24–28.T h e s e
capabilities allow PA imaging to detect acidic TMEs at multiple tumor
depths with high resolution
29–32. Furthermore, molecular labeling
improves speciﬁcity and sensitivity by using imaging probes with
speciﬁc properties to target tumor-associated molecules and enable
quantitative pH mapping within the TME33–35. In recent years, several
pH-activatable PA probes, including organic polymers and inorganic
nanomaterials
36–38, have demonstrated real-time intratumoral pH
monitoring with high speci ﬁcity and sensitivity 39,40. While these
advances address the critical challenge of characterizing tumor acidity,
existing approaches remain limited in detecting TME acidity during
cancer progression and metastasis, in which proton levels are low and
ﬂuctuating dynamically
41. In this context, dynamic studies of TME
acidity evolution across different stages of development are crucial for
gaining a comprehensive understanding of tumor progression,
metastasis, and therapeutic responses, ultimately guiding the devel-
opment of more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
In this work, we develop a highly speci ﬁc and sensitive PA mole-
cular imaging strategy to detect TME acidity across tumor initiation,
progression, and metastasis (Fig. 1a, b). We identify ATP6V0C, a key
regulator of proton transport via V-ATPase, as a critical mediator of
TME acidiﬁcation through bioinformatics analyses and validation in
clinical hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) samples. Elevated ATP6V0C
expression is strongly correlated with poor prognosis in HCC patients,
suggesting that ATP6V0C-mediated TME acidiﬁcation is a critical dri-
ver of HCC growth and metastasis. To monitor TME pH dynamics, we
design a pH-responsive ratiometric PA sensor (PPS) with high sensi-
tivity and speciﬁcity for H
+ detection. Using PPS-assisted PA imaging,
we visualize TME acidiﬁcation in murine HCC models and capture pH
dynamics throughout tumor progression and metastasis. We further
apply pH-responsive ratiometric PA imaging to delineate tumor
boundaries in resected clinic HCC tissues. Finally, we evaluate a ther-
apeutic potential combining PPS-based H
+-activated photothermal
therapy (PTT) with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), which effectively
suppresses tumor growth (Fig.1c). Overall, these results demonstrate
that PPS-assisted ratiometric PA imaging provides a promising strategy
for highly speci ﬁc and sensitive detection and monitoring of TME
acidity at different tumor stages, elucidate acidi ﬁcation mechanisms
that drive HCC development, and support improved early tumor
diagnosis and precision therapeutic strategies.
Results
Identiﬁcation of ATP6V0C as a prognostic biomarker in
human HCC
To investigate the clinical relevance of ATP6V0C in HCC, we con-
ducted a comprehensive analysis of publicly available datasets in The
Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO).
ATP6V0C expression was signiﬁcantly higher in HCC tissues than in
adjacent nontumor liver tissues (Fig. 2a–d). Notably, ATP6V0C
expression was elevated in advanced-stage HCC (stage III-IV) relative to
early-stage disease (I-II) (Fig.2e), suggesting its potential role in tumor
progression. ATP6V0C, a key subunit of V-ATPase, mediates proton
transport, contributing to extracellular acidiﬁcation within the TME.
This acidiﬁcation promotes key oncogenic processes, including cell
proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion
42,43. Kaplan-Meier survival
analysis revealed that higher ATP6V0C expression was signi ﬁcantly
associated with poor prognosis in HCC patients (Fig.2f), a ﬁnding that
is consistent with the role of ATP6V0C in tumor aggressiveness. Fur-
ther correlation analyses revealed positive associations between
ATP6V0C expression and the expression of molecular markers of
tumor proliferation, metastasis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition
(EMT) (Fig. 2g, h, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). These ﬁndings were vali-
dated in paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues collected from HCC
patients. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immuno-
ﬂuorescence analysis con ﬁrmed the marked overexpression of
ATP6V0C in HCC tissues (Fig. 2i, j). This overexpression likely con-
tributes to TME acidiﬁcation through the role of ATP6V0C in regulat-
ing V-ATPase activity, thereby facilitating tumor progression.
Collectively, theseﬁndings identify ATP6V0C as a critical mediator of
HCC progression and a potential prognostic biomarker. Its role in
promoting TME acidiﬁcation highlights its therapeutic potential, sug-
gesting that targeting V-ATPase-mediated acidi ﬁcation could repre-
sent a promising strategy to improve clinical outcomes in HCC
patients.
Synthesis and characterization of PPS
To investigate the role of V-ATPase-mediated tumor microenviron-
ment acidi ﬁcation in cancer progression, we developed a pH-
responsive ratiometric photoacoustic sensor (PPS) by integrating
polyaniline (PANI) with DSPE-PEG-COOH to improve water stability
(Fig. 2k). PANI was selected because of its distinct protonation beha-
vior: under neutral or basic conditions, deprotonated PANI (emer-
aldine base, EB) loses conductivity and near-infrared (NIR) absorption,
whereas in acidic environments, protonated PANI (emeraldine salt, ES)
has high conductivity and strong NIR absorption
44–46. Optimization of
the synthesis conditions further increased the H + responsiveness of
PPS (Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. 3a –c). The ratio of the absor-
bance values at 780 nm and 1064 nm (Abs780/Abs1064) demonstrated
a strong linear correlation with pH (Supplementary Fig. 3d–f).
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed uniform PPS
nanoparticles with an average diameter of approximately 80 nm
(Fig. 2l), which remained stable in aqueous solution for 7 days (Sup-
plementary Fig. 4). As the pH decreased from 7.0 to 3.0, PPS exhibited
a progressive increase in absorbance at 780 nm, whereas the absor-
bance at 1064 nm remained relatively constant (Fig. 2m and Supple-
mentary Fig. 5). This shift reﬂects the proton-responsive transition of
PANI from the EB to ES state
47. Correspondingly, PA imaging conﬁrmed
as i g n iﬁcant increase in the PA signal at 780 nm under mildly acidic
conditions (pH 6.5), whereas the signal at 1064 nm remained unchan-
ged (Fig.2n). Compared with that at pH 7.4 (80 nm), the particle size of
PPS at pH 6.5 (130 nm) was greater (Supplementary Fig. 6). This may be
attributed to the protonation of functional groups on the particle

PPS-assisted PA imaging in an early HCC model
To evaluate the pH responsiveness of PPS in vivo, weﬁrst preincubated
PPS in buffers at pH 6.5 and 7.4 and injected the probe into the bilateral
muscles of healthy mice. PA imaging conﬁrmed a signiﬁcantly greater
PA signal at 780 nm under acidic conditions (pH 6.5) than at neutral pH
(pH 7.4) (Supplementary Fig. 14). Then, in a subcutaneous HCC model,
PPS was intratumorally injected into both tumor and muscle tissues
(Supplementary Fig. 15a). PA imaging at 0.5 h postinjection revealed a
signiﬁcantly elevated PA
780/PA1064 ratio in tumor tissue, indicating
local acidi ﬁcation (Supplementary Fig. 15b –f). Further intravenous
administration of PPS resulted in peak tumor accumulation at 9 h
postinjection (Supplementary Fig. 16). These results establish PPS as a
promising ratiometric PA probe for monitoring TME pH values.
Next, we evaluated the sensitivity of the PA ratio imaging strategy
using PPS for the early detection of HCC development. During the
initial stages of HCC progression, we hypothesize that the pH of the
local TME dynamically decreases as the tumor grows, accompanied by
neovascularization and hypoxia (Fig.3a). Ultrasound imaging (Fig.3b)
revealed a progressive increase in tumor size following HCC cell
injection, with the tumor mass con ﬁrmed as a solid tumor through
H&E staining (Supplementary Fig. 17). To monitor pH changes during
tumor formation, PPS was intravenously administered on days 0, 1, 3, 5
and 7. Using acoustic-resolution PA microscopy (AR-PAM) (Supple-
mentary Fig. 18) for longitudinal monitoring, we observed a signiﬁcant
increase in the PA780/PA1064 ratio starting on day 3, corresponding to
a decrease in the local pH from neutral (pH 7.33) to acidic (pH 7.08)
(Fig. 3d). By day 7, the TME pH further decreased to 6.42, which is
consistent with previously reported TME values
49,50. Notably,
pretreatment with PPIs signiﬁcantly attenuated the decrease in PA (pH
7.56 to pH 7.09) (Fig. 3c, e), suggesting that PPS is highly sensitive in
detecting changes in the pH of the TME. The PPS-assisted PA imaging
results were comparable to the pH measurements obtained using
clinical PET imaging (Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating that an acidic
TME emerges during the super-early stages of tumor development.
To explore the mechanisms underlying TME acidiﬁcation, tumor
vessel density and tortuosity were quantiﬁed during HCC progression.
Both parameters increased over time (Fig. 3f, g, and Supplementary
Fig. 20), which is consistent with the development of disorganized
tumor vasculature that promotes local hypoxia and the accumulation
of metabolic waste (such as lactate), thereby disrupting pH home-
ostasis in the TME. Immuno ﬂuorescence staining further demon-
strated a progressive increase in hypoxia and angiogenesis markers
following tumor cell injection (Fig.3h–j). Correlation analysis using the
Pearson method revealed a negative correlation between hypoxia and
acidity (r = −0.9586) and a positive correlation between hypoxia and
angiogenesis (r = 0.9949) (Fig.3k). Theseﬁndings demonstrate that an
acidic TME, hypoxia, and angiogenesis appear at the super-early stages
of tumor formation and are closely interconnected. Given their critical
roles in tumor proliferation, metastasis,s and therapeutic
resistance
51–53, these biomarkers represent promising targets for early
therapeutic interventions. PPS-assisted PA imaging offers a highly
sensitive, noninvasive approach for detecting TME acidiﬁcation, with
signiﬁcant potential for super-early tumor diagnosis and monitoring.
PPS-assisted PA imaging in liver orthotopic and metastatic
tumor models
Real-time imaging of H ⁺ levels in liver tumor lesions is crucial for
understanding pathological processes within the acidic TME. Before in
vivo tumor imaging, we evaluated whether other pathological factors
could interfere with pH monitoring. Using two non-tumor disease
models that induce local in ﬂammation or tissue acidosis (acute liver
failure and hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury), PA imaging showed
that PPS exhibited minimal or negligible signal enhancement com-
pared with tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 21), indicating excellent
pH speci ﬁcity and insensitivity to other metabolic components in
the TME.
To extend our ﬁndings beyond subcutaneous tumor models, we
evaluated PPS-assisted PA imaging in liver orthotopic and metastatic
tumor models. Orthotopic HCC and pancreatic cancer liver metastasis
models were established via the in situ injection of HCC cells into the
liver and pancreatic cancer cells into the spleen, respectively (Fig.4a).

PPS-assisted PA imaging-guided lymphadenectomy in a lymph
node metastatic tumor model
Lymph node metastasis is a major route for systemic tumor dis-
semination, and the complete removal of metastatic lymph nodes
(MLNs) is critical for improving patient prognosis
54–56.T oe x p l o r et h e
potential of PPS in detecting MLNs and guiding lymphadenectomy
during navigational surgery, we established a lymph node metastatic
tumor model (Fig.5a). HCCLM3-Luc cells were injected into the mouse
paw, and ﬂuorescence imaging conﬁrmed successful metastasis to the
lymph nodes, with signals localized in the popliteal region (Fig.5b).
PPS was subsequently administered via paw injection, and PA
imaging was performed 30 min later. Compared with that in benign
lymph nodes (BLNs), a signiﬁcantly greater PA signal was detected in
metastatic lymph nodes, corresponding to an acidic microenviron-
ment with an estimated pH of 6.5 (Fig. 5c–e). Guided by PA imaging,
the high-signal regions indicative of MLNs were surgically identi ﬁed
and excised. Reimaging of the surgical area and excised tissues con-
ﬁrmed the successful removal of the high-signal regions (Fig.5f–h). pH
measurement results for the excised tissues were consistent with the
PA imaging results (pH 6.5), and subsequent H&E and IHC staining
conﬁrmed the presence of tumor-invaded lymph nodes (Fig. 5i).
These ﬁndings demonstrate that the PPS can be used to detect
MLNs with high sensitivity and provide precise, image-guided naviga-
tion for lymphadenectomy, highlighting the potential of PPS to

PPS-assisted PA imaging of human HCC samples
To evaluate the clinical potential of PPS, human HCC specimens were
obtained from patients who were histologically diagnosed with HCC
(Table S2). These specimens were treated with PPS for PA imaging
(Fig. 6a). PA signals at 780 nm were signiﬁcantly stronger in the tumor
regions of PPS-treated samples than in the surrounding paracancerous
tissues (Fig. 6b, c). Ratio imaging analysis revealed that the tumor
regions exhibited an acidic environment, with clear delineation of the
tumor margins.
To further validate the applicability of PPS, additional liver cancer
specimens from seven different patients were analyzed. All the speci-
mens presented well-deﬁned tumor boundaries. H&E staining corro-
borated the PA imaging ﬁndings by distinctly differentiating tumors
from normal tissues (Fig. 6d). Quantitative analysis of the PA signal
intensity in PPS-incubated samples indicated that the tumor regions
had lower pH values than paracancerous tissues (Fig. 6e). Ex vivo
imaging of clinical specimens offers a unique opportunity for mole-
cular imaging studies of primary tumors and the TME in patients, as
specimens from HCC patients preserve the molecular characteristics
of the original TME and intratumoral heterogeneity. These ﬁndings
highlight the potential of PPS to selectively identify tumor regions on
the basis of their acid speciﬁcity, providing a promising approach for
precise tumor margin delineation and for guiding surgical resection.

Biosafety evaluation
To assess the potential for the clinical translation of PPS, its biosafety
was evaluated both in vitro and in vivo. As shown in Supplementary
Fig. 37, PPS at concentrations up to 500 μg/mL had no signi ﬁcant
cytotoxicity in normal liver cells (THLE-3) or the liver cancer cell lines
HepG2 and HCCLM3. Hemocompatibility testing demonstrated mini-
mal hemolysis following a 4-hour incubation with red blood cells
(Supplementary Fig. 38). To further evaluate long-term safety, chronic
toxicity studies were performed over a 28-day period following intra-
venous administration of PPS. Comprehensive hematological, histo-
logical, and physiological analyses showed no signiﬁcant abnormalities
in blood parameters, body weight, or major organ histopathology
compared to control mice (Supplementary Fig. 39–42). Furthermore,
histological examinations of major organs revealed no signi ﬁcant
pathological changes compared with those in saline-treated controls
(Supplementary Fig. 43). These ﬁndings demonstrate that PPS has
excellent in vitro and in vivo biosafety, even under prolonged systemic
exposure, supporting its potential for future clinical applications.
Discussion
In this study, we have developed a non-invasive, real-time imaging
strategy to monitor spatiotemporal changes in TME acidity during
tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. By integrating bioin-
formatics analyses with validation using clinical samples, we identiﬁed
ATP6V0C as a key driver of TME acidi ﬁcation during tumor develop-
ment. Elevated ATP6V0C was strongly correlated with increased HCC
acidity and poor patient prognosis. Based on these ﬁndings, we
designed a pH-responsive ratiometric photoacoustic sensor with
excellent biocompatibility. PPS undergoes a pH-induced transition
from EB to ES within the physiological pH range (pH 8-6), enabling
sensitive H
+ activation and ampliﬁed PA signals at 780 nm. Compared
with existing pH sensors, such as small-molecule ﬂuorescent probes
that exhibit limited tissue penetration and single-wavelength PA
probes that are susceptible to nonspeciﬁcs i g n a lﬂuctuations
62,63,P P S
provides intrinsic ratiometric calibration, signi ﬁcantly improving
quantitative accuracy in deep-tissue imaging. This feature establishes
PPS as a promising and reliable tool for precise tumor detection and
targeted HCC therapy.
Leveraging PPS ’s acid-responsive properties, we identi ﬁed a
strong association between early-stage TME acidiﬁcation in HCC and
multiple molecular events, including hypoxia and angiogenesis, which
collectively drive tumor proliferation and metastasis. Our observation
that acidiﬁcation precedes macroscopic tumor growth aligns with the
emerging concept of“acidic niche” formation as a facilitator of tumor
progression
64.T h i sﬁnding extends beyond the well-established role of
acidosis in driving invasion by highlighting its potential as an early
diagnostic biomarker
6,65. In addition, we validated the progressive and
dynamic acidiﬁcation of the TME across various stages of HCC pro-
gression, providing in vivo evidence that proton pump-driven acid-
iﬁcation represents a continuous process in tumor development.
These ﬁndings highlight the potential of proton pump-driven acid
imaging strategies for both HCC diagnosis and therapy monitoring.
Notably, these results were further corroborated in resected clinical
HCC tissues. While PPS-based PA imaging is limited in penetration
depth compared with MRI or PET, it provides superior spatial resolu-
tion (~200 μm) and real-time monitoring of dynamic pH changes.

Molecular docking of ATP6V0C with ESOM
To investigate the interaction between ESOM and ATP6V0C, the 3D
structure of ESOM was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://
pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and converted into mol2 format using
Chem3D software. Structural optimization was performed using
SYBYL software with the Tripos force ﬁeld, and Gasteiger-Huckel
charges were assigned to the ligand. The optimized structure was then
saved in mol2 format for subsequent molecular docking simulations.
The crystal structure of ATP6V0C (PDB ID: 6WLW) was obtained
from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org). The protein
structure was preprocessed in SYBYL ’s Docking module, which
involved removing water molecules, adding hydrogen atoms, and
assigning charges. The active binding site was identiﬁed based on the
co-crystallized ligand in the original complex structure. The prepared
protein was then saved for docking simulations.
Molecular docking was carried out using the Surﬂex-Dock module
in SYBYL, with esomeprazole as the ligand and ATP6V0C (6WLW) as
the receptor. Binding af ﬁnity was quanti ﬁed using the Total Score
function, where higher scores indicate stronger binding interactions. A
Total Score ≥ 7 was considered indicative of signiﬁcant binding. Post-
docking analysis included the generation of 2D interaction diagrams to
visualize and analyze the molecular interactions between ESOM and
ATP6V0C.

Single-cell Transcriptomic analysis
The single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE149614 (10 HCC patients) was
