Liyang Liao & Yoshichika Otani
The emerging ﬁeld of orbitronics seeks to harness the orbital angular momentum (OAM) of electrons as
an information carrier, offering new opportunities for next-generation memory and logic technologies.
A central question for its viability is whether OAM currents can propagate ef ﬁciently across materials.
Recent experiments have revealed highly nonlocal orbital transport phenomena, with characteristic
lengths extending to several tens of nanometers — far beyond expectations based on conventional
orbital quenching. These ﬁndings have fueled a theoretical debate with two opposing paradigms. One
posits that genuine long-range transport arises from symmetry-protected hot spots in the electronic
band structure, which preserve OAM coherence. The other contends that OAM currents are
intrinsically short-lived, and that the observed long-range signatures originate instead from the local
generation of orbital polarization by charge current gradients near boundaries. This perspective
reviews the experimental evidence and theoretical foundations of both views, outlining the future
research strategies needed to resolve this fundamental issue in orbitronics.
Spintronics has signi ﬁcantly advanced data storage and processing by
exploiting the electron’s spin degree of freedom. A major mission for
modern spintronics is to explore efﬁcient current-induced torque to switch
magnetizations, as the writing technique of magnetic memory cells. A clo-
sely relatedﬁeld, orbitronics, is now emerging to harness the orbital angular
momentum (OAM) of electrons. In contrast to spin, OAM offers distinct
advantages, such as higher efﬁciency in torque generation and the potential
for realization even in light metals with weak spin-orbit coupling (SOC)
1–4.
The foundation of many orbitronic concepts is the Orbital Hall Effect
(OHE), where a longitudinal electric ﬁeld drives a transverse current of
OAM5–7 (Fig. 1a). It is well-known that OAM is quenched in solids because
the non-spherical crystal electric ﬁeld from neighboring atoms lifts the
energy degeneracy of the atomic orbitals, pushing the electrons into speciﬁc
spatial orientation and preventing the free circulation required to generate
OAM. However, in the non-equilibrium states, e.g., under an external
electricﬁeld, OAM can be regenerated through the interference of different
orbitals, e.g., the hybridization betweend and p orbitals, known as OHE. It
creates an accumulation of orbital polarization at the boundaries of a
material (Fig.1b). This orbital accumulationc a nt h e nb eu s e d ,f o re x a m p l e ,
to exert powerful torques on adjacent magnetic layers. However, the viability
of orbitronics hinges on a critical, yet unresolved question: how far can
orbital currents propagate before they relax?
Experimentally, the characteristic length scale of OAM transport is
characterized by the decay lengthλ of edge OAM accumulation (Fig.1b). An
increasing number of experiments indicate that OAM transport exhibits a
highly nonlocal character. For example, enhanced orbital torque has been
reported in normal metal/ferromagnet bilayers when the individual layer
thicknesses are increased
8,9. Such observations suggest that orbital effects
persist well beyond the atomic scale, motivating further investigation of their
underlying transport mechanisms. In-plane nonlocal transport experiments
have further revealed an orbital propagation length of approximately
100 nm in Cu/CuO
x heterostructures10. Time-domain terahertz (THz)
spectroscopy on a variety of Ni-based bilayers, including Ni/Ti, Ni/Mn, Ni/
Cu, and Ni/Pt, has consistently demo nstrated that orbital contributions
dominate over spin, with linear O AM propagation and decay lengths
reaching up to ~80 nm in Ni/W11–14. Complementary magneto-optical Kerr
effect (MOKE) measurements on single-layer Ti and Cr ﬁlms have also
detected clear OAM accumulation at the surface, yielding orbital decay
lengths of ~74 nm in Ti15 and ~6.6 nm for Cr16.
Together, these results exhibit material-dependent length scales that
signiﬁcantly exceed atomic dimensions. Thisdiversity of observations strongly
suggests that orbital angular momentum can persist and inﬂuence transport
phenomena over mesoscopic distances, thereby posing fundamental ques-
tions regarding the mechanisms that protect or regenerate OAM in solids.
Plenty of work has been done regarding this nonlocality of OAM. The
efforts can be divided into two major categories: To understand the non-
locality of OAM, one needs to either (a) explain the long propagation length
of OAM, or (b) ﬁnd another degree of freedom, which can mediate the
nonlocality of OAM, such that OAM itself does not propagate but can be
generated locally through a propagated degree of freedom. In this per-
spective, weﬁrst introduce two represented pictures, namely the“hot spot”
picture and the local generation picture, corresponding to (a) and (b)

The “Hot Spot” picture for long-range transport
The ﬁrst major theoretical framework developed to explain the non-
locality of OAM is the “hot spot” model17. While the crystal ﬁeld gen-
erally lifts orbital degeneracy at the Fermi surface and quenches OAM,
this model posits that at certain points or lines in momentum space (the
Brillouin zone), crystal symmetries preserve near-degenerate orbital
states (Fig. 2). These symmetry-protected regions are referred to as
“hot spots.”
This model considers a normal metal (NM)/ferromagnetic metal
(FM) bilayer where OAM is injected from the NM layer to the FM layer
with hot spots in its momentum space. Electrons with momentum near
such hot spots act as the principal carriers of long-range OAM currents.
Upon injection of an orbital polarization, contributions from states away
from hot spots decay rapidly, whereas those associated with hot spots
maintain coherence between nearly degenerate orbital states (e.g.,d
xy and
dyz). The persistence of their relative phase enables the formation of long-

This model, therefore, attributes the long orbital relaxation length to
the symmetry protection at hot spots. The orbital symmetry of these regions
dictates the possible orientation of OAM; for instance, degeneracy between
d

Kramers degeneracy of spins, which allows spin orientation along all three
spatial axes. Consequently, the dynamics of OAM sustained by hot spots are
expected to differ fundamentally fromspin dynamics under external per-
turbations. For example, in the presence of an orthogonal magnetic ﬁeld,
spins undergo Hanle precession because of their isotropic degeneracy. In
contrast, OAM with strongly anisotropic lifetimes depending on the orbital
direction would exhibit a markedly different counterpart to Hanle
precession
18,19.
The limitations of this hot-spot model lie in the momentum space
matching conditions and the disorder effects. Under the assumption that
the transport contribution uniformly distributes in the momentum-space
volumes, the hot-spot should not have a large contribution to the OAM
transport, since the volume of the hot-spot region in momentum space is
tiny compared with the full Brillouin zone. Therefore, only a limited
fraction of the injected OAM current can propagate through the hot
spots, if there is no additional coherence transport mechanism for the hot
spots to attract the OAM. Such a mechanism could be band structure
matching between NM and FM, some quantum tunneling, or a quantum
walk similar to excitons in photosynthesis. More justiﬁcation on whether
such a condition is met in real samples. Also, as pointed out by Tang et
al.
20, the disorder effect may kill the intrinsic OHE. It is unclear whether
the hot-spot-mediated long-range OAM transport can survive in samples
with disorder, given that it is rather challenging to fully quantum-
mechanically consider the disorder effect.
For long-range transport, the hot-spot framework accommodates
both diffusive and ballistic regimes. In the diffusive case (Fig. 3a)

the ballistic regime (Fig. 3b)11, t electrons traverse without scattering, and
the transport delay is governed by the group velocity of carriers. Such
ballistic transport results in a linear time-dependence of OAM
propagation.

The local generation picture
In contrast to the hot-spot model, a second and more radical perspective
asserts that genuine long-range OAM transport is not possible. This view is
supported byﬁrst-principles scattering calculations performed by Rang and
Kelly, who simulated the injection of an orbital current into a disordered
metal
22,23. Their results indicate that the OAM current decays within only a
few atomic layers (<1 nm), consistent with the conventional picture of
strong orbital quenching.
If the OAM current itself is intrinsically short-ranged, the key
question becomes what the observed experimental signals
actually represent. This issue is addressed by a quantum kinetic
theory developed by Valet et al.

contributions to the total OAM density, coming from the

transport but rather a local quantum coherence between different
electronic bands.
Within this framework, the intraband OAM current is indeed short-
ranged, consistent with the ﬁrst-principles results of Rang and Kelly. It
appears only at second order in the spin –orbit coupling and is highly
sensitive to disorder-induced corrections
20. In contrast, the observed
OAM accumulation — the interband contribution — is not transported
from a remote source but is locally generated wherever spatial gradients
of the charge current exist, independent of SOC. Near surfaces or
interfaces, the charge current distribution is non-uniform due to
boundary conditions, giving rise to a ﬁnite “electron ﬂow vorticity ”
(∇×j
charge). This vorticity acts as a source term that locally creates orbital
polarization (Fig. 3c).
According to the local generation model, the observed “long”
characteristic length does not correspond to an intrinsic OAM diffusion
length. Rather, it re ﬂects the spatial extent of charge current gradients,
which is typically governed by the electron mean free path (MFP). Since
MFPs in metals such as Cr and Ti can reach several to tens of nanometers
at room temperature, the resulting locally generated OAM accumulation
may mimic the appearance of long-range transport. In this framework,
the decisive factor is therefore not the intrinsic lifetime of OAM, but the
transport properties of the charge carriers themselves. Compared with
the ﬁrst picture focusing on hot spots, this picture is based on several
ideas, working at a toy-model level. However, it considers the disorder
effect more comprehensively, providing alternative insight on OAM
nonlocality in the case that the disorder effect destroys the coherence of
OAM transport.
It is worth mentioning that some recent results suggest that dis-
orders and grain boundaries might also enhance the OAM transport. The
OAM can be generated locally by the orbital Rashba-Edelstein effect at
grain boundaries or defects in the sample
4,26, which reduces the distance
OAM needs to travel, effectively improving the apparent OAM propa-
gation length (Fig.3d). The relevant inversion symmetry breaking could
come from the asymmetry in the growing condition of the bottom and
top of a grain, as shown in bulk spin-orbit torque research
27. Also, the
disorders and grain boundaries might enhance the OAM transport
through reduced quenching of the OAM due to the weakened crystal
ﬁeld effects9,28. These arguments, based on cutting-edge experiments, call
for more systematic theoretical studies on the disorder effects on OAM
transport.
Distinguishing ﬂow from ﬁction
Resolving the contrasting interpretations of orbital transport requires
carefully designed experiments and further theoretical reﬁnement. Several
promising directions can be identiﬁed:
Anisotropy and crystal symmetry
The hot-spot model links long-range OAM transport to speciﬁc features of
the electronic band structure and crystal symmetry, predicting a strong
anisotropy with respect to crystallographic orientation and current direc-
tion. By contrast, the local generation model attributes OAM accumulation
to charge current gradients, making it dependent on sample geometry and
boundary conditions. Systematic transport measurements on high-quality
single crystals with controlled orientations could therefore provide mean-
ingful insights; the disappearance of long-range signals along particular axes
would favor the hot-spot picture.
Time-domain dynamics
Time-domain studies offer another powerful approach. Terahertz emission
experiments have revealed linear propagation delays consistent with ballistic
transport, supporting the existence of propagating OAM currents11,12.T h e
next step is to assess whether quantum kinetic models of local generation
can reproduce such temporal signatures, given that the establishment of
current gradients is limited by the momentum relaxation time. Dis-
crepancies in temporal response would help to distinguish between trans-
port and local generation mechanisms.
Geometric engineering
The local generation framework further predicts that OAM accumula-
tion should scale with engineered current vorticity. This suggests that
nanostructures incorporating constrictions, bends, or patterned
interfaces
29 could be used to deliberately enhance or modulate orbital
signals. Observing such geometry-dependent effects, independent of a
distant OAM source, would lend strong support to the local generation
mechanism.

Collectively, these strategies will provide critical tests of the competing
models and help establish whether orbital angular momentum can indeed
propagate over mesoscopic distances, or whether the observed effects are
primarily manifestations of local current inhomogeneity.
Outlook
In orbitronics, a broad range of experimental studies has demonstrated
that OAM can manifest over unexpectedly long distances in metals,
challenging the conventional view of rapid orbital quenching. These
ﬁndings have given rise to two different interpreting pictures, namely
long-range OAM transport and local OAM generation through other
degrees of freedom. In representing models, the former invokes
symmetry-protected hot spots in the electronic band structure that
enable genuine long-range orbital transport, and the latter attributes the
observed signals to locally generated orbital accumulation arising from
charge current gradients.
While each framework captures essential aspects of the data, their
implications for material design and device applications are markedly
different. If long-range transport is con ﬁrmed, future research will focus
on identifying and engineering materials with robust hot-spot symme-
tries. Conversely, if local generation dominates, the emphasis will shift
toward geometric and interfacial engineering to optimize current gra-
dients. It is also possible that both mechanisms coexist, with their
relative importance dictated by material properties and experimental
conditions.
Clarifying the balance between these mechanisms will be crucial for
establishing the fundamental transp ort properties of orbital angular
momentum in solids. Such understanding will not only advance the con-
ceptual foundations of orbitronics but also guide the discovery of materials
and device architectures that harness the orbital degree of freedom for next-
generation quantum technologies.
