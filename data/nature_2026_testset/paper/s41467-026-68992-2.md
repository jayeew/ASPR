Smart ﬁbers, particularly liquid crystal elastomer (LCE) ﬁbers, are pivotal in
soft robotics and adaptive textiles. However, existing fabrication methods are
limited to simple monolithic structures, hindering the realization of complex
actuation behaviors. To overcome this limitation, we develop a continuous
extrusion platform for producing Janus LCE ﬁbers that emulate asymmetric
biological structures, such as plant tendrils, thereby enabling sophisticated
actuation. This approach seamlessly integrates an LCE network with a dynamic
covalent polymer network, allowing programmable on-demand liquid crystal
orientation for actuation via dynamicbond exchange. The resulting Janus
ﬁbers exhibit enhanced mechanical properties and multifunctional cap-
abilities, including adaptive object manipulation, stimuli-responsive direc-
tional motion, and scalable integration into smart fabrics for thermal
management. By unifying material intelligence with structural program-
mability, this work advances the development of bioinspired soft robotic
systems with enhanced environmental adaptability.
Fibrous artiﬁcial muscles1–6 are crucial for a wide range of applications,
including prosthetic limbs 6,a d a p t i v et e x t i l e s7–10, and miniature
actuators5,11–14. Among polymer-based smart ﬁbers (e.g., responsive
gels15,16, shape-memory polymers17,18, dielectric elastomers19,a n dt h e r -
mally expandable polymers), liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) stand out
due to their high reversible strain and rapid response
20. Their actuation
behavior is determined by two key factors: geometric structure and
liquid crystal orientation, where the former dictates their application
scenarios and the latter governs their ability to undergo reversible
deformation under external stimuli. Building on the seminal study that
established the basic principle for producing LCEﬁbers via mechanical
stretching followed by photopolymerization to achieve mesogen
alignment
21, several advanced fabrication techniques have emerged.
These include melt drawing 22, direct ink writing 23,24,a n d
electrospinning25,26, all proven effective in fabricating LCE ﬁbers with
strong actuation properties. Their structures, however, are typically
constrained to simple and monolithic forms, which limits the potential
for achieving complex actuation behaviors.
In nature, plants like tendrils exhibit sophisticated deformations
through asymmetricﬁber ligniﬁcation, forming helical structures that
are capable of multimodal bending and coiling, thereby adapting to
the environment
27. Mimicking such mechanisms in arti ﬁcial muscles
requires replicating intrinsic structural complexity, yet existing
approaches struggle to achieve this. Pioneering work demonstrating

Therefore, we present in this work a continuous asymmetric
extrusion platform for the seamless integration of an LCE network (for
actuation) with a dynamic covalent polymer network (for program-

polymer precursors must exhibit matched extrusion viscosities, iden-
tical curing conditions, and synchronized curing kinetics. Through
precise chemical design, we employ UV-curable LCE and the dynamic
covalent polymer to enable continuous Janus ﬁber production. The
resulting Janusﬁbers exhibit adaptive object manipulation, directional
motion under thermal/infrared laser stimuli, and navigation cap-
abilities in complex environments. This work merges material intelli-
gence with structural programmability, paving the way for
multifunctional soft robots with bioinspired environmental
adaptability.
Results
Bioinspired continuous fabrication of Janusﬁbers
As Fig. 1a illustrates, plant tendrils usually induce anisotropic con-
traction through asymmetric ligniﬁcation of gelatinous ﬁber (g-ﬁber)
cells, which ﬁnally leads to helical coiling to adapt to the
environment27. Such tendrils can be considered as natural Janus
structures capable of switching between“on” and “off” states through
the modulation of intrinsic curvature 38. This characteristic enables
them to be sufﬁciently compliant for interaction with the environment
while remaining mechanically robust enough to perform functional
tasks (Fig. 1b). Inspired by this, we design a continuous asymmetric
spinning platform to fabricate Janusﬁbers (Fig. 1ca n dM o v i eS 1 ) .T h i s
platform co-extrudes two precursors through a conical Janus nozzle,
followed by an in situ ultraviolet (UV) light curing. These two
precursors are composed of LCE and polyurethane (PU) precursors,
respectively (Fig.1d). Speciﬁcally, the LCE precursor is synthesized via
the thiol-Michael addition reaction among 1,4-bis[4-(6-acryloylox-
yhexyloxy)benzoyloxy]-2-methylbenzene (RM82), 3,6-dioxa-1,8-octa-
nedithiol (DODT), and pentaerythritol tetrakis(3-mercaptopropionate)
(PETMP). The PU precursor is prepared in accordance with the pro-
cedure described in the literature
33. The chemical structures of both
precursors are characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance, as shown
in Fig. S1. Janus ﬁbers that contained these two materials are then
partially cured through the photoinitiated free radical polymerization
of (meth)acrylate from two precursors.
To further induce mesogens alignment in LCE, the ﬁbers are
mechanically drawn using a differential-speed winding system, fol-
lowed by a secondary UV curing process, which allows the materials to
be fully cured to lock the strain-induced orientation. This dual-step
curing process separates geometric structure formation from func-
tional orientation ﬁxing, enabling programmable on-demand actua-
tion of LCE. Subsequent thermal annealing facilitates the ordered
reorganization of hydrogen bonds through dynamic bond exchange
within the PU segments, ultimately strengthening the mechanical
integrity of the ﬁbers
35.
The experimental details of the whole process can be found in
the Supplementary Information. All processing steps can be con-
ducted under ambient conditions without the need for oxygen
degassing. This facilitates greater accessibility and scalability of the
process, while ensuring the maintenance of chemical ﬁdelity. Con-
sequently, the Janus ﬁber obtained through this method exhibit a
well-deﬁned biphasic morphology in cross-section at the micro-
scopic level (Fig. 1e) and possess the potential for scalable produc-
tion at the macroscopic level (Fig. 1f). Compared with conventional
LCE ﬁbers reported in the literature
5,29,39–52, it demonstrates not only a
higher elastic modulus but also greater breaking strength, which can
be attributed to the mechanical reinforcement provided by the PU
component (Fig. 1g).
The successful continuous fabrication of Janus ﬁbers relies on
achieving precise compatibility in both rheological behavior and
polymerization kinetics between the LCE and dynamic covalent PU
network. Gel permeation chromatography results reveal that they
possess the molecular weight of 1.1 × 10
5 and 4.4 × 104 g/mol, respec-
tively (Fig. S2). The stable co-extrusion capability can be attributed to
the high viscosity (5500 and 2200 Pa·s) and the shear-thinning beha-
vior of both precursors (Fig. 2a and Fig. S3). In contrast, viscosity
mismatch or excessively low viscosities disrupt interfacial stability,
leading to distorted Janus interfaces or droplet-like co-extrusion
morphologies, underscoring the necessity of maintaining an appro-
priately matched rheological window for continuous ﬁber formation
(Figs. S4 and S5). More importantly, the photopolymerization kinetics
of the two precursors exhibit remarkable synchronization. Upon
exposure to 365 nm UV light, the LCE precursor undergoes rapid
photopolymerization, as evidenced by time-resolved infrared spec-
troscopy and the corresponding increase in acrylate conversion (Fig.
2b and Fig. S6). According to Flory’s gelation theory, the LCE precursor
reaches the gel point after 2 s of exposure (calculations provided in the
Supporting Information). Similarly, the PU precursor displays com-
parably fast crosslinking kinetics, with the gel fraction exceeding >21%
within 2 s (Fig.2c). These rapid curing dynamics enable the concurrent
formation of both networks, thereby ensuring ﬁber solidi ﬁcation
during the initial stages of UV curing.

Programmable and reprogrammable actuation of Janusﬁbers
The inherent soft elasticity of LCE53 and the dynamic bond exchange in
PU enable various programming strategies for shaping and actuating
Janus ﬁbers. The ﬁrst strategy involves direct mechanical stretching
(Fig. 3a i). At room temperature, LCE exhibits soft elasticity and retains
its stretched shape after the force is removed (Fig. S17). In contrast, PU
shows entropic elasticity and returns to its original shape once the
force is released. As a result, the Janus ﬁber can coil due to the asym-
metric contraction of the two materials after mechanical program-
ming. Further heating would remove the soft elasticity of LCE, causing
the coiling to disappear. Representative images of the process are

Dynamic bonds enable efﬁcient reprogramming of liquid crystal
mesogen orientation, allowing the desired actuation (Fig. 4a). Unlike
conventional methods that incorporate dynamic bonds into the LCE
network through copolymerization
29,54–56, our Janus design retains
efﬁcient reorientation while preserving the strong mechanical prop-
erties of dynamic covalent polymers. After programming, the loosely
coiled helical con ﬁgurations are capable of reversibly switching
between loose and tight helices and exhibit excellent stability during
two hundred heating and cooling cycles (Movie S3), and this reversible
actuation is maintained even when small external loads are applied
(Fig. S23). Finite element analysis (FEA) is utilized to predict this
actuation, and the numerical simulations show close agreement with
the experimental results (Fig.4b). More importantly, these structures
can be reprogrammed into diverse helical architectures through
dynamic bond exchange (Fig. 4c and Movie S4). Cyclic testing
demonstrates that the ﬁbers exhibit no signs of actuation fatigue
within 200 cycles (Fig. S24). After 200 actuation cycles, the interfaces
of Janus ﬁber remained intact, demonstrating thatﬁbers produced via
our fabrication method exhibit excellent structural stability (Fig. S25).
Additional FEA simulations of all three helical structures are conducted
(Fig. S26), showing excellent agreement with the experimental results.
This characteristic enables the potential application of Janusﬁbers as
soft robots.

Heterogeneous structural design of Janus LCE ﬁbers can also

The continuously fabricatedﬁbers can be incorporated into smart
fabrics through conventional textile manufacturing techniques. Dur-
ing the weaving process, Janus LCEﬁbers are integrated as weft threads
in a plain weave structure, enabling active functional control while
maintaining mechanical integrity. The resulting textiles display a uni-
form weave free of defects, conﬁrming theﬁbers’robustness under the
shear and tensile stresses experienced during the weaving process
(Fig. 5e). When stretched, the mechanical stretching induces a struc-
tural transformation in the Janus LCE ﬁbers, generating coiled archi-
tectures that increase fabric porosity and loft density. This geometric
reconﬁguration signi ﬁcantly enhances thermal insulation perfor-
mance, as demonstrated in Fig. 5f and Fig. S31. And the mechanically
induced thermal insulation enhancement is reversible, as mild heating
initiates structural recovery to the original compact form. The highly
tunable insulation properties render the material exceptionally sui-
table for wearable and adaptive thermal management applications.
Discussion
In this work, we develop a continuous fabrication strategy for Janus
ﬁbers that synergistically integrates a stimuli-responsive LCE network
with a structurally programmable dynamic covalent PU network. This
distinct architecture endows the ﬁbers with both robust mechanical
properties and versatile actuation capabilities, overcoming the struc-
tural and functional limitations of conventional LCEﬁber systems. By
incorporating localized programming strategies and hierarchical
geometric design, we achieve multimodal reversible actuation
encompassing linear-to-helical transformations, autonomous coiling,
directional locomotion, and gradient-driven crawling within conﬁned
spaces. Furthermore, the scalability and structural integrity of the
Janus design are demonstrated through its seamless incorporation
into smart textiles, where theﬁbers enable tunable thermal insulation.
Collectively, these ﬁndings highlight the transformative potential of
Janus ﬁbers as multifunctional building blocks for soft robotics and
adaptive wearables, establishing a foundation for the next generation
of ﬁber-based intelligent material systems.

Programming strategy 1: direct mechanical stretching
Janus ﬁbers were programmed by subjecting them to tensile strains
ranging from 20 to 100% at a temperature of 25 °C, followed by the
release of the applied strain. The deformed shape was retained due to
the soft elasticity exhibited by LCE. The degree of coiling (N) was
quantiﬁed as the number of coils per unitﬁber length (N = n/H), where
“H” denotes the original length of the unprogrammedﬁber and “n” is
the number of visible coils formed after programming. In addition, the
coil diameter and helix pitch was measured to assess the morpholo-
gical response of the ﬁbers under different programming strains.
Programming strategy 2: thermal annealing
Janus ﬁbers were programmed by applying a tensile strain of 60% at
90 °C and subsequently annealed for varying durations (1-10 min)
under the same temperature, followed by immediate strain release.
The resulting deformed shape was attributed to stress relaxation
caused by dynamic bond exchange in PU. The resulting coiled con-
ﬁgurations were then evaluated as a function of annealing time, and
the coil density (N), coil diameter, and helical pitch were further
quantiﬁed and compared across different annealing times.
Programming strategy 3: spatial programming
Janus ﬁbers were ﬁrst stretched to 50% strain at 25 °C. Near-infrared
(808 nm) laser irradiation (2000 mW·cm−2, 5 min) was then applied in a
spatially selective manner to predeﬁned regions along the ﬁber. The
laser was scanned longitudinally along the ﬁber axis at a controlled
speed to locally activate dynamic covalent bond exchange. No obser-
vable contraction was detected during programming, indicating that
the non-oriented LCE domains and applied external stress prevented
deformation gradients, preserving the programming of the PU phase.
Upon releasing the applied strain, the laser-irradiated regions —
designated as Programming 2— retained their elongated shape, while
the non-irradiated regions ( Programming 1) spontaneously relaxed
into helical coils. This spatially patterned programming yielded a sin-
gle ﬁber comprising both linear and helical segments. When heated to
70 °C, the ﬁber exhibited thermally induced bimodal switching: the
initially linear segments ( Programming 2) transformed into helices,
while the preformed helical segments (Programming 1) relaxed into
linear con ﬁgurations. This dual-mode integration highlights a pro-
grammable and reversible strategy for spatially encoded multimodal
actuation within a single Janus ﬁber.
