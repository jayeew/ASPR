On-chip integrable circularly polarized light (CPL) detectors hold signiﬁcant
potential for applications in opticalcommunications, bioimaging, quantum
information, and intelligent sensing, enabling chip-scale chiral light detection
and information processing. However, current developments in materials and
structural designs struggle to achieve wide-spectral detection capability while
maintaining high circular polarization discrimination. This limitation sig-
niﬁcantly constrains the practical applicability of such detectors in complex
environments and object detection scenarios. Herein, we propose a CPL
detector based on a twist-stacked black phosphorus (b-p) system. By utilizing
twisted b-p as an artiﬁcially constructed unit with chiral response and
designing a symmetry-stacked sandwich structure in space, we achieve bipolar
circular polarization detection and imaging across a wide wavelength range
(from visible light to mid-infrared), with peak responsivity reaching 0.1 A/W.
This design demonstrates both theoretically and experimentally the feasibility
of utilizing a twist-stacked b-p system for wide-spectral CPL detection. This
advancement offers valuable insights for addressing the bottlenecks in chip-
integrated CPL detection technology.
Circularly polarized light (CPL) detection is widely used for various
applications in sensing and imaging 1,2. The two-dimensional (2D)
design (2D materials and 2D structures) for CPL detection enables the
integration of traditional CPL detection schemes, which require spatial
degrees of freedom, into compact on-chip detectors, offering limitless
possibilities for chip-level CPL detection technology. Currently, the
mainstream methods for designing CPL detectors can be divided into
two major categories (Fig. 1a): the ﬁrst approach relies on materials
with chiral molecular structures to achieve CPL detectors with high
responsivity
3–12. However, their responsive wavelength is determined
by the bandgap of the chiral materials, usually limited to short wave-
lengths ranging from ultraviolet to near-infrared (below 1000 nm).
Importantly, these types of devices are often susceptible to inter-
ference from unpolarized light and linearly polarized light. The second
widely adopted approach employs arti ﬁcial plasmonic structures,
which can offer greater circular dichroism, with the wavelength

Mechanism of twist-stacked black phosphorus CPL detector
Due to the anisotropic optical absorption properties of b-p (left sub-
ﬁgure of Fig.1b), numerous studies have reported the design of b-p as a
photodetector sensitive to linearly polarized light
21–27. However, none
have successfully achieved the detection of more complex polariza-
tion information, such as CPL. The controlled twisting and stacking of
multilayer 2D materials at speciﬁc angles has emerged as a promising
strategy for introducing optical chirality into non-chiral systems
28.
However, such an approach has largely remained at the level of optical
characterization, and it remains challenging to achieve chiral-light
detection through complex photoelectric conversion processes. Here,
twist b-p structures can break mirror symmetry to some extent, pro-
viding a platform for responding to CPL (right subﬁgure of Fig.1b)
29–32.
The basic unit of this design consists of two twisted, anisotropic b-p
layers with different thicknesses, producing a chiral response to CPL
(Fig. 2a). By controlling the twist angle, we can construct chiral units
that respond to CPL, referred to as the twist 1 and twist 2, respectively,
as shown in the left sub ﬁgure of Fig. 2b. However, relying solely on
chiral units for CPL detection poses several challenges: ﬁrstly, each
part of the b-p in the chiral units also responds to linearly polarized
light, inevitably introducing interference from linear polarization
components, which results in a low discrimination ratio. Secondly, the
chiral units generate relatively weak polarized photocurrents in
response to CPL, limiting the detection capability of the device.
To solve these issues, we designed the chiral units in a vertically
stacked sandwich structure, as shown in the middle sub ﬁgure of

Additionally, due to the difference in work functions between the
thicker (middle-layer) and thinner (top and bottom-layer) black
phosphorus layers, symmetrically distributed homojunctions are
formed in the vertical direction
25,34–38. This symmetric homojunction
design helps mitigate the photocurrent from linearly polarized (LP)
components during detection, as illustrated in the right sub ﬁgure of
Fig. 2b. This is because the photocurrent generated by LP light mainly
arises from the separation of photogenerated carriers in each b-p layer
under the inﬂuence of the built-in electric ﬁeld of the homojunction.
Therefore, the symmetric design of the homojunction leads to LP
photocurrents ﬂowing in opposite directions, which partially cancel
each other out.
Speciﬁcally, as shown in Fig. 2c, when light passes through a
quarter-wave plate (QWP) and illuminates the device, the twist 1 unit
generates three components of photocurrent: CPL photocurrent at the
interface and two LP photocurrents from the top b-p and middle b-p
layers. Similarly, the twist 2 unit produces CPL photocurrent as well as
LP photocurrents from the middle b-p and bottom b-p layers. The
circularly polarized photocurrent arises from the CPGE generated by
two twist units with the same twist angle direction, resulting in cur-
rents ﬂowing in the same direction, which allows for their accumula-
tion. The LP photocurrent is generated by the top b-p, middle b-p, and
bottom b-p layers, which ﬂow in opposite directions due to the in ﬂu-
ence of the symmetric potential distribution. The LP photocurrent
from the middle b-p will be canceled out, and the LP components from
the top b-p and bottom b-p are signiﬁcantly weakened. Ultimately, this
design greatly reduces the interference from LP components during
circular polarization detection.
To explain the mechanism underlying the generation of the CPGE,
we theoretically constructed two bilayer-twisted systems with twist
angles φ ~ 15° and 60°, and calculated their electronic structures, as
shown in Fig.2d, e
39–42. In the case of a twist angle of ~15°, the bands are
relatively ﬂat along the Γ-Y high-symmetry path near the energy gap,
with both the conduction band minimum and the valence band max-
imum at Γ. Under illumination, it is easy to excite charges near this
position, providing a conduction current. We further analyzed the
spatial distribution of the electronic states near theΓ point and found
that part of the states at the conduction band minimum are distributed
in the inter-layer regions, which can provide carrier conduction
channels in the vertical inter-layer direction. The case of a twist angle
of ~60° exhibits similar characteristics, especially that part of the
electronic states at the conduction band minimum is also distributed
in the inter-layer regions. The twist angle used here is not unique; we
have also performed calculations for other angles (see Supplementary
Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1–1 of Supplementary Note 1).

Circular polarization detection
Next, we experimentally investigated the circular polarization detec-
tion mechanism of the twist-stacked b-p device. Based on the
calculation results, we fabricated a trilayer twist-stacked b-p device.
The middle b-p and the top b-p form a twist unit 1 with a twist angle
of approximately 60°, while the middle b-P and the bottom b-p form
a twist unit 2 with a twist angle of approximately −15°, as shown in
Fig. 3a. By performing near-infrared (1210 nm) re ﬂective circular
dichroism (RCD) measurements on trilayer twist-stacked b-p,
we observed high RCD values in the overlapping region of the trilayer
b-p, indicating a strong chiral response in this area, as shown
in Fig. 3b. This is dif ﬁcult to observe in bilayer-stacked devices
(Supplementary Figs. 2–3a n d2 –4 of Supplementary Note 2 provide
more comprehensive testing information). Motivated by this pro-
minent characteristic, we conducted photocurrent measurements
under circularly polarized illumination. When we connected the
electrodes to the top b-p layer and the bottom b-p layer for photo-
current mapping tests, we observed a clear generation of photo-
currents with opposite polarities under RCP and LCP illumination in
the overlapping region of the trilayer b-p. This observation is con-
sistent with the theoretical calculations of CPEG.

Wide-spectral response characteristics of the device
For the mainstream methods currently used for on-chip circular
polarization detection, including chiral materials and metasurface-
assisted approaches, challenges remain in achieving high circular
polarization discrimination over a broad wavelength range. The design
proposed in this work ﬁlls this gap by enabling CPL detection with a
broad wavelength range, high responsivity, and high discrimination
simultaneously.
The multilayered stacked structure offers multiple electrode
conﬁgurations (Fig. 4a), allowing the device to operate in different
modes to meet the detection requirements of speci ﬁc scenarios. For
example, the photovoltaic effect under zero bias can offer a high on-off
ratio, while the photogating effect under applied gate voltage can
provide enhanced responsivity. When the electrodes are connected on
the intra-layer (C –D), the source-drain current of the device under
different wavelength illumination (633 nm, 1550 nm and 2700 nm), all
at the same power (50 µW), varies with the applied bias as shown in
Fig. 4b. The device exhibits responsiveness across a broad spectrum,
from visible to infrared light (see mid-infrared absorption and
response measurements in Supplementary Fig. 3–1 of Supplementary
Note 3), and as the bias increases, the photocurrent also increases,
as depicted in the inset. The maximum responsivity can reach up to
1 A/W. Under extremely weak illumination (~1µW) test conditions, the
source-drain current response as a function of bias voltage for the
interlayer connection (A–C) is shown in Fig. 4c. The interlayer exhibits
clear junction-like photovoltaic characteristics, indicating the pre-
sence of homojunctions between the layers. In addition, depending on
the connection method of different ports, the device can also
demonstrate bipolar linear polarization detection capability, switch-
able dual operating modes and tunable circular polarization rule with
fast temporal response characteristics, as shown in Supplementary

Discussion
In this work, we innovatively develop and design twist-stacked b-p
structures for on-chip CPL detection to address the long-standing
spectral limitation issue. The proposed twist-stacked b-p system suc-
cessfully achieves high circular polarization discrimination (bipolar
detection) and high responsivity (up to the A/W level) while enabling
wide-spectral detection from the visible to the mid-infrared range at
room temperature. This work primarily demonstrates, through design
and measurement, an elegant strategy for introducing chiral proper-
ties into achiral systems. Building on this work, the strategy of using
twist-induced symmetry breaking combined with stacked-layer design
to amplify chiral photocurrents can also be extended to more stable
transition-metal dichalcogenide materials. This study provides a viable
approach for the next generation of high-performance, wide-spectral,
chip-scale CPL detection.
Methods
Device fabrication
The twist-stacked b-p devices were fabricated via a dry transfer
method. The entire fabrication process was conducted within a glove
box with water and oxygen levels maintained below 1 ppm. First, b-p
ﬂakes were mechanically exfoliated onto a Polydimethylsiloxane
(PDMS) substrate and then transferred onto a silicon substrate. The
thickness of the b-pﬂakes was determined by optical contrast. A stack
was assembled by sequentially picking up three individual b-P layers
using a Polycarbonate (PC)/PDMS stamp. Subsequently, the entire
stack was transferred onto prepatterned bottom electrodes Cr/Au (2/
30 nm) to establish electrical contact. A top BN was picked up using
another PC/PDMS stack to cover the whole stack. Finally, a selected
graphiteﬂake was transferred onto the top hexagonal boron nitride (h-
BN) layer to function as the top gate electrode.
To ensure high-precision layer stacking and reliable electrical
contacts, we employed the following approach:
First, we developed a custom high-precision transfer system. The
rotation motor integrated into our ’Angwei’transfer stage allows for
accurate angular control, enabling twist angles with an uncertainty of
only ±0.1°.

Characterization methods
For the photocurrent scanning mapping setup, the 633, 1550 and
2700 nm lasers were directed by a single-modeﬁber to the homemade
setup. The light passes through a lens, a polarizer, and a quarter-wave
plate. The laser beam was focused by an objective into a sub-
micrometer diameter on the sample surface. Cisystems SR200N was
used as the black-body radiation source, and the maximum tempera-
ture can reach 1200 K. For the electrical measurement setup, the
longitudinal voltage and gate voltage were applied using a Keithley
2604B source meter. An FS Pro Integrated Semiconductor Parameter
Testing System (Noise Analyzer) was used to measure the photo-
current, noise, and resistance of the device.
Simulation
We utilized the density functional theory (DFT) code, speciﬁcally the
Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP), to optimize the crystal
structure and calculate the fundamental electronic properties. The
self-consistent calculations were performed using a k-mesh of size
5 × 5 × 1 for the two considered twisted bilayer systems. The energy
cutoff was set to be 500 eV, and the tolerance for the total energy was
set to be less than 10
−5 eV. Exchange interactions and electron corre-
lation were treated within the generalized gradient approximation
proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof.
