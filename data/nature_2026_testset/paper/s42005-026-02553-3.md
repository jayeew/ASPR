The exploration of tunable superconductivity in strongly correlated electron systems is a central
pursuit in condensed matter physics, with implications for both fundamental understanding and
potential applications. The Laves phase CeRu
2, a pyrochlore compound, exhibits a three-dimensional
(3D) kagome lattice type geometry giving rise to ﬂat bands and degenerate Dirac points, where band
structure features intertwine with strong multi-orbital interaction effects deriving from its correlated
electronic structure. Here, we combine muon spin rotation ( μSR), uniaxial in-plane stress, and
hydrostatic pressure to probe the superconducting state of CeRu
2. Uniaxial stress up to 0.22 GPa
induces a dome-shaped evolution of the critical temperature Tc, with an initial plateau, successively
followed by enhancement and suppression without any structural phase transition. Stress is further
found to drive a crossover from anisotropic to isotropic s-wave pairing. In contrast, hydrostatic
pressure up to 1.9 GPa leaves T
c largely unchanged but alters the superﬂuid density from exponential
to linear behavior at low temperatures, indicative of nodal superconductivity under hydrostatic
pressure. These ﬁndings identify CeRu2 as a prime platform for multifold tuning of superconductivity in
a 3D correlated material.
The kagome network, composed of corner-sharing triangles, naturally gives
rise to a unique diversity of electronic features such asﬂat bands, van Hove
singularities, and Dirac fermions, producing peculiar interaction proﬁles
resulting from destructive interference for theﬂat band and the associated
sublattice frustration mechanism around the van Hove singularities 1.I n
kagome metals such as CsV3Sb5
2 where several van Hove singularities are
placed near the Fermi level, these effects conspire to reveal a wide range of
unconventional electronic ground states including magnetism, nematic
order, charge ordering, superconductivity, and more. In the uprising context
of geometry-induced quantum effects, ﬂat bands have become another
central theme in the study of correlated electron systems, can be provided for
in a kagome-type lattice environment, opening up yet another realm of
exotic phenomena
1–6.
Although ﬂat bands are typically associ ated with two-dimensional
(2D) kagome systems3,4,7–9— e . g . ,C o S n ,F e S n ,( L a , Y ) R u3Si2
4,10–15,C e2Ru3Si16,
AV3Sb5 and AM 6X6, recent attention has turned to three-dimensional
analogs.
In particular, the pyrochlore lattice which consists of corner-sharing
tetrahedra and hosts a 3D-kagome sublattice. They mimic the
electronic isolation of 2D layers than ks to its unique crystallographic
symmetry (space group Fd3m)17 and the geometrically driven destructive
interference that suppresses inter-sublattice coupling1. Known pyrochlore
compounds18,19 such as CuV 2S4 and CaNi2 have also emerged as fertile
ground forﬂat-band physics and topological states. In CaNi2, ﬂat bands and
Dirac cones have been observed 19 by angular resolved photoemission
(ARPES) experiments.

To gain insight into the stress and pressure induced changes of the SC
gap structure, we next provide a qualitative framework that illustrates how
such changes may arise. At ﬁrst glance, the notion that an anisotropic
perturbation— such as uniaxial stress— can enhance the isotropic character
of anisotropic s-wave superconductor may appear counterintuitive. How-
ever, this behavior can be naturall y understood within the context of
unconventional s-wave pairing. In suchsystems, the superconducting gap is
not dominated by a simple isotropic constant component but instead arises
from a complex superposition of momentum-dependent harmonic con-
tributions. As a consequence, even a moderate stress perturbation can
modify the balance among competing electronic ordering tendencies,
thereby altering the harmonic composition of the anomalous propagator
(i.e., the particle-particle wave function). Depending on how these com-
peting channels are affected, the resulting superconducting state may exhibit
either enhanced or reduced gap anisotropy.
For CeRu
2, a plausible scenario consistent with our data is that the
system initially hosts a moderately anisotropic unconventional s-wave state.
Upon the application of uniaxial stress, the competing subleading ordering
tendencies may be suppressed or reshaped such that the preferred harmonic
composition of the extended s-wave gap leads to a reduced difference
between the maximum and minimum gap values on the stress-distorted
Fermi surface, resulting in a more isotropic gap. By contrast, under
hydrostatic pressure, starting again from a moderately anisotropic extended
s-wave gap, we envision that pressure enhances the gap anisotropy to the
extent that accidental nodes emerge. This behavior is reminiscent of phe-
nomena previously observed in several iron pnictide superconductors
42,44.
Detailed future theoretical studies, for example using functional renorma-
lization group or random phase approximation approaches, may provide
further insight into the microscopic mechanisms underlying this behavior.
Conclusion
We present theﬁrst comprehensive study of uniaxial stress and hydrostatic
pressure effects on the microscopi c superconducting properties of the
pyrochlore compound CeRu 2, which hosts a three-dimensional (111)
kagome lattice. Our keyﬁndings include: (1) a non-monotonic evolution of
the superconducting transition temperature under uniaxial stress applied
along the kagome plane; in the absence of any structural changes, this
behavior suggests an unconventional superconducting state; (2) stress-
induced modiﬁcations of the superﬂuid density, consistent with a crossover

Methods
Single crystal growth
The single crystal of CeRu2 was grown using the Czochralski pulling method
with a prepared polycrystalline ingot i5n a tetra-arc furnace. Firstly, the
spheroidal ingot of mass 10 g was prepared by arc melting stoichiometric
amounts of lumpish cerium and ruthenium, which was then placed on the
water-cooled copper hearth of the t etra-arc furnace under argon atmo-
sphere with a titanium ingot used as an oxygen getter. The current of 16A
was applied into the ingot to melt it and the velocity of the hearth rotation
was 0.3 revolutions per minute. A columnar single crystal was successfully
grown after several hours.
Sample characterization
The quantitative elemental composition, atomic ratios and homogeneity of
the CeRu2 crystal were measured using X-ray ﬂuorescence spectroscopy
( X R F )o na nA M E T E KO r b i sP CM i c r oEDXRF spectrometer system. Ele-
mental multiple-points and distribution maps for cerium and ruthenium
were collected from a cut surface of the crystal in air by applying a white X-ray
beam generated by a rhodium tube set to 35 kV and 500μA, with a primary
beam spot focus of 30μm. An area of approximately 2 × 2 mm
2 on the crystal
surface was probed using EDX. The Ce:Ru ratio in the crystal was conﬁrmed
to be stoichiometric (1:2) within thelimits of the measurement accuracy.
The orientation of the single-crystal sample was determined using a
Siemens KRISTALLOFLEX 760 Laue X-ray diffractometer equipped with a
Mo point-focus X-ray source and a Coolview Fast Digital Imagers (FDI)
Photonic Science Laue dual camera. The Laue backscattering pattern con-
ﬁrms that the [111] crystallographic direction is orthogonal to the exposed
surface of the aligned CeRu
2 crystal, demonstrating that the applied uniaxial
stress is oriented along the [111] direction.
Hydrostatic pressure cell
A double-wall piston-cylinder cell, constructed from CuBe and MP35N
materials and speciﬁcally designed forμSR experiments under pressure, was
utilized to generate pressures up to 1.9 GPa35,36. Daphne 7373 served as the
pressure-transmitting medium. The pressure calibration was established by
measuring the superconducting transition of a small indium plate inside the
sample volume from AC susceptibility. The pressure cell was optimized for
maximum ﬁlling factor. Approximately 40% of the muons stopped within
the sample during the experiments.
Uniaxial stress cell
For the μSR and AC susceptibility measurements, we used a cryo-com-
patible, piezoelectric-driven uniaxial pressure cell45–47,c o m p a t i b l ew i t hμSR
experiments and designed toﬁt the He cryostat of the Dolly instrument at
PSI’s πE1 beamline (see Fig. 1c). The piezo actuators enable continuous
in situ pressure tuning. The CeRu 2 single crystal (7 × 4.4 × 0.6 mm 3,
oriented along [111]) was mounted on a detachable grade-2 titanium
holder using Stycast-2850 FT epoxy. The holder, detailed in our previous
work46,i n c l u d e sﬂexures to minimize torques and is designed to maximize
muon acceptance by allowing missed muons to reach the veto counter.
Hematite foils masked the sample plates to absorb muons outside the target
area, resulting in 40% muon stopping ef ﬁciency. A pair of 100-turn coils
was positioned near the sample for in situ AC susceptibility measurements
to track under various stress conditions. The muon-facing area was
4 × 4.5 mm
2.
By using the force-sensor calibration described in detail elsewhere45,48,
we estimated the force applied to CeRu2.W e ﬁnd that 1 μV (change in the
force sensor reading from the zero force position) corresponds to ~ 31 N
and the maximum applied force is ~ 560 N. Our sample had a cross section
area of 2.6 mm2, which results in a maximum applied stress of ~ 0.22 GPa.
Magnetotransport measurements
Magnetotransport measurements were carried out in a standard four-probe
method using the Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS, Quan-
tum Design). The diagonal arrangement of voltage contacts was used and
the magnetoresistance and Hall resistance were obtained by symmetrization
and anti-symmetrization of the measured data, respectively.
Muon spin rotation experiments
Transverse-ﬁeld (TF) μSR experiments with uniaxial stress experiment and
high pressure were performed on the VMS instrument and the GPD
instrument, equipped with an Oxford Sorption Pumped
3He Cryostat type
Heliox/Variox, at the Swiss Muon Source (SμS) at the Paul Scherrer Institut,
in Villigen, Switzerland.
In a μSR experiment nearly 100 % spin-polarized muons μ+ are
implanted into the sample one at a time. The positively charged μ+ ther-
malize at interstitial lattice sites, where they act as magnetic microprobes. In
a magnetic material the muon spin precesses in the localﬁeld B
μ at the muon
site with the Larmor frequency νμ = γμ/(2π) Bμ (muon gyromagnetic ratio
γμ/(2π) = 135.5 MHz T−1). Using the μSR technique, important length
scales of superconductors can be measured, namely the magnetic penetra-
tion depthλ and the coherence lengthξ. If a type-II superconductor is cooled
below Tc in an applied magnetic ﬁeld ranged between the lower (Hc1)a n d
the upper (Hc2) critical ﬁelds, a vortex lattice is formed which in general is
incommensurate with the crystal lattice with vortex cores separated by
much larger distances than those of the unit cell. Because the implanted
muons stop at given crystallographicsites, they will randomly probe theﬁeld
distribution of the vortex lattice. Such measurements need to be performed
in a ﬁeld applied perpendicular to the in itial muon spin polarization (so
called TF conﬁguration).
Zero-ﬁeld and high transverse-ﬁeld experiments were carried out to
probe normal state properties on the HAL instrument in aﬁeld range of 0.01
to 8 T. The sample in the form of compressed pellet (diameter 8 mm) was
placed on the silver sample holder and mounted in the cryostat. The TF- and
ZF-µSR data were analyzed by using MUSRFIT program
49.
