Global heavy metal(loid) (HM) pollution in soils threatens food security and human health, yet HM
mobility— governed by chemical fractions — remains poorly understood at large scales. Here we
develop an eXtreme Gradient Boosting model for classifying soil HM dominant fractions using a
globally compiled dataset of 9489 ﬁeld observations. We show that organic carbon and pH positively
affect high-mobility of HMs in soil. Using mercury as a case study, we identify both known and
previously unreported high-mobility hotspots. The global map indicates that mercury has high mobility
across 17.85% of global regions. Combining population and cropland distribution maps with model
predictions shows that an estimated 15.1 million people and 100.9 million hectares of farmland are
situated in high-mobility regions, with Asia being disproportionately impacted. This study facilitates
the efﬁcient identiﬁcation of dominant HM fractions on a large scale, providing a critical foundation for
developing targeted HM stabilization/solidi ﬁcation strategies.
Soil, a nonrenewable resource, directly or indirectly sustains 95% of the
human food supply1. The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organi-
zation warns that by 2050, 90% of global soil resources could face risks from
factors such as soil erosion, overuse of fertilizers and pesticides, and
industrial pollution
2. Consequently, soil quality is critically linked to food
safety. Soil pollution by heavy metal(loid)s (HMs) is frequently overlooked
in the context of soil quality, despite its detrimental impacts on crop yields
and food safety
3. The inherent persistence, toxicity, and bioaccumulation
potential of HMs lead to their accumulation in soils over decadal timescales,
exacerbating global en vironmental challenges4,5. Prolonged exposure to
HM-polluted soils can impair soil functions, contaminate food chains, and
contribute to chronic health issues.
There are two main sources of HMs in soil: geogenic and
anthropogenic. Geogenic HMs originate from natural soil parent
materials, which continuously release HMs during geologic weathering
and soil-forming processes
2,4. Anthropogenic activities, such as mining
and mineral processing, metal smel ting, chemical manufacturing,
industrial emissions, and sewage irrigation, have been identi ﬁed as the
primary sources of HM pollution 6. HMs are retained in the soil through
various mechanisms, including adsorption to clay minerals and iron

Results and discussion
Optimal ML model for global HM fractions
In our study, we extracted 9489 HM fractions data from 219 published
surveys, culminating in a dataset. This dataset incorporated information,
including HM concentration, soil properties, and element descriptors.
Recognizing the need for a robust and unbiased model, we systematically
conduct model comparisons, data processing, and hyperparameter tuning
(see ‘Methods’). The performance of the optimal eXtreme Gradient
Boosting (XGBoost) model on the training and test sets are shown in Fig.1.
The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for the
four fraction classes, accountingfor the full range of possible classiﬁcation
thresholds, averaged 0.91 on the training set withﬁve-fold cross-validation
and 0.92 on the test set (Fig.1a, b). The confusion matrices indicated that the
numbers of true positives on both the training and test sets were relatively
high, reﬂecting that many samples were correctly classiﬁed by the XGBoost
model (Fig.1c, d). Owing to the data imbalance, that is a higher proportion
of the residue (F4) fraction samples, the model exhibited overﬁtting on F4
fraction, resulting in the highest number of correctly classiﬁed samples for
this fraction. This also led to a portion of F1 fraction, reducible (F2) fraction,
oxidizable (F3) fraction samples being incorrectly classiﬁed as F4 fraction.
Despite the imbalanced dataset, the model exhibited robust performance,
achieving an F1 score of 0.80, an average precision (AP) of 0.86, an AUC of
0.92, a sensitivity of 0.67, and a speci ﬁcity of 0.90 at the default cutoff
threshold of 0.5 on the test set (Fig.1e). Furthermore, to mitigate potential
overﬁtting arising from spatial dependen ce, spatial cross-validation was
applied to the training dataset, yielding an F1 score, AP, AUC, sensitivity,
and speciﬁcity of 0.54, 0.56, 0.67, 0.58, and 0.80, respectively (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 1). Overall, theoptimized XGBoost classiﬁcation model exhibited
strong generalization ability and robustness, enabling efﬁcient and accurate
global classiﬁcation of dominant HM fractions.

Discussion
This study advances beyond total HM concentrations by providing a robust
tool to predict dominant HM fractions in soils, revealing mobility hotspots
for risk assessment. Using an optimized XGBoost model trained on 9489
data points, we identiﬁed that soil OC and pH were positively correlated
with F1-F3 fractions and thus HM mobility. The optimal model was inte-
grated with global observational Hg data to produce a high-resolution map
of high-mobility Hg. This map identiﬁed that high-mobility areas covered
17.85% of the global land area and potentially affect 15.1 million people and
100.9 million hectares of cropland. Asia experiences the greatest impact,
with approximately 9.9 million people and 37.3 million hectares of cropland
exposed. Unlike prior works assuming uniform concentrations, our inte-
gration of observed Hg data provides a ccurate, high-resolution maps for
targeted remediation.
Nevertheless, when compared withtraditional Community Bureau of
Reference (BCR) detection techniques, which are often both costly and
time-consuming, the advantages of our methodology become evident. Our
framework, implemented on a server equipped with an NVIDIA Tesla V100
GPU, markedly reduced computational time. Training the model required
only 45.73 s, and evaluating 10,000 soil samples for HM fractions took
approximately 10 s. In contrast, BCR detection techniques typically require
about 48 h to determine the HM fractions of a single soil sample
51.T h e
experimental process can also be prolonged and prone to risks if the
investigator lacks sufﬁcient expertise in the detection procedures. Further-
more, the high economic cost of conducting these analyses makes large-
scale detection challenging. Although BCR detection techniques provide
high analytical accuracy, they remain susceptible to human error. In con-
trast, our proposed framework is trai ned and validated using rigorously
veriﬁed experimental datasets, enabling it to achieve both ef ﬁciency and
reliability. Overall, the framework establishes a new paradigm for large-scale
classiﬁcation of dominant HM fractions in soils, facilitating rapid and cost-
effective environmental assessments.
Given the global severity of Hg pollution and the availability of global
Hg datasets, this study used Hg as a case to analyze the global distribution of
dominant Hg fractions. The structureof the proposed framework is highly
ﬂexible, allowing it to be readily scaled to other HM. Furthermore, several
publicly accessible and widely used datasets on soil properties, such as the
Harmonized World Soil Database version 2.0 ( https://gaez.fao.org/pages/
hwsd) and SoilGrids version 2.0 ( https://soilgrids.org/), can facilitate the
deployment and transferability of the trained ML models across regions.
This study enables the rapid and efﬁcient identiﬁcation of dominant
soil HM fractions, thereby facilitating the delineation of critical hotspot
areas prior to the implementation of pollution remediation strategies. These
ﬁndings highlight the importance of incorporating soil HM fraction
information into policy formulation to achieve more precise and effective
pollution control. Moreover, remediation strategies should be tailored to
local environmental conditions. Speciﬁcally, the framework proposed in this
study allows governments to identify the dominant HM fractions in regional
soils and determine the corresponding high-mobility HMs. This approach
can support rapid source identiﬁcation and guide the selection of targeted
remediation methods, such as phytoremediation, bioremediation, or
microbial remediation
4.
Although this study incorporateda sm u c hg l o b a ld a t ao ns o i lH M
fractions as possible to construct an optimized XGBoost model, several
limitations remain. The model was d esigned to use actual soil HM con-

Methods
In this research, we employed a ML model to classify the dominant fractions
of soil on a global scale (Fig. 5). The dataset, comprising 9489 data points
from 219 sources, was split into training and test sets at an 8:2 ratio. Model
selection, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning were performed on
the training set with ﬁve-fold cross-validation. The SHAP method was
integrated with the optimal model to investigate the complex relationships
between driving factors and dominant HM fractions in soils. Ultimately, the
model delineated global regions with high Hg mobility and identiﬁed the
associated population and farmland exposure.

Optimal ML model construction
The global dataset was randomly divided into two subsets: 80% of the data
served as the training dataset and was subjected toﬁve-fold cross-validation
to calibrate the model, whereas 20% was used as the evaluation dataset to
assess the predictive performance of the calibrated model. The distributions
of the dominant HM fractions were comparable between the training and
evaluation subsets. Preliminary experiments evaluated the performance of
ten ML models with default hyperparameters for predicting dominant HM
fractions using all predictive variabl es, including extremely randomized
trees, random forest, adaptive boosting, gradient boosting, XGBoost, sup-

2,15,58. The ten selected ML methods with default
hyperparameters were repeated 50 times to determine the most suitable
model and number of repetitions required to achieve model convergence.
Recognizing the need for a robust and unbiased model, we compared ML
algorithms using the F1-score. The results demonstrated that the XGBoost
model outperformed the other models in classifying HM fractions,
achieving an F1 score of 0.77 (Supplementary Fig. 14). A convergence test
indicated that the XGBoost model stabilized after 15 iterations; therefore, 15
iterations were subsequently adopte d for all calculations (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 15).
To reduce model complexity and constrain the range of feature values,
all features were standardized prior to model training. In addition, to
mitigate the class imbalance in the dataset, sample weights were assigned
based on class frequency. This approach allowed minority classes to receive
higher penalty weights during loss co mputation, thereby enhancing the
model’s ability to identify rare classes
2.
Feature selection is pivotal in model construction, aiming to eliminate
redundant variables to mitigate overﬁtting and multicollinearity, enhance
model performance and interpretability, and reduce computational costs59.
In this study, a two-step approach was implemented to ensure that theﬁnal
feature set excluded redundant information. In the ﬁrst step, recursive
feature elimination (RFE) was applied to select features from 37 predictive
variables. RFE, a widely adopted method for feature selection, iteratively
removes the least inﬂuential variables on the basis of feature importance
60.
Feature importance was assessed as the mean decrease in node impurity
using the Gini index, and variables were iteratively removed from lowest to
highest importance until only one remained. The feature subset with the
highest model accuracy was subsequently selected. The 32 element
descriptors collectively characterized the physicochemical properties of
HMs; however, RFE cannot handle multicollinearity among variables. To
address this, a second step calculated the Pearson correlation coefﬁcient (r)
to evaluate the collinearity strength among variables. Variables exhibiting
high collinearity (r ≥ 0.5) were identi ﬁed, and only the most important
variables, as determined by feature importance, were retained to minimize
multicollinearity in the model
61. Ultimately, following standardization of the
input data, the above-mentioned two-step feature selection was conducted,
resulting in the retention of 12 kay features with low collinearity (Supple-
mentary Table 3).

Statistical methods
All calculations and analyses were performed using Python 3.8.16 with the
following Python packages: pandas (1.4.4), NumPy (1.24.4), XGBoost
(2.0.3), LightGBM (4.5.0), CatBoost (1 .2.5), seaborn (0.13.2), Matplotlib
(3.7.5), SHAP (0.44.1), and scikit-learn (1.3.2). Graphical representations
were generated using Origin 2021. Maps were produced using ArcGIS
Desktop software (10.8.2) and interpolated using empirical Bayesian kri-
ging. The optimal ML model was built using XGBoost (2.0.3). The mean and
standard deviation for each evaluation indicator were computed on the basis
of 15 separate splits of the dataset. Student ’s t test was used for
signiﬁcance tests.
