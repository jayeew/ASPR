The oxidative enrichment and isotopic fractionation of cerium (Ce) in contact with vernadite ( δ-MnO2)
serve as a proxy for past redox conditions in both terrestrial and marine environments. However, the
molecular processes that govern the scavenging of Ce from the dissolved 3 + to the insoluble 4 +
oxidation states remain obscure. Adsorption experiments on synthetic δ-MnO
2 suggest that aqueous
Ce(III) precipitates as ceric hydroxide (Ce(OH)4), an unknown mineral. Here, the atomic-scale structure
of Ce in natural vernadite from ferromanganese crusts collected across the Paciﬁc, Atlantic, and Indian
Oceans was examined using advanced high-energy-resolution extended X-ray absorption ﬁne
structure spectroscopy. The ﬁndings provide direct evidence for the uptake of Ce as mononuclear
Ce(IV) complexes at the layer-edge sites (DES complex) and Mn(IV) vacancy sites of vernadite. Density
functional theory-based Gibbs free-energy calculations indicate that hydrolysis of the DES complex
promotes the oxidation of Ce(III) to Ce(IV). Quantum mechanical calculations predict that the
equilibrium
136Ce/140Ce isotope fractionation factor between Ce(III) dissolved in seawater and the
Ce(IV) complexes can reach 1.2-1.3 ‰ at 25 °C, indicating that the 136Ce/140Ce ratio has high potential
as a new paleoredox proxy.
Deep seawaters from the modern ocean are depleted in cerium (Ce) relative
to the other rare-earth elements (REE)1. The net removal of Ce from the
water column results from its oxidative scavenging from the dissolved 3+ to
the insoluble 4+ oxidation state in contact with ferromanganese (FeMn)
(oxyhydr)oxides, which grow as FeMn crusts and nodules on seamounts
and abyssal plains. The positive Ce-enrichment anomaly (Ce/Ce *)o f
hydrogenetic FeMn deposits relative to the other REE 2,3, and resulting
negative Ce anomaly of carbonate an d phosphate marine deposits, is a
standard indicator of redox cond itions in both modern and ancient
environments4–10. For instance, early studies of REE in conodonts from a
wide range of sediments indicated a dominance of anoxic conditions (no Ce
anomaly) in early Paleozoic oceans , while more recent oxic conditions

Concluding remarks
For several decades, progress in the crystal chemistry of Ce in FeMn deposits
has been at a standstill, hindered by the inability to measure Ce-EXAFS
spectra due to the lack of a high-lumi nosity wavelength-dispersive spec-
trometer needed toﬁlter out parasiticﬂuorescence lines from other elements
in the matrix. In this study, the use of this advanced instrument enabled
direct analysis of the atomic-scale structure of Ce in naturalδ-MnO
2 at the
thousand ppm level (mg/kg), which is about one-tenth of the concentration
at which Ce was observed to precipitate as Ce(OH) 4 during adsorption
experiments12.
HERFD–EXAFS measurements and their interpretation using ato-
mistic models indicate that Ce is oxidatively adsorbed as a mononuclear
DES complex at the edge and vacancy sites of theδ-MnO
2 layers. Therefore,
Ce isotopes do not seem to fractionate in FeMn crusts from the Paci ﬁc,
Atlantic, and Indian oceans through the mechanism observed when Ce
precipitates in contact withδ-MnO
2 during adsorption experiments, where
Ce(OH)4 is formed 12,14. Polymerization of Ce(IV), followed by its pre-
cipitation as Ce(OH)4, may begin with the deprotonation of the H2Ol i g a n d
(i.e., by hydrolysis) from the mono nuclear DES and vacancy complexes
during the electron transfer from Ce(III) to Mn(IV) or Mn(III). In a manner
similar to Cr(III)
92, the hydrolyzed Ce oligomers could serve as nuclei for the
heterogeneous precipitation of Ce(OH)4 on the surface of δ-MnO2 under
conditions that are undersaturated relative to homogeneous precipitation.
This research provides a foundation to advance understanding of Ce
cycling in natural systems through the combination of HERFD-XANES/
EXAFS spectroscopy,
136Ce/140Ce measurements, and ab initio calculations
based on spectroscopy-derived atomic structures. Although the low abun-
