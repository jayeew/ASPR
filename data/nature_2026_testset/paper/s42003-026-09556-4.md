Cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) storage roots exhibit signiﬁcant variation in starch content among
cultivars, yet the metabolic and molecular mechanisms governing carbon allocation between storage
and structural components remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated carbon partitioning in
two cassava cultivars with distinct starch phenotypes: high-starch FX01 and low-starch SC16. Using
13C isotope labeling coupled with metabolomic analysis, we traced the pathway of carbohydrates
through primary and secondary metabolism. The results revealed that SC16 exhibits enhanced
photosynthetic capacity and elevated soluble sugar content in storage roots, whereas FX01
demonstrates superior starch synthesis due to its ef ﬁcient glucose and fructose phosphorylation.
Conversely, SC16 exhibits a faster conversion of
13C-labeled ferulic acid, directing carbon ﬂow
towards lignin biosynthesis via the phenylpropanoid pathway. Further, by silencing the MeCOMT8
gene, encoding a key enzyme in ferulic acid biosynthesis, we observed a reduction in lignin content
and an increase in ADP-glucose levels in the MeCOMT8-silenced cassava plants, suggesting a
regulatory link between these competing pathways. Our research elucidated that the variations in
carbon allocation between starch and lignin biosynthesis among different cultivars are ﬁnely
orchestrated through the speci ﬁc-step alteration of metabolic ﬂux. These ﬁndings provide potential
candidate targeted points and valuable insights for high-starch breeding in cassava.

Metabolic differences in photosynthesis, sugar transport and its
conversion in cassava cultivars
Improved photosynthesis leads to increased plant productivity and
yield26,27. Leaf photosynthetic products are transported to the roots, pri-
marily forming starch and vascular tissues during the development of the
cassava storage root system. Metabolic competition for carbon ﬂux
between the structural components (such as lignin and cellulose) and
starch accumulation is common in storage crops (Fig. 1a). Our previous
study demonstrated a negative correlation between starch content and
lignin deposition in cassava storage roots, highlighting a trade-off in
carbon allocation
5. To dissect the divergence in carbon ﬂux, FX01 and
SC16 cultivars identiﬁed as high starch and low starch were used in this
study. The results showed that the net leaf photosynthetic rate in SC16
was signi ﬁcantly higher than that for FX01 (Fig. 1b), which was con-
sistent with the higher ribulose-1, 5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase
(RuBisCO) activity in SC16 (Fig. 1c). This is supported by the fact that a
higher net leaf photosynthetic rate positively correlates with higher PEP
carboxylase activity
28. Stomatal conductance and transpiration rate were
also observed to be signi ﬁcantly higher in SC16 compared to FX01
(Fig. 1d, e). Nevertheless, an intriguing observation was that the
enhanced photosynthesis in SC16 failed to result in an increased starch
content within its storage roots (Fig.1f), whereas lignin content exhibited
a higher trend in SC16 (Fig. 1g). Sugar transport can affect carbon ﬂux
allocation through transporters such as SWEETs, sucrose transporters
(SUTs), and sugar transport protein (STP)
11,29– 31.T h e r e f o r e ,w ea l s o
quantiﬁed soluble sugar and sucrose content in different cassava tissues
to investigate the potential differences in sucrose transport between the
two cassava cultivars (Figure. S1). Soluble sugar content in roots was
higher in SC16 than that of FX01. Despite the higher sucrose content in
leaves for cultivar FX01, it was similar in root between two cultivars,
suggesting that sucrose availability in storage roots was not the primary
determinant underlying the divergent starch accumulation phenotypes
(Fig. S1d). When combined with the higher sucrose synthase activity
observed in roots of FX01 (Fig. 1h), these results indicated that the
metabolism ef ﬁciency of sucrose following unloading, rather than
sucrose transport or availability, served as the key driver of the con-
trasting starch accumulation patterns. Sucrose hydrolysis catalyzed by
cell wall invertase (CWI) varied in storage roots between cultivars. CWI
activity was signiﬁcantly higher in SC16 storage roots compared to FX01
(Fig. 1i). This enzyme is likely to play a minor role in cassava sucrose
metabolism due to the less enzymatic activity than other enzymes.
Notably, SuSy activity was signi ﬁcantly higher in FX01 storage roots
compared to SC16 (Fig. 1h), suggesting more efﬁcient sucrose catabolism
inducing starch accumulation in the high-starch cultivar. SuSy catalyzes
the reversible conversion of sucrose and UDP to UDP-glucose and
fructose, serving as the primary route for sucrose metabolism in the
symplastic pathway. The resulting UDP-glucose is converted to glucose-
1-phosphate (G1P) by UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, and G1P is
subsequently converted to G6P by phosphoglucomutase. G6P is then
transported into plastids, where it is converted back to G1P by plastidial
phosphoglucomutase. G1P is subsequently used to produce ADP-glucose
via ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, which serves as the substrate for
starch biosynthesis
32. To evaluate the capacity of sucrose synthesis,
sucrose-phosphate synthase (SPS) activity was determined under both
substrate-limiting and saturating conditions. No signiﬁcant difference of
SPS activity was observed between two cultivars under substrate-limiting
conditions. Whereas, SC16 showed signi ﬁcantly higher SPS activity
under saturating conditions, suggesting the greater sucrose synthesis
capacity dependent on substrate supply (Fig. 1j).

Discussion
Generally, a higher photosynthetic capacity in crops is associated with
increased plant biomass production and yield
26,27. Consequently, the selec-
tion for high photosynthetic ef ﬁciency emerges as a pivotal long-term
strategy in crop breeding programs. In this study, we investigated the
intricate changes in metabolicﬂux, and observed that a higher net photo-
synthetic rate in cassava cultivars with more RuBisCO enzyme activity is not
necessarily accompanied by higher starch content in storage roots (Fig.1).
Cultivar SC16 exhibited higher soluble sugar content in storage roots than
FX01, particularly elevated sucrose levels (Fig. S1). However, this sucrose
accumulation does not reﬂect superior unloading capacity, as cassava relies
on symplasmic transport via plasmodesmata, which is an apoplastic-
independent process
7. Instead, the higher sucrose content in SC16 storage
roots stems from metabolic backlogging caused by inefﬁcient sucrose cat-

Plant materials
Plant growth experiments were conducted at the Life Science and

used: Fuxuan01 (FX01), obtained from the university, and Huanan16

USA) equipped with a Thermo Fisher Syncronis aQ column (100 × 2.1 mm,

(B) at aﬂow rate of 0.3 mL min
-1. The gradient started with 95% A for 2 min,
switched to 95% B for 18 min, held steady for 3 min, and then returned to
the initial conditions for a total run time of 25 min.
To establish the optimal pH for the reaction, buffers of 100 mM citric

perature that yielded the most favora ble results was determined across a
range of 16-40 °C, with each assay period lasting 60 min. Kinetic parameters
(Km and Vmax) of the recombinant proteins were ascertained by varying
the concentration of caffeic acid (5-1000μM) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.0),
with incubations performed at 37 °C for 60 min.

plants grown under identical conditions. All experiments were conducted
with at least three biologically independent replicates (n ≥ 3) unless other-
wise stated in ﬁgure legends, with each replicate analyzed individually to
determine the maximum biological variation. Speciﬁcs a m p l es i z e s :n =3f o r

The data are presented as mean values derived from the three inde-
pendent replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-

p-value of less than 0.05 (P < 0.05) was suggestive of a statistically signiﬁcant
