Focal adhesions (FAs) are mechanosensitive structures that mediate force transmission between cells
and the extracellular matrix. While Traction Force Microscopy (TFM) quanti ﬁes cellular tractions
exerted on deformable substrates, Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET)-based tension probes,
such as vinculin tension sensors, measure molecular-scale forces within FA proteins. Despite their
potential synergy, these methods have rarely been combined to explore the interplay between
molecular tension and cellular tractions. Here, we introduce a framework integrating TFM and FRET-
based vinculin tension sensors to investigate FA mechanics across scales. At cell level, tractions and
vinculin tension increased with substrate stiffness. At FA level, vinculin tension correlated solely with
vinculin density, while tractions scaled with FA area, orientation, total vinculin content and vinculin
density. Direct comparison of tractions to vinculin tension revealed a complex, heterogenous
relationship between these forces, possibly linked to diverse cell and FA maturation states. Sub-FA
analysis revealed conserved spatial patterns, with both tension and traction increasing towards the
cell periphery. This multiscale approach provides an integrated work ﬂow for studying focal adhesion
forces, helping to bridge the gap between vinculin tension and cellular tractions.
Mechanobiology is an interdisciplinary ﬁeld that investigates the reci-
procal relationship between mechanical forces and biological processes.
Mechanotransduction— the process by which cells sense and respond to
mechanical signals from their environment — plays a crucial role in
fundamental cellular functions such as proliferation, differentiation and
migration, as well as in pathological conditions like ﬁbrosis, cancer and
cardiovascular diseases
1–5. Deeper understanding of mechanotransduc-
tion offers insights into cellular behavior and disease mechanisms,
leading to innovative therapies that restore the mechanical equilibrium in
the cellular microenvironment.

While TFM and FRET-based tension probes are powerful techniques
on their own, they offer insights into cell-matrix forces at different levels.
TFM quantiﬁes relatively large forces that are sufﬁcient to induce measur-
able substrate deformations, depending on factors like substrate stiffness.
These forces originate from subcellular regions, such as FAs and lamelli-
podia. In contrast, FRET-based tension probes report the molecular forces
at the nanoscale. To date, only two studies have combined FRET tension
probes with cell-ECM force measurements. Chang et al. performed TFM on
cells expressing vinculin tension sensors and reported a slight inverse linear
correlation between mean traction and mean FRET ratio at FAs
28. However,
their analysis was limited by the low throughput of just four cells28.S a r a n g i
et al.24 simultaneously mapped traction forces using a micropillar array and
vinculin tension within FAs. This approach revealed how changes in trac-
tion forces corresponded to variations in vinculin tension. However, their
in vitro system con ﬁned FAs to the top of micropillars, which may not
accurately reﬂect native FA behavior and could inﬂuence their dynamics or
functionality.
In this study, we established an experimental and computational fra-
mework integrating hydrogel-based TFM, which enables FA formation on
an unrestrictive, continuous substrate, with vinculin tension sensors to
investigate the interplay between vinculin tension and traction forces at FAs.
Our ﬁndings showed that tractions increase with FA area, angle, vinculin
content and density, while vinculin tension was mainly regulated by vinculin
density. At the cell level, we observed both tension and traction magnitude
increase with increasing substrate stiffness. At the FA-level, we identiﬁed
three correlation patterns between vinculin tension and traction - direct,
inverse and uncorrelated - suggesting varied cellular and FA states. At the
sub-FA level, vinculin tension and traction followed a similar trend along
the longitudinal axis of FAs, consistent across different cell and FA popu-
lations. The framework advances ou r understanding of subcellular
mechanotransduction and provides a robust foundation for future studies of
cellular force dynamics and mechanobiology.
Results
Integrated workﬂow for TFM and FRET-based tension probes
To integrate TFM with FRET-based tension probes, we developed a custom
protocol to overcome the technical challenges of combining these methods
(Fig. 1a, details in the methods section). Subcellular FRET imaging requires
high numerical aperture (NA) object ives, which have a limited working
distance of ~300μm. To ensure that cells on the polyacrylamide (PAA) gel
remained within the objective’s working distance, we fabricated thin, defect-
free PAA substrates using 250μm-thick silicone spacers and #0 coverslips.
The spacers also createdﬂat gel surfaces, aligning all focal adhesions within
the same z-plane, thereby facilitatingFRET imaging. Accurate calculation of
tractions exerted by individual mouse embryonic ﬁbroblasts (MEFs)
required a low cell density of ~25 cells per mm
2. At this density, cell
spreading necessitated an optimal collagen coating, which was achieved by
using an acidic solution
35. To quantify vinculin tension, we employed the
VinTS-tCRMod-GGSGGS7 sensor described by LaCroix et al.29, hereafter
referred to as VinTS. This optimized VinTS construct contains a
Clover–mRuby2 FRET pair linked by a hep tameric GGSGGS extensible
domain, which elongates under piconewton loads and thereby modulates
the energy transfer. Vinculin
−/− MEFs transiently expressing VinTS were
seeded on the collagen-coated gels and imaged 24 hours later, by which
point the cells had developed well-spread morphologies and mature focal
adhesions, ensuring meaningful correlation between traction and vinculin
tension.
Similar to sample preparation, combining TFM and FRET probes
required a speci ﬁc data acquisition protocol (Fig. 1b). Due to the
dynamic behavior of ﬁbroblasts, which induces relatively fast changes in
cell morphology, it is crucial to minimize the time interval between the
TFM and FRET data acquisition. First, bead images of the stressed state
were acquired, followed by Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging Microscopy
(FLIM) images of the donor channel to calculate FRET ef ﬁciencies
36–39
.
To analyze the vinculin content and the morphology of the FAs, ﬂuor-
escence images of the acceptor channel – which is invariant under dif-
ferent FRET ef ﬁciencies – were acquired. These steps were repeated for
~5–10 cells per sample. Next, cells were removed using sodium dodecyl
sulfate (SDS), allowing the relaxation of the PAA gel, and bead images of
the relaxed state were acquired for all measured cells, completing the data
acquisition process.
Stressed and relaxed state bead images were subsequently analyzed
using a previously established 2D TFM work ﬂow
40. Displacement ﬁelds
were calculated using free-form deformation (FFD) image registration of
bead positions between the two states41 (Fig. 1c, top row). Traction ﬁelds
were derived from the displacement ﬁelds using a Tikhonov-regularized
Fourier transform traction cytometry algorithm (Fig.1c, top row)21,40.T o
calculate the FRET ef ﬁciency of the VinTS, ﬂuorescence lifetimes were
analyzed using the phasor approach 42–46. Phasor-transformation of the
donor-speciﬁc FLIM data can be used to directly retrieve FRET efﬁciencies
from a so-called FRET trajectory line. This line extends from the average
phasor position of the donor in the absence of acceptor (0% FRET; τ
D),
calculated from Clover-only transfected cells, to the position of background
signal (100% FRET; τ
BG), obtained from non-transfected cells (Fig. 1c,
middle row). The FRET efﬁciency of VinTS-expressing cells was then cal-
culated from the position of the acquired photons on the FRET trajectory.
TFM, FRET and acceptor intensity data were integrated using a custom
MATLAB code. A binary mask created by Ilastik47 (Fig.1c, bottom row) was
used to segment the FAs, allowing each FA to be assigned traction, FRET
and intensity information, together with morphological features such as FA
area and orientation.

Cell tractions and vinculin tension increase with stiffness at the
cell level
To evaluate how substrate stiffness inﬂuences cellular mechanics and FA
properties, we analyzed cell-averaged values of tractions, FRET efﬁciencies,
and FA features in MEFs seeded on PAA gels (Fig. 2a). Experiments were
performed on gels with a Young’s modulus of 4.35 ± (s.d.) 0.20 kPa (referred
to as 4.5 kPa) and 13.10 ± 0.23 kPa (referred to as 13 kPa), as characterized
by shear rheological measurements. These stiffness values were chosen to
ensure the substrates were stiff enough to support cell spreading but soft
enough to undergo detectable deformations. Cells on the stiffer substrate
spread more (Fig. 2b) and exerted higher tractions (Fig. 2c), indicating
enhanced cell –ECM interactions and cell contractility with increasing
stiffness. Since FAs are the primary structures through which cells transmit
forces, we investigated whether FA structural (number, total area, density,
average area, eccentricity and orienta tion) and molecular (total vinculin
content and vinculin density) properties change with substrate stiffness.
Interestingly, none of the tested properties showed signiﬁcant differences
between the two selected stiffnesses (Supplementary Fig. 1).
Next, we investigated whether increased tractions correlated with
changes in vinculin tension within FAs. For this, we determined cell-
averaged FRET efﬁciencies of FAs in VinTS-expressing cells seeded on soft
and stiff PAA gels, as well as on glass. Cell-averaged FRET ef ﬁciency
decreased with increasing stiffness: highest on the soft gel, intermediate on
the stiff gel, and lowest on glass (Fig. 2f). To validate that the observed
decrease in VinTS FRET ef ﬁciency reﬂects increased mechanical tension
across vinculin, we performed several control experiments. First, we used a
cytosolic, force-insensitive control sensor
30 (tCRMod GGSGGS7, hereafter
referred to as TSMod; Fig. 2d), which exhibited consistently high FRET
efﬁciencies across all substrate conditions (Fig.2f). Second, we conﬁrmed
that ﬂuorophore localization did not in ﬂuence FRET ef ﬁciency, as the
lifetime of unquenched Clover was consistent between the cytosol and FAs
(Supplementary Fig. 2a) and matched reported values 48.T h i r d ,i n t e r -
molecular FRET was ruled out by co-expressing Clover- and mRuby2-
tagged vinculin constructs (Supplementary Fig. 2a); Clover lifetimes
remained constant across a broad range of expression levels (Supplementary
Fig. 2b). Finally, donor lifetimes remained stable throughout the imaging
period used, indicating that photobl eaching did not affect our measure-
ments (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Taken together, these results conﬁrmed that
the observed changes in VinTS FRET efﬁciency reﬂect changes in molecular
tension across vinculin.

analysis were lower than those reported for the same Clover–mRuby2 pair
in the literature

quantiﬁed the unloaded FRET ef ﬁciency of TSMod using a conventional
sensitized-emission (SE) approach, as in Gates et al. 49 (Supplementary
Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 10). In this ratiometric, intensity-based
method, TSMod exhibited an unloaded FRET ef ﬁciency of 48.6 ± 3.3%
(Supplementary Fig. 3), closely matching the 49.6% as reported by LaCroix
et al., conﬁrming that the sensor behaves as expected in our system. This

molecular state. In FLIM-based analysis, this ensemble averaging is addi-

Among all parameters, FA orientation emerged as the most robust
predictor for tractions (Fig.3e). In both soft and stiff gels, 85% of the cells

area showed a signiﬁcant association with traction in 44% of cells on soft gels
and 74% on stiff gels. The number of cells showing signiﬁcant correlations
with total vinculin content increases from 33% (4.5 kPa) to 57% (13 kPa),
and for vinculin density from 33% to 37%. Together, these results indicate
that local tractions are governed by a combination of geometric (size,
orientation) and molecular (vinculin load-bearing capacity) cues. Further-
more, the higher incidence of signiﬁcant correlations at 13 kPa indicates that
the coupling between FA features and tractions is reinforced in a stiffer
mechanical environment.
The correlation between vinculin tension — as reported by FRET
efﬁciency–and FA features was generally weaker and less consistent than
for traction forces. Signi ﬁcant associations between FRET and FA area,
FA orientation or total vinculin content were found in fewer than 20% of
cells at either stiffness, and these included both positive and negative
correlations (Supplementary Fig. 5). Vinculin density was the only
parameter that showed a relatively consistent correlation with FRET
efﬁciency: a positive correlation was observed in 41% of cells on 4.5 kPa
gels and 34% on 13 kPa gels, whereas negative correlations were rare
(<4% for both stiffness, Fig. 3e). In contrast to the traction data, substrate
stiffness had little to no effect on the relationship between vinculin
tension and FA features.
Overall, these results suggest that local tractions are jointly modulated
by FA geometry and total vinculin content or density, whereas vinculin
tension, as inferred from FRET efﬁciency measurements, depends primarily
on the local packing density of vinculin molecules within the focal adhesion,
with more dense vinculin displaying lower tension.
Correlating vinculin tension to traction at FA level reveals cell-to-
cell heterogeneity
At cell level, both cell-averaged tractions and vinculin tension increased with
substrate stiffness (Fig.2c, f), suggesting a positive correlation between cell-
level traction and vinculin tension. To assess if this relationship is also
present at the scale of individual adhesion sites, we next examined whether
traction and vinculin tension are correlated at the FA level.
Similar to the previous section, mean tractions and FRET efﬁciencies
were calculated for each individual FA and normalized to the maximum
values within each cell. While no clear correlation between FA-averaged
traction and FRET efﬁciency was observed when pooling data across all cells
(Supplementary Fig. 7a), a cell-speciﬁc regression analysis revealed distinct
traction–vinculin tension correlations in ind ividual cells (representative
examples in Fig. 4a–f). For each cell, we performed a linear regression
between the normalized mean traction and the normalized mean FRET
efﬁciency of its FAs and tested whether the slope differed signiﬁcantly from
zero, as in the previous section. Based on the sign and signi ﬁcance of the
slope, cells were classiﬁed as showing a direct, inverse, or no correlation
between tractions and vinculin tension. Note that since a higher FRET
efﬁciency corresponds to a lower vinculin tension, a negative slope between
mean traction and FRET efﬁciency indicates a direct correlation (i.e., higher
tractions correspond to higher vinculin tension, Fig.4a, d), while a positive
slope reﬂects an inverse correlation between traction and molecular tension
(Fig. 4b, e).

correlation between traction and vinculin tension, while 17.6% displayed a
signiﬁcant direct correlation and only 5.9% an inverse correlation (Fig.4g).

Discussion
This work introduces an integrated approach for investigating FA
mechanics, combining simultaneous measurements of molecular tension
and cellular tractions. By improving experimental sample preparation,
streamlining data acquisition, and developing custom software, we achieved
a highly detailed comparison of FA forces and the tension experienced by
vinculin. These methodological advancements allowed us to explore the
spatial coordination of cellular traction and vinculin tension, revealing new
insights into the adaptability of FAs in force regulation and
mechanotransduction.
Fluorescence imaging of VinTS-expressing cells combined with TFM
enabled us to systematically assess how FA morphology and vinculin con-
tent relate to cellular tractions and vinculin tension. Our cell-speci ﬁc
regression analysis revealed that traction magnitude is most strongly cor-
related with FA orientation relative to the cell centroid, with radially aligned
adhesions tending to support higher tractions. To a lesser degree, the FA
area, total vinculin content, and vinculin density also showed positive cor-
relations with traction, particularly on stiffer substrates. Similar relation-
ships between tractions and FA properties, such as size and intensity, have
been previously reported
52–54, although these trends were found to hold
primarily for nascent and growing adhesions at the cell’s leading edge, and
not for trailing or disassembling FAs55. Moreover, as noted by Zhou et al.56,
FA features such as area or vinculin content alone are insufﬁcient to predict

illustrating the variability of this relationship at the single-cell level. This

lowering the force experienced by each and preventing bond failure.
Considering the roles of actomyosincontractility and FA properties in
regulating tractions
60–63 and vinculin recruitment13,17,64,65, we investigated
whether a relationship exists between vinculin tension and traction at the FA
level. While no correlation between FA-averaged vinculin tension and
traction was observed when cells were pooled, analyzing individual cells
revealed cell-to-cell variability. Nearly a quarter of the cells showed a direct
correlation, where higher FA vinculin tension corresponded to higher
tractions. A smaller subset, however, exhibited an inverse relationship,
where decreased vinculin tension corresponded to elevated tractions. This
variability likely, at least in part, reﬂects differences in the way cells interact
with the ECM. We propose that highly engaged cells, such as those actively
spreading or migrating, exhibit a directcorrelation between vinculin tension
and traction. In contrast, cells with deteriorating cell-ECM interactions,
such as those retracting or shrinking, may display an inverse correlation.
In these cases, tension is released from peripheral FAs while central FAs
remain under tension. This observation aligns with prior studies indi-
cating that peripheral FAs are more dynamic and prone to disassembly
compared to central FAs
28. Retracting FAs, which are linked to high
tractions before disassembly8,55, are known to experience lower vinculin
tension26. Notably, a signi ﬁcant portion of cells exhibited no clear cor-
relation between vinculin tension and traction. This may suggest that
these cells were in a stable, non-migratory state, with less dynamic cell-
ECM interactions during imaging or that their FAs were in mixed
functional phases, with some leading FAs promoting spreading and
others trailing during retraction. These observations emphasize the
complexity of FA dynamics, where both direct and inverse correlations
emerge based on the state of the cell and FA behavior. Combining our
TFM-FRET approach alongside tools to modulate actomyosin activity,
such as ROCK Inhibitor Y27632, myosin inhibitor Blebbistatin or MLCP
inhibitor Calyculin A, combined with time-lapse imaging, could provide
deeper insights into these dynamic relationships.
Recognizing cell-to-cell variability in the relation between vinculin
tension and traction, we investigated whether these two exhibit distinct
correlations at sub-FA level. In agreement with earlier works

spatial analysis revealed distinct patterns of vinculin density, traction, and
tension along the FA length, reﬂecting a mechanism for force transmission
that is independent of cellular state. In the proximal half of FAs, closer to the
cell center, vinculin density graduall y increased, driving an increase in
traction despite the low and stable molecular tension. In contrast, the distal
half, closer to the cell periphery, showed a decreasing vinculin density
alongside an increasing vinculin tension, suggesting that higher vinculin
tension compensates for reduced vinculin density to sustain force trans-
mission. These results suggest a spa tial coordination between vinculin
density and tension to regulate tractio ns, highlighting the adaptability of
FAs in force generation and mechanot ransduction. Unexpectedly, unlike
the FA level results, vinculin density and tension were not negatively cor-
related at sub-FA level. Previous studies using the vinculin conformation
sensor, have shown that vinculin adopts an open, active conformation more
frequently at the proximal end of FAs
6,10. This suggests that vinculin density
alone may not regulate tension; instead, the amount of activated vinculin,
capable of engaging in force transmission, could be the key factor. We
hypothesize that the molecular tension is lower at the proximal end, where
activated vinculin is more abundant, and higher at the distal end, where
vinculin activation decreases. Overall, these results indicate that vinculin
tension is regulated at two levels: vinculin density determines overall tension
at the FA level, while vinculin activation controls tension distribution at the
sub-FA level. To explore this further, future studies could combine TFM-
FRET with vinculin conformation sensors or semi-digital vinculin tension
sensors
32,66,67 to investigate how vinculin activation state and its spatial
distribution shape the relationship between vinculin tension and tractions.
The substantial scatter in the FA data across all experiments in our
study suggests that the relationship between traction and vinculin tension,
averaged per FA, is complex. Rather than depending solely on vinculin
tension, tractions likely arise from thecombined contributions of vinculin
tension, its density and activation state, along with force transmission
through other structural proteins like talin. For instance, low vinculin ten-
sion paired with high active vinculin density could generate tractions similar
to high tension with low active vinculi n density. Additionally, vinculin
tension and tractions may operate on different temporal scales and do not
necessarily adjust synchronously in response to mechanical changes. Fur-
thermore, since stressed-state bead images for TFM were acquired before
FLIM imaging, vinculin tension changes during FLIM acquisition might not
be reﬂected in the traction data.
While our study focuses speciﬁcally on vinculin tension, it is important
to recognize that total force transmission from the actomyosin cytoskeleton
to integrin-based focal adhesions involves a coordinated network of other
proteins, including talin, paxillin, focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and many
others. As such, the tension measured by vinculin-based sensors re ﬂects
only one aspect of the broader mechanical landscape. The heterogeneous
traction–tension relationships observed at the FA level may partly re ﬂect
differences in the local protein composition and connectivity across indi-
vidual adhesions. Future work employing orthogonal sensors - for example,
talin tension sensors
66,67 or DNA-hairpin sensors 68 to measure integrin
forces - could provide a more compre hensive picture of how forces are
distributed across distinct molecul ar linkages within focal adhesion
complexes.

taneously measuring tractions and molecular tension within FAs, enabling
new insights into their mechanics. We showed that the relationship between
vinculin tension and traction depends on cellular states and FA context.
Cell-level analysis showed that both tractions and vinculin tension increase
with increasing substrate stiffness. FAlevel analysis revealed a correlation
between vinculin density and tension, allowing FAs to balance forces and
sustain effective tractions. Furthermore, FA level analysis revealed a dif-
ferential relationship between tractions and vinculin tension, possibly
reﬂecting the cellular state and activity. Sub-FA analysis showed a conserved

Statistics and reproducibility
At least three independent technical replicates were performed for each
experiment, and biologically independent samples were analyzed. The
experiments were not randomized, and researchers were not blinded during
the experiments and outcome assessment. Data quanti ﬁcation was con-
ducted using automated tools. Graphs were generated using MATLAB
R2022b or GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software). The source data
ﬁles provide the data points of graphs andthe associated statistical tests and
p values. Sampled cells were excluded only if they did not meet the
requirements for traction recovery (e.g., if the displacement ﬁeld was
unreliable due to large stage shifts between the stressed and relaxed state or if
the assumption of a displacement/st ress-free boundary was violated).
Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data
following a normal distribution, a tw o-tailed, unpaired, parametric Stu-
dent’s t test was applied, while the nonparametric Mann-WhitneyU test was
used for non-normally distributed data. Statistical signiﬁcance was deﬁned
