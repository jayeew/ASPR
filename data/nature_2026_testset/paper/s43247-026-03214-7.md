Magma reservoir organization is hidden from view but important to systematic understanding of active
volcanoes and intrusive igneous rocks. Seismic anisotropy in active silicic reservoirs suggests
horizontally elongated melt sheets or sills, but it remains unclear if shallow silicic reservoirs in low strain
rate settings without recent recharge maintain similar organization. Here we present new 3-D seismic
tomography of the upper crustal reservoir beneath the tectonically quiescent Valles Caldera using
dense nodal arrays. Results reveal >15% radial anisotropy consistent with up to 17-24% melt in
horizontally elongated volumes interspersed with melt-poor layers, despite net crystallization since
about 0.5 Ma and little contemporary strain. Structural similarity of Valles Caldera to more dynamic
modern systems indicates that intrinsic reservoir processes like iterative magma injection, crystal
settling, and melt compaction organize long-lived horizontally-layered structure, largely independent
of tectonic setting and recharge rate.
Constraints on how continental magma reservoirs are organized are
important for the development of models of volcanic hazards, concentration
of mineral resources, and long-term geologic evolution of the crust
1,2.
Organization is potentially inﬂuenced by many factors including regional
tectonic setting3,4, reservoir longevity, time -varying magma supply rates,
and volatile contents5– 7. Even among large silicic reservoirs, there is con-
siderable evidence for varied structures 8, but there is also evidence for
commonalities such as long-lived d eep heat sources and multiple melt
staging depths where differentiation occurs 9. Studying silicic magmatic
systems across different tectonic settings and volcanic life-cycle stages can
help advance insights into the processes that control reservoir organization.
Geological ﬁeld sampling and increasingly high-resolution geochem-
ical analysis provide growing eviden ce for discrete melt batches incre-
mentally building large silicic reservoirs based on crystallization chronology
and bulk and trace element heterogeneity 10– 15.S u c hﬁne-scale analytical

much larger scale, about 1– 10 km or more, resolution of the aggregate size
and shape of voluminous reservoirs

The ﬁnding of pronounced radial anisotropy in the Valles magma
reservoir at about 2 – 15 km depth, implies that the organized sill-like
structures can be maintained over 105 years time scales during which the
reservoir undergoes net crystallization. It also implies that the large silicic
reservoir organization that creates ra dial anisotropy operates similarly
across a wide range of regional tectonicstrain regimes. These insights arise
from the distinctive life-cycle stage and tectonic setting of Valles among
other modern silicic magma reservoirs known to have strong radial
anisotropy
22,43,44. It has a low regional tectonic (horizontal) strain rate
(Fig. 1c; ref. 33), limited local vertical surface deformation consistent with a
lack of contemporary magma recharge 27, and scarce seismicity 27,32.B y
contrast, other calderas known to have strong radial anisotropy in middle to
upper crustal magma reservoirs such as Yellowstone, Long Valley, Toba,
and Okmok, reside in areas with more active tectonic deformation and/or
evidence of modern magmatic deformation associated with surface inﬂation
or swarm-like seismicity
19,55– 59. Despite these differences in tectonic and
magmatic activity, the similar organization of seismic anisotropy across
such diverse settings may be a consequence of the voluminous and long-
lived reservoirs effectively overprinting the inﬂuence of regional tectonics
and local structure preceding reservoir assembly.
Horizontal layering from intrinsic magma reservoir processes
Similar organization of anisotropy in large silicic magma reservoirs across a
variety of settings suggests that it arises due to intrinsic magmatic processes
rather than interactions with speciﬁc tectonic regimes or inherited struc-
tures. We consider reservoir assembly through iterative magma recharge,
crystal settling, and crystal mush compaction as processes that are likely to
contribute to the widespread development of horizontally elongated melt-
rich volumes that create radial ani sotropy. Iterative magma recharge
through ascending dikes being deﬂected by discontinuities and forming new
sills is a widely used model for magma reservoir construction
53,60.T h em o d e l
appears applicable to the earlier evolution of the Valles reservoir based on
geochronologic evidence for many small eruptions between the two caldera
forming events at 1.6 and 1.23 Ma and an average recurrence interval of 5.5
ka for post-caldera eruptions between 0.5– 1.23 Ma30,31. The short recurrence
interval and sustained activity suggest that magma recharge occurred more
rapidly than heat loss, enabling successive intrusions to be de ﬂected and
stacked beneath pre-existing sills
53.

Modern estimates of the melt fractions within sills of about 17– 24% are
similar to or below the terminal porosity of about 20– 30% estimated based
on exhumed silicic intrusions
63. Thus, most modern melt is likely subject to
slow crystallization in sub-horizontal layers with weak inter-connectivity
across a large composite volume at near-solidus temperatures, which are
needed to support high temperature gradients (Fig.1d). Latent heat release
due to gradual crystallization would promote reservoir longevity through
times of slow or absent recharge, and this effect is delayed until the last stages
of crystallization for h ydrated silicic magmas
65,66 such as post-collapse
rhyolites from Valles with about 4.4– 6 wt% water67.
In the absence of increased magma recharge, the sills would eventually
completely crystallize and coalesce into plutons13. Some of the structural
contacts within the former magma reservoir may be preserved while others
may heal or be disrupted by subsequent deformation
68. Upon complete
crystallization (but long before potential outcrop exposure), the seismic
radial anisotropy signature is expected to disappear or substantially fade
because the contrasting seismic velocities in modern systems arise due to
partial melt adjacent to slightly sub-solidus rock (Fig.4). This evolution is
exempliﬁed by the about 5 Ma Heise Caldera,located along the Yellowstone
hotspot track, which no longer exhibits positive radial anisotropy in contrast
to the active Yellowstone caldera
22.
If deep magma supply were to be rejuvenated it is possible that local
connectivity between sills could increase and aid the formation of eruptible
melt volumes. Despite net reservoir crystallization since about 0.5 Ma28,a n
episode of magma recharge is suggested for the most recent 69 ka Valles
eruptions that totaled about 14 km331,69, which is a small fraction of the total
melt inferred in the modern sill complex. Organization into sill-like struc-
tures may be important to understanding the transient stability of magmatic
systems undergoing protracted crystallization, until they are perturbed by
changes in recharge or tectonic stresses that may link multiple sills and
enhance magma mobility. Much more voluminous caldera-forming erup-
tions would likely require a sustained increase in deep recharge for 10
4-5
years to augment and re-melt a larger fraction of the highly crystalline
modern reservoir (e.g.,
70).
