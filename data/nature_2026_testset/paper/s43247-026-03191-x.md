Rainfall pulses generate rapid increases and subsequent declines in soil moisture (SM), yet global
ecosystem responses during SM dry-downs remain poorly quanti ﬁed. Using 6502 soil dry-down
events identiﬁed from global eddy-covariance observations, we compared carbon ﬂuxes during
dry-downs (treatment) to ﬂuxes during the same periods without dry-downs in other years (control).
During early dry-downs days, gross primary production (GPP) and respiration both exceeded controls,
with stronger GPP gains enhancing net carbon uptake. This enhancement persisted for several days
before diminishing as SM decreased and atmospheric dryness intensi ﬁed. Latent and sensible heat
ﬂuxes also rose initially, but latent heat enhancement weakened over time, accompanied by enhanced
sensible heat. Machine-learning analyses show that photosynthetic capacity and radiation drive
positive GPP anomalies, while water limitations induce negative ones. Satellite data supported these
patterns, whereas Earth system models underestimate their magnitude. These ﬁndings highlight
transient pulse responses and support the broader applicability of the pulse-reserve paradigm.

and growth have complex and sometimesopposing effects, especially when
SM drops below a critical threshold (θcrit) and down-regulation mechanisms
appear8–11. Quantifying changes in carbon and water ﬂuxes during dry-

The “pulse-reserve” hypothesis, which posits that there is a transient
increase in plant growth following rainfall events, has been documented in
dry regions3,13,14. However, changes in carbon, water, and energy ﬂuxes
remain unclear at global scale, leaving a gap in our understanding of the
ecosystem response to moisture puls es. Previous studies have mostly
examined carbon ﬂux responses to drying at individual sites and have

experiment to investigate the short-term ecosystem responses and asso-

SM modulates carbon, water and energy exchanges between the land
and the atmosphere
19–22. In moist soils, moderate soil drying can enhance
photosynthetic biochemical activity and nitrogen uptake, thereby increasing
gross primary production (GPP) 23. However, for prolonged intervals
between rainfall events, SM deﬁcits impose pronounced stress on plants11,24.
Plant responses to SM during post moisture pulse vary widely in magnitude
and duration, depending on soil, roots and plants characteristics as well as
on atmospheric conditions, resulting in a nonlinear response of ecosystems
to SM
23,25,26. These responses, shaped by traits such as stomatal regulation
and rooting depth, not only determine ecohydrological processes but also
inﬂuence land-atmosphere feedbacks by modifying transpiration, sensible
heat ﬂux, and the subsequent development of the boundary layer
22.C o n -
sequently, the magnitude and duration of post-rainfall ecosystem responses
to moisture pulses during soil dryingare expected to differ among vegeta-
tion types and environmental conditions.

secutive days as the“treatment”in a natural experiment and compared them

These analyses were conducted using ﬂux tower measurements together
with global daily GPP and solar-induced ﬂuorescence (a proxy for GPP).

Soil dry-down characteristics and associated carbon and

Across 215 globally distributed ecosystems withﬂux tower measurements,
we identiﬁed 6502 soil dry-down events, deﬁned as periods of at least 10

post moisture pulse
We further examined carbon, water, and energyﬂuxes during post moisture
pulse across dryland (long term mean AI≤ 0.65) and non-dryland ecosys-
tems (AI > 0.65). For a 10-day dry-dow n, both exhibited early enhance-

Global pattern of GPP response during post moisture pulse
To determine whether the positive GPP response during post moisture
pulse is broadly applicable across ecosystems, we mapped the global pattern
of ΔGPP using daily GPP gridded ﬁelds from the FLUXCOM (RS +
METEO). In agreement with in-situ observations, GPP generally increased
during early soil dry-downs across most terrestrial ecosystems (Fig.4 and
Supplementary Fig. 9). This enhancement weakened as SM continued to
decline, and prolonged drying led to an increasing fraction of areas with
negative ΔGPP (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Fig. 10). Analyses based on
daily solar-induced chlorophyll ﬂuorescence (SIF) as a proxy for GPP
yielded similar results (Supplementary Fig. 11).
Across all pixels, the medianΔGPP was 0.14 [0.04, 0.26] gC m
−2 for SM
declines lasting more than 10 days, 0.12 [0.02, 0.26] gC m−2 for SM declines
of more than 15 days, and 0.09 [0, 0.24] gC m−2 for SM declines of at least
20 days (Fig. 4f). Stronger positive responses occurred in India, Russia,
southeastern China, and southeastern Brazil, whereas smaller increases were
observed in central Asia and southern Afghanistan (Fig. 4a-c). As drying
progressed, ΔGPP tended to become negative in regions such as central
Australia, Spain, and western North America (Fig. 4a-d). The decrease in
GPP during the late stages of the dry-down, driven by SM de ﬁcits and
increased VPD, offsets the earlier gains inΔGPP, resulting in a negative total
ΔGPP. Prolonged soil dry-downs result in more regions experiencing
negative totalΔGPP. PositiveΔGPP persists for approximately 9, 13.5, and
17 days during dry-downs lasting ≥10, 15, and 20 days, respectively
(Fig. 4d,f; Supplementary Fig. 12), after which ΔGPP generally became
negative, contributing to substanti al cumulative GPP losses in affected
regions (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Comparison with Earth System Models
Finally, we diagnosed GPP changes during soil dry-down fromﬁve Earth
System Models (ESMs) in Coupled Model Inter-comparison Project
Phase 6 which contributed daily outputs (Methods, Supplementary
Table 3). Consistent with observations, ESMs indicated that GPP initially
increased during post moisture pulse but diminished with longer dry-
downs (Supplementary Figs. 20f and 21). However, compared to results
derived from FLUXCOM GPP, ESMs signi ﬁcantly underestimated
ΔGPP. The underestimation reached 67%, 83%, and 90% for dry-downs
lasting ≥10, 15, and 20 days, respectively (Fig. 5 and Supplementary
Fig. 22), particularly in regions such as central Argentina, India, and
southeastern China. When calculating the relative change inΔGPP using
the ﬁve ESMs, we found consistent underestimation (Supplementary
Fig. 23). Signi ﬁcant differences were also found among the models in
their ability to capture GPP changes during post moisture pulse (Fig.5a-c
and Supplementary Fig. 23). Additionally, ΔGPP maps generated by
different ESMs exhibited notable spatial discrepancies (Supplementary
Fig. 20a-e), especially in regions such as Australia and southern South
Africa. We also found that both SM
control and magnitude of SM loss in
the models were generally lower than in ERA5 (Supplementary Fig. 24).
These ﬁndings indicate that current ESMs are unable to reliably project
SM dynamics and the responses of GPP during post moisture pulse
across global ecosystems, highlighting the urgent need for improved
representation of water-stress responses and soil-atmosphere interac-
tions to enhance the accuracy of future model predictions.

Conclusions
Vegetation plays a central role in terrestrial carbon and water cycles by
controlling and responding to changes in SM availability. Rainfall is pre-
dicted to be less frequent but more intense in the future across most
regions
24,39, fundamentally altering moistureavailability to plants. Although
many studies have investigated ecosystem responses to water stress, eco-
system carbon and waterﬂuxes changes during post moisture pulse remain
insufﬁciently characterized at global scale. Based on global eddy covariance
measurements, this study treated soil dry-down events as natural experi-
ments for understanding ecosystem responses. Our ﬁndings showed that
the increases in ecosystem carbon and waterﬂuxes during the early stage of
dry-downs are widespread, with G PP increasing more than ecosystem
respiration, thereby enhancing ne t carbon sink. This enhancement can
persist for several days before gradually declining as SM decreases further.
Importantly, the early-stage increase in carbon and waterﬂuxes is observed
worldwide, rather than being limited to drylands. Using explainable
machine learning to analyze daily ΔGPP, we identify photosynthetic
capacity and radiation as the primary factors of positiveΔGPP, while water
availability dominates in regulating negative ΔGPP. These ﬁndings were
supported by global GPP and SIF datasets. Globally, we observed that
positive ΔGPP persists for 9, 13.5, and 17 days during dry-downs lasting
≥10, 15, and 20 days, respectively, before transitioning to negative values for
the remaining duration. This shift ultimately leads to negative totalΔGPP in
some regions. Furthermore, we found a lower SM threshold for

Soil dry-down identiﬁcation
Soil dry-down events are characterized by the continuous decline in SM over
time, typically occurring between rainfall events. After a moisture pulse, SM
increases and then gradually decreases until the next pulse. To identify these
events, we used time-series SM data from daily in-situ measurements. In this
study, soil dry-down events were included in the analysis if they exhibited at
least 10 consecutive days of SM decline
56–58. To explore the effects of varying
dry-down durations, we also set thresholds for dry-down lengths of at least
15 and 20 days.
We characterized soil dry-down events using three key metrics:
duration, magnitude, and frequency. Duration represents the number of
days a dry-down event last. Magnitude is de ﬁned as the change in SM
between the ﬁrst and last day of the dry-down period. Frequency refers to
the annual occurrence of dry-down events. To assess the relative
importance of these characteristics, we applied ridge regression to ana-
lyze the in ﬂuence of soil texture (sand fraction), vegetation status
(maximum leaf area index, LAI
max), and climatic variables, including the
aridity index (AI, de ﬁned as the ratio of precipitation to potential eva-
potranspiration), vapor pressure deﬁcit (VPD) during soil dry-down, and
mean annual precipitation (MAP).

post moisture pulse, we compared ﬂuxes during dry-down periods with
those from control periods in other years. Control periods were deﬁned as
the multi-year mean of the same calendar days (day of year, DOY) in other
years without dry-down events, during which SM did not decline for 10
consecutive days. We further compared SM during control periods

Drivers of GPP changes during post moisture pulse
To evaluate possible mechanisms driving GPP changes during post
moisture pulse, random forest (RF) models were used to quantify the effects
and relative importance of 10 factors such as vegetation characteristics,
climatic factors, and dry-down properties (Supplementary Table 2) on daily
ΔGPP. This design explicitly accounts for the fact that key environmental
conditions, such as vegetation status (e.g., LAI), can independently mod-
ulate GPP responses during post moisture pulse. Alongside the site-
measured VPD, RAD, Ta, and SM data, we obtained the site-scale LAI
max
from the literature26,59,60, and LAI for the rest of the sites was derived from the
mean annual maximum of MCD15A3H from 2010 to 2015. Further details
on predictive variables can be found in Supplementary Table 2. To minimize
multicollinearity among predictors, we applied the variance inﬂation factor
(VIF) analysis, excluding variables with VIF values greater than 4. The
relative importance of each predictor in explainingΔGPP was then assessed
using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), a game-theoretic approach
capable of quantifying both the individual contribution and the nonlinear
interactions of predictors
61,62. This analysis was conducted separately for
daily positive and negative ΔGPP at the global scale. Ultimately, two RF
models explained 58% and 63% of the spatial variation in positive and
negative ΔGPP during soil dry-down events.
