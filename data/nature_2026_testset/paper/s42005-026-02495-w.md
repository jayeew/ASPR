Boron-rich lithium borocarbides are promising candidates for phonon-mediated high-temperature
superconductors due to their metallic σ-bonding electrons. Here, we use the cluster expansion
method to identify energetically stable con ﬁgurations (colorings) of Li 2B3C and Li 3B4C2, which are
characterized by a distinctive pattern of alternating B-B and B-C zigzag chains. Surprisingly, the
optimal conﬁguration of Li
2B3C exhibits an extremely low superconducting transition temperature of
Tc < 0.03 K, which is attributed to the suppression of deformation potentials near the Fermi level
caused by the speci ﬁc electron ﬁlling of B-B zigzag chains. However, the σ-bonding electrons at the
Fermi level are highly sensitive to external strain or pressure. Speci ﬁcally, applying a −5%
compressive uniaxial strain can signi ﬁcantly enhance the electron-phonon coupling and the
Eliashberg spectral function, boosting up Tc to 37 K. This work not only presents a strategy for
achieving high critical temperatures in Li nBn+1Cn+1 compounds, but also provides valuable insights
into the complex interplay between electronic structure and superconducting interaction.
The allure of high-temperature superconductivity continues to fascinate the
scientiﬁc community worldwide. The met allization of strong chemical
bonding electrons is considered as a pivotal factor in the formation of high-
temperature superconducting pairings in copper oxides

hydrogen-rich materials under ultrahigh pressure3.
The search for new superconductorswith high transition temperature
(Tc) has attracted tremendous interest since the discovery of super-
conductivity. Inspired by the discovery of high- Tc superconductivity in
cuprates and MgB 2, the metallization of σ-bonding electrons has been
proposed as a universal guiding principle4 for discovering high-temperature
superconductors. This concept has been widely applied to numerous
phonon-mediated superconductors
3,5– 13.
A σ-bond is the strongest covalent bond formed by the direct overlap of
two atomic orbitals, each housing a single electron. In contrast, a σ-anti-
bonding state typically corresponds toa repulsive interaction in a diatomic
molecule. However, in solid materials,σ-antibonding states can also play a
crucial role by contributing to the attractive interaction that binds electrons
together, as highlighted in ref. 14. Electronic bands originating from
hybridizedσ-bonding orσ-antibonding orbitals are often situated far below
or above the Fermi level.
As discussed in refs. 4,14, there are a number of ways to metalize σ-
hybrized electrons. One way of inducing metallization involves the appli-
cation of external pressure, as demonstrated in the hydrogen-rich high-T
c
superconductor, SH3, under ultra-high pressure3. This external pressure,
combined with the chemical forces exerted on hydrogen atoms by neigh-
bouring atoms, can stabilize crystal structures. It can also alter the electron
ﬁlling factor, raising the Fermi level crossing the bands formed by the
antibonding-σ electrons. An alternative approach is through the exploita-
tion of the crystal ﬁeld effect, as exempli ﬁed by magnesium diboride
MgB
2
4,5,15,16.I nM g B2, the crystal structure consists of alternating layers of
magnesium atoms arranged in a triangular lattice and boron atoms in a
honeycomb lattice. The crystal ﬁeld effect induces a strong attraction
between the Mg
2+ ions and theπ electrons in the boron layers, lowering the
energy of the bands associated with the hybridized π orbitals. This inter-
action elevates the energy of theσ-hybridized boron orbitals, bringing them
into intersection with the Fermi level. A more general and straightforward
strategy is to manipulate the Fermilevel by doping. Copper-oxide high-Tc
superconductors are typical examples of this type.

interest in exploring other superconductors that exhibit metallicσ-bonding

Electronic structures
We calculated the electronic band structures of LinBn+1Cn+1 (n =2a n d3 )
with DFT method, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The results indicate a clear cor-
relation between the electronic structures and the speciﬁca t o m i ca r r a n g e -

bands near the Fermi level are predominantly derived from thesp

employs thepz-like Wannier functions (WFs) centered on B or C atoms and

Owing to the similar lattice structures and chemical stoichiometries,
the band structures of LinBn+1Cn+1 share some common features. First, all
σ-bonding bands exhibit a highly two-dimensional behavior, as a result of
their MgB 2-like lattice. Besides, due to carbon ’s lower electronegativity
compared to boron, the B-Cσ-bonding bands lie at a lower energy level than
their B-B counterparts. Consequently, as illustrated in Fig.2,o n l yt h eπ and
B-B σ-bonding bands intersect the Fermi level in all LinBn+1Cn+1.

(Gao et al.), the B-Bσ-bonding WFs andπ orbitals dominate the electronic

However, compared with earlier work, an unique characteristic
emerges in the electronic structures of our optimal coloring conﬁgurations,
as a result of their unique structural characteristics. As shown in Fig.1(a-b)
and detailed in Table S2, the B-B bridge bonds are shorter than those within
the zigzag chains. This results in larger energy splitting between the bonding
and antibonding orbitals across the bridge bonds, leading to lower on-site
energies of the B-B σ-bonding WFs in the bridge bonds than those in the
zigzag chains. Consequently, the B-B σ-bonding bands from the bridge
bonds (along with B-Cσ-bonding bands) are fully occupied, while the B-Bσ-
bonding bands from the zigzag chains, in addition to theπ bands, intersect
the Fermi level, as depicted in Figs. 2(a-b). Both compounds exhibit com-
parable doping levels, with approximately 1/2 hole perσ-bonding WF from
the B-B zigzag chains (setting aside self-doping fromπ bands). This simi-
larity is not coincidental; the structural characteristics of these compounds
ensure that all bridging σ-bonding WFs are fully occupied, thereby mini-
mizing the electronic energy. We believe that this phenomenon might be a
general feature across other Li

When the perturbation of a bond-stretching phonon mode is introduced,
always some bonds are elongated while other bonds are shortened.
Consequently, the elongated, weakened bonds lead to smaller energy
splitting between the corresponding bonding and antibonding bands,
while the shortened, strengthened bonds lead to larger energy splitting.
For example, in our case, the top of the bridge B-B σ-bonding bands is
lifted up while the top of zigzag chain B-B σ-bonding bands is lowered
down, because the B-B bonds at the bridge sites are elongated while the
B-B bonds in zigzag chains are shortened in the A

unaffected. Notably, the electronic nature of such scenario is that the
Fermi level is positioned near 3/4-ﬁlling in Li2B3C (ours), as we analyzed
in last section. Hence, small deformation potential and weak EPC might
be general across other Li nBn+1Cn+1 compounds.

only the presence ofσ-bonding character but also a signiﬁcant deformation
potential. Therefore, for the emer gence of phonon-mediated high- Tc

mation potentials, which are typical ly associated with strong covalent
bonding or antibonding states. This would help us understand and design
new high-temperature superconductors under ambient pressure, such as
boron-doped Q-carbon
50 and 2D metal-intercalated borocarbides51– 53.

signiﬁcant changes in the electronic structure. As illustrated in Figs.5(c-d),
t h eb r i d g eB - Bσ-bonding bands cross the Fermi level, giving rise to elliptical
cylindrical Fermi surfaces near theΓ point. Notably, the top of these bridge
B-B σ-bonding bands now are above the Fermi level, as highlighted by the
dashed purple circles in Fig.5(c). Compared with the dispersive part, these
states are more sensitive to bond-str etching vibrations. As a result, this
change in electronic structure generates a large deformation potential,
leading to a substantial boost in EPC.
The application of compressive strain primarily alters the bond angles
and slightly reduces the bond lengths along the b-axis in Li
2B3C (ours).
Concurrently, the lattice constantsa andc experience minor elongation. These
structural modiﬁcations lead to an increase in the hopping parameters from
the bridge sites to the zigzag chain sites, while the hopping parameters within
the zigzag chains decrease, as detailed in Table S3. For instance, the hopping
parameter between W4 and W2 changes from−1.36 eV to −1.46 eV. This
adjustment enhances the hybridization between the bridge B-Bσ-bonding
bands and the zigzag chain B-B σ-bonding bands. Additionally, the com-
pressive strain along theb-axis raises the on-site energies of theσ-bonding
WFs at the bridge sites for both B-B and B-C bonds, as detailed in Table S2.
Analyzing Eq. ( 2), it is important to emphasize the critical role of
DOS at the Fermi level in determining EPC
12,54,55. For instance, despite a
strong deformation potential, a low DOS can yield weak EPC, as seen in
boron-doped diamond
9. This phenomenon explains why the super-
conducting transition temperature ( Tc) remains low under −3% com-
pressive b-axis strain. Slightly below the −3% compressive b-axis strain,
the bridge B-B σ-bonding bands just intersect the Fermi level, triggering a
Lifshitz transition. However, such small Fermi surfaces contribute negli-
gibly to DOS at the Fermi level, leading to weak EPC in Li 2B3C (ours), as
shown in Fig. S7(c). As the strain is further increased, more hole are doped
in the bridge B-B σ-bonding WFs (Fig. S7(d-f)), enlarging the Fermi
surfaces (mainly comprised of bridge B-B σ-bonding WFs) and boosting
DOS, even though the deformation potential decreases. The interplay
between these two opposing factors results in the characteristic dome-
shaped strain-T
c relationship.
Conclusion
In this paper, we identify the optimal conﬁgurations of Li2B3Ca n dL i3B4C2
using the cluster expansion method, demonstrating that these conﬁgura-
tions are thermodynamically more stable than previously reported struc-
tures. In these optimized conﬁgurations, both B-B and B-C zigzag chains
emerge within each Bn+1Cn-1 plane, resulting in unique electronic structures
that differ qualitatively from those reported in earlier studies6,19– 21.N o t a b l y ,
in LinBn+1Cn+1 (ours) (n =2 ,3 ) ,o n l yt h eσ-bonding bands associated with
the B-B zigzag chains cross the Fermi level, exhibiting weak hybridization
with the σ-bonding bands in the B-C zigzag chains. Additionally, the σ-
bonding bands in the B-B zigzag chains are approximately 3/4- ﬁlled and
display signiﬁcant dispersion along their corresponding directions in reci-
procal space. In contrast, other σ-bonding bands remain fully occupied,
including the bridge B-B and all B-Cσ-bonding bands.
Our calculations of EPC and deformation potential for Li
3B4C2 (Gao
et al.) and Li2B3C (ours) reveal that both the EPC and deformation potential
are signiﬁcantly weaker in Li2B3C (ours) than in Li3B4C2 (Gao et al.). As a
result, the superconducting transition temperature of Li2B3C (ours) is less
than 0.03 K, dramatically lower than the predictedTc of 50 K for Li 3B4C2
(Gao et al.). Nonetheless, since the σ-bonding bands in Li2B3C (ours) are
already present above the Fermi level, there remains signiﬁcant potential to
transform Li2B3C (ours) into a phonon-mediated high-Tc superconductor
by applying a uniaxial strain. Our strain-dependent calculations show that
applying a −5% compressive strain along the b-axis in Li2B3C (ours) can
markedly alter the electronic band structure around the Fermi level and
signiﬁcantly enhance the deformation potential on the Fermi surface,
thereby raising T

potential can be measured by modern experimental techniques
nowadays
56– 59.
