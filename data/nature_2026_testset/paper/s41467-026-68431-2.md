Marine heatwaves (MHWs), characterized by multiple days of exceptionally
elevated sea surface temperature (SST), have profound marine ecological
impacts, but their effect on precipitation, particularly extreme rainfall over
coastal regions, remains unknown. Using multi-platform observational data
since 2000, here we show that SST gradients of MHW intensify surface wind
speeds and drive downwind surface wind convergence and upward motions
by enhancing vertical turbulentﬂux over the warm water. The induced
anomalies lead to substantially increased local precipitation with spatial scale

MHW. Furthermore, in global coastal regions, about 5%-25% of extreme rainfall
over land (>99% wet-day) occurs in the downwind direction of nearby MHWs.
Averaged land precipitation of the extreme rainfall events in the downwind
direction of a strong MHW increases by 20%-30%, or 4-8 mm/day, from the
amount without an inﬂuence from MHWs, exacerbating ﬂood-related fatal-
ities. Our ﬁnding identiﬁes an impact of MHWs on coastal extreme events
with important implications for affected communities, particularly given the
projected increase in MHW intensity and frequency under greenhouse
warming.
A marine heatwave (MHW) is a discrete multiple-day period of anom-
alously warm sea water exceeding a local temperature threshold at a
speciﬁc location, exerting profound ecological and socioeconomic
impacts
1– 6. The extreme temperatures could push marine ecosystems
beyond the limits of their resilience, altering primary productivity,
displacing ocean habitats, and causing pervasive and devastating
ecological damages, such as mass coral bleaching and harmful algal
blooms
3,7– 9. The impacts destroy life-sustaining ecosystems, increase
human-wildlife conﬂicts, and causeﬁshery disasters, leading to various
direct and indirect socioeconomic consequences as large as several
billions of US dollars
10– 14.
Previous studies found that MHWs can be driven by atmospheric
processes, including favorable conditions such as clear sky or low wind
speeds increasing local ocean heat uptake, and teleconnections from
remote sources15,16. In contrast, our understanding of the impact of
MHW on the atmosphere, especially precipitation, is limited, and the
underlying mechanism explanations are con ﬁned to several speci ﬁc
regions. One study examined a statistical relation between MHW and
precipitation over the ocean in the northern South China Sea, but with
no physical mechanism provided, and found that MHWs coexist with
positive oceanic rainfall anomalies in fall but negative anomalies dur-
ing spring and summer on a seasonal time scale
17. Two additional
studies examined MHWs as large-scale climate modes and investigated
their inﬂuence on the Indian summer monsoon, and showed that by
altering atmospheric circulation and moistureﬂux, MHWs may affect
the monsoon through teleconnections
18,19. Another recent study

Results
MHWs enhance precipitation globally
Precipitation usually results from upward motion of moist air, and
atmospheric motions at different scales are fundamentally driven by
the temperature gradient at the lower boundary. Oceanic large-scale
warm currents and mesoscale eddies, both exhibiting considerable
horizontal temperature gradient, can induce wind convergence and
upward motion, and trigger precipitation directly above
23– 28.M H W s
exhibit a localized warm core with sharp horizontal temperature gra-
dients (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), which induce upward motion and
thus precipitation. The local warm extreme of a MHW can be con-
sidered as its core. The sea surface temperature anomaly (SSTA) gra-
dient and radius of an individual MHW can be estimated from the
amplitude and horizontal scale of aﬁtted Gaussian function curve (see
‘Scale and intensity of MHW’in “Methods”). The global averaged SSTA
gradient of MHW is approximately 0.85 °C per 100 km, and the radius
of a MHW typically ranges from 100 to 300 km (Supplementary
Fig. 1c, d).
Vertical mixing and pressure adjustment are two primary
mechanisms whereby atmospheric adjustments and precipitation
respond to SSTA gradients
25,27– 32. For a monopole warm anomaly of sea
water, the response of vertical mixing indicates an intensiﬁed vertical
turbulent ﬂux, resulting in increased sea-surface wind speed over the
warm water and convergence in the downwind direction. The response
of pressure refers to a decrease in near-surface air density and lower
sea level pressure, leading to convergence directly over the warm
water. Thus, these two mechanisms are characterized by different
patterns of convergence and upward motion of air. Previous studies
have reported that the vertical mixing is the preferred mechanism for
processes on a scale of 10
2 km, suggesting a downwind precipitation
enhancement of local warm SST25,26,33.

Mechanism of MHW-enhanced precipitation
The downwind precipitation enhancement in the composite analysis is
consistent with the theoretical expectation of the vertical mixing
mechanism. The mechanism relates to adecrease in the vertical stability
of the atmosphere as air moves a bove warm water. The decreased
stability leads to an intensi ﬁcation of turbulent motion in the atmo-
spheric boundary layer, thus increasing the boundary layer height and
downward momentum transport. Subsequently, near-surface wind is
intensiﬁed over the MHW, inducing an upwind divergence and a
downwind convergence near the sea surface (Fig.2a). For a monopole
warm SSTA distribution, a monopole structure of wind speed accel-
eration and the corresponding dipole structure of wind divergence/
convergence ﬁeld are the key features of the vertical mixing
mechanism
25,34, different from the pressure adjustment mechanism27,33.
The downwind convergence induces an upward motion and gathers the
near-surface moist air advected over the warm water, increasing the
downwind total column water vapor. The combined effect of upward
motion and water vapor intensi ﬁcation leads to an enhancement of
precipitation in the downwind direction of the MHW (Fig.2a).
An additional composite analysis of atmospheric imprints of
MHW ﬁnds a good agreement with the vertical mixing mechanism. The
horizontal distributions of boundary layer height, surface wind, water
vapor and the three-dimensional ﬁeld of vertical air velocity around
each individual MHW are provided by global observational and rea-
nalysis datasets, and then projected into the rotated MHW-centered
coordinate for the composite analysis (see ‘Atmospheric imprints of
MHWs’ in “Methods”). An observed increase in boundary layer height
directly above the core of the corresponding MHW suggests an
enhancement of downward turbulent momentum ﬂux (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 6a) and a high-speed monopole of surface wind emerges at the
same place (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 6b). The dipole divergence/
convergence wind ﬁeld is featured with stronger downwind con-
vergence and weaker upwind divergence (Supplementary Fig. 6c). The
resulted upward air motion penetrates to the upper troposphere (200
hPa) in the downwind direction of MHW, and a weaker downward
counterpart is located in the upwind direction (Fig. 2b). The
enhancement of the total column water vapor is also observed in the
downwind direction of the MHW ’sc o r e( F i g . 2c, Supplementary
Fig. 6d). The above results have been validated using station-based
data (Supplementary Fig. 7). Considering that low cloud cover and
wind speed are favorable conditions for MHWs, the resulting
enhancement of local precipitation and wind speed (Figs.1b, 2c) stems
from SST perturbations induced by the MHW. Although the timescales
of atmospheric response of vertical mixing and pressure adjustment
are not necessarily different
26,28,30, additional analysis indicates that the
contribution of the pressure adjustment mechanism is statistically
insigniﬁcant (see ‘Quantiﬁcation of pressure adjustment mechanism’
in “Methods”). Thus, the vertical mixing mechanism is a key process
whereby a MHW increases the downwind precipitation.
MHWs exacerbate extreme rainfall over land
A substantial part of high-impact MHWs occur near the densely
populated coastal areas
2,21, which could potentially in ﬂuence the
nearby precipitation over land. Composite analysis with only land
precipitation data ﬁnds an enhancement of precipitation in the
downwind direction of near-shore MHW (Supplementary Fig. 8a).
Here, the coastal region within twice the radius of a MHW, speciﬁcally
the downwind half, is regarded as being under the inﬂuence of MHW.
We ﬁnd that 5%– 25% of coastal extreme rainfall is within the inﬂuence
range of nearby MHW (Fig. 3a) (see ‘Analysis of inﬂuence of MHW on
extreme rainfall’in “Methods”). Further analysis demonstrates that the
horizontal distribution of the extreme rainfall precipitation over land
shares a similar pattern in the rotated MHW-centered coordinate,
which exhibits a substantial increase in the downwind direction of a
MHW. Compared to conditions without MHWs, the averaged pre-
cipitation of an extreme rainfall event increases by ~6 mm/day or ~25%
in the downwind direction of a MHW based on both remote sensing
and rain gauge data (Fig. 3b, d).
Economic and human losses in the extreme rainfall events increase
with precipitation rate, commonly through natural disasters such as
ﬂoods within the in ﬂuenced area of extreme rainfall

the downwind direction of MHWs (see‘Analysis of inﬂuence of MHW on
extreme rainfall’ in “Methods”). As the SSTA gradient of a MHW grows,
the average precipitation intensity of extreme rainfall events increases
substantially. MHWs with a substantial SSTA gradient could lead to a
4– 8 mm/day increase in precipitation intensity, increasing by 20%– 30%
compared to conditions without MHWs (Fig. 3c, e). The averaged
downwind extreme rainfall precipitation reaches its maximum two days
after a MHW (Supplementary Fig. 8c), indicating a causal effect of MHW
on the enhancement of precipitation within extreme rainfall events.
Thus, MHWs exacerbate the nearby extreme rainfall over land.
The increase in precipitation in extreme rainfall events is seen
when different datasets and methods are used (see ‘Robustness test
using different datasets and methods’ in “Methods” and Supplemen-
tary Fig. 9). Since 10%– 30% ﬂood events are located in the downwind
direction of a nearby MHW (Supplementary Fig. 10a), such an inten-
siﬁcation of precipitation is expected to exacerbate the damages and
losses. Consistently, a 30% increase in the fatalities caused by ﬂoods
located in the downwind direction of a MHW is observed, comparing
with ﬂoods free of a MHW in ﬂuence (Supplementary Fig. 10b),
although other factors could in ﬂuence both the MHWs and ﬂood-
related fatalities.
Discussion
Our ﬁnding that extreme rainfall over land is exacerbated by the sea
surface temperature gradient of a MHW is supported by a vertical
mixing mechanism, in which the warm water increases the vertical
turbulent momentum ﬂux and surface wind speed just above the
MHW, inducing surface wind convergence and upward motion of
moist air in the downwind direction of the MHW. In association, during
an extreme rainfall event over land with a concurrent nearby MHW, the
averaged downwind land precipitation is enhanced to 4 – 8m m / d a y ,
which is 20%– 3 0 %h i g h e ri nc o m p a r i s o nw i t ht h a ti ns i t u a t i o n sw i t h o u t
a nearby MHW. Consistently, the number of fatalities is about 30%
higher per event during the ﬂood events that occur in the downwind
direction of MHWs than during ﬂoods without the inﬂuence of MHW.
MHWs have occurred frequently since the early twentieth cen-
tury, and are projected to increase in duration, frequency and intensity
under greenhouse warming, especially in the subtropical and mid-
latitude regions
37– 40. The oceanic mesoscale eddies are also abundant
within these regions41, and warm core eddies are considered as one of
the key drivers of MHWs 15,42– 44. Approximate 24% of the local SST
extreme points of MHW are located within a radius of a mesoscale
eddy (see ‘Robustness test using different datasets and methods ’ in
“Methods”). While precipitation patterns are similar for MHWs with or
without mesoscale eddies (Supplementary Fig. 11), the presence of
warm core eddies, along with favorable atmospheric conditions, pro-
motes MHW occurrence, especially in eddy-rich regions. Long-
duration MHWs exhibit a stronger rainfall response than the short-
duration ones (Supplementary Fig. 12), and precipitation enhancement

SSTA distribution within the moving window is projected into a polar
coordinate centered at the SSTA local extreme, and the local extreme
point is considered as the core of the corresponding MHW. Third, a
Gaussian function is used to ﬁt the SSTA in relation to the radial dis-
tance to MHW’s core (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The radius of MHW is
given by the horizontal scale of theﬁtted Gaussian function, deﬁned as
twice the standard deviation of the Gaussian function. The SSTA gra-
dient used to represent the intensity of MHW is obtained by dividing
the amplitude of the ﬁtted Gaussian function by the radius of MHW.
The composited SSTA pattern in the rotated MHW-centered

proﬁles, indicating that the averaged SSTA structure of MHW can be
characterized by a Gaussian function.

Quantiﬁcation of pressure adjustment mechanism
There is no detectable sea level pressure signal response to MHW in
the composited sea level pressure anomaly (SLPA) in Supplementary
Fig. 15. The composited total and random-test SLPA are nearly identical
to each other, both in the northern and southern hemispheres. Their
residuals are one order of magnitude smaller and are not statistically
signiﬁcant ( p > 0.01), which suggests that the composited sea level
pressure anomaly associated with MHW is weak and negligible. To
further quantify the contribution of the pressure adjustment
mechanism, we computed the correlation coef ﬁcients between the
across-wind sea surface temperature Laplacian and across-wind
divergence

remains statistically insigniﬁcant (correlation coefﬁcients <0.01).
Analysis of inﬂuence of MHW on extreme rainfall
Employing two decades of global gridded precipitation data, extreme
rainfall over land is identiﬁed as daily precipitation intensity exceeding
the 99th percentile threshold of wet days ( ≥1 mm/day), calculated
locally at each land grid point. An extreme rainfall is considered under
the inﬂuence of a MHW if it is located in the downwind direction of the
corresponding MHW and within twice the radius range to the MHW’s
core. Thus, the probability of extreme rainfall affected by MHW is
computed by dividing the number of MHW-affected extreme rainfall
days over the total number of extreme rainfall days in each land
grid (Fig. 3a).
For the MHW-affected extreme rainfall events, the precipitation
intensities are averaged in relation to the SSTA gradient of the cor-
responding MHW (red line in Fig. 3c), which establishes a relation
between the intensity of MHW and the resulting precipitation
enhancement in extreme rainfall events. A random composite test
with the same standard is also performed, and their downwind
average precipitation intensity sets the baseline represented by the
black dashed line in Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 9. The precipitation
anomaly of MHW-affected extreme rainfall (Fig. 3b) is composited
using the same method as Fig. 1b, except that only MHW coinciding
with extreme rainfall are selected. To examine the causality between
marine heatwaves and extreme rainfall over land, a lead-lag
correlation analysis similar to Fig. 1d was performed (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 8c).
To ensure the robustness of our results against the de ﬁnition of
extreme rainfall, two additional thresholds are tested: the 99.5th per-
centile of daily rainfall on wet days ( ≥1 mm/day) and the 99.9th per-
centile of daily rainfall over all days. The composited precipitation
anomalies of extreme rainfall events using different thresholds show
consistent patterns and comparable amplitudes (Supplementary
Fig. 16), conﬁrming the reliability of our results.
