Rainfall sustains multiyear La Niña
Feng Tian 1,2 , Rong-Hua Zhang 2,3 ,C h u a n y uL i u1,2 & Cong Guan4
Historical observations and climate models indicate that multiyear La Niña
events are growing more frequent. These prolonged events cumulatively alter
tropical Paciﬁc rainfall, which in turn affects regional mixed-layer salinity
(MLS). However, how cumulative rainfall anomalies modulate multiyear La
Niña through salinity effects remains unclear. Here, using observations and
model experiments, we show that the positive MLS anomalies in the
western–central equatorial Paciﬁc—driven initially by oceanic dynamical pro-
cesses and later by rainfall deﬁcits during multiyear La Niña—reinforce these
prolonged La Niña events. Quantitatively, the rainfall reduction enhances La
Niña amplitude by 14% in the ﬁrst year and 32% in the second year. MLS
anomalies in the western–central equatorial Paciﬁc initially trigger equatorial
Kelvin wave adjustments, causing rapid surface cooling in the eastern equa-
torial Paciﬁc. Subsequent slow ocean circulation responses lead to basin-wide
equatorial Paciﬁc cooling. The superposition of rapid and slow oceanic
responses creates cumulative positivefeedback from rainfall on the second-
year La Niña. These ﬁndings identify rainfall–salinity feedbacks as a key
mechanism sustaining multiyear La Niña events, with implications for ENSO
prediction and hydrological cycle.
Our understanding of the diversity of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation
(ENSO) has improved considerably over the past four decades 1–4,a s
evidenced by the various types of El Niño events, the amplitude
asymmetry between El Niño and La Niña, and the tendency for La Niña
to persist for more than 2 years, known as multiyear La Niña events
5–9.
Recent studies have indicated a marked increase in the occurrence
frequency of multiyear La Niña events during the historical period
10,
and climate models project a further signiﬁcant rise in their frequency
throughout the 21st century11. Consequently, the importance of mul-
tiyear La Niña events to global weather and climate is garnering
increased attention from the scientiﬁcc o m m u n i t y

Recent studies on the formation and development of multiyear La
Niña events have predomin antly focused on ocean –atmosphere
interactions13–18. A widely accepted hypothesis is that multiyear La Niña
events are often preceded by a strong El Niño 19. The surface wind
anomalies associated with a strong El Niño can lead to a signi ﬁcant
discharge of heat content in the equatorial ocean, requiring the tro-
pical Paciﬁc coupled system to take more than a year to return to a
neutral state. Studies have also emphasized the impacts of the mid-
latitude Paciﬁc Ocean and other basins on multiyear La Niña events,
particularly the North Paciﬁc
20,21. In addition, the persistence of mul-
tiyear La Niña events can be attributed to a delayed heat content
recharge driven by anomalous Ekman transport, nonlinear thermo-
cline feedbacks, and interactions with the Indian and Atlantic
Oceans

equatorial ocean undergoes sustained cooling for over 2 years —a
timescale that implies slow ocean dynamical processes (e.g., vertical
mixing, wind-driven large-scale circulation, and rainfall-induced sali-
nity changes) may play a pivotal role.

rainfall play a critical and often overlooked role. Rainfall fundamentally

timescales, from the mean state to interannual variability

demonstrating that multiyear La Niña events exhibit a self-amplifying
nature through a cumulative rainfall–salinity feedback mechanism.
Results
Asynchronous MLS responses to rainfall and ocean dynamics
Interannual anomalies of rainfall and MLS are closely tied to ENSO
evolution. In the Niño4 region (5°S –5°N, 160°E–150°W), interannual
variations in MLS and rainfall exhibit strongly synchronized ﬂuctua-
tions, with a Pearson correlation coefﬁcient of −0.68 (statistically sig-
niﬁcant at the 95% con ﬁdence level, two-tailed t-test). This robust
negative correlation underscores a strong linkage between rainfall
deﬁcits and positive MLS anomalies in the western-central Paciﬁc. To
characterize the MLS-rainfall relationship during multiyear La Niña
events, we selected six such events between 1980 and 2022 for com-
posite analysis (Fig. 1a). Two of these events (1998 –2000 and
2020–2022) persisted into a third year while maintaining a cooling
state. Throughout these events, persistent negative rainfall anomalies
coincided with positive MLS anomalies in the western-central Paciﬁc,
though their amplitudes varied—likely reﬂecting differences in event
intensity. A pronounced negative rainfall anomaly was observed near
the dateline, associated with persistent surface cooling in the western-
central equatorial Paci ﬁc( F i g . 1b, c). Climatologically, low-salinity
waters (e.g., <34.8 psu) dominate the western-central equatorial Paci-
ﬁc, sustained by abundant rainfall. During multiyear La Niña, the iso-
haline shifts westward by ~10°, indicating a westward expansion of
higher-salinity water (Supplementary Fig. 1). Together with concurrent
rainfall deﬁcits, this zonal migration of isohalines suggests that zonal
advection contributes to MLS variability during certain phases of
multiyear La Niña. Similar features are reproduced across multiple
reanalysis and observational datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1).
Nevertheless, the speciﬁc roles of rainfall versus oceanic dyna-
mical processes during these events remain unclear. To address this,
we employed an MLS budget analysis using ORAS5 data to quantify the
contributions of different processes. We focused on three key periods
for the Niño4 region: MAMJ(0), ND(0)JF(1), and ASON(1). These
represent, respectively, a rapid MLS increase during the ﬁrst-year La
Niña, the MLS peak within that year, and a second MLS peak late in the
second-year La Niña (Supplementary Fig. 2). Here, “(0)” denotes the
ﬁrst year and “(1)” the second year of a multiyear La Niña event. The
MLS budget analysis in the Niño4 region reveals that the dominant
processes contributing to salinity anomalies vary by phase: (a) During
MAMJ(0), as the ﬁrst-year La Niña develops, surface waters in the
Niño4 region undergo saliniﬁcation primarily driven by ocean dyna-
mical processes, notably zonal advection and vertical mixing asso-
ciated with mixed layer deepening (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the
contribution from freshwaterﬂux (E-P) is minor. (b) During ND(0)JF(1),
at the ﬁrst MLS peak, a distinct rainfall reduction emerges. This
reduction in rainfall results in a signiﬁcant positive contribution from
freshwater ﬂux to MLS anomalies. Conversely, horizontal advection
counteracts this increase, driving a temporary decrease in MLS. (c)
During ASON (1), at the second MLS peak, positive MLS anomalies are
again primarily attributable to reduced rainfall.

Effects of rainfall on ocean stratiﬁcation during multiyear
La Niña
Given the signi ﬁcant impact of rainfall on MLS during multiyear La
Niña events, we next investigate how these rainfall-induced MLS
anomalies, in turn, affect SST by modulating the upper-ocean struc-
ture. We analyze the in ﬂuence of MLS changes on upper-ocean stra-
tiﬁcation and vertical mixing, and further explore the salinity-mediated
pathway through which rainfall affects SST, using GC-OGCM experi-
ments. Figure4b, c show composite potential temperature and salinity
anomalies during multiyear La Niña events, revealing negative tem-
perature and positive salinity anomalies in the western–central equa-
torial Paciﬁc. These anomalies collectively result in positive density
anomalies beginning in the summer of the ﬁrst-year La Niña (Fig. 4a).
The composite shows that negative anomalies in the squared buoy-
ancy frequency (N²) develop below the mixed layer starting in Sep-
tember(0) and persist until the end of the event (Fig.4d). This indicates
a weakening of stratiﬁcation in the western Paciﬁc upper ocean due to
a reduced vertical density gradient, which would enhance vertical
mixing and entrainment at the mixed layer base
41.
Positive salinity anomalies play a crucial role in enhancing
instability near the mixed layer base. When the effect of salinity is
excluded, the composite N² anomaly shows a pattern similar to the full
ﬁeld, indicating that temperature is the primary driver of N² evolution
(Fig. 4e). Nevertheless, the in ﬂuence of salinity on N² becomes sub-
stantial near the base of the mixed layer. This occurs because salinity-
induced density anomalies are concentrated within the mixed layer
(Fig. 4c), thereby reducing the vertical density gradient (Fig.4f). Given
the key role of salinity in determ ining density at this depth, the
resultant reduction in strati ﬁcation enhances vertical mixing and
entrainment, ultimately contributing to surface cooling.

Discussion
The impact of rainfall-induced salinity changes on ENSO has been
extensively documented
42,44,49. However, prior research has pre-
dominantly focused on their local and transient effects, which are
mediated through salinity-induced changes in upper-ocean
stratiﬁcation
50,51. In this study, we highlight a more prolonged inﬂuence:
when positive salinity anomalies persist for over 2 years during multiyear
La Niña events, they engage large-scale ocean circulation processes that
assume an increasingly signiﬁcant role in sustaining the event (Fig.7).
It is important to note that our ﬁndings, while robust within our
experimental framework (Supplementary Tables 2, 3), rely on a single
OGCM which exhibits certain biases in simulating ENSO amplitude
(Supplementary Fig. 8). In addition, while numerous studies using
diverse models have investigated the effects of rainfall on the tropical
Paciﬁc, how rainfall-induced salinity changes modulate the predict-
ability of multiyear La Niña requires further veriﬁcation. Future work
should employ a broader suite of coupled ocean–atmosphere models
to improve predictability of these prolonged events
52.
As multiyear La Niña events attract increasing scientiﬁc attention53,54,
it is essential to investigate how the underlying oceanic dynamics may
evolve under future warming scenarios. Considerable debate persists
regarding the dominant ocean –atmosphere coupling mechanisms
responsible for these successive events

GC-OGCM
The GC-OGCM is a reduced-gravity, primitive equation, sigma-
coordinate model that encompasses the entire tropical Paciﬁc( 3 0 ° S -
30°N, 120°E-76°W). This OGCM contains a mixed layer at the surface and
19 vertical layers beneath it according to sigma coordinates. A key
characteristic of the model is that the mixed layer depth is prog-
nostically calculated by a bulk mixed layer model developed by ref.70.
Additionally, the zonal resolution of the ocean model varies, with a
resolution of 1° in the central basin, reﬁning to 0.4° near the western and
eastern boundaries. The meridional resolution is set to 1/3° near the
equator and gradually increases to 2° poleward of 20° latitude. A sponge
layer is implemented at the northern and southern boundaries from 20°
to 30°. In this sponge layer, the temperature and salinity are gradually
relaxed back to observed climatologicalﬁelds. In the OGCM, the heat
ﬂux is explicitly calculated via an atmospheric mixed layer model, where
only the wind speed, cloud cover, and solar radiation are prescribed
71,72,
consequently, latent and sensible heat ﬂuxes are interactively deter-
mined by the surface ocean conditions in the OGCM. In the GC-OGCM,
freshwaterﬂux entering the ocean inﬂuences oceanic processes through
two primary pathways:ﬁrst, as a virtual salt ﬂux; and second, by mod-
ifying the vertical velocity at the top layer via the natural boundary
condition, which subsequently modulates the exchange of heat and
momentum
26,73. The model is initialized by the World Ocean Atlas (WOA)
ﬁelds and then spin-up for 30 years forced by the NCEP/NCAR clima-
tologicalﬁelds (i.e., wind stress, radiation, and freshwaterﬂux).

domain encompasses the entire tropical Paci ﬁc and North Paci ﬁc
regions (40°S–65°N, 110°E–70°W), with a horizontal resolution of 1/
3° × 1/3° to adequately resolve equatorial currents in the equatorial
region, and it features 50-s-coordinate levels in the vertical direction

Experiment I: Isolating the contributions of rainfall to MLS
In the control simulation (rain inter), rainfall is prescribed as an inter-
annually varyingﬁeld. Both the GC-OGCM and ROMS are forced from
1979 to 2022 with daily NCEP/NCAR atmospheric data, from which
surface ﬂuxes are calculated online using bulk formulae. In the sensi-
tivity experiment (rain
clim), rainfall is prescribed as its climatological
mean for the period 1979–2022. The differences between the raininter
and rain clim experiments represent the oceanic response to inter-
annually varying rainfall.
Given that the contribution of heat ﬂux to MLS variability is rela-
tively small over short timescales and acts primarily through its
modulation of buoyancy ﬂux and density (which subsequently in ﬂu-
ence mixing and circulation), the direct driver of MLS variability in the
rain
clim experiment is ocean dynamical processes induced by wind
forcing. Therefore, we did not explicitly distinguish the contributions
of heat and momentum ﬂuxes in this experimental framework.
Experiment II: Impacts of interannual rainfall anomalies on
multiyear La Niña
To examine the impacts of rainfall-induced salinity changes on the
ocean physical state, the GC-OGCM was forced with composite surface
winds from NCEP/NCAR during multiyear La Niña events over a two-year
period. A series of experiments were conducted as follows (Table S3):
In the climatological rainfall experiments, the observed monthly
climatological rainfall (1980–2022) was prescribed to inﬂuence upper-
ocean vertical velocity and salinity over the 2-year integration. In the
ﬁrst set of sensitivity experiments, the composite interannual rainfall
anomalies during multiyear La Niña events were doubled and super-
imposed onto the climatological rainfallﬁeld (hereafter referred to as
the Rainfall X2 experiment). The difference between the Rainfall X2
and climatological rainfall experiments characterizes the effect of
ampliﬁed rainfall anomalies on ocean salinity and temperature. The
doubling of rainfall anomalies was necessary because the GC-OGCM,
due to its speci ﬁcc o nﬁguration, simulates weaker interannual MLS
variability than observed. In this setup, rainfall inﬂuences both mixed-
layer salinity and velocity (via the natural boundary condition)
26,74;
thus, the experiment conﬁrms the effect of rainfall on both MLS and
SST. A parallel experiment using the original, undoubled composite
rainfall anomalies (Rainfall X1) was also conducted to test sensitivity.
In the second set of sensitivity experiments, a constant rainfall
anomaly of–9m md a y⁻¹w a sa r t iﬁcially imposed on the climatological
rainfall ﬁeld in the western-central equatorial Paci ﬁc( 1 4 0 ° E–180°,
5°S–5°N) from June(0) to December(1) of the multiyear La Niña event
(referred to as the constant rainfall experiment). In this conﬁguration,
the rainfall anomaly was designed to in ﬂuence ocean salinity exclu-
sively, without modifying the initial vertical velocity (i.e., the natural
boundary condition). This was achieved by disabling the impact of
rainfall anomalies on vertical velocity within the model code. The
region and magnitude of the rainfall anomaly were chosen to closely
reproduce the observed MLS anomalies. The results show that the
simulated salinity response is consistent with observations, though
some differences remain—as analyzed in the Results section.
