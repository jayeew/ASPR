Despite proven oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) efﬁcacy, adherence
barriers among men who have sex with men (MSM) persist. Economic viability
of digital adherence tools in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)
requires validation. We assessed the 6-month within-trial cost-effectiveness of
a digital intervention for real-time enhancement of oral PrEP adherence
(DIRECT-PrEP) in China, from health system, multipayer, and societal per-
spectives. Costs were derived from hospital records, and effectiveness was
evaluated using quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) and PrEP-adherence days
(PADs). Among 442 participants (247 intervention, 195 standard care), incre-
mental costs per QALY gained for the smart pillbox intervention were
$19,575.93 (health system), $19,656.66 (multipayer), and $19,844.42 (societal)
— all below the cost-effectiveness threshold of three times per capita gross
domestic product ($31,200). Costs per PAD gained were $30.46–$30.86,
achieving >95% probability of cost-effectiveness at willingness-to-pay thresh-
olds of $47.50–$48.50 per PAD. Notably, the intervention demonstrated
greater cost-effectiveness perQALY among participants stratiﬁed as more
socially vulnerable ($16,259.77–$16,490.41) compared with those classiﬁed as
less socially vulnerable ($23,514.83–$23,834.38). These results establish the
smart-pillbox digital intervention as acost-effective strategy for oral PrEP
adherence within primary healthcare settings in LMICs, particularly when
prioritizing more socially vulnerable populations, though long-term scalability
requires further validation.

This study addresses two critical research gaps in digital health
interventions for LMICs. First, it provides the economic evaluation of
electronic medication monitors for oral PrEP adherence using LMIC-
speciﬁc cost metrics, including cost per adherence-unit gained and
incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs). Second, it adopts an
equity-focused lens to assess the affordability of electronic medication
monitors among more socially vulnerable and less socially vulnerable
MSM by examining device durability and maintenance within LMIC
resource constraints, thereby aligning with priorities for sustainable,
device-led innovations.
Ultimately, by demonstrating the superiority of electronic medi-
cation monitors in cost per adherence-unit metrics and scalability
within LMIC health systems, this study aims to inform global oral PrEP
rollout strategies— advancing health equity through frugal, context-
adapted medical devices.
Results
Participant characteristics
Figure 1 presented the overall study design. Among the 516 partici-
pants who completed the baseline assessment, 247 were randomized
to the smart pillbox intervention group and 195 to the control group
(Supplementary Fig. S1). Baseline health utilities were comparable
between groups, and no signi ﬁcant between-group differences in
direct healthcare utilization costs were observed at baseline (Supple-
mentary Table S1).
Cost components
Table 1 summarized the 6-month cost and health outcome pro ﬁles.
From the health system, multipayer, and societal perspectives,
the mean (standard deviation [SD]) cumulative total costs in the
intervention group were $1826.77 (434.88), $1846.00 (434.71),
and $1950.76 (434.82), respectively. The corresponding values
for the control group were $1373.68 (585.34), $1390.19 (585.21),
and $1488.52 (585.23). Healthcare utilization costs represented
the predominant cost component for both groups (intervention:
78.37%; control: 78.69%; Supplementary Fig. S2), while intervention-
related costs accounted for 8.08% of total intervention
expenditures. Unadjusted intergroup cost differences were detailed
in Table 2.
Direct costs: testing and intervention
Costs related to testing averaged $140.27 per participant. The mean
intervention cost was $157.66 per participant (Table1), of which $28.99
(18.39%) was allocated to the smart pillbox. Personnel expenditures
constituted the majority of intervention costs at $121.32 per partici-
pant (76.95%). Costs associated with questionnaire administration and
follow-up support were $2.88 per participant, representing 1.83% of
total intervention expenditures (Supplementary Fig. S2).

the intervention group ($1528.85 [434.89]) compared with the control

Discussion
This is an economic evaluation conducted alongside a randomized
controlled trial assessing the smart pillbox intervention for improving
oral PrEP adherence among MSM in LMICs. Conducted as the largest
trial in this domain to date, our multicenter evaluation demonstrated
that a 6-month, medication-tailored smart pillbox intervention yielded
favorable cost-effectiveness outcomes. The intervention consistently
showed ICERs below three times China’s per capita GDP across health
system, multipayer, and societal perspectives. Notably, the smart
pillbox was more cost-effective and provided greater health promo-
tion value in the more socially vulnerable group than in the less socially
vulnerable group. Because the less socially vulnerable group derived
no signi ﬁcant bene ﬁt, no widening of health disparities occurred
across SDH strata. Instead, this digital intervention served as an equi-
table resource to mitigate inequalities. These ﬁndings support the
implementation of the smart pillbox as a cost-effective adherence
strategy for oral PrEP among MSM, while underscoring the need for
contextual adaptation in diverse global settings.
We demonstrate that the smart pillbox is a cost-effective and
equity-promoting intervention in low-resource settings. Our analysis
demonstrates superior cost-effectiveness in the more socially vulner-
able group, with ICERs 31% lower than in less socially vulnerable group,
revealing its potential as an equity-promoting technology. In regions
like Sub-Saharan Africa, about 50% of rural populations lack stable
internet access
26. Traditional SMS-based PrEP reminders often fail low-
literacy groups due to text comprehension barriers and incur recur-
rent costs from mobile network dependencies. The MPYA trial in
Kenya, for instance, showed reduced ef ﬁcacy among women with
lower education
17. Conversely, smart pillbox bypasses these con-
straints through ofﬂine monitoring (e.g., door-open sensors) and SDH-
responsive design (e.g., visual/audio alerts for low-literacy users). This
reduces marginal costs by shifting expenditure from recurrent con-
nectivity fees to one-time hardware procurement. Policymakers
should pair subsidies stratiﬁed by level of social vulnerability with task-
shifted maintenance programs training community health workers
(CHWs) in basic repairs, as seen in rural South Africa’sc o m m u n i t y - l e d
models that enhanced device use

In our study, cost-effectiveness differed across SDH strata,
underscoring the need to consider the mechanisms driving these
disparities. As a key sexual minority group, MSM experience heigh-
tened vulnerability due to SDH factors that encompass both behavioral
patterns and structural determinants. These factors jointly shape PrEP
uptake, persistence, and the feasibility of digital health tools. Evidence
shows that high-risk behavioral pro ﬁles are strongly associated with
elevated HIV acquisition among MSM
9, while socioeconomic con-
straints such as unstable housing and limited income signi ﬁcantly
undermine PrEP adherence in LMIC settings 12. Psychosocial factors,
particularly stigma and discrimination, further exacerbate adherence

10. Illustratively, a

$8.29 per participant, failed to reach daily-wage workers who feared
losing income during clinic waits, highlighting how unaddressed
socioeconomic barriers erode nominal ef ﬁciency gains. Against this
backdrop, smart pillboxes enhance adherence by automating data
capture during routine use, aligning with ﬁndings that user-friendly
digital devices can improve uptake among vulnerable groups in
resource-limited settings
46. Collectively, these ﬁndings suggest that
stratiﬁed cost-effectiveness reﬂects broader contextual burdens rather
than intervention ef ﬁcacy alone. In our trial, the greater cost-
effectiveness among socially vulnerable groups likely resulted from
the smart pillbox reducing barriers such as forgetfulness in unstable
living environments and privacy concerns linked to stigma, which are
often insufﬁciently addressed in conventional care.
This study presents the economic evaluation of a smart pillbox in
LMICs. Methodological innovations include real-world SDH integra-
tion (simultaneous modeling of sexual behavioral, economic, educa-
tional, and marital determinants — beyond common dichotomized
approaches) and holistic cost accounting (including participant-
incurred expenses such as travel and lost wages, typically excluded
in high-income country trials). The smart pillbox intervention enables
the collection of objective passive monitoring data, reducing bias
associated with self-reported measures. Additionally, it can operate
ofﬂine, providing a distinct advantage for implementation in low-
resource settings with limited internet or smartphone access. Smart
pillboxes enhance ef ﬁciency and precision — critical amid unprece-
dented funding cuts to HIV services. They facilitate early responses,
optimize resource allocation, and empower communities to address
daily challenges through actionable, equitable solutions.
However, six limitations warrant caution. First, the 6-month fol-
low-up likely underestimates long-term costs and outcomes. Although
our evaluation demonstrates robust short-term cost-effectiveness, it
does not capture maintenance-phase impacts. Many digital interven-
tion studies rely on static models that may overestimate cost-
effectiveness by ignoring long-term adherence and system adapta-
tions. In contrast, dynamic models suggest sustained value, but further
research is needed to incorporate full cost components, subgroup
heterogeneity, and the entire technology lifecycle into long-term
assessments
47. Second, our health economics evaluation of oral PrEP
relied on 2020 Truvada pricing, which may not re ﬂect subsequent
price reductions. Moreover, the introduction of domestic generic oral
PrEP formulations has substantially lowered costs, potentially enhan-
cing the cost-effectiveness of digital PrEP interventions among popu-
lations vulnerable to SDH. Third, although our sensitivity analyses
examined multiple statistical methods and cost perspectives, they
primarily varied patient-level costs and therefore produced only
marginal changes in ICER estimates. This approach did not adequately
account for system-level uncertainties— such as the balance of ﬁxed
and variable program costs, potential scale effects in digital interven-
tions, and the health system’s ability to maintain long-term adherence
gains. These structural drivers may have a much larger impact on cost-
effectiveness and thus limit the generalizability and policy relevance of
our ﬁndings. Future studies should incorporate scenario analyses and
system-level modeling to identify conditions under which more sub-
stantive economic differences may emerge. Fourth, system-side costs
were reported as point estimates (e.g., per-participant software
development costs) without clarifying ﬁxed versus variable cost pro-
portions, limiting scalability assessment in larger populations. Fifth,
while our societal perspective cost analysis incorporated compre-
hensive indirect costs, we acknowledge the inherent limitations in fully
quantifying intangible expenditures such as informal caregiving time,
household productivity losses, and potential accommodation expen-
ses. Last, our China-focused sampling strategy may limit general-
izability to other LMIC contexts, particularly regions with more
fragmented health systems, such as Sub-Saharan Africa. Differences in
digital infrastructure, health system capacity, and resource availability
mean that both costs and effectiveness may not translate directly.
Contextual adaptation of cost parameters, informed by local epide-
miological and economic proﬁles, will therefore be critical to ensure
that both efﬁcacy and cost-effectiveness are maintained when scaling
up implementation in diverse settings. Although these limitations
warrant future validation through multinational trials, the analysis
provides a foundational blueprint for equity-focused device evaluation
in resource-limited settings.
The smart pillbox reconﬁgures oral PrEP adherence in LMICs by
transforming structural constraints (of ﬂine needs, objectively and
passively collected monitoring data, and SDH disparities) into catalysts
for equity-centric innovation. Our ﬁndings conﬁrm that optimizing
medical devices for low-resource settings requires not only durability
but also intentional design that prioritizes vulnerable populations.
Policymakers should integrate such devices into universal health
coverage essential packages using vulnerability-indexed pricing, while
researchers should prioritize hybrid hardware –software models to
balance robustness with scalability. As LMICs address the syndemic of
infectious diseases and health inequities, this intervention demon-
strates a fundamental principle: equitable health technology mustﬁrst
reach marginalized populations facing system-level barriers, rather
than initially targeting easier-to-reach groups.
Methods
The prospective interventional China Real-world Oral Intake of PrEP
(CROPrEP) study evaluated the HIV prevention ef ﬁcacy of TDF/FTC
among MSM in four cities: Beijing, Shenzhen, Chongqing, and
Shenyang
48. Participants were strati ﬁed by baseline preference into
daily or event-driven PrEP subgroups, or a non-PrEP group. Daily PrEP
participants received one TDF/FTC tablet per day. The event-driven
PrEP regimen involved administering two tablets 2–24 h before sexual
intercourse, followed by single tablets 24 and 48 h after the initial dose.
If additional sexual activity occurred within 48 h of the initial dose,
participants transitioned to daily dosing until 48 h after theﬁnal sexual
act. Subsequent sexual encounters triggered reinitiation of the event-
driven regimen. Cross-over between daily and event-driven groups was
permitted during follow-up.

Intervention
This intervention leveraged an integrated technology-support model
linking digital monitoring with clinical care to improve oral PrEP
adherence. Key components included a multidisciplinary team, tai-

adherence and data integrity. Suppl ementary Fig. S13 illustrates the
electronic medication monitoring device and its operation.
(1) Multidisciplinary team collaboration:The intervention involved
a coordinated team of researchers, experienced physicians, and
technical support personnel. Researchers facilitated proactive mon-

initiation.
(2) Customized participant engagement protocols:Each participant

materials and demonstration videos for daily and event-driven PrEP

pilot testing with researchers and users, emphasizing participant-
centered engagement to promote self-management and adherence
motivation.
(3) Structured monitoring and follow-up schedule: Participants
underwent scheduled follow-ups at 3 and 6 months ( ± 7 days), which
included biological testing (e.g., HIV screening, HIV RNA pooling PCR,
syphilis serology, HSV-2 antibody testing, routine blood and urine
tests, liver and renal function tests, blood glucose and lipid pro ﬁles,
and bone mineral density assessment) and online questionnaires
assessing sociodemographic and behavioral factors. Continuous
remote monitoring using the smart pillbox logged adherence data via
cloud-based systems accessible through a WeChat Mini Program.

(5) Supplementary technical and operational resources:The inter-

technical assistance, and the provision of NB-IoT-enabled smart pill-
boxes with 600 mAh rechargeable batteries and automatic cloud
logging. Post-study, devices were returned per a predeﬁned protocol
to ensure sustainability and data security. Supplementary resources
such as customized instructional videos and a dedicated management
platform facilitated seamless implementation.

Statistical analysis
We assessed efﬁcacy using intent-to-treat analysis and trial-wide data
from DIRECT-PrEP’s economic evaluation. The within-trial cost-effec-
tiveness analysis compared the smart pillbox intervention with the
control group across three perspectives: health system, multipayer,
and societal. The healthcare perspective incorporated only direct
medical expenditures. The multipayer perspective included health
sector expenditures, intervention costs, and indirect costs associated
with food and transportation. The societal perspective encompassed
all additional indirect costs, including participants’opportunity costs
related to healthcare engagement and productivity losses due to ill-
ness. As recommended for public health economic evaluations, we
prioritized the societal perspective because it provides an inclusive
assessment of all costs affecting both beneﬁciaries and payers, thereby
offering a comprehensive evaluation of societal impact (Supplemen-
tary Note 3, Supplementary Table S6)
40.
We quanti ﬁed cost-effectiveness using the ICER, calculated by
dividing the difference in mean costs between the digital intervention
and control groups by the corresponding difference in health out-
comes (measured in both QALYs and PADs). The ICER represented the
marginal cost required to achieve one additional unit of health beneﬁt
when implementing the smart pillbox intervention versus
standard care.
Because a speci ﬁc WTP threshold has not been established in
China
37, we adopted the cost-effectiveness threshold framework based
on WHO-CHOICE and previous publications39–41, corresponding to 1–3
times China’s 2020 per capita gross domestic product (GDP). This
yielded an evaluative range of $10,400 –$31,200 per QALY gained,
based on China ’s economic parameters52. WTP thresholds were also
estimated using a VSL conversion42, yielding a point estimate of 1.45
times GDP per capita, with a lower bound of about 1.16 times and an
upper bound of about 2.90 times GDP per capita. Regarding PADs, in
the lack of prede ﬁned WTP benchmarks, we derived the theoretical
threshold required to attain a 95% probability of cost-effectiveness
through probabilistic sensitivity analysis.
We applied the two-sided SUR model to specify cost and effec-
tiveness regression equations, producing incremental cost and utility
estimates. To address sampling uncertainty, we used a nonparametric
bootstrap resampling approach with 5000 iterations. This method
generated an empirical joint distribution of incremental costs and
effects by repeatedly sampling with replacement from the original
dataset
53,54.
These bootstrap replications were used to construct 95% con-
ﬁdence intervals (CIs). The resulting uncertainty estimates were
visualized on cost-effectiveness planes, providing a comprehensive
depiction of the variability in our cost-effectiveness estimates. There
were no missing data in this trial; all essential medical records were
fully available and analytically adequate.
We subsequently developed cost-effectiveness acceptability
curves (CEACs) to estimate the probability that the intervention was
cost-effective across a range of WTP thresholds for QALYs, analyzed
from multiple economic perspectives. These CEACs provided policy-
makers with robust probabilistic evidence of cost-effectiveness, while
accounting for key uncertainties inherent in economic evaluations
39.
To evaluate the robustness of our primaryﬁndings, we conducted
comprehensive sensitivity analyses. First, we accounted for residual
confounding by controlling for baseline outcome measures (age,
income, casual partner status, and PrEP regimen). Second, we reas-
sessed cost-effectiveness using two-sided GLM as well as OLS regres-
sion models. Third, we reanalyzed cost-effectiveness after including
healthcare expenditures attributable to Truvada. Fourth, we con-
ducted a sensitivity analysis that disaggregated system costs intoﬁxed
and variable components, treating overhead expenses as variable.
Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis based on an alternative
pooled utility value for HIV/AIDS.
All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1. Two-sided
independent samples t-tests and two-sided Mann –Whitney U tests
were applied for normally and non-normally distributed continuous
variables, respectively. Estimates were reported with 95% conﬁdence
intervals, using two-tailed tests with α =0 . 0 5 (P < 0.05). The trial was
registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR1900025604).
