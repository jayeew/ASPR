must remain below 200 °C. Conversely, the conventional CVD growth
of VACNTs signiﬁcantly exceeds 700 °C. Although efforts to lower the

To circumvent these limitations, the transfer of the as-grown
VACNTs to target substrates has gained attention as an alternative to
direct growth (Table 1). Although well-established methods exist for
transferring two-dimensional (2D) materials
26– 31, similar strategies have
been attempted for VACNTs to enable their delamination from the
growth substrate
32– 34. However, these approaches typically involve
chemical etching to reduce interface adhesion. During wet etching and
drying, the etchant often causes structural deformation owing to
capillary forces upon evaporation
32. Although VACNTs can be
detached from the growth substrate using gas-phase etching, estab-
lishing intimate contact with a new substrate remains a challenge 33.
Manual pressing of the transferred VACNTs may lead to the undesired
mechanical collapse of the vertical architecture
34.
Alternatively, some methods employ liquid polymers to inﬁltrate
the VACNT forest, thereby facilitating the transfer toﬂexible polymeric
substrates18,35. However, these polymer in ﬁltration techniques sig-
niﬁcantly reduce the exposed surface area of the VACNTs and destroy
their upright morphology. To address these drawbacks, welding pro-
cesses with laser irradiation
36 or thermo-compression37– 39 have been
explored to fuse the tips of the CNT strands onto the target substrate.
Despite enabling VACNT attachment, these welding processes require
high local temperatures (>200 °C) and offer poor substrate compat-
ibility, often causing thermal damage to the VACNTs and the under-
lying substrate.
Ice has been proposed as a transient adhesive because of its
strong interfacial bonding arising from van der Waals (vdW) forces,
hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interaction
40,41. Ice can tempora-
rily create robust adhesion, enabling its use in various material transfer
processes
40,41. However, when the residual ice melts and evaporates at
ambient pressure, a transient liquid phase inevitably forms. This gen-
erates capillary forces that collapse delicate nanostructures. Wei et al.
42
formed the ice layer by spraying atomized water, which resulted in
non-uniform coverage and required large water volumes. The sub-
sequent evaporation caused severe aggregation and deformation of
ﬁne VACNT features (Supplementary Fig. 1). In our previous study
43,
the initial ice coverage is improved by condensing vapor onto a cooled
substrate, enabling more uniform ice ﬁlms and achieving partial
preservation of VACNT micropatterns down to ~100µm. Nevertheless,
the residual ice was still removed by evaporation at atmospheric
pressure. As a result, capillary forces acted on the CNT bundles,
leading to local densi ﬁcation and tilt of vertically aligned structures,
particularly in micropatterns (Supplementary Fig. 2). Collectively,
these studies demonstrate the feasibility of ice-assisted transfer but
also reveal that evaporation-based removal cannot fundamentally
prevent capillary-induced deformation. Importantly, previous ice-
based VACNT transfer approaches demonstrated only the con-
ceptual use of ice as a temporary adhesive, without achieving com-
plete, deformation-free transfer of micropatterns or extending the
process to device-level integration.
Herein, a residue-free and versatile VACNT transfer process is
proposed that utilizes thin-ice formation followed by sublimation.
Speciﬁcally, water vapor from the ambient environment condenses
on the cooled acceptor substrate and temporarily bonds to the
VACNT array via vdW forces. After transfer, sublimation under
reduced pressure bypasses the liquid phase, thus averting capillary-
induced collapse and preserving the pristine upright CNT archi-
tecture. Because this entire process operates at or below room
temperature without additional chemical etchants, it readily
accommodates various types of substrates (e.g., silicon, metals, and
polymers). This method achieved a high transfer yield of over 95% by
optimizing ice thickness. Furthermore, the scalability of the process
was experimentally con ﬁrmed by transferring diverse VACNT pat-
terns in sizes ranging from below 10 µm to 1 cm. Additionally,
removing the crust layer – which physically bridges the top ends of
adjacent CNT strands – eliminates interstrand connectivity and thus
enables area-selective transfer onto planar microstructures as well as
suspended MEMS platforms, preserving the VACNTs in the desired
regions. Even after the transfer, the VACNTs retained their high
electrical/thermal conductivity and optical absorption, underscoring
their effectiveness in preserving material characteristics. By lever-
aging the versatility of the transfer process, thermal interface mate-
rials (TIMs) and infrared-absorbing layers in infrared sensors were
successfully fabricated. Overall, by eliminating the liquid phase and
incorporating crust-layer removal, the process overcomes the
structural instabilities inherent to prior ice-assisted transfers. This
reﬁnement provides a robust route for integrating pristine VACNT
architectures into microsystem platforms that were previously
incompatible with CNT transfer. Overall, the sublimation-based ice
removal and interstrand decoupling engineering establish a transfer
approach that fundamentally improves the structural ﬁdelity and
geometric versatility of VACNT integration, enabling device-level
implementations that were not attainable with earlier transfer
methods.

Ice sublimation-based transfer of VACNTs
Inspired by the strong adhesion formed when an ice-covered surface
comes in contact with another material, an ice-sublimation-based
transfer process (ISTP), involving the formation and sublimation-based
removal of a thin ice layer, was developed (Fig.1a). The VACNT arrays
were synthesized on a donor substrate in diverse patterns using CVD,
their structural characteristics provided (Supplementary Figs. 3 – 5).
The ice layer served as a transfer medium. The VACNTs adhered
strongly to the ice layer and were subsequently transferred to the
acceptor substrate. As the ice sublimates, it leaves no residue or visible
watermarks, enabling clean transfer (Supplementary Fig. 6) and pre-
venting the capillary-induced collapse of the CNT strands. Figure 1b
illustrates the transfer mechanism at the microscale level, focusing on
applying adhesive force to the VACNTs during the transfer process.

(≈35 µm) is formed through condensation of ambient vapor under
freezing conditions. After the ice comes in contact with the VACNTs, it

Transfer to various substrates
Figure 2a illustrates the ISTP procedure. A transfer setup was devel-
oped, integrating a substrate cooling system composed of Peltier
elements and a water-cooled chiller, together with a vacuum chamber
(Supplementary Fig. 12). First, the acceptor substrate was mounted on
the Peltier cooler, cooled to −10 °C, and maintained for 3 min. Under
these conditions, ambient air at 25 °C and 50% relative humidity con-
tacted with the −10 °C substrate surface, causing water vapor to con-
dense and freeze immediately, thereby forming a thin, uniform ice
layer. The as-grown VACNTs were brought into contact with the ice
layer by inverting the donor substrate onto the acceptor substrate. The
Peltier cooler was then turned off for 30 s, allowing the acceptor
substrate to warm gradually to room temperature and melt the ice
layer within seconds. By limiting the melting time, water did not sig-
niﬁcantly evaporate, preventing capillary-induced deformation and
preserving the vertical alignment of the VACNTs. During this interval, a
uniform compressive pressure of 20 kPa was applied to the donor
substrate to ensure intimate contact between the VACNT tips and the
acceptor substrate. A notably lower pressure resulted in incomplete
contact, whereas a higher pressure risked deforming the CNT struc-
ture. After the VACNT tips moved downward and contacted the
acceptor substrate, the Peltier cooler was reactivated for 3 min to
reform the ice layer around the VACNT tips and establish a strong
adhesive force. Subsequently, the donor substrate was lifted vertically,
leaving the VACNTs adhering to the acceptor substrate. After the
transfer, the vacuum chamber covered the cooling setup to prepare
for ice sublimation. The pressure inside the chamber decreased to
1 Torr while the Peltier cooler remained on to prevent the ice from
melting. The temperature of the acceptor substrate was gradually
increased by lowering the input voltage of the Peltier cooler. The ice
layer sublimates directly from solid to vapor without passing through
the liquid phase below the triple point pressure ( ≈4.6 Torr). This

Controlling transfer quality with ice
The adhesive strength for the VACNT transfer directly correlates with
the amount of ice. A thicker ice layer increases the contact area
between the VACNTs and the ice, thereby enhancing the overall
adhesive force. To enable successful transfer, the net adhesive force at
the ice– CNT interface must surpass the bonding force anchoring the
VACNTs to the donor substrate. To quantify and control the ice
thickness, we monitored its evolution using a laser displacement sen-
sor (LJ-X8060, KEYENCE) under controlled ambient conditions (25 °C,
50% RH). The thickness increased predictably with cooling time across
all tested substrates, conﬁrming that it can be adjusted by tuning the
cooling duration (Supplementary Fig. 18). The typical ice layer thick-
ness used in this study was 10 – 30 µm, which was suf ﬁcient for CNT
embedding and adhesion during the refreezing step. The transfer yield
was experimentally assessed to investigate the relationship between
ice layer thickness and transferred VACNT area. Figure 3as h o w st h e

To demonstrate that the sublimation-based ice-removal step
effectively prevents capillary-induced structural collapse in VACNTs,
the transfer results with and without ice sublimation after transfer
(Fig. 3c, d) were compared. Figure 3c shows the VACNT square pat-
terns transferred onto the acceptor substrate and subjected to ice
sublimation. The phase change from solid to gas eliminated the liquid
phase, preventing capillary forces from densifying the CNT structure.
Consequently, the VACNT patterns maintain their original vertically
aligned structures. Although a transient melting step occurs during
transfer, no shrinkage or aggregation was observed. This stability
arises from the conﬁned interfacial behavior between the CNT tips and
the transient liquid layer. Melting occurs mainly at the CNT– ice inter-
face, forming a thin liquidﬁlm localized near the CNT tips. Because of
the intrinsic hydrophobicity of CNTs, this liquid does not inﬁltrate the

Integrated VACNTs as infrared absorber
VACNTs show high absorptance over 0.98 in the long-wave infrared
(LWIR, 8 – 12 μm) range, making them promising candidates for
absorber materials in uncooled microbolometers
17,53. While VACNT-
based bolometers have been investigated, system-level integration
with readout circuits remains unrealized, primarily due to the incom-
patibility of their high-temp erature synthesis with CMOS
technology
54,55. To avoid the thermal constraints of direct VACNT
synthesis, VACNTs were integrated as light absorbers into an LWIR
sensor through ISTP. Figure6a illustrates the structure of the VACNT-
based light absorber and the sensing mechanism of the LWIR sensor.
When exposed to LWIR radiation, a portion of the incident light is
directly absorbed by the VACNT strands. The unabsorbed light,
instead of escaping, undergoes multiple internal reﬂections between
the vertically aligned CNT walls within the high-density forest struc-
ture. These repeated re ﬂections effectively trap the light within the
array, allowing it to be progressively reabsorbed by adjacent CNT
strands, resulting in near-total absorption. Absorbed radiation is con-
verted to heat and is conducted to the sensing layer through the
VACNTs. As the temperature of the sensing layer increased, its resis-
tance changed based on the temperature coef ﬁcient of resistance,

intensity. Figure 6ba n dT a b l e2 compare the thermal conductivities

Discussion
An innovative ice layer sublimation-based transfer process was devel-
oped to overcome the limitations of the application of VACNTs. They
were successfully integrated as thermal interface materials for smart-
phones and infrared sensor absorbers. By implementing a substrate
cooling system, a thin ice layer was formed on the acceptor substrate
and the strong adhesion between the ice and the VACNTs was lever-
aged to detach them from the growth substrate. Under low-pressure
conditions, the ice layer sublimated without passing through the liquid
phase, thereby preserving the structural integrity and functional
properties of the VACNTs. This me thod enables residue-free VACNT
transfer at room temperature, eliminating process compatibility issues
arising from high-temperature synthesis. It supports a wide range of
substrates, including polymers for ﬂexible electronics, and accom-
modates patterns from the microscale to centimeter-scale. Conse-
quently, the exceptional physical and chemical attributes of VACNTs
can be incorporated into diverse functional devices, thereby enhan-
cing their performance and enabling the development of novel device
architectures.
Methods
Preparation of VACNTs and acceptor substrate
VACNT synthesis: VACNTs were synthesized on thermally oxidized Si
substrates via CVD. Speci ﬁcally, 1 cm × 1 cm SiO
2 pieces were diced
from 4-inch wafers, and a 3 nm-thick iron (Fe) layer was deposited via
sputtering. To obtain various micropatterned VACNTs, a lift-off pro-
cess was employed to pattern the Fe catalyst. The Fe-deposited SiO
2
substrates were placed inside a quartz tube connected to gas inlets and
introduced into a furnace. After heating to 720 °C under a nitrogen
(N
2) atmosphere, a catalyst reduction step was performed in ammonia
(NH3) for 10 min, followed by the introduction of acetylene (C2H2)g a s
for a few minutes. Following the synthesis, the furnace temperature
was reduced to room temperature under an N
2 atmosphere to obtain
Si substrates with the synthesized VACNTs.
Crust layer removal process: to eliminate the interstrand con-
nectivity among the VACNTs for area-selective transfer, the crust layer
located at the top of the VACNT array was removed using reactive ion
etching (RIE). The VACNTs were etched using an inductively coupled
plasma reactive ion etching (ICP-RIE) system (LAT, ICP-RIE system)
with the following parameters: chamber pressure of 10 mTorr, O
2 ﬂow
rate of 8 sccm, Arﬂow rate of 32 sccm, source power of 40 W, and base
power of 10 W for three cycles of 1 min each. After etching the crust
layer on the top of the VACNTs, the CNT surfaces were treated with CF
4
plasma to impart hydrophobicity (CF 4 ﬂow rate: 40 sccm, source
power: 100 W, duration: 45 s).
Acceptor substrate preparation: Si and SiO2 substrates were pre-
pared by dicing the 4-inch wafers into pieces. On the diced Si sub-
strates, chromium/gold (Cr/Au) layers with thicknesses of 10 and
100 nm were deposited on diced Si substrates using a sputtering sys-
tem (Cressington, 108 auto). Flexible substrates were prepared using
200 µm-thick PET ﬁlms, while stretchable substrates were fabricated
by curing Eco ﬂex
TM mixed at a 1:1 ratio. SiO 2 substrates with micro-
structured patterns were fabricated via wet etching. This process
involved patterning SiO
2 to serve as an etch mask, followed by wet
etching of Si in a 10 wt% tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH)
solution at 100 °C, forming trenches approximately 50 μmd e e pa n d
resulting in a microstructured substrate.
VACNT transfer system conﬁguration
The experimental setup for VACNT transfer comprised a substrate
cooling system and a vacuum system for ice sublimation. The substrate
cooling system comprised a Peltier-based water-cooled cooler upon
which a stainless heat sink and Peltier element (2 cm × 2 cm, rated
voltage: 5 V) were sequentially stacked, with thermal grease applied at
each interface. A thermal pad was attached on top of the Peltier ele-
ment, followed by the attachment of the acceptor substrate onto
which the VACNTs would be transferred.
The vacuum system was designed to reduce the pressure in the
substrate-cooling system and was composed of a detachable acrylic
cover connected to a vacuum pump. The cover and vacuum pump
were linked via tubing and a vacuum gauge (Instrutech, CVM201GCA)
was installed to monitor the vacuum level. In addition, a vent valve was
incorporated to allow the chamber to return to atmospheric pressure
after ice sublimation.
Transfer yield calculation
The yield of the transfer process is deﬁned as the ratio of the VACNT
area transferred to the acceptor substrate to the total VACNT area
intended for transfer. To calculate the VACNT area, images of the
donor substrate containing VACNTs were captured using a ﬁxed-
position camera (Basler, a2A3840-45ucBAS), followed by images of the
acceptor substrate after transfer. Image processing software (ImageJ)
was utilized to quantify the VACNT areas. The images were converted
to grayscale, with the VACNT regions appearing black and the sub-
strate regions appearing white; the number of black pixels was coun-
ted. The transfer yield was calculated as the ratio of the number of
black pixels representing VACNTs on the acceptor substrate to the
number of black pixels representing VACNTs on the donor substrate.

experiment was conducted alongside measurements using commer-
