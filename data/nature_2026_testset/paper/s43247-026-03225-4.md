Glacier response to climate change results in rapid glacier water resource loss. Positive glacial
regulatory processes (GRPs) are processes that buffer glacier water resource losses. Nevertheless,
these processes are not systematically quanti ﬁed, which may lead to uncertainty in glacier water
resource sustainability assessments. Here, we employ a glacier water balance model to track glacier
water resource transport trajectories and quantify contributions of positive GRPs across High
Mountain Asia (HMA). The combined impacts of positive GRPs could mitigate ~236-255 Gt of HMA
glacier water resource loss (~9%-13% of total mass loss) in the 21
st century (2015-2100), suggesting
underestimated glacier water resource sustainability. These contributions result from precipitation
pattern transition (~0.9%-1.9%), nonlinear ﬂuctuations in glacier dynamics (~4.7%-7.1%), rapid
expansion of dead ice (~2.0%-2.4%), and retention of glacial meltwater by exposed depressions
(~1.0%-1.3%). We highlight the importance of accurately assessing glacier water resource loss for
water sustainability and sea level rise projections.
Climate change has driven rapid global glacier retreat1,2, linearly related to
rising temperatures in this century, prompted concerns of glacier water
resource sustainability
1,3–5. However, certain processes can amplify or buffer
glacier water resource losses6–8, causing glacier water resources evolution
trajectories to deviate from direct temperature responses. We deﬁne these
mechanisms as glacial regulatory processes (GRPs), which encompass a
suite of physical mechanisms that regulate glacier water resource loss rates,
such as debris-cover feedbacks, supraglacial lake expansion, albedo varia-
bility, and hydrological changes within and beneath the ice
9–15(Fig. 1;S u p -
plementary Note 1). These processes introduce large uncertainties in long-
term glacier water resource simulations, and quantifying their impacts is
essential for establishing comprehe nsive glaciological frameworks and
accurately evaluating glacier water resources.
Numerous processes affecting glacier water resource evolution under
climate warming were quantiﬁed in previous studies, and these processes
may be positive (mitigating glacier water resource loss, e.g., meltwater
retained by glacial lake)
9,10, negative (exacerbating glacier water resource
loss, e.g., albedo reduction, lake-terminating glaciers and terminus
calving)7,11–13, or neutral (manifesting transitional or competing impacts,
e.g., cloud cover variations inducing competing warming and cooling
effects)
14,15 (Fig. 1). However, existing studie s predominantly focus on
processes amplifying glacier water resource depletion, such as darkening
glacier surface16,17, long-range transport of light-absorbing particles18,19 and
accelerated mass loss by lake-terminating glaciers8,20, or individual positive/
neutral GRPs, e.g., the effect of local circulation15 and meltwater retention by
glacial lake20–22. As such, there remains a lack of systematic quantiﬁcation of
positive GRPs to assess their combined contribution to the sustainability of
glacier water resources. Therefore, t his study focuses on a collection of
positive GRPs to clarify the resili ence of HMA glacier water resources
resisting climate warming. Systematic quantiﬁcation of these positive GRPs
could serve to understand the comprehensive impacts of positive GRPs on
the sustainability of glacier water resources under climate change scenarios
in this century.

Discussion
The widespread occurrence of GRPs within glacier water resources systems
plays a crucial role in regulating glacier water balance. The lack of research
on these processes may introduce large uncertainty in estimating glacier
water dynamics. To fulﬁll the current research gap in systematically eval-
uating the comprehensive contribution of GRP, this study focused on sev-
eral positive GRPs, which may buffer glacier water resource losses, aimed at
quantifying integrated contribution to glacier water resource sustainability.
Notably, there are also positive GRP contributions that remain unquanti-
ﬁable within this reduced-complexi ty modeling framework (e.g., local
cooling induced by enhancedsensible heat exchange, Fig.1).
Our results reveal that the rate o f glacier mass loss may be over-
estimated due to the lack of comprehensive quantiﬁcation of positive GRPs.
Thus, the vulnerability of the world’s water towers and the sea level effect of
glacier shrinkage may be exaggerated. In general, positive GRPs mitigate
glacier mass loss by 210.5 ± 12.6–226.6 ± 12.5 Gt (or 7.7% ± 0.4%–11.4% ±

In terms of prospects, these results are essential for ecological main-
tenance and sustainable developmentof downstream basins, especially in
endorheic regions. Projected precipitation increase is lower for endorheic
regions than for exorheic regions (precipitation increase in endorheic

possible future drought stress. In addition, the application of our model on a
global scale can be used to correct overestimates of glaciercontributions to

stakeholders, and can be used to supp ort rational adaptive measures to
harmonize ecological impacts and socio-economic development.
Our GRP simulation methodology is potentially applicable worldwide
and can be used as a valuable tool for similar studies of glaciated regions
worldwide to improve our understanding of the global response of glaciers
to future climate warming. A full-process-based and accurate assessment of
glacier mass loss on a global scale is of profound importance for regional
water integration and allocation, as well as for mitigating global effects of
SLR. Our model interface allows for expansion and integration of additional
glacial elements (e.g., glacial lakes
8, light-absorbing particles18,19). While the
model overcomes the limitations of high temporal and spatial resolution
inputs
55, it can potentially be used to study transient and short-term glacier
processes (e.g., basal slide, surge, and avalanche), as well as to support
potential glacial lake outburstﬂood assessments56–58.
Furthermore, while this study provides a case for developing more
comprehensive glacier evolution models, several considerations remain for
subsequent extension to other glacier-covered regions worldwide and for
further reﬁnement. Partial parameter selections in this study exhibit strong
regional speciﬁcity (e.g., degree-day factors, precipitation tuning factors),
necessitating the selection of parameters aligned with the study region ’s
characteristics to adjust the model structure. Simultaneously, both the mass
balance and glacier dynamics components within glacier evolution models
incorporate varying degrees of simpliﬁcation. Since there are unresolved
uncertainties in reﬂecting actual glacier evolution, developing glacier evo-
lution models based on full physical processes is essential. Despite the large
resource demands this entails, it could facilitate more accurate under-
standing of glacier evolution and its underlying mechanisms. Energy-mass
balance model combined with full-Stokes glacier dynamics model provides
the most advanced approach for realistic glacier evolution simulation.
However, the model is highly dependent on accurate physicalﬁeld forcing
inputs. A major challenge that urgently requires resolution is how to gen-
eralize these glacier models— which are sensitive to input parameters and
physical processes — across vast glacierized regions lacking ﬁeld
observations.

Uncertainty and validation
To address the issue of uncertainties associated with complex potential
ﬂuctuations, we conducted Monte Carlo simulations of the MBG.R model
to increase the randomization of vari ables (e.g., precipitation, debris
thickness). 10 sets of random input parameters were generated for each of
the 112 GCM datasets to correct model parameters, allowing 1232 model-
ling parameters to be applied to each modelled glacier. Relevant parameter
settings can be found in SupplementaryTab. 6. Debris evolution, ice cliffs,
and supraglacial lakes were difﬁcult to quantify over the long term, and we
could not meet the computational de mands of the iterative updating of
model initial states for time step breakpoints. A recent study compared the
effects of the absence of debris and debris expansion on enhanced ablation
and suggested that debris expansion in this century would contribute no
more than 3% to enhanced ablation
7. Moreover, ablation bias due to ice cliffs
is partially included in the debris–ablation enhancement curve7. Therefore,
we only accounted for current debris coverage in our model. Large-scale
ﬁeld observations of lake-terminating glaciers, bedrock lithology, surface
fragmentation, and light-absorbing particles are missing and the relation-
ships of these elements with glacial mass balance are unclear. Thus, these
elements have not been included in our model. However, the model
interface has been preset to allow expansion and inclusion of additional
elements.
In the glacier dynamics simulations, we repeated GCM ensemble runs
