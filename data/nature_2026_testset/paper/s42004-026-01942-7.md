Laundry, a mundane chore with roots stretching back millennia, stands as a
vital pillar in our daily lives, maintaining cleanliness, hygiene, and overall
well-being for individuals and society1–3. Once a laborious task performed by
hand, laundry is now a convenient routine facilitated by modern washing
machines that perform a streamlined cleaning process, using water to soak,
beat, and spin dirt, stains, and germs out of clothes and linens with advanced
detergents boosting the cleaning power of water
4,5. Despite dramatic
reductions in water consumption – from more than 120 L per cycle in
conventional top-loading machines to ≤60 L in modern front-loading
systems6– laundry practices remain fundamentally predicated on the
assumption that cleaning is inherently wastewater-generating, with inno-
vation focused on reducing water use rather than questioning the necessity
of wastewater production itself.
Life-cycle assessment studies indicate that the use phase constitutes a
major contributor to the energy and resource footprint of household deter-
gents, largely due to repeated rinsing steps required to remove detergent
residues
7. In typical laundering processes, rinsing can account for a substantial
fraction of total water consumption per cycle, which is necessary to limit
residual surfactants that may cause skin irritation or respiratory discomfort in
sensitive populations8,9. At the same time, effective soil removal under con-
ventional laundering conditions still relies on signiﬁcant detergent input,
contributing to the continued growth inglobal detergent production, which is
projected to increase steadily in the coming decade10,11. Regulatory frameworks
such as EC No. 648/2004 have establis hed important requirements for
detergent biodegradability under standardized laboratory conditions, repre-
senting a signiﬁcant advance in mitigating environmental impact8,9. However,
it has been reported that degradation kinetics in real aquatic and wastewater-
treatment environments can differ substantially from standardized test con-
ditions, and the environmental fate of degradation intermediates remains
incompletely characterized
8,9. In addition, detergent residues are often detected
in treated efﬂuents, while mechanical agitation during laundering promotes
the release of microplasticﬁbers from synthetic textiles, introducing further
sources of persistent pollutants into aquatic systems12–14.
As a result, routine household laundry continuously converts potable
water into wastewater containing detergent residues and textile-derived
microplastics, contributing to cumulative environmental loads, especially
where wastewater treatment is limited

Results and Discussion
The appeal of self-cleaning surfaces arises from their ability to remove
contaminants through simple water action with minimal external input,
offering a sustainable route to reduce consumption of energy, water, and
chemical cleaning agents21,22. Nature-inspired nanostructured surfaces have
been developed to mimic self-cleaning behaviors in biological organisms.
Yet the essence of self-cleaning surfaces lies in their chemistry nature, tai-

their low polarity and strong afﬁnity for many substrates. On low-surface-

rinsing
24. For this reason, hydrophilic coatings with elevated surface energy
have been investigated, as enhanced water wetting can reduce oil–substrate
adhesion and promote removal under ﬂowing or rinsing conditions 25.
Despite these advances, maintaining stable hydrophilicity under practical
conditions remains nontrivial. Many reported hydrophilic self-cleaning
surfaces rely on molecular arrangements or nanoscale features that are
susceptible to surface reconstruction or molecular reorientation upon
drying in air, which can reduce wettability over time26.T i O₂-based coatings
provide a notable example where hydrophilicity can be restored through
UV-induced processes, although their performance is inherently dependent
on illumination conditions and may not be universally applicable
27.
In the present work, the observed self-cleaning behavior is not attrib-
uted to photocatalytic activity or bactericidal surface chemistry. While
PDADMAC is known to possess antimicrobial properties
28, its contribution
at the outermost surface of the studie d polyelectrolyte multilayer (PEM)
coating is minimal. Instead, the surface is dominated by sulfonate groups
from PVS, indicating that the interfacial response is governed primarily by
hydration effects. Accordingly, the oil-removal behavior observed here is
interpreted as arising from a highly hydrated interface that weakens oil
adhesion, rather than from chemical degradation or antimicrobial action.
This distinction clariﬁes the scope of the present results and suggests that
interfacial hydration can be explored independently as a design parameter in
self-cleaning surface systems.
Here we demonstrate that alternating sprays of PDADMAC and PVS
create uniform self-cleaning PEM coatings on substrates with diverse surface
energies and textures (Fig. 1a), enabling removal of oily grimes, biological
stains, and harmful germs with simple water action, owing to the complete
and dense hydration layer formed by the ultra-high density of sulfonate
groups. These self-cleaning coatings demonstrate excellent stability, dur-
ability, and reusability against chemical corrosion and physical wear, sup-
porting their application as fabric coatings and their potential to transform
traditional detergent-assisted laundryroutines into detergent-free practices.
Fabrication of self-cleaning fabric coatings
Alternating deposition of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes to form
multilayer thin ﬁlms represents a well-established strategy for surface
modiﬁcation29–33. In the present study, PDADMAC and PVS do not form
stable multilayers via conventional dip-assisted layer-by-layer assembly,
attributable to the low molecular weight of PVS (4–6 kDa) and insufﬁcient
chain entanglement during equilibrium adsorption (Fig. S1). In contrast,
stable polyelectrolyte multilayers were obtained using an alternating spray-
assisted deposition protocol with a s ingle rinse between layers (Fig. S2).
Under these non-equilibrium conditions, both ﬁlm mass and thickness
increased monotonically with PDADMAC/PVS bilayers ( N)( F i g .1b),
indicating successful multilayer buildup. Owing to the strong and persistent
hydration of sulfonate groups
34, PVS-capped PEMs exhibit markedly
enhanced water wettability compared with PDADMAC-cappedﬁlms (Fig.
S3) and were therefore selected for subsequent self-cleaning studies (PEM-

Conclusions
In summary, we demonstrate that alternating spray deposition of PDAD-
MAC and PVS create self-cleaning PEM coatings on various natural and
synthetic fabrics, regardless of their surface chemical or textural features.
The self-cleaning capability is a ttributed to formation of a complete
hydration layer driven by high-density sulfonate groups. This hydration
layer effectively cleanses sur faces of various contaminants – including
beverage and food stains, oily grimes, harmful bacteria, and odor-causing
fungi– with just a simple water rinse. Therefore, self-cleaning fabric coatings
eliminate fundamental reliance of laundry practices on detergents, trans-
forming them from traditional deterg ent-assisted, multi-step process –
comprising 1 washing cycle and 4 rinsing cycles– to a single detergent-free
rinsing cycle. This detergent-free laundry achieves ~82% saving in water,
electricity, and time, while eliminating discharge of detrimental detergents
into natural waterways. Furthermore, self-cleaning fabric coatings exhibit
excellent stability and durability against chemical corrosion and physical
wear, withstanding over 100 uses, granting detergent-free laundry long-
term ﬁnancial beneﬁts with increasing laundry frequency.
Overall, the self-cleaning fabric coating highlights potential to shift
laundry routines from insidious cle an-water consumers and wastewater
contributors into mindful guardians of freshwater. Inspired by Nature ’s
ingenuity, self-cleaning technologies continue transforming scientiﬁcd i s -
coveries into technological breakthroughs with implications across myriad
industrial sectors. In this context, the present success will hopefully stimu-
late further innovative solutions enabled by self-cleaning surfaces to address
longstanding challenges in various down-to-earth applications, maximizing
water and energy efﬁciency. Further research could focus on scaling up the
coating process for industrial adoption, and exploring extensions into other
application areas such as medical textiles to reduce bacterial infections,
shaping our daily lives in a more sustainable and water-conscious way.
