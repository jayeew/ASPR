Signiﬁcantly, the limited resources and complexity of system
layout in micro/nanoscale integrated photonics circuits pose serious
challenges to the effective management of optical signals19,20. Conse-
quently, the capability toﬂexibly adjust the direction of optical signals
emerges as paramount for achieving efﬁcient and accurate connection
of the optical path21. To date, considerable efforts have been devoted
to developing strategies for fabricating bent organic crystalline
materials that respond to external stimuli
22,23. However, the application
of mechanical stimulation, such as curving or twisting, often induces
stretching or compression of molecular arrangements at the curved
interface (left of Fig. 1A)
24–26, which can compromise structural integ-
rity and degrade optical performance. Moreover, maintaining both
structural stability and functional integrity in low-ductility organic
molecular crystals during deformation remains particularly challen-
ging. Owing to their limited tolerance to stress and strain, such crystals
are prone to cracking or loss of functional properties under mechan-
ical force
27,28. It is therefore imperative to develop effective methods
for achieving and stabilizing crystal deformation to advance applica-
tions in micro/nanoscale integrated photonics
29.
In this study, we demonstrate the spontaneous bending of organic
crystals with well-deﬁned bending angles, without applying external
mechanical force (as illustrated on the right side of Fig. 1A). This
behavior is achieved through a cocrystal engineering strategy that
introduces directionally distinct noncovalent interactions. As illu-
strated in Fig. 1B, a system based on a single polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbon molecule with isotropic intermolecular interactions

Results
Directional noncovalent interaction strategy
The present work employs polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon perylene
as the representative electron donor (denoted as donor 8) and 2,4,6-
trimethylbenzene-1,3,5-tricarbonitrile (TBT) as the electron acceptor
(denoted as acceptor 11). Using a typical solution evaporation
method
30–32, the two components were co-crystallized from a
dichloromethane (DCM)/ethanol (EtOH) mixture (2 mmol L ⁻¹) on a
substrate maintained at 25 °C, yielding smooth-surfaced one-dimen-
sional structures (Supplementary Fig. 1). Detailed experimental pro-
cedures and the resulting crystal structures are provided in
the Supplementary Information. The obtained crystallographic data
(Supplementary Table 1) reveals that the perylene-TBT cocrystal
(cocrystal 8&11) possesses a triclinic structure. As shown in Supple-
mentary Fig. 2, the cocrystal 8&11 exhibits anisotropic noncovalent
interactions, featuring strong charge-transfer character along one
direction and weaker π–π stacking in the orthogonal direction. Nota-
bly, we observed that when the substrate temperature was raised to
30 °C, a slip line appeared within the crystal (Supplementary Fig. 3).
This phenomenon is attributed to the temperature sensitivity of the
weaker π–π interactions.
The Supplementary Movie 1 captured via ﬂuorescence micro-
scopy (FM), clearly shows the existence of slip lines during crystal
growth and solvent evaporation, and then leads to the spontaneous
crystal bending. The self-assembly bending process is further illu-
strated schematically in Fig.1C and through the corresponding bright-
ﬁeld images captured over time, which are presented in Fig. 1D1–D4.
During the self-assembly bending process, the crystal is induced into a
slipped state before 1.09 s, subsequently undergoing bending through
a rotation to an angle of ~155° at 1.11 s, after which the bending angle
gradually decreases to ~148° at 2.06 s. This continuous bending pro-
cess demonstrates that the crystal is a single entity, thereby excluding
the possibility of epitaxial growth between two separate crystals.
The self-assembly bending process operates via a
supersaturation-controlled strategy
33,34, wherein the concentration of
the mixed solution plays a crucial role. As shown in Supplementary
Fig. 4, at a relatively low mixture concentration of 2 mmol L
−1 and high
temperature substrate of 30 °C, cocrystal exclusively in slipped state,
without the presence of bending microrods. However, as the solution
concentration increases, the in ﬂuence of supersaturation on the
morphology of molecular crystals becomes increasingly apparent.
Speciﬁcally, the proportion of slippage microrods decreases gradually
as the solution concentration increases, while the proportion of
bending microrods increases correspondingly. When the solution
concentration reaches 5 mmol L
−1, and the bending microstructures
dominate the crystal form. This phenomenon is ascribed to the high-
concentration solvents that cause the continuous growth of molecules
at both ends of the slippage microstructures, in which generates
interfacial stress on the slip surface
35. In order to relieve the stress and
reach a thermodynamically stable state, the slippage crystals are
spontaneously bent through rotation process
36.
The proportion of DCM in the mixed solvent (DCM/EtOH) to the
morphology of the microstructures provides further evidence that the
bending structure exhibits greater thermodynamic stability. Speci ﬁ-
cally, at a constant high solution concentration of 5 mmol L
−1 with a
relatively low proportion of DCM, the volatile speed of the DCM/EtOH
solvent is relatively slow due to the volatility of EtOH. Therefore, the
self-assembly process is mainly dominated by the high solution con-
centration (i.e., the thermodynamic factor), which eventually initiates
the formation of thermodynamically stable bent microstructures
Supplementary Fig. 5A). As the proportion of dichloromethane (DCM)
gradually increases, the bent microstructures transform into shorter
and thicker forms, while long and thin rod-like structures emerge
simultaneously (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Additionally, when the DCM
proportion exceeds 60 vol%, the volatile speed of the mixed solvent
becomes relatively rapid. Consequently, the self-assembly process is
primarily inﬂuenced by the evaporation rate (i.e., the kinetic factor),
ultimately leading to the formation of dynamically stable slipped rod-
like structures (Supplementary Fig. 5C–E). The thermodynamic stabi-
lities of the bent microstructures and slipped microrods were analyzed
based on the feasible activation barriers and free energy of crystals
depicted in Supplementary Fig. 5F The results indicate that the bent
microstructures exhibit greater thermodynamic stability compared to
the slipped microrods (i.e., G
V <G R). Furthermore, the activation bar-
rier of the bent microstructures is higher than that of the slipped
microrods, and a higher mixture cocrystal solution concentration is
therefore required to surmount this barrier
37.
The imaging and analysis of a typical 8&11 slipped cocrystal are
presented in Fig. 2A–D. The representative scanning electron micro-
scopy (SEM) image depicted in Fig. 2A reveals a distinct slipping
interface, which is further accentuated in the TEM image presented in
Fig. 2B, showing the 73.8° slipping angle. In addition to the experi-
mental results, theoretical analysis also supports that the temperature-
induced slipping process is ascribable to the relatively weak interac-
tions within the crystal plane with the largest interlayer spacing, i.e.,
the plane with the weakest interlayer interactions. Speci ﬁcally, the
simulated growth morphology of the cocrystal 8&11 (Supplementary
Fig. 6A) allowed to identify the (001) crystal plane as the one with the
largest interlayer spacing (Supplementary Table 2). Combining the
simulated growth morphology with XRD data, we further con ﬁrmed
that the crystal ’s top surface corresponds to the (110) crystal plane
(Supplementary Fig. 6B). The theoretical dihedral angle between the
(001) and (110) crystal planes was calculated to be 73.5° (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 6C), which is in close agreement with the actual bending angle
of 73.8° observed in the cocrystal 8&11. Consequently, slippage occurs
along the [100] direction at relatively high temperatures (Fig.2C). The
slip angle distribution of microrods derived from an analysis of TEM
images indicates that the slipping angles deviated symmetrically about
an average value of 73.6° (Fig. 2D). Notably, the difference area of the
shared interface with the ratio of α
1/α2 = 0.98 was attributed to
asymmetric solute deposition resulting from the concave substrate.
This asymmetric area creates an interfacial stress
38, as illustrated in
Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 7. Consequently, both ends of the
slipped rod-like microstructure continue growing until the slipped
microstructure gradually rotates by 180° and bends to release inter-
facial pressure (Fig. 2B, F).
The imaging and analysis of a typical bent cocrystal 8&11 are
presented in Fig. 2E–H. The representative SEM image (Fig. 2E)
indicates that the bent microstructure displays a bending angle of
73.8°, which is further shown by the corresponding TEM image
(Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the bending angle is consistent with the
observed slip angles (Fig. 2B, D), conﬁrming that the microstructure
underwent a 180° rotation prior to bending. The schematic illus-
trating the proposed rotation and bending mechanism is presented
in Fig. 2G. The bending angle distribution of microrods in Fig. 2H
indicates that the dihedral angles between the (001) and (110) planes
deviated asymmetrically about a modal value of 73.8°, which is
basically consistent with the ideal bending angle of 75.8° between the
(110) and (001) planes (inset of Fig. 2H). To ensure our analysis is
representative and reproducible, we randomly selected areas from
several independently prepared samples, as shown in Supplementary
Fig. 8, a large number of bent microstructures with the angle of
146–148° are distributed in different areas of substrate. Meanwhile,
the optical images of cocrystal 2&11 before and after bending further
demonstrate how the rotation and bending of the slipped crystals
release interfacial strain (Supplementary Fig. 9). The SEM images

Impressively, the continuous process from slippage to bending is
visually demonstrated by molecular dynamics simulation39.A ss h o w n
in Supplementary Fig. 11A, donor and acceptor molecules aggregate
under the charge transfer interaction to form a stable rod-like crystal
nucleus within 20 ns. However, the charge transfer interaction is dis-
turbed by the increasing temperature of the system, which leads to the
initial slip phenomenon in the crystal nucleus, and results in the energy
of the system increases signiﬁcantly within 20–40 ns. With the further
ordered rearrangement and aggregation of molecules at the sliding
interface, metastable sliding rod-like crystals are gradually formed. It is
worth noting that the crystal of this slippage structure is not in the
lowest energy stable state. Therefore, when the molecules continue to
aggregate at both ends of the rod-like structure until the stress on the
sliding plane reaches a critical threshold, the slippage crystal will bend
through the rotation mechanism to release the stress. As shown in
Supplementary Fig. 11B, slight rotations of the slippage crystal sig-
niﬁcantly reduce the energy of the system within about 20 ns. Subse-
quently, after a position relaxation of the regions at both ends of the
slippage structure, it rotates rapidly, eventually forming a thermo-
dynamically stable bent crystal structure.
Universality of directional noncovalent interaction strategy
The results depicted in Fig. 3 demonstrate that the directional non-
covalent interaction strategy can be applied into a diverse array of
molecular systems, including donors 1–9 and acceptors 10–13 (Fig.3A).
This strategy effectively controls the slipping, rotation, and bending
mechanisms within cocrystal systems at relatively a high temperature
substrate, as demonstrated by the corresponding FM images pre-
sented in Fig. 3B–L. The introduction of either electron acceptor into
one of the series of electron donors resulted in slippages within the 1D
cocrystals (Fig. 3B1–L1), and the corresponding self-assembly pro-
cesses yielded bent crystals, characterized by sharp boundaries
between domains and bending angles that ranged from 61.8° to 85.0°
(Fig. 3B2–L2). The detailed crystallographic parameters were pre-
sented in Supplementary Tables 3–12 and Supplementary Fig. 12. The
simulation results were further applied to develop the schematics
presented in Fig. 3B3–L3 illustrating the spatial relationships between
the respective bending planes. As observed for cocrystal 8&11 in
Fig. 2C, the temperature-induced slipping process in these cocrystals
consistently occurs within the planes exhibiting the largest interlayer
spacing. This mechanism has been further conﬁrmed across multiple
organic crystals (Supplementary Figs. 13, 14). The reason for the crys-
tals undergoing slippage at elevated temperature can be evaluated in
greater detail according to schema tics illustrating the noncovalent
interactions within cocrystal 3&11 shown in Fig. 3M. The newly intro-
duced acceptor molecule in the 3&11 cocrystal system forms aniso-
tropic intermolecular interactions involving a strong charge transfer
(CT) interaction with an energy of E
New = −20.7 kcal mol-1 with the
donor molecule, which weakens the original intermolecular interac-
tion to relatively weak π-π interactions with an energy of
E
Original= −2.5 kcal mol−1. Consequently, the microcrystal undergoes
slipping, rotation, and bending along the weaker interaction asso-
ciated with the (001) crystal plane (Fig. 3D1–D2). The magnitudes of
noncovalent interactions presented in Fig. 3N( a l s or e f e rt oS u p p l e -
mentary Figs. 15–24) further validate the propensity of cocrystals to
slip, rotate, and bend in response to their relatively weaker interactions
following the introduction of a stronger interaction. The central
emission wavelength of these cocrystals spans from 420 to 760 nm,
with the corresponding Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage (CIE)
coordinates close to that of pure blue, green and red light (Fig.3Oa n d
Supplementary Fig. 25), respectively. Furthermore, the results from
thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calori-
metry (DSC), reveal that typical slipped and bent cocrystals do not
exhibit any prominent peaks (Supplementary Fig. 26), suggesting that
this spontaneous deformation process does not involve a phase
transition
40.

The anisotropic optical propagation phenomenon observed in the
bent crystals 8&11 is mainly attributed to the change in the direction of
the transition dipole moment (TDM). Speci ﬁcally, photons tend to
propagate along the direction perpendicular to the TDM, and the
direction change is shown in Fig. 5J. This issue was investigated based
on the results obtained from time-dependent density functional theory
(TDDFT) calculations (Supplementary Fig. 30A). The calculations
revealed that the TDM of cocrystal 8&11 were oriented at an angle of
12.0° between molecules 8 and 11. When considered in conjunction
with the molecular packing mode, the TDM were found to be oriented
at an angle of 47.0° relative to the [010] growth direction (Supple-
mentary Fig. 30B). Consequently, light originating from T-1 tends to
propagate upwards towards the top surface, whereas light from T-2’ is
inclined to propagate downwardstowards the bottom surface (Fig.5J).
This directional difference arises because section B was rotated by 180°
prior to the bending process. The results displayed in Fig. 5Ip r o v i d e
evidence supporting the proposed rotation of the slipped microcrystal.
Discussion
In summary, organic bent crystals with speci ﬁcb e n d i n ga n g l e sy e t
devoid of fractured bending interface were successfully fabricated based
a directional noncovalent interaction strategy. The precise adjustment
of the directional noncovalent interactions demonstrated that, the weak
molecular interactions within the cocrystal system undergo a slipping
effect when subjected to a simulate d high-temperature environment,
and then leads to the spontaneous bending of the slipped crystal
through rotational mechanisms to release interfacial pressure. Notably,
this strategy is versatile and has been demonstrated in a wide range of
bent crystals with precise bending angles spanning from 61.8° to 85.0°.
Furthermore, hierarchical bent microstructures with 2–6b e n d sw e r e
successfully fabricated by increasing the substrate temperature pro-
gressively within the range of 34 –40 °C. Finally, the bent cocrystal
exhibited prominent photonic switching functionality according to its
anisotropic optical properties. This general noncovalent interaction
strategy serving as the complementary approach to the bottom-up
synthesis, provides new perspectives on the precise synthesis of organic
complex crystals at the micro/nanoscale, which lays the structural
foundation for sophisticated optoelectronic functions.
Methods
Synthesis of hierarchically slipped microcrystals
A solution of acceptor and donor molecules (1:1 molar ratio) in DCM
was prepared with a concentration of 2 mmol L⁻¹. Then, 10 mL of this
stock solution was rapidly injected into 10 mL of EtOH. Immediately,
200 µL of the resulting mixture was drop-cast onto a pre-heated quartz
substrate using a micropipette. The substrates were maintained at
precise temperatures of 30 ± 0.2 °C and 32 ± 0.2 °C, respectively, using
an electric temperature control platform. After complete solvent
evaporation (typically within 5–7 min), the resulting microstructures
were characterized. The slight difference in substrate temperature (30
vs. 32 °C) ﬁne-tuned the self-assembly kinetics, leading to micro-
crystals with distinct single-slip (1-slip) and double-slip (2-slip) con-
ﬁgurations, respectively.
Synthesis of hierarchically bent microcrystals
A more concentrated stock solution (5 mmol L ⁻¹ in DCM, 1:1 molar
ratio of acceptor/donor) was used. Similarly, 10 mL of this solution was
injected into 10 mL of EtOH. The mixture was drop-cast onto quartz
substrates maintained at a series of precisely controlled temperatures:
30, 32, 34, 36, 38, and 40 °C (±0.2 °C). After complete solvent eva-
poration, microstructures identiﬁed as 1-bend, 2-bend, 3-bend, 4-bend,
5-bend and 6-bend were observed on the respective substrates.
