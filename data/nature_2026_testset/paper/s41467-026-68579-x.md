Many circulating liquid biopsy tumor biomarkers such as ctDNA9,
cfDNA10, metabolites11, and proteins12 are developed rapidly. A special
interest has gone into the investigation of protein glycosylation 13,14.
Previous studies provided sufﬁcient evidence, that the alterations in
protein glycosylation play a crucial role in the onset and further
development of liver diseases and malignancies
15,16. Serum glycomics,
especially serum N-glycomics, has be proved to be a promising strat-
egy for biomarker discovery for liver diseases since the majority ofN-
glycans found in serum are associated with liver-secreted proteins 17.
The disturbance of the liver homeostasis leads to dysregulation of
glycosylation-relevant enzymes in the Golgi apparatus and endo-
plasmic reticulum of the hepatocytes. Therefore, N-glycomic altera-
tions might reﬂect a disruption of liver function, or act as biomarkers
with complementary surveillant or diagnostic value for AFP.
Several serum N-glycomics studies have previously reported
alterations associated with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and rela-
ted liver diseases, including branching, fucosylation, and sialylation
changes in both serum and tissue contexts
13,18–21. These pioneering
studies have provided important biological insights and laid the
foundation for considering glycans as potential biomarkers. However,
despite these advances, current N-glycomic studies still face several
critical limitations: (i) relatively small sample sizes that limit the ability
to capture patient heterogeneity, (ii) lack of rigorous covariate control
(e.g., liver function) in statistical analyses, (iii) limited or absent
external validation, which may in ﬂate performance estimates, (iv)
incomplete characterization of sialylation patterns and linkage-speciﬁc
differences across disease progression stages, and (v) limited inte-
gration with glycoproteomics to resolve site-speciﬁc contributions of
key glycans.
This study advances HCC glycomics research by addressing these
gaps. First, we established a large-scale cohort encompassing 1074
Chinese subjects across the hepatocarcinogenesis spectrum, from
healthy controls through chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis to HCC. Sys-
tematic trajectoryN-glycome analysis revealed dynamic glycosylation
remodeling patterns closely correlated with progressive hepatic
functional deterioration, while also distinguishing malignancy-speciﬁc
glycome traits. Second, transcending conventional single-omics
approaches, we implemented a multi-dimensional analytical frame-
work integrating quantitative glycomics, site-speci ﬁcg l y c o p r o -
teomics, and transcriptomic proﬁling to provide mechanistic depth.
Third, leveraging AutoML and explainable AI, we constructed robust
diagnostic models validated across geographically distinct cohorts,
demonstrating strong generalizability for clinical translation. Toge-
ther, these innovations build upon prior work while substantially
extending its scope, providing not only stage-speci ﬁc glyco-bio-
markers but also mechanistic insights into their biosynthetic
regulation.
Results
Study design, data collection, data quality, andN-glycome
overview
The workﬂow of this study is summarized in Fig.1. The study included
three independent cohorts (discovery cohort Guangzhou-I, GZ-I,
n = 744; external validations Guangzhou-II, GZ-II, n = 186 and Xuzhou,
XZ, n = 144) covering healthy controls and liver disease stages (detailed
demographics in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 8). In
total, 1,074 participants were recruited. Serum N-glycome pro ﬁles
were collected for all individuals by MALDI-TOF MS. Identiﬁcation was
done using in-house tool GlyHunter and quantitative information was
obtained by applying median quotient normalization to peak inten-
sities after removing N-glycans with over 50% missing values (see
Methods for details). A subset of GZ-I cohort (n = 120) was used for LC-
MS based N-glycoproteomics and proteomics analysis. Another subset
of GZ-I cohort (n = 186) was used for sialic acid linkage speci ﬁc N-
glycomics analysis. We used GZ-I cohort for bioinformatic analysis,
model training, and internal model validation, and used GZ-II cohort
and XZ cohort for external model validation. Detailed schematic of
sample allocation is in Methods and Supplementary Fig. 1.
Quality control (QC) assessments demonstrated the high techni-
cal reproducibility of our analytical workﬂow. Median coefﬁcients of
variation (CVs) were 0.08, 0.07, and 0.11 for the GZ-I, GZ-II, and XZ
cohorts, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2a). QC samples exhibited
strong correlations, with a median Pearson’s correlation coefﬁcient (r)
of 0.98 and a minimum of 0.91 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore,
median inter-plate and inter-cohort correlations were both 0.98,
indicating consistent performance across plates and cohorts (Sup-
plementary Fig. 2b –c). These results collectively con ﬁrm the robust
stability and reproducibility of our analytical workﬂow, enabling reli-
able analysis of the serum N-glycome.
The GZ-I cohort serves as the main study population, as all
downstream bioinformatics analyses apart from diagnostic model
evaluation were performed on this cohort. In total, we detected 201N-
glycan compositions (Supplementary Fig. 4a –d, Supplementary
Data 2), of which 121 were detected in all four groups (Supplementary
Fig. 4b). After rigorous ﬁltering (detection in >50% samples, literature
structural validation
22,23), 64 annotated N-glycans were retained for
analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4d, Supplementary Data 2), with >80%
detection rate in most cases (Supplementary Fig. 4e).

Mapping site-speciﬁc N-glycosylation patterns in serum
While previous analyses have characterized global N-glycome altera-
tions associated with hepatopathy progression, a critical question
remains: how do site-speci ﬁc glycosylation events collectively con-
tribute to the serum glycome? To address this, we conducted ortho-
gonal N-glycoproteomics and proteomics analyses in DDA accucision
mode on a subset of the GZ-I cohort ( n = 120, 30 per group) to tackle
two key challenges: (1) determining whether glycomic changes result
from altered glycosylation enzyme activity (site-level regulation) or
shifts in glycoprotein abundance (global proteome variation), and (2)
identifying the primary glycoprotein carriers of disease-associated
glycans. We identiﬁed 3057 glycopeptides, 2824 site-speciﬁc glycans,
477 glycan compositions, 276 glycosylation sites, and 168 glycopro-
teins (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Of the 961 protein groups detected in
global proteomics data, 107 overlapped with those identi ﬁed in gly-
coproteomics (Supplementary Fig. 16b). Additionally, of the 201 gly-
cans detected in glycomics data, 148 were also identi ﬁed in
glycoproteomics (Supplementary Fig. 16c). Among all site-speci ﬁc
glycans (deﬁned as unique combinations of protein sites, proteins, and
glycan compositions), 2083 were consistently detected across all
samples and technical replicates.
We generated pseudo-glycome pro ﬁles by aggregating the
quantitative data of all site-speci ﬁc glycans sharing identical glycan
compositions (Fig.5a, Supplementary Fig. 17). These pseudo-glycome
proﬁles exhibited high concordance with the actual glycome, with an
average Pearson’s correlation coefﬁcient (r) of approximately 0.95. It
should be noted that the pseudo-glycome represents an estimation
strategy by aggregating signals across different proteins and glyco-
peptides, rather than a strict quantitative approach. While the high
consistency with the real glycome is indeed remarkable, this agree-
ment should be interpreted with caution given the potential biases
introduced by signal summation. Nevertheless, this approach
demonstrates the potential to interpret glycomics phenotypes at the
site-speciﬁc level, leveraging glycoproteomics as a complementary
tool. Notably, glycans displayed diverse site-speci ﬁc contributions
(Fig. 5b–d). For example, the most abundant glycan in the serum N-
glycome, H5N4S2, showed uniform contributions from multiple gly-
cosylation sites (Fig. 5b). In contrast, H5N5F1S1 was predominantly
associated with IGHM N46 and IGHA2 N92 (or IGHA2 N207) (Fig. 5c).
Glycans with similar structural features exhibited similar site con-
tributions (Fig.5d). For instance, tri- and tetra-antennary, fucosylated,
multisialylated glycans were primarily derived from A1AG1 N93,
whereas immature, agalactosylated complex or hybrid glycans were
mainly associated with IGHG1 N180 (or IGHG1 N299). These ﬁndings
reveal the heterogeneous site-speciﬁc contributions to the serum N-
glycome, predominantly driven by a subset of highly abundant
glycoproteins
27.

selecting only those SSGs exhibiting signi ﬁcant alterations indepen-

Alterations in N-glycosylationrelated enzymes and regulators
Protein N-glycosylation is a highly regulated process that includes
sequential steps of initiation, trimming or processing, branching and
elongation, and capping, which are controlled by the ordered actions
of a variety of glycosyltransferases, glycosidases, and other regulators
in the ER and Golgi compartments
28. To understand whether altera-
tions in these enzymes and regulators are involved in driving the N-
glycosylation changes observed in HCC, we analyzed gene expression
data from TCGA-LIHC dataset, focusing on 307 genes annotated to
involve in human N-glycosylation pathway in Reactome database
(Supplementary Data 7). A global upregulation of glycogenes was
observed in tumor liver tissues (Fig. 6a). We also found that a small
portion of the tumors exhibited glycogene expression patterns similar
to those of healthy tissue, implying the heterogeneity of glycosylation
in HCC tumors.
Differential expression analysis (DEA) revealed 118 upregulated
glycogenes (FDR < 0.05, log
2FC > 1) and only two downregulated gly-
cogenes (FDR < 0.05, log 2FC < −1) (Fig. 6b), indicating an overall
enhancement of N-glycosylation in tumor cells. Notably, DPAGT1, a
critical enzyme catalyzing the transfer of GlcNAc to dolichol phos-
phate, the initial step of N-glycan biosynthesis, exhibits a signiﬁcant
upregulation (1.9-fold). Similarly, multiple ALG family genes (e.g.,
ALG1/3/5/6/12), responsible for assembling the lipid-linked oligo-
saccharide precursor, also display increased expression levels (1.5 to 2-
fold). The oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) complex, essential for
transferring glycan precursors onto nascent polypeptides, is notably
upregulated. The catalytic subunit STT3A exhibits a 2.3-fold increase,
while other OST subunits, including DDOST, RPN1, RPN2, and TUSC3,
also show signi ﬁcant overexpression. This suggests that tumor cells
might enhance the ef ﬁciency of N-glycan attachment, facilitating the
glycosylation of a broader range of glycoproteins. In parallel, the ER-
resident molecular chaperones calnexin (CANX) and calreticulin
(CALR), which mediate glycoprotein folding quality control, are
upregulated (~2-fold). Collectively, these ﬁndings indicate that HCC
cells augment both the initial glycan biosynthesis and transfer steps,
reinforcing an enhancedN-glycosylation landscape.
Further supporting this trend, multiple N-acetylglucosaminyl-
transferases (GnTs) involved inN-glycan branching and elongation are
substantially upregulated. MGAT1 (GnT-I), an important enzyme cata-

structures, is upregulated (~1.4-fold), which is consistent with its
known association with dedifferentiation and malignancy in HCC
29.

Development and validation of machine learning
diagnostic models
Twenty-six N-glycans with a missing proportion less than 1% were
selected for training machine learning-based diagnostic models
(Methods). Of these, 20 exhibited signi ﬁcant differences across the
four groups, with 14 differing between healthy controls (HC) and
hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), 17 between chronic hepatitis B (CHB)
and HCC, and 13 between liver cirrhosis (LC) and HCC. All 26 glycans
with reliable detection were included in the models to capture
potential non-linear interactions. The GZ-I cohort was randomly divi-
ded into a training set (n = 526) and an internal validation set (n =2 1 8 ) .
Two external validation sets ( n = 186 for GZ-II cohort; n =1 4 4 f o r X Z
cohort) were used to assess the models’ generalizability (Fig.7a). Three
models were developed to classify HCC versus HC, HCC versus CHB,
and HCC versus LC samples. This approach supports a pre-screening
strategy, enabling tailored HCC diagnosis for patients with pre-existing
chronic liver diseases (Fig. 7b).
Optimal algorithms and hyperparameter con ﬁgurations were
selected using state-of-the-art AutoML techniques, guided by cross-
validation performance on the training set (Methods). All three models
demonstrated high classiﬁcation performance across the internal and
two external validation sets (Fig. 7c–e). On the internal validation set,
Model 1 (HCC vs. HC) achieved an ROC AUC of 0.93 (95% CI
0.892–0.975), Model 2 (HCC vs. CHB) an ROC AUC of 0.90 (95% CI
0.838–0.955), and Model 3 (HCC vs. LC) an ROC AUC of 0.84 (95% CI
0.770–0.907). Notably, all three models maintained consistent per-
formance in the external validation cohorts (GZ-II and XZ), with only a
slight decrease (Fig. 7c–e, Supplementary Fig. 21a), despite potential
differences in population characteristics and medical facilities,
demonstrating robust generalization and resilience to distribution
shifts.
Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), the most widely used biomarker for
HCC, is limited by its low sensitivity. In contrast, ourN-glycome-based
machine learning models demonstrated superior diagnostic perfor-
mance, detecting up to 80% of AFP-negative HCC cases (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 21b), highlighting their potential as a robust and sensitive
alternative for HCC diagnosis. Compared to AFP alone (Supplementary
Fig. 21c), all three models exhibited signiﬁcantly superior performance
(DeLong test, P = 0.001, 0.005, and 0.026 for Models 1, 2, and 3,
respectively). Combining glycans with AFP did not enhance perfor-
mance (Supplementary Fig. 21d), indicating that the N-glycome alone
captures all diagnostic information provided by AFP for HCC detec-
tion. These results highlight the high diagnostic performance and
robustness of our N-glycome-based models, which not only outper-
form the current clinical biomarker AFP but also demonstrate con-
sistent effectiveness across two independent external validation
cohorts.
We then evaluated the diagnostic performance of N-glycome
proﬁles when no pre-screening is available, i.e., differentiating HCC
and all non-HCC samples. The model, Model 4, had comparable per-
formance (ROC AUC: 0.87, 95% CI 0.818 –0.915) (Fig. 7f), better than
AFP (Supplementary Fig. 21c –d). To further assess the potential for
early diagnosis, we evaluated the performance of the four models
across different TNM stages (Supplementary Fig. 21e). All four models
achieved ROC AUCs above 0.80 for stages II, III, and IV, and they
retained acceptable diagnostic power even for stage I HCC patients,
with AUC values reaching 0.859 (HCC vs HC). These ﬁndings demon-
strate that our models hold strong promise for the early detection
of HCC.
We also evaluated the clinical utility of predicted probabilities from
Model 4 (PP4), for patient stratiﬁcation (Fig. 7g–l). PP4 exhibited sig-
niﬁcant differences across all groups, including the three control
groups (healthy controls, chronic hepatitis B, and liver cirrhosis;
Fig. 7g), indicating its potential as a reliable marker for monitoring liver
disease progression. Furthermore, PP4 demonstrated signiﬁcant asso-
ciations with ALBI stages (Fig.7h) and Child-Pugh classiﬁcations (Fig.7j),
with consistent patterns observed within HCC samples (Figs. 7i, k).
These ﬁndings highlight PP4’s robust capacity to reﬂect liver function
status. Additionally, PP4 showed signi ﬁcant variation across TNM
stages, with higher values corresponding to more advanced cancer
stages (Fig.7l), underscoring its utility in cancer staging.
We utilized the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) technique
to assess feature importance across the four diagnostic models
(Fig. 7m–p). Three glycans —H6N5F1S3, H5N4F1, and H3N4F1 —con-
sistently ranked as the top three features in all models, maintaining the
same order. Additionally, three other glycans —H5N2, H6N5S3, and
H5N4S1—consistently placed between the 4th and 6th ranks, though in
varying orders. The signiﬁcance of these six glycans was corroborated
by permutation feature importance analysis (Supplementary Fig. 21f).
Furthermore, the importance of these glycans was validated by train-
ing ML models using only the six most informative glycans (Supple-
mentary Fig. 22). The models maintained nearly the same performance
across all three cohorts, with only a slight, non-signi ﬁcant decrease
(DeLong test, P > 0.05). Individually, these glycans exhibited modest
classiﬁcation performance compared to the models (Supplementary
Fig. 23), suggesting that their collective contribution arises from
synergistic, non-linear interactions. These glycans originate from
diverse glycosylation sites: H5N2 is predominantly linked to IGHM
N46, H5N4F1 and H3N4F1 are primarily associated with immunoglo-
bulins (e.g., IGHM, IGHG1, IGHA2), while H6N5F1S3, H6N5S3, and
H5N4S1 have varied site origins (Supplementary Fig. 24a–f).

validation in Western populations with differing HCC etiologies.

validation of key glycosites is needed. Another limitation of our study

directly assessing the utility of PP4 for risk monitoring in cirrhotic

a powerful tool for liver disease management. By decoding the “gly-
cosylation language” of hepatic pathophysiology, we move closer to
personalized monitoring frameworks and enzyme-targeted therapies
for HCC.

Statistics & reproducibility
We collected as many samples as possible, far exceeding the sample
size estimated by GPower at a target power of 0.95. Throughout the
experimental procedures, samples were randomly positioned in 96-
well plates, and the experimenters were blinded to sample identities.
Quality control (QC) samples were distributed randomly in each plate
to ensure and evaluate reproducibility. During statistical analysis, we
manually excluded a small number of samples based on age and sex
(see Results) to balance these variables across groups. Even so, we
additionally controlled for age and sex as confounding variables in the
statistical analyses to eliminate potential biases. In the machine-
learning stage, all available samples were used, and no samples were
excluded.

Subtyping was performed with the ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.68.0)

For better clustering glycans with similar expression pattern along

Clustering (MCSC) algorithm (Supplementary Fig. 25). This algorithm
is speciﬁcally designed to cluster variables in the presence of natural
