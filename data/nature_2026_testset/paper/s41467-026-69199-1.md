Near-infrared and short-wave infrareddual-band detection has emerged as a
pivotal enabling technology in across diverse applicationsspanning material
identiﬁcation, biological diagnostics, and machine vision. Current dual-band
device architectures basedon vertically stacked photodetectors such as those
employing two-dimensional materials or back-illuminated colloidal quantum
dots remain constrained by limited large-area manufacturability and incom-
patibility with standard readout integrated circuits. Here, we report a top-
illuminated p-i-n-i-p dual-band photodetector using two distinct sizes of
solution-processed PbS colloidal quantum dots, which enables bias-switchable
spectral response between near-infrared and short-wave infrared regimes. The
device achieves a speciﬁc detectivity exceeding 1×1011 cm·Hz1/2·W−1 in both
bands, with short-wave infrared crosstalk of 0.5% and near-infrared crosstalk of
7.7%. The successful fabrication of a monolithic integrated 128×128 dual-band
focal plane array showcases a functionaldual-band infrared imager. This work
establishes a scalable and silicon-compatible platform toward high-perfor-
mance, low-cost dual-band infrared imagers.
Near-infrared (NIR, 780 –1100 nm) and short-wave infrared (SWIR,
1100–2500 nm) imaging technologies are garnering signiﬁcant inter-
est for applications spanning machine vision1, material identiﬁcation2,
and biological imaging3. Their utility in non-destructive analysis stems
from the sensitivity of these spectral bands to characteristic overtone
and combination vibrations of molecular bonds such as C –H, O –H,
N–H, and S–H
4, enabling the discrimination of materials based on their
distinct chemical ﬁngerprints. Furthermore, the differing scattering
and absorption characteristics of materials in the NIR and SWIR
regions result in distinct penetration depths for these two spectral
bands
5. This complementary behavior makes NIR/SWIR dual-band
imaging a highly effective approach for comprehensive material
characterization. A representative example can be found in agri-
cultural quality control, where the technique allows simultaneous
evaluation of surface texture and internal defects — such as bruising
and non-uniform ripening in apples 6. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, inte-
grating dual-band imaging into optical sorting systems enhances
machine vision for intelligent production, leading to more accurate
and automated quality grading. By routing only premium produce to
the fresh market and diverting substandard items to processed pro-
ducts (e.g., jam production), this technology helps minimize food
waste and strengthen supply chain sustainability.

Conventional dual-band NIR/SWIR imaging systems typically
employ hybrid con ﬁgurations of silicon (Si)-based NIR and indium
gallium arsenide (InGaAs)-based SWIR detectors, requiring complex
optical alignment and post-acquisition image fusion
7. Although such
systems achieve useful performance, their large footprint and high
cost restrict deployment in resource-constrained or portable settings.
Monolithic integration of dual-band photodetectors with readout
integrated circuits (ROICs) on a single substrate represents a promis-
ing alternative, enabling substantial miniaturization and system-level
cost reduction. Among integrated device architectures, vertically
stacked photodiodes with back-to-back junction design allow bias-
selectable spectral response while maintaining high pixel utilization,
an essential feature for high-resolution dual-band imaging
8.
Dual-band NIR and SWIR photodetectors based on back-to-back
junction architectures have been demonstrated using a variety of
material systems, including inorganic thinﬁlms (e.g., Si, Ge, GeSn)
9,10,
two-dimensional (2D) materials (e.g., MoS2, black phosphorus)11,12,a n d
colloidal quantum dots (CQDs) (e.g., PbS, HgTe) 13–15. However, Ge-
based photodetectors suffer from relatively low detectivity in the SWIR
range. Meanwhile, the practical deployment of 2D materials is ham-
pered by the absence of scalable synthesis and reliable hetero-
integration strategies, which limit their compatibility with focal plane
arrays (FPAs) manufacturing workﬂows
9.
In contrast, CQDs, particularly those based on PbS, have emerged
as a versatile material platform for both NIR and SWIR detection, owing
to their bandgap tunability, solution processability, and inner com-
patibility with ROICs
16.P e j o v ić et al. pioneered a vertically stacked n-p-
n PbS CQDs photodiode architecture that enables bias-selectable dual-
band detection across the NIR and SWIR regimes14. Nevertheless, this
device was implemented on a glass substrate using a back-illuminated
conﬁguration, rendering it incompatible with monolithic integration
onto ROICs. Furthermore, CQDs-based dual-band photodetectors
have so far been largely con ﬁned to single-pixel demonstrations,
where performance is compromised by high dark current and non-
negligible spectral crosstalk, thereby impeding their adoption in
practical imaging applications
17.
Here, we report a top-illuminated, p-i-n-i-p-structured dual-band
photodetector based on two distinct sizes of PbS CQDs, enabling bias-
switchable spectral response across the NIR and SWIR ranges. Through
band engineering, the device achieves a speciﬁc detectivity exceeding
1×1 0
11 cm·Hz1/2·W−1 in both bands, along with SWIR spectral crosstalk
of 0.5% and NIR crosstalk of 7.7%. By monolithically integrating the
CQDs photodetector array with a custom-designed ROIC featuring
bidirectional current sensing, we demonstrate a 128×128 dual-band
infrared FPA capable of NIR and SWIR imaging. This work establishes a
scalable, Si-compatible route to high-performance dual-band infrared
FPAs using solution-processed semiconductors.

Optical transparency was achieved through implementation of a

Through optimized synthetic protocols, we obtained different-
sized PbS CQDs with absorption peaks tailored to the NIR and SWIR
spectral windows, respectively (Fig.1c). Transmission electron micro-
scopy (TEM) conﬁrmed uniform size distributions, with mean particle
diameters of 2.9 nm and 7.5 nm (Supplementary Fig. 3). The batch-to-
batch reproducibility of the SWIR PbS CQD synthesis was illustrated in
Supplementary Fig. 4. Cross-sectional TEM image of the device
(Fig. 1d) demonstrates that the spin-coated PbS-I and PbS-II layers
exhibit good uniformity and surface smoothness. Energy dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis further con ﬁrms the presence of
sharp interfacial transitions between adjacent functional layers.
The dual-band photodetector operates under a back-to-back
diode conﬁguration, as schematically depicted in Fig.1e. By reversing
the bias direction across the device terminals, the NIR/SWIR photo-
diode can be selectively switched to a reverse-biased state, enabling
spectral response to corresponding wavelength bands (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 5). Additonally, the energy band alignment of the hetero-
structure before contact formation (Fig.1f and Supplementary Fig. 6)
shows a small conduction band offset (<0.4 eV), enabling ef ﬁcient
extraction of photogenerated electrons, while the signiﬁcant valence
band offset (>1.7 eV) creates a unipolar barrier that effectively blocks
hole transport.
Under forward bias, the built-in electric ﬁeld in the PbS-I layer
sweeps out NIR-generated carriers, whereas those produced in the
PbS-II layer are obstructed by the valence-band barrier (Fig. 1g). Con-
versely, under reverse bias, SWIR photocurrent generated in the PbS-II
layer is effectively collected, while NIR-generated carriers in PbS-I layer
are conﬁned (Fig. 1h). By exploiting a large valence band offset as a
unipolar barrier, this design not only curbs dark current by blocking
majority carriers without sacriﬁcing minority-carrier photocurrent, but
also spatially conﬁnes photogenerated carriers collection to respective
layers, thereby minimizing spectral crosstalk.
Dual-band performance characterization
The dark current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics (Fig.2a) exhibits
typical dual-band operation, with measured dark current densities as
low as 3.9 μA·cm
−2 at −1 V and 0.69 μA·cm−2 at +1 V, indicating effective
suppression of dark current. To elucidate the underlying transport
mechanisms, we conducted a detailed simulation analysis of the dark
current components. Under reverse bias, the dark current in the SWIR
PbS diode is dominated by generation-recombination (G-R) processes.
In contrast, under forward bias, trap-assisted tunneling (TAT) con-
stitutes the dominant contribution to the dark current in the NIR PbS
diode, which may be attributed to defect states introduced during ITO
sputtering
18, as corroborated by Supplementary Fig. 7. Notably, the
dark current levels attained in this dual-band photodetector are
comparable to those reported for single-junction devices under
reverse bias (Supplementary Table 1). To furhter evaluate the role of
the barrier layer in dark current suppression, we performed a com-
parative analysis of devices incorporating ZnO and tetra-
butylammonium iodide (TBAI)-treated PbS CQDs barrier layers
(Supplementary Fig. 8). The ZnO barrier layer reduces dark current by
three orders of magnitude relative to the TBAI-based counterpart,
identifying barrier height modulation as the principal mechanism for
majority carrier conﬁnement.

Optimization of spectral crosstalk
Spectral crosstalk represents a critical performance parameter for
dual-band photodetectors, as it directly impacts their ability to accu-
rately distinguish signals from distinct spectral bands
20. In the current
device architecture, the incomplete absorption of NIR light in the
relatively thin PbS-I layer leads to measurable NIR-to-SWIR crosstalk
under reverse bias. Although increasing the PbS-I layer thickness can
enhance NIR absorption and reduce spectral crosstalk, this approach
entails signi ﬁcant trade-offs. Owing to the inherently low carrier
mobility and high density of surface trap states in CQDs ﬁlms, exces-
sive thickening PbS-I layer promotes carrier recombination during
transport, thereby degrading responsivity in both the NIR and SWIR
bands (Supplementary Fig. 21). Given this constraint, we exploited
bias-dependent spectral response as an alternative strategy to mitigate
spectral crosstalk and achieve selective band detection. To quantify
the effect of bias modulation, we conducted calibration experiments
to establish a comprehensive responsivity matrix spanning various
biases and wavelengths (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 22).
The responsivity matrix reveals four distinct operational regions
deﬁned by bias ranges. In Region I (0.1 V to 1 V), the device exhibits a
strong response in the NIR band with minimal SWIR contribution.
Region II ( − 0.25 V to 0.1 V) is marked by weak photoresponses across
the entire spectrum. Region III ( − 0.25 V to −0.5 V) yields a strong
response combined with a weak NIR signal, whereas Region IV (− 0.5 V
to −1 V) produces a broad spectral response covering both NIR and
SWIR bands. In Region IV, the bottom diode (PbS-II) is reverse-biased,
and the top diode (PbS-I) is forward-biased (Fig. 1h). Under illumina-
tion, the resulting photocurrent is primarily contributed by electron-
hole pairs generated in PbS-II due to its broadband absorption char-
acteristics from NIR to SWIR. As the applied voltage decreases into
Region III, electron-hole pairs are simultaneously generated in both
diodes. However, under NIR illumination, opposing photocurrent
components cancel out, preserving the SWIR selective detection
(Fig. 3b ) .R e g i o nI I ,t h eb i a si si n s u fﬁcient to efﬁciently extract carriers
across the inter-layer energy barriers, leading to minimal responses in
both bands. Under positive voltage in Region I, only the top diode
operates in reverse bias, enabling selective NIR detection (Fig. 1g).
Thus, spectral photoresponse can be ﬁnely tailored through external
bias tuning.

Dual-band infrared imaging
A custom ROIC was designed to operate under both positive and
negative bias conditions, with its schematic diagram illustrated in
Supplementary Fig. 33. Under reverse bias, the photodetector gen-
erates a cathodic photocurrent that is injected into the capacitive
trans-impedance ampliﬁer (CTIA) input node, leading to a linear
decrease in output voltage during integration. Conversely, under for-
ward bias, the anodic photocurrent ﬂows out of the CTIA input,
resulting in a rising output voltage. By carefully tuning the DC bias
levels of the reference voltage and the internal operational ampliﬁers,
the output swing range is symmetrically conﬁgured to accommodate
both polarities of photocurrent integration. The fabrication process of
the dual-band FPA is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 34. The resulting
dual-band FPA features an array size of 128 × 128 with a 15 μmp i x e l
pitch, as shown in Fig. 4a. The dual-band imaging system, depicted in
Fig. 4b, utilizes a lens to focus tungsten light reﬂected from the target
onto the dual-band FPA. The ROIC controls the bias voltage of the FPA,
while a dedicated circuit board collects and processes the signals,
enabling image reconstruction through custom software. The system
achieves a frame rate of 333 Hz with a 2 ms integration time and 1 ms
readout time. The complete experimental setup is presented in Sup-
plementary Fig. 35.

To demonstrate the dual-band imaging capability, we constructed
a test scene comprising the Fudan University emblem printed on white
paper, partially overlaid with acrylic paint, and a Si wafer. Figure 4c
compares images captured using a standard complementary metal-
oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) image sensor with those captured by
the dual-band FPA operating in NIR and SWIR modes. In the NIR mode,
the dual-band FPA clearly resolved the emblem pattern beneath the
acrylic paint, leveraging the strong NIR transmission through organic
materials. Conversely, in the SWIR mode, the FPA successfully imaged
the emblem pattern obscured by the Si wafer, utilizing the character-
istic of SWIR light to transmit through Si. These results highlight the
complementary nature of NIR and SWIR imaging modalities, achieving
precise target identi ﬁcation in complex, layered scenarios through
spectral discrimination. As shown in Fig. 4d, different colored inks
exhibit distinct absorption behaviors across the NIR and SWIR bands.
Red ink remains undetectable in both NIR and SWIR images, whereas
green ink is clearly visible under NIR illumination but becomes faint
in the SWIR. In contrast, black ink is discernible in both spectral
bands. These marked differences in spectral response enable the
design of effective anti-counterfeiting markers. Figure 4e further
illustrates the differential absorption properties of water, acetone,
polyethylene (PE), and ink in the NIR and SWIR regions. When con-
tained in white PE bottles, water and acetone are not visible to the
naked eye under visible light. The NIR image reveals the water
meniscus but not that of acetone, whereas both menisci are clearly
observed in the SWIR image, with water appearing darker due to
stronger absorption. The ink markings on the bottle surface are
prominent in the NIR image but fade in the SWIR image. Additionally,
Supplementary Fig. 41 shows imaging results of the NIR/SWIR dual-
band FPA in other scenarios. Collectively, these results con ﬁrm that
the distinct NIR and SWIR absorption can be effectively utilized for
spectral identiﬁcation.

Discussion
In summary, we have developed a dual-band photodetector based on a
p-i-n-i-p unipolar barrier architecture incorporating two distinct sizes
of PbS CQDs as the NIR and SWIR absorbers. This structure enables
bias-switchable spectral selectivity, achieving peak detectivities of
3.04 × 10
11 cm·Hz1/2·W−1 in the NIR and 1.04 × 10 11 cm·Hz1/2·W−1 in the
SWIR band, respectively. By systematically optimizing the operating
voltage, spectral crosstalk was reduced to 0.5% for SWIR and 7.7% for
NIR. Monolithic integration of the photodetector with a custom-
designed ROIC enabled the realization of a 128×128 FPA with a 15 μm
pixel pitch. The resulting FPA demonstrates bias-selectable imaging
across both NIR and SWIR spectral regimes, showcasing its potential
for applications in target discrimination, food safety inspection, and
biological imaging.
Methods
Synthesis of PbS colloidal quantum dots
NIR-absorbing PbS CQDs were synthesized using a hot-injection
approach. A mixture of PbO (0.45 g), oleic acid (OA, 1.5 mL), and
octadecene (ODE, 22.5 mL) was degassed under vacuum (10
−3 mbar) at
100°C for 1 h until a clear solution formed. Under N 2 atmosphere,
bis(trimethylsilyl) sulﬁde (TMS 2S, 180 µL in 3 mL octadecene) was
swiftly injected, leading to rapid nucleation and a color change to dark
brown. The resulting PbS CQDs were puriﬁed with hexane and acetone,
then redispersed in octane at a concentration of 50 mg·mL
−1.
SWIR-absorbing PbS CQDs were synthesized using a two-step hot-
injection process. Lead oleate was ﬁrst formed by dissolving PbO
(0.446 g) in a mixture of ODE (22.5 mL) and OA (2.5 mL) under N 2 at
130°C for 1 h. The ﬁrst sulfur precursor (53µLT M S2Si n3m LO D E )w a s
injected and reacted for 20 min, followed by injection of the second
sulfur precursor (30 µLT M S
2S in 3 mL ODE) and further reaction for
15 min. The resulting PbS CQDs were puriﬁed with hexane and acetone,
then redispersed in octane at a concentration of 50 mg·mL−1.
Ligand exchange
The solution-phase ligand-exchange process was conducted in a N 2-
ﬁlled glovebox. For NIR-absorbing PbS CQDs, a ligand solution con-
taining PbI2 (0.01 M), PbBr2 (0.04 M), and ammonium acetate (0.06 M)
in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF). An octane solution of OA-capped
PbS CQDs (20 mg·mL
−1) was added, and the mixture was vigorously
shaken for 5 min to introduce phase transfer into DMF. After three
washes with hexane, the PbS CQDs were precipitated with toluene,
isolated by centrifugation, vacuum-dried for 20 min, and redispersed
in a butylamine (TBA)/DMF mixture (1:1 by volume) at 400 mg·mL
−1.
For SWIR-absorbing PbS CQDs, a mixed halide-based ligand
solution was prepared by dissolving PbI 2 (0.05 M), PbBr2 (0.04 M),
PbCl2 (0.05 M), and sodium acetate (0.044 M) in DMF. A PbS CQDs
solution (~6 mg·mL−1 in octane) was added, and the mixture was vig-
orously shaken to transfer the CQDs into the DMF phase. After
removing octane, the solution was washed three times with octane.
The PbS CQDs were then precipitated by adding toluene, centrifuged,
vacuum-dried for 20 min, and redispersed in a DMF/TBA mixture (1:1
by volume) at 300 mg·mL
-1.
Device fabrication
The dual-band PbS CQDs photodetectors were fabricated on Si/SiO 2
substrates pre-patterned with 5 nm Ti/10 nm Au bottom contacts. A
50 nm PbS-EDT layer was ﬁrst deposited via a layer-by-layer method.
The OA-capped PbS-CQDs were spin-coated at 2000 r.p.m. for 40 s,
treated with a 0.01% volume ratio of EDT in acetonitrile for 20 s and
then washed by acetonitrile, and this process was repeated twice.
Subsequently, the SWIR-absorbing layer (PbS-II, ~110 nm) was spin-
coated at 4,000 r.p.m. for 60 s from a 300 mg·mL
−1 CQDs inks. Next, a
50 nm C60 layer was thermally evaporated, followed by the deposition
of a 50 nm ZnO layer via magnetron sputtering, serving as the electron
transport layers. Furthermore, the NIR-absorbing layer (PbS-I,
~140 nm) was then spin-coated at 4000 r.p.m. for 60 s using
400 mg·mL
−1 CQDs ink and capped with two additional PbS-EDT layers.
Finally, a transparent ITO top electrode was deposited by magnetron
sputtering at room temperature.
Dual-band FPA fabrication
The ROIC for the dual-band FPA was fabricated in a 0.18 μmC M O S
process. After cleaning the chip, a Ti/Au (150/50 nm) bottom electrode
was patterned by UV lithography and electron beam evaporation,
expanding the pixel size to 12 × 12 μm. Two PbS-EDT layers were
deposited via spin-coated and solid-state ligand exchange. Subse-
quently, SWIR-absorbing PbS CQDs (PbS-II) were spin-coated from
solution using a liquid-phase ligand exchange process and annealed at
85°C for 15 min. Then, a 50 nm C
60 layer was thermally evaporated,
followed by a 50 nm ZnO layer deposited as electron transport layers
by magnetron sputtering. NIR-absorbing PbS CQDs (PbS-I) were spin-
coated, annealed under the same conditions, and covered with two
additional PbS-EDT layers via solid-state ligand exchange as hole
transport layers. Finally, a 100 nm ITO top electrode was sputtered to
complete the device structure. The completed dual-band FPA was
wire-bonded to a testing circuit board for performance evaluation.
Material characterization
Optical absorption spectra were recorded using a Hitachi U-4100 Plus
spectrophotometer. Cross-sectional TEM samples of PbS CQDs dual-
band photodetectors were prepared using the focused ion beam lift-
out technique on a Tescan GAIA3 system. TEM imaging and energy
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping were collected using a
Thermo Fisher TALOS F200X system. Ultraviolet photoelectron spec-
troscopy (UPS) was performed under ultrahigh vacuum (~10
−10 mbar)
using a KRATOS AXIS Ultra DLD system with a monochromatic HeI
(21.22 eV) excitation source.
Device characterization
The J-V curves and time-dependent photocurrent under dark condi-
tions and 940 nm illumination were measured using an MS200 system
equipped with a Keithley 6482 semiconductor analyzer. The transient
response and −3dB bandwidth were recorded using the same setup,
together with a PicoScope 4262 oscilloscope and an SR570 low-noise
current preampliﬁer (Stanford Research Systems). Noise currents of
the PbS CQDs photodiode were me asured using a semiconductor
parameter analyzer (FS-Pro, Primarius Technologies). The EQE spectral
at each wavelength was determined using a lock-in ampliﬁer (Stanford
Research Systems, SR865A), a chopper, an SR570 low-noise current
preampliﬁer, and a Horiba iHR350 spectrometer equipped with a
xenon lamp and a tungsten lamp.
