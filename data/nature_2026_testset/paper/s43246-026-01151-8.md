The control of heat transport in organic materials has attracted increasing attention for developing
thermal transistors and other heat-regulating devices. Here, we demonstrate continuous and
reversible modulation of the thermal conductivity ( κ
?) of polymer poly[2,5-bis(3-alkylthiophen-2-yl)
thieno[3,2-b]thiophene] (PBTTT) ﬁlms along the lamellar-stacking (out-of-plane) direction via
electrochemical doping. κ? values were quantitatively evaluated under gating conditions using a gold-
based time-domain thermore ﬂectance system. Upon electrochemical doping, κ? increased by 28%,

providing insight into the mechanism behind the increased κ

electrochemical doping enables reversible and controllable tuning of thermal conductivity, a key
requirement for thermal management applications, paving the way for future advancements in
thermally tunable organic materials.

TDTR measurements
In the TDTR measurements, as illustrated in Fig.1a, a pump pulse train with
a wavelength of 1550 nm, modulated at 400 kHz, irradiated the Mo (3 nm)/
Au (98 nm) transducer from the glass substrate side. Here, the Mo thinﬁlm
serves as an adhesion layer between the substrate and the Au electrode. The
generated heat dissipates bidirectionally—from the transducer to the sub-
strate and from the transducer tothe ion gel through the PBTTT thinﬁlm.
The change in reﬂectance of the transducer surface, caused by the surface
temperature rise induced by pump-laser heating, was detected by a delayed
probe pulse train with a wavelength of 520 nm, which was recorded as the
lock-in phase signal. Due to the sufﬁciently thick substrate and ion gel, the
entire sample can be modeled as a multilayer structure consisting of a glass
substrate, transducer (Mo/Au), PBTTT, and ion gel, with both ends treated
as semi-inﬁnite media. To ensure accurate evaluation of the thermophysical
properties of the PBTTT thin ﬁlms, we conducted preliminary TDTR
measurements on two different con ﬁgurations: substrate/transducer and
substrate/transducer/ion gel. From these measurements, we determined the
thermal effusivity (b), thermal diffusivity (α), and volumetric heat capacity
(C) of each layer, as well as the interfacial thermal resistance (R) between
adjacent layers. Details of the TDTR measurements and the obtained
parameters are provided in the “Methods” section and Supplementary
Table S1. To conﬁrm the validity of determined thermophysical values, we
changed the heat penetration depth by tuning the modulation frequencies:
200 and 400 kHz (Supplementary Fig. S1). We note that electrochemical
doping also occurs in the PBTTTﬁlm on top of the Au transducer. This was
conﬁrmed by UV –vis absorption measurements performed on PBTTT
ﬁlms deposited on Au electrodes, which show a characteristic bleaching of
visible absorption upon electrochemical doping (Supplementary Fig. S5),
indicating that electrochemical doping proceeds in the probed region.

Theoretical modeling
By considering the local structure of theﬁlms, phonon transport along the
lamellar stacking direction is primarily governed by non-covalent bonding
between PBTTTs, particularly near t he alkyl side chains. In these local
regions, phonon transport may exhibit coherent behavior rather than dif-
fusive. To evaluate the in ﬂuence of gate-induced TFSI intercalation on
thermal transport, we analyzed vari ations in the phonon transmission
across adjacent PBTTTs, with and without intercalation, using the atomistic
Green’s function (AGF) method
52,53 implemented in the QuantumATK
(2017. 2) software 54. The intercalated TFSI molecules are expected to
interact with the surrounding PBTTT matrix through Coulombic interac-
tions resulting from charge transfer into the host, in addition to van der
Waals interactions. However, accurately resolving the spatial charge dis-
tribution within TFSI and PBTTT remains a challenge. Additionally, pre-
vious studies on the intercalations of TFSI and F4TCNQ molecules into
PBTTT ﬁlms suggest that molecular size may in ﬂuence both the inter-
calation capability and the resultant change in κ
? of the host matrix.
Therefore, in this study, we modeled the intercalated TFSI molecule using a
charge-neutral bis(triﬂuoroethyl)amine (BTA) molecule, which is com-
parable in size to TFSI but lacks sulfoxide groups.
To describe interatomic interactions within PBTTT, BTA molecule,
and between them, the ReaxFF forceﬁeld parameterized by Müeller et al.55
was employed. The crystalline PBTTT structure, as illustrated in Fig.4a, was
constructed by reshaping a primitive unit cell into an orthorhombic-like cell
and replicating it along thex-a n dy-directions, which are perpendicular to
the heat conduction direction (i.e., the lamellar stacking direction). Based on
previous X-ray diffraction (XRD) results, intercalated dopants are expected
to reside near the alkyl side chains in expanded lamellar regions, rather than
being distributed within the polymer planes ( π–π spacing)56. However,
accurately identifying their positions is challenging because the intercalated
molecules can induce structural deformation in the side chains during the
intercalation process. Therefore, the doped structures cannot be uniquely
determined. Nevertheless, we focused on the position of the intercalated
BTA molecules relative to the alkyl side chains and considered two inter-
calated conﬁgurations: (i) BTA molecules were inserted near the alkyl side
chains after expanding the lamellar stacking distance. The fully optimized
structure is hereafter referred to as doped model 1 (Fig.4b). (ii) The second
conﬁguration, doped model 2 (Fig. 4c), was obtained by rotating the side-
chain angles in doped model 1 while keeping the lamellar stacking distance
ﬁxed, followed by structural optimization.
The primary structural differences between the two doped models are
whether the BTA molecules are aligned along the extension of the alkyl side
chains of neighboring PBTTT and the degree of distortion in the long-
itudinal alignment of the side chains. Alternatively, in terms of lamellar
stacking, doped model 2 can be regarded as an incommensurate stacking of
doped model 1. The resulting expansion in the lamellar stacking distance for
both doped models is approximately 11.1% compared to the crystalline case.
While this increase is smaller than the experimentally observed value for
nearly fully intercalated PBTTT samples (~30%)
36, it can be considered
reasonable given that additional effects associated with charge transfer were
not included in the present simulations. In the AGF calculations, two
additional structures with the same con ﬁguration were attached to the
optimized structure along the heat conduction direction, serving as semi-
inﬁnite thermal baths through which phonons are injected and transmitted.
Phonon transmission functions for the crystalline and doped PBTTT
structures were subsequently calculated over the two-dimensional Brillouin
zone transverse to the transport direction. Thermal conductance and the
corresponding effective thermal conductivity ( κ
ph) were then obtained
based on the Landauer formalism57 and the geometric dimensions of each
structure (see the details in the Supplementary Methods).

Conclusions
In summary, we evaluated the variation in thermal conductivity of PBTTT
thin ﬁlms under electrochemical gating using a custom-developed Au-
TDTR measurement system. The application of a gate voltage clearly altered
the TDTR signals, and corresponding continuous modulation of thermal
conductivity was observed, from 0.198 W m

0.253 W m−1 K−1 in the doped state at VG = −1.8 V, representing a max-

applying two different gate voltages modulates the thermal conductivity of
the ﬁlm in a reversible and repeatable manner. In addition, theoretical
