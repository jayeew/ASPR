Arginine vasopressin (AVP) and oxytocin (OT) are peptide hormones critical
for various physiological processes. Vasopressin receptor 1 A (V1aR), a primary
AVP target, is promising for central nervous system (CNS) disorders therapies,
yet the mechanisms of antagonism and oligomerization remain poorly
understood. Here, we present structures of human V1aR in its apo state and in
complexes with antagonists: atosiban, balovaptan, and SRX246. Structural
analyses reveal a dimeric V1aR assembly, validated by functional assays and
imaging in cells. The apo structure shows aﬂat extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) with
unpaired cysteines, undergoing signiﬁcant conformational changes upon
ligand binding. Antagonist-bound structures, combined with mutagenesis and
radioligand binding assays, uncover distinct binding modes and key deter-
minants for antagonism and selectivity. Theseﬁndings provide a compre-
hensive understanding of V1aR assembly and dynamic regulation, offering
valuable insights for structure-guided development of new antagonists tar-
geting dimeric V1aR for CNS disorders.
Arginine vasopressin (AVP) and oxytocin (OT) are structurally homo-
logous neuropeptides that regulate a variety of physiological pro-
cesses through the AVP/OT receptor family: oxytocin receptor (OTR)
and three vasopressin receptors (V1aR, V1bR, V2R)

Gq/11 proteins, activating calcium-dependent signaling and protein

Results
The overall structure of human V1aR
To obtain the structure of human V1aR, we constructed an expression
vector containing full-length wild-type human V1aR with a signal
peptide followed by a FLAG tag at its N-terminus. However, the protein
aggregated heavily upon puri ﬁcation using anti-FLAG resin under
various detergent conditions. To improve the biochemical behaviors
of the proteins, we truncated part of the unstructured N-terminus of
V1aR and introduced two mutations, S341C and N344K, which are a
subset of the eight mutations used previously to stabilize OTR
29 for
structural studies. The resulting construct, named V1aRcryo,w a sp u r -
iﬁed to homogeneity using Lauryl Maltose Neopentyl Glycol (LMNG)
and Cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS) as detergents. V1aRcryo in its apo
state eluted as a monodisperse peak on size-exclusion chromato-
graphy (Supplementary Fig. 1a), which made it a suitable construct for
cryo-EM analysis. The SDS-PAGE gel showed two bands, which likely
correspond to the monomeric and the SDS-resistant dimeric species.
To evaluate the functionality of the construct, we performed cell-
based ligand-binding assays to compare the ligand-binding properties
of V1aR
cryo with those of the wild-type receptor. V1aRcryo expressed at a
similar level to the wild-type protein (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Although
the N344K mutation renders V1aR signaling-incompetent 39,l i g a n d
binding property is mostly preserved. AVP binding to V1aR cryo was
modestly compromised, similar to the effects of equivalent mutations
on OT binding to OTR

Table 3). Notably, V1aR cryo remains highly sensitive to antagonists
atosiban, balovaptan, and SRX246, enabling investigation of their
molecular mechanisms, which are the focus of the current study
(Supplementary Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 3).
Next, we prepared V1aR
cryo in its apo form (V1aR cryo_apo) and
complex with atosiban (V1aR cryo_atosiban), balovaptan
(V1aRcryo_balovaptan), and SRX246 (V1aR cryo_SRX246) for cryo-EM
analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1a, c–e). The initial map of V1aRcryo was of
very limited resolution (data not shown), probably due to a lack of
sufﬁciently large features outside the micelle for accurate particle
alignment. To address this problem, we performed in vitro nanobody
screening (details are provided in Supplementary Information) and

We then subjected these four samples to single-particle cryo-EM
analysis and determined high-resolution structures at an overall
resolution of 2.6-3.0 Å assessed by the gold standard of Fourier shell
correlation (FSC) = 0.143 (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4, and
Supplementary Table 1). The high-quality maps for all four structures
allow for reliable model building of the majority parts of V1aR,
including all transmembrane (TM) regions as well as extracellular
loops (ECLs). Decent local densities also facilitated accurate docking of
ligands and placement of cholesterol molecules, as well as the
assignment of water molecules in the structure of V1aR
cryo_SRX246
(Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 5). The ﬂexible N-terminal and
C-terminal region of V1aR and some cytosolic loops between TMs such
as intracellular loop 2(ICL2) were not modeled due to maps not
resolved very well in those regions. Although Nb1 was not resolved well
in the ﬁnal high-resolution reconstructions, the 6 Å low-pass-ﬁltered
map reveals extra densities adjacent to the cytosolic face of V1aR that
closely match the shape of Nb1 (Supplementary Fig. 5e), indicating that
Nb1 engages a conformationally heterogeneous epitope on the intra-
cellular side of the receptor. We therefore infer that Nb1 serves as a
ﬁducial mask suf ﬁcient to enable early particle classi ﬁcations and
global low-resolution alignment. However, its ﬂexible orientation
relative to the receptor leads to partial signal averaging and eventual
loss of nanobody density during high-resolution reﬁnement.
The dimer architecture
Previous studies have demonstrated that V1aR can form homodimers
or heterodimers with related receptors such as V2R and OTR37,38.B i o -
luminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) analyses have shown
that these dimeric arrangements remain stable regardless of agonist or
antagonist treatment, suggesting that ligand binding does not sig-
niﬁcantly alter the dimerization state
38. These insights have also
facilitated the development of a dimeric agonist for OTR40. Unlike the
previously reported monomeric structures of other receptors in this
family, our cryo-EM structures reveal V1aR in a dimeric form, observed
in both the apo state and antagonist-bound states (Fig. 1). We also
observed a small population of particles with a smaller diameter in raw
images, likely corresponding to the monomeric species, and tried to
select these during particle picking. However, due to their small size
and low abundance, these particles were lost during subsequent data
processing. In contrast, no clear trimeric or tetrameric particles were
detected in our cryo-EM datasets-unlike CXCR4, for which recent cryo-
EM studies have visualized trimeric and tetrameric assemblies
41.
The dimer interface of V1aR is quite extensive, primarily mediated
by TM1, with additional contributions from the extracellular region of
TM2 and the cytosolic region of TM7 (Fig. 2a). A comparison of the
four structures shows that the dimers of V1aR_apo, V1aR_balovaptan,
and V1aR_SRX246 share highly similar overall architectures. However,
the two copies of V1aR_atosiban adopt a slightly different angle within
the membrane compared to the others (Supplementary Fig. 6a), sug-
gesting relative plasticity of the dimer interface.

The ligand-free state
ECL2 in GPCRs is well-known for its length variation and structural
diversity, playing crucial roles in receptor signaling
57–59.O u rs t r u c t u r e s
reveal that ECL2 of V1aR adopts distinct conﬁgurations in the apo state
compared to the antagonist-bound states (Fig. 3a), whereas the ECL2
conformations in the antagonist-bound states are very similar (Sup-
plementary Fig. 6b). In the antagonist-bound states, ECL2 adopts an
“up” conformation, forming an antiparallel β-sheet fold that projects
away from the binding pocket at an approximately perpendicular
orientation relative to the membrane (Fig.3a). This “up” conformation
of ECL2 creates a large, solvent-exposed cavity capable of accom-
modating ligands. In the apo state, the majority of ECL2 was modeled
reliably, supported by well-de ﬁned local densities (Fig. 3ba n dS u p -
plementary Fig. 6c). Notably, two segments that form the antiparallel
β-sheet fold observed in the antagonist-bound states adopt alpha-
helical conformation in the apo structure: one becomes a continuation
of TM4, while the other inserts into the ligand-binding pocket (Fig.3a
and Supplementary Fig. 6c). The remainder of ECL2 (residues 193–201,
sequence between the two beta-sheet segments) is visible as con-
tinuous density in the 6 Å low-pass- ﬁltered map (Supplementary
Fig. 5f), but could not be con ﬁdently modeled. Given the relatively
planar orientation of ECL2 relative to the membrane in the apo
structure, we designate this arrangement the“ﬂat” conformation.

Comparison of AVP/OT family-antagonist interactions
We ﬁrst observed that SRX246 exhibits a modular architecture, with
distinct chemical moieties occupying spatially separable regions of the
V1aR binding pocket. Leveraging this feature, we used SRX246 as a
reference scaffold to compare antagonist binding modes across
receptors in the AVP/OT family
29,35. For clarity, substructures extend-
ing into zones A and D were collectively assigned to the “upper
pocket”.T h e “upper pocket” comprises the extracellular-facing por-
tion of the orthosteric cleft, including the interface with the extra-
cellular loops (ECLs). In contrast, moieties corresponding to zones B
and C were assigned to the “bottom pocket” that is the deeper trans-
membrane cavity (Fig. 6a).
We then aligned available antagonist –receptor complex struc-
tures to the V1aR–SRX246 complex to compare ligand binding poses.
This analysis revealed that AVP/OT-family antagonists can be broadly
grouped into three classes based on their penetration depth and
extent of pocket engagement. Class I is represented by atosiban, a
macrocyclic nonapeptide antagonist that, like SRX246, spans nearly
the full width and depth of the orthosteric site —engaging both the
upper and bottom pockets. It further extends toward the pocket
mouth of the binding pocket to make auxiliary contacts with polar
residues on TM1. Class II is exempliﬁed by mambaquaretin-1 (MQ1), a
compact, toxin–like mini-protein antagonist. In contrast to SRX246,
MQ1 primarily occupies the upper pocket and forms extensive con-
tacts with the ECLs, with limited insertion into the deeper transmem-
brane cavity. Class III consists of small-molecule antagonists such as
retosiban, balovaptan, and tolvaptan. These ligands are enriched in
hydrophobic and heteroaromatic ring systems and preferentially
occupy the bottom pocket. Unlike peptide- or protein-like antagonists,
these compounds make limited contact with the ECLs and instead
exploit the hydrophobic subcavityat the base of the orthosteric site.
Discussion
Due to its involvement in CNS functions such as social behavior, cog-
nition, and mood regulation, V1aR, a member of the AVP/OT receptor
family, represents an attractive therapeutic target for the treatment of
CNS disorders. In this study, cryo-EM structures of V1aR in its apo form
and antagonist-bound states, combined with cell-based analyses and
radioligand binding experiments, provide key insights into the
receptor’s dimeric assembly, the unusual conformation of ECL2 in the
apo state, and the mechanistic details of antagonism mediated by
three distinct antagonists.
In contrast to the monomeric structures of other receptors within
the same family, the V1aR structures presented here reveal a relatively
stable dimeric assembly, consistent with previous studies of receptor
assembly in cells
37,38. Sequence alignment of receptors within this
family revealed the conserved properties of residues involved in
dimerization (Supplementary Fig. 12), suggesting that receptors within
this family may homodimerize or heterodimerize through a similar
interface and mechanism. Nevertheless, monomeric V1aR may also
exist at the cell membrane, which could allow for dynamic dimer
exchange, either homodimerization of V1aR or heterodimerization
with other receptors in the AVP/OT f amily. Although the dimer inter-
face exhibits some degree of ﬂexibility, the interaction modes remain
consistent, featuring polar interactions on the extracellular side–with
K52
1.33 identiﬁed as a key residue–and hydrophobic interactions within
the membrane environment, mediated by multiple residues with small
hydrophobic side chains. These structural ﬁndings were further vali-
dated using the smPB assay, which con ﬁrmed the homodimeric
assembly of V1aR on the cell surface, even under low expression levels.

To compare ligand selectivity and subtype determinants, we
enumerated residue-level contacts for each antagonist and mapped
them onto the corresponding receptor structures (Supplementary

partitioned into a major subpocket and a minor subpocket. The major
subpocket, enclosed by TM4/TM5/TM6, is dominated by conserved
transmembrane residues that primarily mediate hydrophobic packing.
Notably, M220

across the AVP/OT family and acts as a local shape determinant for the
TM4/TM5/TM6 hydrophobic cavity; in line with our functional data,
this residue is an important contributor to the selectivity of SRX246 for
V1aR over other family members. The minor subpocket, bordered by
TM7/TM1/TM2/TM3, provides mixed hydrogen-bond and hydro-
phobic interactions. Marked sequence diversity on TM7 renders the
geometry and polarity of this region highly variable among subtypes.
Notably, A334

contributes to the selectivity of balovaptan for V1aR. These ﬁndings
suggest that the rational design of a new antagonist capable of
simultaneously favoring interactions with both A334

that were identi ﬁed to have strong effects on binding are V217

In summary, the current study provides a comprehensive under-
standing of the dimeric assembly of V1aR, uncovers its apo state with a
distinct ELC2 conformation, and elucidates the binding modes and
mechanisms of antagonism for three distinct antagonists. Theseﬁnd-

receptors within the same family, paving the way for future in silico
rational drug design of small-molecule and biologic antagonists.
