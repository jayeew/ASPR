Neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE) is a severe complication of systemic lupus
erythematosus (SLE). Despite its high morbidity, the exact pathogenesis of NPSLE remains poorly
understood, and effective therapeutic options remain unavailable. Here we show gut bacterium
Lactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri) and its metabolite indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) play key roles in the
progression of NPSLE. L. reuteri and IAA induce behavioral de ﬁcits, microglial activation, pro-
inﬂammatory cytokine secretion, neuronal loss, and blood brain barrier (BBB) disruption in female
lupus-prone mice. Mechanistic studies show that IAA activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)
and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathways in microglia, thereby
upregulating inﬂammatory responses and exacerbating neuroinﬂammation. These ﬁndings suggest a
critical role for gut-microbiota-metabolite-brain axis in NPSLE pathogenesis and provide insights into
potential therapeutic targets.
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disorder
characterized by excessive production of autoantibodies, immune complex
formation, and persistent inﬂammation that leads to multi-organ damage
1.
Neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) is a severe complication of SLE that affects
both the neurological and psychiatric systems. Patients with NPSLE fre-
quently present with symptoms such as cognitive dysfunction, seizures,
headaches, and various psychiatric disorders, all of which signi ﬁcantly
impair patients’ quality of life and prognosis. The prevalence of NPSLE
remains still uncertain, but current reports indicate a range between 12%
and 95% in SLE patients

causes of death in SLE patients next to lupus nephritis3–5. Besides its high
morbidity rates and poor prognosis, the pathogenesis of NPSLE remains
unknown and only limited studies are available.
Gut microbiota resides in the gastrointestinal tract and plays a crucial
role in regulating host physiology, metabolism, immune response, and self-
defense against pathogenic microorganisms
6,7. Recent studies demonstrated
signiﬁcant associations between gut m icrobiota composition and SLE
occurrence and severity 8,9. An imbalance in the ratio of Firmicutes to
Anaplasma phagocytophilum has been observed in patients with SLE 10.
Gut microbiota exerts both harmful and bene ﬁcial effects in different
animal models of lupus 11. The gut-brain axis is a bidirectional commu-
nication system linking the gut and the central nervous system (CNS).
This interaction is mediated through metabolic, neural, and endocrine
pathways
12. Increasing evidence suggests that gut microbiota plays a
role in the development of CNS-related diseases, including Alzheimer ’s
disease, anxiety disorders, and depression, through mechanisms such as
metabolite production, neurotr ansmitter modulation, and other

However, the relationship between gut microbiota and NPSLE remains
unclear. In this study, we report thatLactobacillus reuteri (L. reuteri)and its
metabolite indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) are closely associated with the pro-
gression of NPSLE. Mechanistically, IAA promotes the activation of the aryl

tion of the STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3) sig-
naling pathway and upregulation of inﬂammatory factors, thereby driving
the progression of NPSLE.

Behavioral tests
The elevated plus maze test (EPM) consists of two open armss (30 × 5 cm)
and two closed armss (30 × 5 x 15 cm), arranged in a cross conﬁguration,
with the intersection considered the central zone. Mice were placed in the
central zone, and the number of entries into each arm during a 5-min period
was recorded using the DigBehv Video Analysis System (Shanghai, China).
In the open ﬁeld test (OFT), mice were individually placed in a black
open-ﬁeld box measuring 40 × 40 x 30 cm, situated in a quiet room to
minimize external disturbances. Each mouse was gently introduced into a
corner of the box and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. The box was
divided into peripheral and central areas based on a grid, and a video
tracking system was used to record the mice ’s movements. The primary
measure of anxiety was the time spent by the mice in the central area.
The sucrose preference test (SPT) was performed to evaluate
anhedonia-like behavior in mice. Brie ﬂy, mice were habituated to two
identical bottles containing water f or 24 h to eliminate side preference.
Subsequently, one bottle was replaced with a 1% (w/v) sucrose solution,
while the other contained plain water. The positions of the two bottles were
switched every 12 h to avoid location bias. Mice were allowed free access to
both bottles for 24 h. The volume of sucrose solution and water consumed
was recorded, and sucrose preference was calculated using the formula:
Sucrose preference (%) = Volume of sucrose solution/Volume of sucrose
solution+ water consumed.
In the novel object recognition test (NOR), two identical objects were
placed in a box, and the mice were allowed to move freely for 5 min. After
1 h, one of the objects was replaced with a novel object, and the mice were
reintroduced to the box for an additional 5 min of exploration. The time
spent exploring the familiar and novel objects was recorded. The Dis-
crimination Index (%) was calculated as [(time spent exploring the novel
object)− (time spent exploring the familiar object)]/[(time spent exploring
the novel object)+ (time spent exploring the familiar object)].
The Morris water maze (MWM) test was conducted in a 100 cm
diameter pool divided into four quadrants. A platform with a diameter of
7 cm was positioned 1 cm below the wat er surface. During the training
phase, each mouse underwent four trials per day forﬁve consecutive days. In
each trial, the mouse was released into the water from one of four different
quadrants, with a maximum trial duration of 60 s. If the mouse failed toﬁnd
the platform within this time, it was gently guided to the platform and
allowed to remain there for 20 s beforebeing returned to its home cage. On
day 6, the platform was removed, and the trajectories of the mice were
recorded over a 60-s period. On the sixth day (probe trial), the hidden
platform was removed, and each mouse was released from a random
quadrant and allowed to swim for 60 s. The number of platform crossings,

Fecal microbiota from NPSLE patients aggravates brain damage
in mice
To evaluate whether gut microbio ta from patients with SLE or NPSLE
induces brain damage, we prepared fecal suspensions from these patients
and performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to wild-type C57BL/
6J mice daily via gavage after two weeks of intestinal decontamination with
antibiotics mixture (Fig.1A). After mice received FMT for two weeks, we
performed both elevated plus maze test and openﬁeld test to assess mouse
anxiety-like behaviors. Compared to the FMT-SLE mice, FMT-NPSLE mice
spent signiﬁcantly shorter time in the open armss from the elevated plus
maze test (Fig.1B) and exhibited a trend toward reduced exploration of the
center region in the open ﬁeld test (Fig. 1C), suggesting increased anxiety-
like behaviors in mice received FMT from NPSLE patient gut microbiota.
Importantly, there was no signiﬁcant difference in total distance traveled in
the OFT between the two groups, suggesting that the behavioral differences
were not due to altered locomotor ac tivity (Supplementary Fig. 1B). To
assess depression-like behavior, we conducted the sucrose preference test.
FMT-NPSLE mice showed a reduced pr eference for sucrose solution
compared to FMT-SLE mice, indicating the presence of anhedonia, a core
feature of depressive-like behavior (Supplementary Fig. 1A). We performed
the novel object recognition test to e valuate mouse cognitive function
changes. FMT-NPSLE mice exhibited a signiﬁcantly lower discrimination
index than that of FMT-SLE mice, indi cating reduced exploration of the
novel object (Fig.1D). Subsequently, we performed the Morris water maze
test to assess mouse learning and mem ory abilities. During the training
phase, FMT-NPSLE mice demonstrated longer escape latencies than the
FMT-SLE mice (Fig. 1E). During the testing period, FMT-NPSLE mice
crossed the target platform at fewer times and spent less time in the target
quadrant than did the FMT-SLE mice (Fig. 1F, G and Supplementary
Fig. 1C), indicating potential cognitive impairments in FMT-NPSLE mice.

Discussion
NPSLE is a severe manifestation of SLE and is associated with high mor-
bidity and mortality. Yet, its exact pathogenic mechanisms remain uncer-
tain. This study addresses this knowledge gap by identifyingL. reuteri as a
key contributor to the exacerbation of NPSLE. Speciﬁcally, we demonstrated
that L. reuteri and its metabolite IAA activate the AHR/STAT3 signaling
pathways in microglia, leading to the upregulation of pro-in ﬂammatory
cytokines. These ﬁndings reveal a previously underexplored mechanism
through which gut microbiota drives neuroinﬂammation in NPSLE.

integrity35,36. In the present study, antibiotic pretreatment induced a trend of
increased BBB permeability in wild-type mice. Notably, mice that received
FMT from SLE patients exhibited a si milar degree of BBB disruption to
those subjected to antibiotic treatm ent alone, suggesting that the SLE-
derived microbiota neither exacerba ted nor ameliorated the antibiotic-
induced damage. In stark contrast, FMT from NPSLE patients resulted in
the most profound increase in BBB permeability. Theseﬁndings collectively
suggest that the gut microbiome in NPSLE patients may harbor a unique
propensity to compromise BBB integrity, potentially distinguishing it from
the microbiota associated with SLE patients. As a commonly regarded
probiotic, L. reuteri has been associated with strengthening the intestinal

A limitation of this study is that we examined the role ofL. reuteriand
IAA only in the MRL/lpr mouse model of lupus, without investigating their
effects in other spontaneous or induced lupus models. The challenging
nature of sample collection resulted in a small clinical sample size, high-
lighting the need for a larger cohort i n future clinical investigation. In
conclusion, our study demonstrates thatL. reuteri and its metabolite IAA
activate microglia and exacerbate NPSLE through the AHR/STAT3 path-
way (Fig.7). Observations from this study helped understand the role of gut
microbiota and microbial metabolites in brain damage from NPSLE
patients suggested possible therapeutic targets for mitigating CNS damage
in NPSLE.
