Cytotoxic T lymphocytes play a crucial role in anti-tumour immunity, with
granzyme B (GrB) being a decisive factor in this process. Developing a GrB-
based delivery system to mimic T cell-based immunotherapy holds promise
but remains challenging. Here, we present an artiﬁcial metalloenzyme (nano-
zyme) with GrB-like protease activitycapable of inducing caspase-dependent
cell apoptosis. The nanozyme is based on the site-speciﬁc binding of Pd(II) ions
to recombinant human heavy chain apo-ferritin nanocages to generate a
binuclear catalytic centre consistingof two Pd atoms with bridging cysteine
ligands, monodentate methionine and histidine residues, and two water
molecules. We show that encapsulationof the Pd-ferritin complex within
membrane-fused lipid nanoparticles comprising surface-displayed single-
chain antibodies affords GrB-mimicking nanozyme-based nanovesicles cap-
able of receptor-mediated delivery of the nanozyme into the cytoplasm of
tumour cells and induction of caspase-dependent apoptosis. This study pro-
vides valuable insights into the construction of nano-delivery systems with
artiﬁcial GrB activity and presents a promising therapeutic option for solid
tumours.
The primary objective of tumour immunotherapy is to generate and
deliver tumour-speciﬁc cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) to tumour
sites1,2. The induction of cell apoptosis by granzyme B (GrB), which is
the predominant serine protease stored within secretory granules of
CTLs, is widely regarded as the key mechanism employed by CTLs to
eliminate malignant tumour cells
3,4. Upon recognition of the target
tumour cells, the activated CTLs deliver GrB through the pore-forming
protein perforin into the cytoplasm of target cells, thereby initiating
cell apoptosis by mediating the cleavage of intracellular pro-caspases.
One representative example that utilises this mechanism is chimeric
antigen receptor (CAR)-T therapy, where T cells are genetically
engineered to express a single-chain variable fragment (ScFv). This
innovative approach has demonstrated promising outcomes in the
treatment of speciﬁc cancer types, such as acute lymphoblastic leu-
kaemia and lymphomas
5–7. However, in solid tumours, CAR-T cell
therapy continues to encounter several challenges, including but not
limited to off-target effects, T cell exhaustion and ineffective T cell
inﬁltration
8–12. These issues largely stem from the insuf ﬁcient recog-
nition of tumour cells by CAR-T cells and the physical barriers that
hinder effective CAR-T cell delivery. Thus, it is crucial to explore
potential alternative strategies for overcoming the obstacles of T cell-
based immunotherapy.

In recent years, the development of cell-mimicking delivery sys-
tems has emerged as a promising approach in the ﬁeld of nanomedi-
cine. Leveraging their nanoscale size, these systems are engineered to
enhance targeting capabilities and overcome biological barriers,
improving the delivery of therapeutic agents to solid tumours by
replicating the natural characteristics and functions of cells
13–18.F o r
example, a smart strategy for mimicking CTL killing was developed by
arming platelets with GrB and perforin
19. These biomimetic nano-
complexes exhibit the ability to capture circulating tumour cells and
actively target nascent metastatic lesions, resulting in effectively sup-
pressing lung metastasis in tumour models. However, the high cost
and intrinsic instability of GrB-based delivery systems represent major
barriers to their practical application. These challenges highlight the
potential of synthetic delivery vehicles embedding arti ﬁcial GrB-like
enzymes as an alternative strategy for tumour immunotherapy, an area
in which GrB mimics have rarely been reported.
In this work, we describe an alternative approach using recom-
binant human heavy chain apo-ferritin nanocages (iron-free form of
ferritin nanocages, FTn) to construct a palladium (Pd)-containing
metalloenzyme (nanozyme, FTn-Pd) that speciﬁcally cleaves the same
substrates as natural GrB and when encapsulated within synthetic
vesicles induces caspase-dependent cell apoptosis (Fig. 1a). We use
protein crystallography analysis to determine the location of the Pd(II)
active sites on the surface of the 24-mer FTn and to reveal the potential
catalytic mechanism associated with nanozyme-mediated substrate
cleavage. Having established the GrB-like activity of the nanozymes, we
develop a conceptual design for GrB-mimicking nanozyme-containing
nanovesicles (GMNVs) that recapitulate the membrane properties of

Ferritin-based nanozymes with granzyme B-like activity
One of the critical factors for the de novo design of desired arti ﬁcial
enzymes is the choice of an appropriate protein scaffold. In this regard,
we recently employed recombinant human heavy chain FTn as a highly
symmetric protein scaffold to synthesise various metal-based nano-
particles/clusters for mimicking peroxidase activity
20,21,s c a v e n g i n g
superoxide radicals22 or inducing NO generation 23.I nt h i ss t u d y ,w e
sought to utilise FTn as a nanoscale scaffold for the development of
nanozymes with distinct metal-ligand active sites engineered speciﬁ-
cally for replicating the protease activity of GrB. Initially, we prepared a
range of metal-protein complexes by direct binding of different metal
ions to FTn and tested their nanozyme activity in the presence of the
GrB-speciﬁc substrate Ac-IETD-pNA (Ac-Ile-Glu-Thr-Asp-para-nitroani-
line) (Fig. 2a, b). Of the 10 metal-ferritin complexes studied, most
showed no (Ru, Rh, Ir, Cu, Au, Co, Fe) or minimal activity (Mn, Pt).
However, binding of Pd(II) to FTn (FTn-Pd) resulted in high protease-
like activity with amide bond cleavage of the derivatized tetrapeptide
substrate being completed, with 60 min in the enzymatic reaction
buffer at 37 °C (Fig. 2b). Notably, FTn-Pd did not exhibit peroxidase
(POD)-mimicking activity (Supplementary Fig. 1a), a primary inducer of
free radicals (such as ·OH). However, upon reduction of the bound
Pd(II) to Pd(0) using sodium borohydride (NaBH
4), the resulting FTn-
Pd(0) exhibited POD-mimicking activity (Supplementary Fig. 1b).
Given these observations, we investigated whether FTn-Pd could
directly induce the cleavage of pro-caspase-3 and therefore mimic the
speciﬁc functional operation of native GrB. Western blot analysis of the
reaction mixture indicated that the concentration of the cleaved active
form of caspase-3 increased progressively at higher concentrations of
FTn-Pd (Fig. 2c). To further validate the GrB-like activity of FTn-Pd
while excluding POD-like activity, inhibitors for GrB and POD were
introduced in the pro-caspase-3 cleavage assay. Western blot analysis
demonstrated that all three GrB inhibitors signi ﬁcantly impeded the
production of cleaved caspase-3, whereas POD inhibitors did not
(Fig. 2d), conﬁrming the GrB-like activity in the developed nanozyme.

To further understand the GrB-like activity, we ﬁrst investigated
the structural properties of FTn-Pd. Transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) imaging of the recombinant apo-ferritin prior to exposure to
Pd(II) ions showed a hollow structure after negative staining, with an
outer shell diameter of 12 nm and inner cavity of 8 nm (Fig. 2f). In
contrast, intact FTn-Pd nanocages were directly observed under TEM
without negative staining, presumably due to the increased electron
density associated with the incorporation of Pd atoms. Inductively
coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) indicated the presence of
a total of 105 Pd atoms per FTn, corresponding to ca.4P da t o m sp e r
protein subunit. The Pd atoms were strongly bound and did not readily
detach from the protein when incubated in dialysis buffer over time,
regardless of the presence of caspase-3 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Pro-
tein crystallography was employed to determine the Pd binding
regions within FTn. Protein crystals were prepared using the hanging
drop vapour diffusion method, and the structure of the FTn-Pd crystals
was reﬁned to 1.85 Å resolution (Supplementary Table 1). Two promi-
nent Pd binding regions, site A (M37/C90/C102/H105) and site B
(H173), were revealed within the structurally intact polypeptide shell
(Fig. 2g). Site A was located within a single subunit and contained two
Pd atoms (Pd1 and Pd2) with occupancies of 0.8 and 0.8, respectively
(Supplementary Table 2), resulting in a total of 24 site A Pd(II) atoms
exposed on the exterior surface of each protein nanocage. The
binuclear centre consisted of two bridging cysteine residues along
with monodentate histidine (Pd1) and methionine (Pd2) side chains,
and two water molecules coordinated with either Pd1 or Pd2. In con-
trast, site B consisted of a single Pd atom (Pd3), which was coordinated
to four histidine residues of four different FTn subunits located along
the six four-fold axes associated with molecular channels in the poly-
peptide shell
25,26. To elucidate whether the sites played a pivotal role in
determining GrB-like activity, two FTn mutants were constructed by
modifying all four residues of site A (mutant H4-FTn; M37A/C90R/
C102A/H105Q) and the single histidine of site B (mutant H1-FTn;
H173A). In both cases, an approximately 50% reduction in the level of
Pd binding was observed (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). However, muta-
tion at site B (H1-FTn-Pd) did not affect the GrB-like activity. In con-
trast, cleavage of Ac-IETD-pNA by H4-FTn-Pd was reduced to the
background levels observed for FTn or Pd(II) alone (Fig.2h), indicating
that site A was functionally important for GrB-mimicking activity. This
was conﬁrmed by protein crystallography analysis at 1.53 Å resolution,
which revealed that the mutation at site A was associated with the
absence of Pd1 and Pd2 atoms (Fig. 2i).
Speciﬁcity and proposed catalytic mechanism of FTn-Pd
nanozyme
To evaluate the catalytic speciﬁcity of the FTn-Pd nanozyme, several
tetrapeptide substrates were selected, including IEAD (Bid cleavage
site), IQAD (caspase-7 cleavage site), VETD (caspase-8 cleavage site),
IETD (caspase-3 cleavage site), and IETA (mutant caspase-3 cleavage
site). The activities of these substrates in response to FTn-Pd were
examined. Molecular docking analysis showed that IETD had the most
stable docking position, placing it just 3.1 Å from the catalytic centre of
FTn-Pd (site A). In contrast, the distances for IEAD, IQAD, and VETD
were signi ﬁcantly greater, at 13.6 Å, 10.2 Å and 10.0 Å, respectively
(Fig. 3a). When IETD was mutated to IETA, the distance between the
docking site and site A increased to 19.5 Å. The speci ﬁc cleavage of
IETD by FTn-Pd was further con ﬁrmed using HPLC analysis (Fig. 3b).
Additionally, western blot analysis demonstrated that FTn-Pd exhib-
ited no cleavage activity against recombinant pro-caspase-7, pro-
caspase-8 or Bid (Supplementary Fig. 3d–f). These results indicate that
the FTn-Pd speci ﬁcally cleaves caspase-3, without affecting other
similar substrates such as caspase-7, caspase-8 or Bid.

Having demonstrated that the GrB-like activity of the FTn-Pd
nanozyme was determined by the coordination of two Pd(II) atoms at
site A, we sought to elucidate the catalytic mechanism underlying GrB-
like activity. Comparing the protein structures of FTn and FTn-Pd
showed a local conformational change in the protein structure at site A
due to binding of the two Pd(II) ions that gave rise to changes in the
arrangement of amino acid residues and the overall architecture of the
binding pocket (Fig. 3c). Notably, the histidine H105 side chain was
rotated through an angle of 90 degrees. Consequently, the binding
pocket of FTn-Pd exhibited enhanced stability by forming bonds with
Pd1 and Pd2, along with two coordinated water molecules. Molecular
docking analysis of FTn/Ac-IETD-pNA complexes showed two possible
regions for substrate binding in regions delineated by K87/D91/R43/
S36 and R22/N21/E83 (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 3g). Signi ﬁcant
alterations were observed in these potential substrate binding regions
for FTn-Pd/Ac-IETD-pNA complexes, primarily due to the structural
changes associated with site A. Speci ﬁcally, residues Asn25/Asp89/
Asn109 were identiﬁed as the sole binding sites for Ac-IETD-pNA. Sig-
niﬁcantly, these residues were near site A, suggesting that preferential
access to the catalytic active centre (site A) due to proximity binding
was mechanistically important for the catalytic amide bond cleavage of
the GrB substrate.

Based on the structural and molecular docking analysis, we
propose that the Pd-induced 90-degree ﬂip of H105 enables the
histidine side chain to function as a reactive intermediate or proton
donor/acceptor for scissile bond cleavage of the GrB substrate
bound close to site A (Fig. 3e). This is consistent with the role of
histidine in enzyme mechanisms involving proton transfer and
reactive intermediate stabilisation
27,28. Notably, this catalytic site
architecture and mechanism are fundamentally distinct from those
of native GrB
29 (Supplementary Fig. 3h). This distinction provides a
mechanistic explanation for why FTn –Pd selectively cleaves pro-
caspase-3 but does not act on caspase-7, caspase-8, or Bid, all of
which are substrates of native GrB.

Molecular mechanisms underlying FNP-antiHER2-induced
apoptosis
Given that FTn-Pd, as a GrB-mimicking nanozyme, selectively induces
pro-caspase-3 cleavage, we next sought to determine whether FNP-
antiHER2-induced apoptosis is caspase-3 dependent. To do this, we
ﬁrst assessed caspase-3 activation in SKBR3 cells following different
treatments. Western blot analysis showed that treatment with FNP-
antiHER2 led to caspase-3 cleavage, comparable to the effect of
staurosporine (STS, a known caspase-3 inducer), and this cleavage was
effectively blocked by Z-VAD-FMK, an irreversible pan-caspase inhi-
bitor (Fig. 5a). To conﬁrm the functional activity of cleaved caspase-3,
we further analyzed the activation of its primary substrate, poly (ADP-
ribose) polymerase (PARP). Western blot results revealed signi ﬁcant
PARP activation following FNP-antiHER2 treatment, indicating that
cleaved caspase-3 successfully propagated downstream apoptotic
signalling. Furthermore, cells were immunostained with a cleaved
caspase-3 antibody after treatment with FNP-antiHER2, allowing us to
visualise intracellular caspase-3 activation by FNP-antiHER2 nanove-
sicles (Fig.5b). To further validate that FNP-antiHER2 induces caspase-
3-dependent apoptosis, we designed three small interfering RNAs
(siRNAs) targeting full-length caspase-3 mRNA (i.e., cas3 siRNA-1,
cas3 siRNA-2, cas3 siRNA-3) to downregulate intracellular caspase-3
expression. Western blot analysis revealed a signi ﬁcant reduction in
the activation of caspase-3 and its substrate PARP in cells treated with
cas3 siRNAs, compared to those treated with control siRNA (ctrl siRNA)
(Fig. 5c). Additionally, ﬂow cytometry showed a marked decrease in
both early and late apoptosis in cas3 siRNA-3-treated cells following
FNP-antiHER2 induction, compared to control siRNA-treated
cells (Fig. 5d).
Next, we sought to employ RNA sequencing to compare gene
expression in the FNP-antiHER2-treated group versus the control
group. The data analysis identiﬁed 578 down-regulated and 1035 up-
regulated genes in the FNP-antiHER2-treated cells compared to the
control (Fig. 5e). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these
differentially expressed genes highlighted several key functional
impacts of FTn-Pd treatment. As shown in Fig. 5f, enriched GO terms
included the regulation of tumour necrosis factor, the regulation of
apoptotic signalling pathway, and the regulation of extrinsic apoptotic
signalling. Further analysis revealed that these differentially expressed
genes were closely linked to apoptotic pathways (Fig. 5g), suggesting
that the anti-tumour activity of FNP-antiHER2 is medicated through
apoptosis.

We next sought to evaluate the in vivo behaviour of intravenously
administered FNP-antiHER2 in mice bearing SKBR3 tumours with
naturally high HER2 expression. First, we examined the plasma phar-

branes (FNP-M, control) or with membrane-attached single-chain
antibodies (FNP-antiHER2) had signi ﬁcantly prolonged circulation
times compared to LNP alone (Fig. 6a). Typically, the particle con-
centrations in blood plasma at 12 h were determined as 53.5 ± 5.3,
181.5 ± 3.6, 185.1 ± 8.4 ng/mL for the LNP, FNP-M and FNP-antiHER2
constructs, respectively, indicating that cell membrane camouﬂage of
the FNPs improved blood circulation time for LNP. To investigate
in vivo accumulation in SKBR3 subcutaneous tumours, we performed
in vivo imaging of the mice at the indicated time points after admin-
istration of DiR-labelled LNP, FNP-M or FNP-antiHER2. Over time, there
was a gradual increase in tumour accumulation, with maximal accu-
mulation attained at 8 h for both LNP and FNP-M (Fig.6b). In contrast,
the highest tumour uptake of FNP-antiHER2 was delayed to approxi-
mately 20 h. The enhanced uptake of FNP-antiHER2 could be attrib-
uted to both the prolonged blood circulation times and improved
retention of particles in the tumour due to receptor-mediated binding
of anti-HER2. Furthermore, the tumour tissue retention ability of the
LNPs was lower than that of the FNP-M and FNP-antiHER2 nanovesicles,
particularly in the case of FNP-antiHER2. Quantiﬁcation analysis of the
isolated tissue showed that the injected particles were primarily dis-
tributed in the liver, spleen, lung and tumours 48 h post-
administration. Importantly, tumour tissue accumulation of FNP-
antiHER2 was increased compared to both LNP and FNP-M (Supple-
mentary Fig. 5).
