Oxide-based memristors are promising candidates as neuromorphic hardware in energy-ef ﬁcient
edge computing applications for the Internet of Things (IoT). However, achieving simultaneously
optimized memristive and synaptic performance with low-temperature fabrication, compatible with
complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) processes, remains a challenge. Here, we
demonstrate a dual-function electrode selection strategy — controlled oxygen vacancy electrode
reservoir— implemented in a simple, low-temperature-fabricated indium tin oxide (ITO)/WO
3/TiN thin
ﬁlm system. We systematically vary the oxygen stoichiometry in the ITO top electrode, which serves
not only as an electrical contact, but also as a dynamic oxygen reservoir. A high oxygen-vacancy
concentration in the ITO enables optimized memristive performance of low electroforming and
operation voltages, and robust memristive and synaptic endurance. This electrode-focused defect
engineering approach offers a versatile route to advanced memristor design, enabling many
applications in IoT and neuromorphic systems.
Artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) offers transformative beneﬁts across numerous
sectors. Still, traditional AI models primarily depend on resource-intensive
infrastructures for data training, leading to high latency, signiﬁcant energy
consumption, and reliance on continuous network connectivity1–4.T h e s e
challenges become particularly pronounced in time-sensitive applications
such as autonomous driving, indust rial automation, and public safety
monitoring. In response, edge computing and the Internet of Things (IoT)
have emerged as critical complements—or even alternatives—to centralized
AI solutions. Edge computing moves computation closer to where data
originates, enabling real-time processing, reduced latency, and minimal
bandwidth requirements
5–7. Unlike traditional transistor-based com-
plementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) architectures for AI, two-
terminal devices, such as resistive random-access memory (also known as
memristors), have recently gained attention as promising hardware plat-
forms for IoT applications
8–10. Memristors inherently retain data without
power and exhibit synaptic-like behavior, enabling brain-like computing
with real-time, localized data processing at signi ﬁcantly reduced power
costs11–13. Memristors’ compatibility with nonlinear and parallel data pro-
cessing makes them particularly well-suited for handling“small data” sce-
narios typical in edge computing. In IoT applications (especially ﬂexible
electronics)14,15, low-temperature fabrication is beneﬁcial, since those sub-
strates cannot withstand traditional high-temperature fabrication steps16,17.
Beyond IoT, devices requiring conformability to non-ﬂat surfaces further
emphasize the need for low temperatures 18. Thus, achieving high-
performance memristors using low- temperature fabrication methods
represents a crucial advancement over conventional memristor
technologies.
A wide variety of materials, including inorganic oxides, organic
molecules, and hybrid materials, have been employed as resistive switching
media in memristors
19. Among these, binary metal oxides such as Hf-, Ta-,
W-, and Ti-based oxides have been extensively investigated20–24. However,
oxide memristors struggle to simultaneously achieve optimal memory and
neuromorphic performance with CMOS compatibility and low-
temperature fabrication
25. An important factor leading to such limitations
is a sub-optimal distribution of defects, speciﬁcally oxygen vacancies, within
the oxide layer during deposition. These vacancies signiﬁcantly inﬂuence
local electronic structures and cond uctivity, enabling switching between
high and low resistance states byforming or rupturing conductiveﬁlaments
under electrical bias 26. Most previous work has attempted to engineer
oxygen vacancies solely within the switching layer26–28, while ignoring the

Electrode property tuning
ITO has been studied widely as a CMOS-compatible electrode for resistive
switching devices50, but its properties have never been tuned or understood
in terms of optimization of resistive switching device performance. To
clarify why oxygen-engineered ITO yields the excellent memristive and
neuromorphic behavior described above, we systematically examined how
varying the O
2 partial pressure during ITO sputtering tunes the electrode’s
electronic structure and interfacial energetics in the ITO/WO3/TiN stack.

This surface potential tuning has important implications for the band
alignment at the ITO/WO3 interface. KPFM on the ITO/WO3 stack reveals
a larger potential contrast between ITO and WO3 for oxygen-rich ITO. This
is because KPFM measures a local CPD that includes interfacial band
bending54,55, whereas UPS probes the intrinsic work function of isolated
ﬁlms. A higher-work-function ITO electrode depletes WO3 more strongly,
enhancing upward band bending at the interface and thus increasing the
measured potential difference
55. In contrast, lower-work-function (oxygen-
poor) ITO has a smaller energy barrier. In our devices, small energy barriers
effectively reduce the operation voltages of the devices (in our case, <2 V). By
adjustingP
o2
during ITO deposition, we are effectively tuning the interfacial
energetics: smallerPo2
yields an interface that is more reducing and has a
smaller potential difference, which is expected to lower the energy barrier for
forming conductiveﬁlaments (i.e., oxygen vacancy migration). Energy band
diagrams illustrating this alignment are presented in Fig. S10. This is shown
to correlate with the forming voltage behavior in Fig.1d.
In addition to changing the work function, the oxygen content in ITO
dramatically affects its electrical conductivity. Four-point probe measure-
ments (Fig.3e) show that the resistivity of the ITO top electrode increases by
orders of magnitude asP
o2
increases during the sputtering process. For ITO
deposited in pure Ar (Po2
¼ 0%), the ﬁlm exhibits a low resistivity on the
order of 10−5 Ωm. However, atPo2
¼ 40%, the resistivity is found to exceed
10−1 Ωm, a four-order-of-magnitude increase. This trend is in excellent
agreement with the literature on oxygen-doped ITO: adding oxygen during
sputtering reduces the oxygen vacancy concentration (and free carrier
density), causing resistivity to rise almost exponentially, indicating that the
ITO transitions from a degenerate semiconductor at low P
o2
to a more
insulating oxide at high Po2
29,30. It is possible that some oxygen-rich ITO
regions could show resistive switching behavior56. However, even the most
oxygen-rich ITOﬁlm in our study remains highly conductive (~10−1 Ωm)
compared to the WO3 layer (~102 Ωm), so the WO 3 layer dominates the
resistive switching. A low-resistivity ITO electrode (grown with 5% Po2
)
presents a small sheet resistance, allowing the drive current to spread lat-
erally within the electrode before converging into the WO
3 ﬁlament with less
Joule heating inside the electrode. As aresult, the switching event remains
conﬁned to the pre-existing ﬁlament rather than th ermally activating
neighboring regions. The ability to repeatedly cycle the same ﬁlamentary
pathway is beneﬁcial to endurance57.
Overall, by taking together the KPFM, UPS, and resistivity results, we
ﬁnd that lowering the oxygen content in ITO simultaneously reduces the
ITO work function and increases the ITO conductivity. Thus, our all-in-one
approach to modify these two important parameters enables lower opera-
tion voltage, improved memristive endurance, and more stable synaptic
performance.

X-ray diffraction measurements were carried out using a Panalytical
Empyrean Diffractometer system with parallel beam optics, CuKα1 radia-
tion, and a single-point proportional detector.
Electron microscopy
Cross-sectional STEM-EDX samples were prepared using focused ion beam
in a FEI Helios Nanolab SEM/FIB. Allimages were captured using a Thermo
Fisher Scientiﬁc Spectra 300 operating at 300 kV. We used protective car-
bon/Pt capping layers and low-voltage ﬁnal milling (down to 1 –2k V ) t o
minimize damage to the active region78.
Rutherford backscattering spectrometry
The elemental compositions of the ITO thinﬁlms grown on WO3/SiO2/Si
were obtained by RBS in a 5-MV 15SDH-2 tandem accelerator. 2-MeV4He+
ions were employed for RBS measurements, and backscattered ions were
detected at a scattering angle of 170°. Possible ion-channeling effects were
minimized by adjusting the equilibrium incidence angle to 5° with respect to
the surface normal and performing multiple small random angular move-
ments within a range of 2° during data acquisition. Simulations by SIMNRA
software were performed according to the experimental spectra. Because the
In and Sn RBS peaks are almost coincident, the spectra wereﬁtted with the
dominant In signal; afterwards, this total was split by applying the Sn/In
ratio obtained independently from XPS. This coupled RBS –XPS strategy
yields accurate In and Sn atomic compositions
79.
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
XPS was performed with a Thermo Scientiﬁc Escalab 250Xi instrument with
monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV) at ~1 × 10-9 Torr. The
spectra were acquired after gentle sputter cleaning of the sample surfaces by
200-eV Ar ions for 2000 s to remove native surface oxides. Employing this
ultra-low Ar ion energy range minim izes the damage of core levels and
subsequently induced artefacts.
Optical characterization
Raman spectroscopy measurements were performed using a continuous-
wave single-longitudinal-mode 633 nmlaser from Integrated Optics. The
power on the sample was 1.5 μW. The Raman signal was collected through
optics from Thorlabs into an Andor Kymera 328i spectrometer coupled to a
Newton EMCCD camera from Oxford Instruments. Photoluminescence
spectroscopy was performed using a continuous-wave 444 nm laser from
Integrated Optics. The signal was sent through an optical QP50-2-UV-vis
ﬁber to an Oceans Optics QE65000 spectrometer. For the operando mea-
surements, these optical characterizations were performed after the device
was subjected to continuous, slow I –V cycles (at approximately 0.01 Hz)
designed for accelerated fatigue testing. The spectrum was collected with 2 s
integration time. The ITO top elect rodes were 9 nm thick due to optical
measurement requirements. Details of the optical measurements can be
found in our previous work
80.
