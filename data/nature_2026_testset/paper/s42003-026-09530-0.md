Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a major contributor to dementia in the elderly, characterized by progressive
impairments in memory and behaviour. AD is marked by pathological hallmarks: amyloid plaques and
neuroﬁbrillary tangles (NFTs). Despite extensive efforts to understand these hallmarks, there is still a
lack of ef ﬁcient therapeutic approaches due to limited knowledge of the fundamental cellular
mechanisms underlying the disease. One potential avenue of research involves the investigation of the
roles of non-coding RNAs, particularly microRNAs (miRNAs), which bind to the 3 ’ UTRs of target
mRNAs to suppress expression. In our study, we analyse the temporal superior T1 isocortex of control
and AD patients, identifying differentially expressed miRNAs using next-generation sequencing
(NGS). To validate these ﬁndings, we utilize an additional technique, RT-qPCR. Our study conﬁrms the
previous ﬁndings on the dysregulation of miR-129-5p, miR-132-3p, and miR-146b-5p, while also
provides insights into the dysregulation of miR-151a-5p and miR-1-3p. Importantly, the expression
levels of miR-129-5p, miR-146b-5p, miR-132-3p, and miR-151a-5p are signi ﬁcantly correlated with
the neuropathological Braak stages and with biochemically quanti ﬁed tau phosphorylation levels in
brain homogenates. Additionally, we ﬁnd that miR-146b-5p and miR-151a-5p signi ﬁcantly modulate
tau seeding in tau biosensor cell model, highlighting their potential roles in tau pathology.
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia in the
elderly population, resulting in a signiﬁcant socioeconomic burden1.T h e
neuropathological lesions of AD incl ude extracellular amyloid plaques,
composed of amyloidβ (Aβ) peptides derived from the cleavage of amyloid
precursor protein (APP), and intracellular neuroﬁbrillary tangles (NFTs),
composed of hyperphosphorylated tau, forming paired helical ﬁlaments
(PHF)2– 6. Due to the disease ’s multifaceted complexity, effective disease-
modifying therapies remain unavailable7,8. Consequently, there is a critical
need to identify neuroprotective strategies by investigating the molecular
pathways implicated in AD pathology.
Increasing evidence suggests signiﬁcant roles of non-coding RNAs,
including microRNAs (miRNAs; <25 nucleotides), long non-coding RNAs
(lncRNAs; >200 nucleotides), circular RNAs, transfer RNA-derived small
RNAs (tsRNAs), and piwi-interactingRNA (piRNAs) in physiological and
pathological conditions
9– 11. Among these, miRNAs represent a class of short
non-coding RNA molecules that play a crucial role in the epigenetic reg-
ulation of gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. Through speciﬁc
partial sequence base pairing, miRNAs bind to the 3′ untranslated region (3′
UTR) of their target transcripts and reduce their expression by either mRNA
degradation or translational inhibition12– 14.Ag r o w i n gb o d yo fl i t e r a t u r e
shows that miRNAs are dysregulated in the brain, cerebrospinalﬂuid (CSF),
and blood of AD patients 10,15. Importantly, some studies have linked the
miRNAs dysregulation in AD brains to tau and amyloid pathways16– 19.
Currently, the number of studies exploring differential miRNAs in AD
post-mortem brain samples is limited, and additional research is essential to
identify their roles in AD pathology and validate dysregulated miRNAs as a

Methods
Human samples
Samples from the temporal superior T1 isocortex were obtained from
control (n = 13, Braak stages 0 to III) and AD subjects (n = 23, Braak stages
V-VI). Control and AD subjects were diagnosed according to the National
Institute of Aging and Reagan Institute Criteria
21 and scored by neuro-
pathological staging for tau pathology 22. Some of the subjects were also
scored for Thal staging (Table 1)23. Apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype
was identiﬁed for the subjects with informed consent for genetic study
using PCR ampli ﬁcation for genomic DNA and sequencing. Sarkosyl-
insoluble fractions were obtained from the frontal cortex of post-mortem
brain samples. We used brain samples from Neuro-CEB (France) and
ULB LHNN (Belgium) brain banks, as shown in Table1. For Neuro-CEB,
procedure of the brain bank was approved by the ethical committee
“Comité de Protection des Personnes Paris Ile de France VI” and for ULB
LHNN brain bank (BB190052), approval was obtained from the ethical
committee of the medical school of the Université Libre de Bruxelles
(ULB), Belgium (P2009/015). Autopsies and biochemical analyses were
carried out after written informed consent was obtained from family
members. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants
were followed.
Total RNA extraction and quality analysis
Total RNA was extracted from approximately 100 mg of the temporal
superior T1 isocortex brain regions (13 control and 23 AD) using mirVana
PARIS RNA and Native Protein Puriﬁcation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc)
following the manufacturer’s protocol. Extracted RNA samples were treated
with DNase using TURBO DNA-free kit (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc). Quality
of RNA samples was checked using DNF-472 RNA (15 nt) HS RNA Kit on
Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies), and quantiﬁcation was done
using Quant-it Ribogreen assay (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc). Only 19 samples
(9 control and 10 AD) with RIN value≥ 3.5 were proceeded to the miRNA
library preparation and sequencing (Table1 and Fig. 1d).
miRNA sequencing and data analysis
For each sample, a sequencing library was constructed with 200 ng input
RNA using the QIAseq miRNA Library Kit (Qiagen). This kit appends a
19 nt spacer and 12 nt unique molecular identiﬁer (UMI) at the 3′-end of the
original small RNA in the forward read (read 1), enabling PCR duplicate
identiﬁcation and ampliﬁcation bias correction. Libraries were ampli ﬁed
using 15 PCR cycles, size-selected on an 8% native PAGE gel (Invitrogen),
and puri ﬁed. Library quality was assessed with the DNF-474 HS NGS
Fragment (1– 6000 bp) Kit on a Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies)
and quantiﬁed using the“Sequencing Library qPCR Quantiﬁcation Guide”
from Illumina. Equimolar-pooled libraries were spiked with 15% PhiX as an
internal control and sequenced as single-read 76 on a NextSeq 500 platform
(Illumina).
Reads lacking a detectable spacer and UMI were discarded using UMI-
tools (v1.1.2)
24. The remaining reads were assessed for quality and length
using FastQC (v0.12.1) and screened for potential contaminants against a
panel of common laboratory organism genomes using FastQ Screen
(v0.15.3)
25. PCR duplicates, de ﬁned as reads with identical UMI and
sequence, were collapsed to retain only one representative per group.
Known miRNA identiﬁcation, novel miRNA prediction, and expression
quantiﬁcation were performed using the mirPRo pipeline (v1.1.4)
26 with the
latest miRBase (v22.1) annotations and the human genome (GRCh38,
ENSEMBL release 111). The miRBa se database contains 2656 mature
human miRNA sequences27. For novel miRNA prediction, reference data
from all mammalian species in miRB ase were used. Differential gene
expression analysis was done in R (v4.3.3) using the edgeR (4.0.16)
package
28.B r i eﬂy, feature counts were TMM-normalized, and dispersions
were estimated. For pairwise compa rison of gene expressions between
conditions, we used the quasi-likelihood model andF-test for a more reliable
error rate. Correction of thep values for repeated testing was done with the
Benjamini-Hochberg method.
RT-qPCR analysis
Brieﬂy, 30 ng of RNA isolated from post-mortem temporal superior T1
isocortex samples was used for reverse transcription to obtain the cDNA
using the miRCURY LNA RT Kit (339340, Qiagen). Quantitative PCR was
performed according to manufacturer’s protocol using cDNA template,
miRCURY SYBR green master mix (339346, Qiagen), and miRCURY LNA
miRNA assays (339306, Qiagen) (Supplementary Table 4) in CFX96 Touch
Real time PCR (Biorad). Cycle thresholds (Ct) were obtained for target
miRNAs (miR-129-5p, miR-146b-5p,miR-151a-5p, miR-1-3p, miR-132-
3p, miR-18a-5p, miR-132-5p, miR-133a-3p, miR-151b, miR-3065-3p, miR-
129-1-3p) and normalizers. We used both small RNA U6 and two miRNAs
(miR-16-5p and miR-30b-5p) identiﬁed via sequencing experiment as non-
differentially expressed miRNAs as normalizer for relative quantiﬁcation
using 2
−ΔΔC(T) method29.
Preparation of RIPA-soluble fractions
RIPA-soluble fractions were obtained as previously described 30.B r i eﬂy,
200 mg of frozen temporal superior T1 isocortex was homogenized in 10
volumes of ice-cold RIPA buffer (containing 50 mM Tris – HCl pH 7.4,
50 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.25% sodium deoxycholate) with 5 mM EDTA,
1 mM EGTA, complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck), 1 mM PMSF
(Sigma), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2 (Sigma). The homogenate was
rotated for 1 h at 4 °C, centrifuged at 20,000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the
resulting supernatant was used as the RIPA-soluble fractions to quantify
phosphorylated tau (pTau).
Preparation of sarkosyl-insoluble fractions
Sarkosyl-insoluble fractions were obtained as previously described 30,31.
Brieﬂy, 0.5 g of frontal cortex from post-mortem brain samples was
homogenized in 10 volumes of ice-co ld PHF-extraction buffer (10 mM
Tris– HCl pH 7.4, 0.8 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 10% sucrose) and sub-
sequently centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was
obtained, and N-lauroylsarcosine sodium salt (Sigma) was added to have
ﬁnal concentration of 1% (w/v). Following overnight incubation at 4 °C with
gentle agitation, ultracentrifugation was carried out at 180,000 × g for
30 min at 4 °C. Sarkosyl-insoluble pellet containing PHF was gently rinsed
with PBS and resuspended by vortexing and pipetting in PBS. Sarkosyl-
insoluble fraction was sonicated on ice by a probe-sonicator (amplitude 20,
10 pulses of one second with one second of interval). After Bradford protein

Results
Cohort demographics and RNA quality analysis
In our cohort, we grouped subjects with Braak stages up to III as control and
subjects with Braak stages V-VI as AD. In our study, temporal superior T1
isocortex from 36 subjects (control,n = 13; AD, n = 23) was processed for
RNA extraction and quality analysis (Table 1). For these 36 subjects, we
obtained variable RNA quality indicated by RIN with minimum RIN = 2.3
and maximum RIN = 7.6 (Fig.1a, b and Table1). Such variable RNA quality
is reported in several post-mortem brain samples in both control and
AD
20,39.F i g u r e1c illustrates the minimum and maximum RIN values
obtained in several reports exploring differential miRNAs expression in
post-mortem brain samples in AD20,39– 44. In our cohort, correlation analysis
suggested that there is no association between the RIN and PMD (Sup-
plementary Fig. 1a – c). Based on the RIN cut-off of ≥3.5, we selected
19 subjects (control, n =9 ; A D ,n = 10) (Fig. 1ba n dT a b l e1). We have
chosen this cut-off where we can observe the ribosomal RNAs in the digital
gel of the Agilent analyzer (Fig. 1a), suggesting that the samples are not
completely degraded. Also, a recent study showed that the average RIN for
n = 190 samples used was around 3.6
20. This implies that the minimum
quality of the RNA samples used in this study is comparable to the average
RIN quality used in similar studies. Interestingly, in these selected 19 sub-
jects we found a trend for negative correlation between the RIN and PMD
(R = −0.4 and p = 0.094) (Fig.1b), but not in complete group of 36 subjects
(R = −0.18 and p = 0.31) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). All subsequent analysis
were done for only these 19 subjects, unless explicitly mentioned. The
average age at death was 72.11 ± 2.61 years for the control subjects (n =9 )
and 76.6 ± 3.81 years for AD subjects (n = 10) (mean ± standard error of the
mean) (t-test, p = 0.35). Similarly, the average PMD was 19.06 ± 4.39 h for
control subjects and 18.80 ± 3.14 h for AD subjects (mean ± standard error
of the mean) (t-test, p = 0.96). The average RIN was 4.96 ± 0.47 for control
subjects and 5.43 ± 0.50 for AD subjects (mean ± standard error of the
mean) (t-test, p = 0.49). These comparisons showed no signiﬁcant differ-
ences in age, PMD, or RIN values between the control and AD groups.
However, a signiﬁcant difference in sex distribution was observed between
the control group (n = 9; 8 males and 1 female) and the AD group (n = 10; 3
males and 7 females) (Fisher’sE x a c tT e s t ,p = 0.019) (Table1).

Discussion
In this study, we employed two different methods to analyse differentially
expressed miRNAs in post-mortem brain cortical samples from two dif-
ferent brain banks, Neuro-CEB (France) and ULB LHNN (Belgium):
small RNA sequencing for high-throughput screening and RT-qPCR for
validation of selected miRNAs. The RNA quality of subjects, as judged
from their RIN, was variable but comparable to the quality reported in
other studies of post-mortem human brain samples
20,39. Importantly,
consistent with other studies 42,45 we found no correlation between RNA
quality (RIN) and miRNA expression. We identi ﬁed by NGS that 13
miRNAs were differentially expressed in AD versus control. Among them,
8 were downregulated, and 5 were upregulated in AD. Furthermore, in the
RT-qPCR method, we used several normalizers to validate the consistent
direction in fold changes for these NGS-identiﬁed miRNAs. This process
reinforces the reliability of our results and reduces potential biases related

65,74, while its loss increases neuronal proliferation in vivo 75.
However, this phenomenon cannot be studied in HEK tau biosensor cells.
Furthermore, mixed cultures better re ﬂect intercellular interactions, as
responses of one cell type in which miRNA is dysregulated can affect
another
63. This needs to be evaluated in neuronal and inﬂammatory mixed
cultures for further investigation.
Additionally, with respect to therapeutics, it is important to identify
the targets and signaling pathways that may be in ﬂuenced by these
identiﬁed miRNAs. The miRNA-target network analysis revealed that
targets for downregulated miRNAs in AD are associated with KEGG
pathways such as the Neurotrophin signaling pathway, Dopaminergic
synapse, Axon guidance, Long-term depression, Serotonergic synapse,
Cholinergic synapse, and AD. Some of these miRNA-target relationships
need to be validated in AD-relevant models. For instance, miR-132-3p, a
well-documented downregulated miRNA in AD that we also identiﬁed in
this study, is known to exert protective effects by modulating multiple
pathways
16,61. This highlights the potential of replacement therapy, where
restoring these miRNAs could confer beneﬁcial effects. From a diagnostic
perspective, their levels should be systematically investigated in large
cohorts using bioﬂuids such as CSF and blood, particularly those derived
from the brain, and correlated with conventional AD biomarkers,
including total tau (t-tau), phosphorylated tau (p-tau), amyloid-beta (Aβ),
and cognitive scores.
We acknowledge that there are limitations in our study. First, for
identifying the differentially expressed miRNAs, we used Braak stages 0– III
as controls and Braak stages V– VI as AD; the damage to cells and tissues is
higher at Braak stages V– VI. We cannot exclude the possibility that cell or
tissue loss at stages V – VI could in ﬂuence these miRNA pro ﬁles. Larger
cohort studies with equal numbers of Braak stages within 0 – IV will be
necessary to track miRNA changes within Braak stages 0 – IV, before the
pathology becomes aggressive. Furthermore, some AD patients have
comorbid conditions, and larger studies with distinct groups will be needed
to identify the most speciﬁc AD miRNA signatures. Second, our sequencing
analysis included only 19 of 36 samples, limiting statistical power. We
applied an FDR < 0.1, which is less stringent than the conventional <0.05.
Although none of the miRNAs reach ed the conventional FDR < 0.05
threshold, their consistent reporting in previous studies (Table2), together
with their substantial fold changes, supports their potential relevance to AD.
The lack of signi ﬁcance is likely attributable to our limited sample size.
Moreover, when sex is included as confounding factor, we lost the sig-
niﬁcance at the cut off used nominal p value < 0.003 and FDR < 0.1 (Sup-
plementary Table 3). This could be due to large gender imbalance (control
group: n = 9; 8 males and 1 female, and AD group: n = 10; 3 males and 7
females) and small cohort size. The effect of other confounding factors such
as age, PMD, RIN were minimal compared to sex (Supplementary Table 3).
Analysis of all 36 samples from Table1 (control group:n =1 3 ;9m a l e sa n d4
females; AD group: n = 23; 11 males and 12 females) by RT-qPCR con-
ﬁrmed that miR-132-3p, m iR-146b-5p, and miR-129-5p were down-
regulated in AD (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Supporting ourﬁndings, a recent
large cohort study, Religious Orders Study (ROS) or the Rush Memory and
Aging Project (MAP) with cohort size ofn = 617, revealed that some of the
differential expression of miRNAs weobserved (miR-132-3p, miR-132-5p,
miR-146b-5p, miR-129-1-3p, and miR-129-5p) even after adjusting for sex,
age, education, PMI, RIN, batch, su rrogate variables, and co-occurring
pathologies at FDR adjusted p value < 0.05
47. Thus, we considered to be
appropriate to study these miRNAs, but acknowledge that further experi-
ments are needed to prove their relevance for AD. Third, some of the
information on clinical and neuropat hological diagnoses was lacking.
Although we had complete diagnostic information for tau pathology (Braak
staging), data on amyloid pathology (Thal staging) were not available for all
samples in our cohort. Consequently, our analysis focused exclusively on
evaluating the association between differential miRNA expression and tau
pathology, using Braak staging and phosphorylated tau quanti ﬁcation.
Nevertheless, based on the available Thal staging data for amyloid pathology
(13 out of 19 subjects used for sequencing), we observed a similar correlation
between Thal staging and miRNA expression as seen with tau pathology
(Supplementary Fig. 6). This observation aligns with previous ﬁndings
reporting that miR-132-3p and miR-129-5p in the brain are associated with
both amyloid and tau pathology
48. Given that miRNAs can target multiple
genes and inﬂuence multiple biological pathways, we do not exclude the
possibility that these identiﬁed differentially expressed miRNAs also con-
tribute to amyloid pathology. Our s tudy lacks experimental evidence
directly linking the identiﬁed miRNAs to amyloid pathology. A literature
search revealed that among the identiﬁed miRNAs, miR-132-3p has been
associated with amyloid pathology in mouse models. One study demon-
strated that deletion of miR-132/212 promoted Aβ production and amyloid
plaque formation in the brains of 3xTg-AD mice
60. Similarly, in APP/PS1
mice, intracerebroventricular injection of antagomiR-132 increased amy-
loid plaque burden
19. Supporting this ﬁnding, another study showed that
overexpression of miR-132-3p reduced amyloid plaque formation in the
hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice 59. Further studies involving larger, well-
characterized cohorts encompassing both pathological features, along with
experimental models representing bothcombined and distinct pathologies,
are needed to elucidate the associations and regulatory roles of these iden-
tiﬁed differentially expressed miRNAs. Additionally, patient diagnostic data
during disease development, such as cognitive scores and CSF biomarkers,
were lacking in our cohort; therefo re, no association study with miRNA
expression was possible. Lastly, our analysis was based on bulk sequencing.
Future studies using single-cell sequencing are necessary to identify the
speciﬁc cellular sources of these miRNAs. Despite these limitations, this
study provides valuable information on the alteration of miRNA levels in
human post-mortem AD brains and their implications in tau seeding.
F u r t h e rs t u d i e si no t h e rm o d e l sa r en eeded to better understand their reg-
ulatory roles in tau pathology.
In conclusion, our study corroborates the previously reported dys-
regulation of miRNAs in AD and identi ﬁes additional dysregulated
miRNAs in AD brain tissue. In particular, miR-129-5p, miR-146b-5p,
miR-132-3p, and miR-151a-5p expression showed association with tau
pathology in AD brains. However, only miR-146b-5p and miR-151a-5p
showed an effect on tau seeding in cultured cells, suggesting a direct role of
these miRNAs in tau pathology formation. Their mechanisms of action
