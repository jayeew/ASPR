Hydrogen is a clean energy carrier with signiﬁcant promises for a sustainable
future, yet many established production routes operate under undesirable
conditions or constraints, motivatingthe search for alternative production
pathways. Here, our approach uses photothermal oxidation of liquid gallium
to generate hydrogen from both freshwater and seawater. The exposure to
light thermally heats up liquid gallium droplets to a temperature suitable for
fast gallium-water interaction, producing gallium oxyhydroxide and hydrogen.
The light exposure also boosts the reaction by breaking the oxide layer on the
surface of liquid gallium immersed in water, allowing continuous interactions
between the water molecules and the surface of liquid gallium droplets. The
gallium oxyhydroxide that is produced can be electrochemically reduced,
allowing metal regeneration, thereby enabling circular hydrogen production.
In this work, we show that photothermalactivation of liquid gallium provides a
rapid and circular route for generating hydrogen from diverse water sources.
As an Earth-abundant element, hydrogen can be sourced from a variety
of compounds, with water being the most signiﬁcant1. Utilizing mole-
cular hydrogen (H 2) is expected to contribute to the reduction of
greenhouse gas emissions2,3. Its high energy content and clean com-
bustion make H 2 an ideal candidate for a sustainable energy future,
supporting efforts to mitigate climate change and promote environ-
mental health4.
The methods for producing green H 2 are primarily focused on
water splitting via electrolysis, photocatalysis, plasma-based techni-
ques, hydrothermal processes, and biological approaches
5.W h i l ee a c h
method presents speci ﬁc advantages, they also face inherent chal-
lenges related to ef ﬁciency, yield, complexity, scale up, and cost.
Consequently, exploring alternative technological pathways is crucial
to enhancing the feasibility and accessibility of green H
2 in the future5.
In this work, we present an energetically favorable circular
method for H2 generation utilizing only liquid metal gallium (Ga). Low
temperature photothermal oxidation produces H₂ under artiﬁcial or
natural sunlight, and an aqueous electrochemical reduction step
regenerates Ga, completing the cycle.
It is important to mention that the reactions of alloys such as
aluminum (Al) with Ga have been shown to produce H
2 when in contact
with water, however, such systems were never energetically
favorable
6–11. For instance, in the case of Al-Ga, the reaction with water
produces aluminum oxide, which is a very stable compound that
requires high energy input to reduce
6,9. In contrast, the reduction of
Ga(III) species can be performed in aqueous media with standard
electrochemical equipment and using relatively low-energy
conditions.

Our method offers a route for H 2 production by using the liquid
metal-based oxidation behavior of Ga in the liquid state. During the
photothermal oxyhydroxidation of liquid Ga, immersed in water, the
electromagnetic waves emitted by the light source heat up the Ga
droplets, which simultaneously disrupt the oxide layer, interacting
with the naturally formed heteroju nction between the metal and its
oxyhydroxide at the interface. These phenomena enable sustained
energy absorption from the light source, continuously generating H₂
at temperatures below the boiling point of water at atmospheric
pressure, without oxygen evolution, thereby eliminating the risk of
detonation. The liquid state of Ga allows the produced oxyhydroxide
to ﬂake, permitting light and water to continuously reach the surface
of Ga and the heterojunction until the full oxyhydroxidation of Ga is
complete
12. Then the use of electrochemical methods to reduce the Ga
oxyhydroxide (GaOOH) back to metallic Ga establishes a circular
process. Altogether, by coupling the photothermal oxidation and
electrochemical reduction steps of liquid metal Ga, we demonstrate a
simple, safe and energetically favorable route for circular hydrogen
production.
Results
Liquid metals are notably absorptive of energetic electromagnetic
waves, covering a broad range of wavelengths from GHz to ultraviolet
(UV)
13–15. The impinged electromagnetic waves imposed in the visible
light range, in the presence of an oxygen source, promote photo-
corrosion phenomena in metals. Historically, corrosion has been
considered a detrimental process, shortening the lifespan of solid
metal structures.
Here, we use light-induced corrosion to drive liquid Ga-water
interaction for on-demand hydrogen generation. Liquid Ga offers
advantages that facilitate this process. Notably, due to its low melting
point of 29.8 °C, Ga becomes liquid just above this temperature
15,16.I f
the surface of Ga is roughened, for instance by sonication, to make
low-dimensional droplets with higher surface area, Ga can absorb light
and produce heat at a much higher efﬁciency than that of bulk Ga
17,18.
Compared to mercury-based processes, such as the chlor-alkali
method, the Ga-based process presented in this study is more envir-
onmentally benign due to the low toxicity of Ga
19–21.
Finally, the non-polar surface of liquid Ga resists adhesion, pre-
venting most materials from sticking under normal conditions 15,22,23.
This property is important in our circular process, as it enables the
naturally formed oxyhydroxide to exfoliate from the surface of the
liquid Ga. This natural exfoliation prevents the buildup of continuous
oxidation and passivation of the surface, a problem commonly
encountered with solid metals
6,20,24. Instead, the formed GaOOH easily
ﬂakes off from the surface of liquid Ga, continuously exposing the bare
liquid Ga surface to further oxyhydroxidation reactions by water,
leading to the generation of H
2. The presence of light also enhances
thermal oxidation by increasing the temperature 14,25,r e s u l t i n gi n
increased production of GaOOH until all the Ga is consumed (Fig. 1).
The process of photothermal corrosion of liquid metal Ga
demonstrates signiﬁcant thermodynamic advantages for effectively
generating H2 through its reaction with water. Notably, this reaction
progresses even in saline environments, where it continues to produce
H
2 without any degradation in performance. As a result, no additional
desalination treatments are required, unlike with traditional
electrolysers26. Additionally, the reaction is energetically favorable,
further underscoring its viability for sustainable H2 generation. For our
circular process, the theoretical circular ef ﬁciency is obtained using
the following Eq. (1) in which 2 moles of Ga produce 3 moles of H 2:

A key factor in H 2 production through Ga photocorrosion is the
formation of the GaOOH layer. This layer, if not removed, blocks fur-
ther oxidation by limiting water access to the Ga surface. However,
liquid Ga presents a distinct advantage: the oxyhydroxide layer natu-
rally exfoliates due to its lack of polarization on the liquid metal
interface, allowing water to access the surface of Ga, and the photo-
corrosion process can continue (Fig.2c middle image). Light produces
two effects that result in the photocorrosion of Ga. One effect is the
heating of Ga droplets that enhances the photocorrosion in water, and
the second is by producing extra electron-hole pairs at the interface of
Ga and GaOOH
41,42. Below, we present measurements and discuss the
contribution of each factor to Ga photocorrosion.
To understand the effect of direct heat on corrosion, we con-
ducted thermal oxidation experiments without light. We investigated
the H
2 yield at different temperatures associated with those gener-
ated by light exposure (Supplementary Fig. 2, ranging from 90 °C to
60 °C). As shown in Fig.3d, even at 90 °C, H
2 production without light
reached only ~36 mL after 90 min, about a third of what was achieved
with light irradiation. These results highlight the role of absorbed
light in heating up the Ga droplets for driving the photocorrosion of
liquid Ga.
An experiment was also conducted using a solar simulator
equipped with an AM 1.5 G ﬁlter (Supplementary Fig. 10). The light
source was set to deliver an irradiation intensity of 600 mW/cm²
(6 suns) for comparison purposes. Under these conditions, the reactor
temperature reached an average of 59 ± 3 °C, and the hydrogen yield
reached 99.4% after 360 min. This corresponds to a reaction time that
is 4.5 times longer than that required under halogen light irradiation at
the same intensity (Fig. 2d), yet it still achieved a yield close to the
theoretical maximum. In comparison, pure thermal heating at 60 °C
resulted in only a 21% yield after 300 min (Fig. 3f). The experiments
show the effectiveness of light absorption in the visible region than in
the deep infrared, as the halogen lamp has less irradiation intensity in
the infrared than the Sun. For showing the compatibility of our system
for direct implementation into practical solar thermal apparatus, an
additional experiment was conducted using concentrated natural
sunlight (Supplementary Fig. 11), during which the reactor tempera-
ture reached 79.5 °C and a H
2 yield of 98.4% was achieved after 120 min
with peak irradiation of 750 mW/cm2.
Noticeably, however, direct photothermal heating does not
account for the entire photocorrosion contribution. We also propose
that light interacts with GaOOH, a semiconductor with multiple
absorbing band states (Supplementary Fig. 9), generating electron-
hole pairs at the Ga/GaOOH interface
41,42. Afterwards, the quasi-
depletion region at this interface facilitates electron mobility across
the surface for enhancing its further oxidation. To conﬁrm the role of
GaOOH in the reaction mechanism, we conducted an experiment
under acidic conditions (1.0 M HCl) using Ga droplets with an average
size of 640 nm irradiated with a 600 mW/cm
2 halogen light (Supple-
mentary Fig. 12). Under these conditions, GaOOH cannot be formed.
Here, H
2 was initially produced. However, the Ga droplets rapidly
coalesced, and no formation of GaOOH was observed, consistent with
the unfavorable conditions for GaOOH formation in acidic media. After
24 h, 32 mL of H₂ was produced, and approximately 83% of the initial
Ga mass was recovered from the reactor. The initial H2 generation was
attributed to the classic acidic dissolution models of Ga in HCl (Sup-
plementary Fig. 12).

The ﬁnal crucial factor in the circular process proposed in this
work is the reduction of GaOOH back into Ga metal. Following H 2
generation, Ga was successfully recovered from the photothermal
reactor via electrochemical reduction, with a recovery ef ﬁciency of
approximately 95% by weight relative to the initial Ga used (Supple-
mentary Fig. 13). The reported 95% recovery yield is likely a con-
servative estimate, partly due to potential substrate degradation.
Additionally, detached Ga droplets may have partially redissolved in
the acidic electrolyte during recovery, further contributing to mass
loss. XRD analysis of both the Ga recovered on the carbon substrate
and the detached Ga after a reaction cycle revealed the characteristic
broad peak of amorphous Ga, with no detectable gallium oxide crys-
talline by-products or alloying phases (Supplementary Fig. 14), con-
ﬁrming that the Ga remained chemically unaltered during the reaction.

electrolyte at a low concentration (1.0 M HCl), as schematically pre-
sented in Fig. 4d. The Ga electrode reaction (GER) was observed

While alkaline pathways are often employed for Ga electro-recovery,

GaOOH remains stable over several days (see Supplementary Fig. 16).
This dissociation lowers the required applied voltage, making the

GER and HER in our experiments. Additionally, carbon electrodes
mitigate the risk of Ga alloying with metal electrodes, preventing
contamination and ensuring reaction circularity. Other possibilities for
circular recovery involve the dissolution of GaOOH into Ga(III) ions in
sodium hydroxide, followed by electrodeposition onto other metal
electrodes
46. In addition, due to the liquid state of Ga under operating
conditions inside the photothermal reactor, the regenerated metal
avoids crystallographic trapping, ensuring sustained oxyhydroxida-
tion reactivity upon recycling.

Discussion
The goal of a circular economy is to establish sustainable systems that
maximize resource efﬁciency and minimize environmental impact. In
this context, we demonstrated the rapid photothermal oxyhydrox-
idation of Ga in water that produces GaOOH and generates pure H
2.W e
then veriﬁed that GaOOH can be electrochemically reduced back into
Ga. The overall circular ef ﬁciency is 12.9% for pure Ga in play. The
whole circular process offers technology for on-demand H 2 produc-
tion, on-site energy harvesting, and energy transport, providing an
alternative to exporting H
2 in liquid form or in the form of energy
carriers for H2 production. Our ﬁndings suggest that Ga in the liquid
state holds potential in offering an avenue for realizing the pursuit of a
hydrogen economy. Demonstrating the feasibility of using sunlight
and seawater to facilitate Ga oxidation and its subsequent reduction is
indeed compelling for applications in renewable energy and sustain-
able technology development.
Methods
Materials
Gallium and indium (both 99.999% purity) were purchased from
Indium Corporation (USA), and ethanol absolute (99.5% purity) pur-
chased from Merck Australia was used for the sonication process.
Deionized water (18.2 M Ω·cm, total organic carbon (TOC) ≤ 5p p b ,
25 °C, Milli-Q system) was used for the reactions. Simulated seawater
solutions were prepared with a sodium chloride concentration of
0.18 M (NaCl, ≥99%, ChemSupply, Australia). A seawater sample was
collected from nearshore waters at the beach face in the eastern sub-
urbs of Sydney, Australia, andﬁltered through a 0.45µms y r i n g eﬁlter
prior to use.
Synthesis of gallium droplets
The Ga droplets were synthesized through a two-step process invol-
ving thermal and ultrasonic treatment. Initially, 0.2 g of Ga was put into
a borosilicate glass vial (21 mL) and placed onto a hot plate set at 70 °C
for 15 min to ensure complete melting of the Ga. Following the melting
process, 10 mL of ethanol was added to the borosilicate glass vial,
removed from the hot plate, and immediately subjected to ultra-
sonication, which was carried out at a room temperature of 23 °C using
a 1/8 inches sonication probe (SONICS VCX750, 750 W set at 40%
amplitude and 20 kHz) inside a closed isolation cabinet. The sonication
duration varied from 5, 10, 20, and 30 min which resulted in the for-
mation of the distinct- sized Ga droplets. After sonication, the ethanol

is left to evaporate at room temperature of 23 °C, leaving dry Ga dro-
plets inside the vial. To re-disperse the Ga droplets effectively, 10 mL of
DI water was added to the dried droplets. The solution was then sub-

Eutectic gallium-indium alloy (EGaIn, 75.5 wt% Ga and 24.5 wt% In)

Hydrogen production experiments and gallium photothermal
oxidation reactions
Thermal process. Vials were positioned on hotplates to control the
reaction temperature from 40 to 90 °C. The experiments were con-
ducted for up to 24 h or until gas production had ceased.
Photothermal process. Vials were positioned at varying distances (0,
2, 4, and 5 cm) relative to a commercial 50 W halogen lamp (Philips
MR16, GU5.3 base). A sunlight simulator (SunLite 11002-2, Abet Tech-
nologies, USA) equipped with a 100 W Xe arc lamp and an AM 1.5 G
ﬁlter was also used as a light source. Light intensity was measured
using a digital optical power meter (Newport Optical Power Meter,
model 1918-C), with the detector positioned at the same distance and
orientation as the reaction vial. For concentrated natural sunlight
experiments, a 30 × 30 cm Fresnel lens was positioned indoors to
focus natural sunlight onto the reactor, producing a concentrated
illumination area of 1 × 1 cm. As the lens and reactor were indoors, the
incident natural sunlight experienced a fourfold reduction in irra-
diance. The experiment was conducted on an intermittently cloudy
winter day. Additionally, controlled experiments were conducted in
the absence of light and heat sources, with the photothermal reactor
enclosed in a box to effectively shield the reactor from light and heat
interference.
Experiments were conducted until gas production ceased.
Water displacement for the collection of generated
hydrogen gas
The H 2 produced during the reaction was collected using a water-
displacement setup, in which the gas was captured in a 250 mL
graduated glass column ﬁlled with water and inverted in a collection
vessel. As hydrogen accumulated, it displaced the water in the
column, and the gas volume was determined directly from the
change in the water level. Replicate experiments ( n = 3) showed less
than 1% deviation for the measurements at 600 mW/cm² in Fig. 2d,
e. To maintain visual clarity, individual error bars were omitted. A
Nexis GC-2030 gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, Japan) was used to
analyze the gas composition and quantify evolved products during
the experiments. The instrument is equipped with a thermal
conductivity detector (TCD) and a high-precision column suitable
for detecting hydrogen (H ₂). No other gaseous products were
detected.
