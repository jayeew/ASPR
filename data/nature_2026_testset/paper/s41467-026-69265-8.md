Butenolides are important features of many bioactive compounds. Current
petrochemical-based methods for asymmetric butenolide synthesis are
unsustainable. Biocatalysis could enable cheaper, greener, and more sustain-
able alternatives. However, known butenolide biosynthetic pathways are
complex and challenging to harness. Here we show that avenolide, a
4-alkylbutenolide that regulates avermectin production in Streptomyces aver-
mitilis, is assembled from a fatty acyl thi oester by a multifunctional ﬂa-
voenzyme and an iterative cytochrome P450. The ﬂavoenzyme catalyzes
homochiral 4-alkylbutenolide formation via successive desaturation, hydro-
xylation, and lactonization reactions, using dioxygen as the sole stoichiometric
reagent. This lays the foundation for development of sustainable biocatalytic
asymmetric butenolide synthesis.
Butenolides, featuring anα, β-unsaturatedﬁve-membered lactone, are
structural features of numerous biologically active natural products, and
important pharmacophores in several synthetic drugs (Fig. 1). The
electrophilicity of theα, β-unsaturated ester (as a Michael acceptor) and
the nucleophilicity of the dienolate resulting from γ-deprotonation
make butenolides versatile building blocks for the construction of
diverse molecular architectures
1,2. Butenolides have thus attracted
widespread attention from the organic synthesis community.
Two general synthetic strategies for butenolide synthesis have
been developed (Supplementary Fig. 1). One assembles the core ske-
leton by intramolecular (e.g. ring closure metathesis, hetero-Pauson-
Khand reaction) or intermolecular (e.g. acylation-Wittig ole ﬁnation,
[3 + 2] cycloaddition) cyclisation
3–6. The other relies on functionaliza-
tion of elaborated furanone precursors through Mukaiyama-Michael
addition or palladium-catalyzed alkylation
7–9. Both approaches involve
multiple synthetic steps and reagents, making them laborious,
expensive, and unattractive from a sustainability perspective.
Although an array of structurally diverse butenolide-containing
natural products has been discovered (Fig. 1), biosynthetic mechan-
isms for butenolide formation remain relatively poorly characterized.
The main pathways for butenolide formation characterized to date
involve condensation of fatty acid/polyketide-derived β-keto thioe-
sters with various 3-carbon glycolytic intermediates, followed by
diverse tailoring reactions (Supplementary Fig. 2). They typically
involve several enzymes with diverse co-factor requirements, render-
ing the development of efﬁcient biocatalytic asymmetric approaches
to 4-substituted butenolide synthesis challenging.

Diffusible hormones containing or derived from butenolides are
widespread in Streptomyces and related actinomycete genera. They
bind to speci ﬁc transcriptional repressors with nanomolar af ﬁnity,
triggering the production of diverse specialized metabolites 16,17.A l l
classes of such hormones biosynthetically-characterized to date,
including γ-butyrolactones, 2-alkyl-4-hydroxymethyl-furan-3-car-
boxylic acids, and 2-akyl-4-hydroxy-3-methyl-butenolides, are known
to derive from analogous phosphorylated butenolide intermediates
formed via the phosphorylated butenolide synthase (PBS)-catalyzed
condensation of fatty acid-derived β-keto thioesters with dihydrox-
yacetone phosphate (Supplementary Fig. 2a)
18–20.
In contrast, the biosynthesis of avenolide does not involve a PBS.
Instead, enzymes with sequence similarity to acyl-CoA dehy-
drogenases (ACADs)/acyl-CoA oxidases (AOXs) and cytochromes P450
(CYPs), encoded by savA and savB (previously namedaco and cyp17),
respectively, have been proposed to assemble avenolide (Fig. 2a).
Deletion of savA abolishes the production of avenolide, resulting in a
signiﬁcant decrease in avermectin production compared to the wild
type strain
10. However, the precise roles played by SavA and SavB in
avenolide biosynthesis remain elusive.
Here we report elucidation of the roles played by SavA and SavB in
avenolide biosynthesis. SavA catalyzes a unique desaturation-
hydroxylation-lactonization cascade, converting a medium-chain
fatty acyl thioester into the corresponding homochiral
4-alkylbutenolide. In vitro enzymatic assays using chemically synthe-
sized substrates and product standards,
18O2 labelling, and SavA
active site mutants suggest a mechanism for butenolide formation
involving FAD-mediated α, β-desaturation, FADH2-mediated γ-hydro-
xylation, and spontaneous lactoni zation. Intriguingly, dioxygen is
the only stoichiometric reagent; the ﬂavin cofactor plays a purely
catalytic role. Avenolide assembly is completed by SavB, which
catalyzes three sequential regio/stereospeciﬁch y d r o x y l a t i o n so ft h e
alkyl chain.

The structural features of avenolide prompted us to hypothesize
that the carbon skeleton of the molecule derives from a 10-methyl-
dodecanoyl thioester intermediate in fatty acid biosynthesis via a
dehydrogenation-hydroxylation-cyclisation cascade and three suc-
cessive hydroxylation reactions. The desaturation reaction required to
form the cis double bond in the butenolide was proposed to be cata-
lyzed by SavA, which is homologous to ACADs/AOXs. Such enzymes
introduce α, β-trans double bonds into acyl-CoA thioesters during
each cycle of fatty acid β-oxidation in peroxisomes or mitochondria
(Fig. 2b)
22,23. FAD is an essential co-factor for these enzymes. SavB,
which is homologous to CYPs, was proposed to catalyze three suc-
cessive hydroxylation reactions at C-9 and C-10 of the 10-methyl-
dodecanoyl thioester. We considered two alternative scenarios
(Fig. 2a) for butenolide formation: (i) in addition to C-9 and C-10, SavB
catalyzes hydroxylation at C-4, enabling subsequent lactonization, or
(ii) SavA is multifunctional and catalyzes C-4 hydroxylation and lac-
tonization, in addition to cis double bond formation.
CYPs typically use O
2 as a co-substrate, utilizing it to produce a
reactive ferryl-oxo complex that inserts an oxygen atom into a C-H
bond of the substrate (Fig. 2c)
24. To examine the involvement of O2 in
avenolide biosynthesis, the S. albido ﬂavus M1/pSET152::savAB was
grown under an 18O2 atmosphere. High-resolution MS analysis of the
resulting avenolide showed that up to three 18O atoms were incorpo-

needed to fully deﬁne the roles played by SavA and SavB in avenolide
biosynthesis.

It is known that ACADs and AOXs shares a common reductive-half
reaction in which a conserved catalytic base, typically a Glu residue23,
removes the α-proton of fatty acyl-CoAs, resulting in concomitant
transfer of a hydride from theβ-position of the substrate to N-5 of FAD.

to FAD and the general base in the active site of the enzyme. The
FADH

Molecular docking
Docking study was performed within the Schrödinger Maestro soft-
ware package (version 12.8.117) based on the united atom scoring
function. The structure of pantetheine thioester1 was prepared using
t h eL i g P r e pp r o g r a mf r o mS c h r ö d i n g e r’s Maestro software suite and
the OPLS4 forceﬁeld was utilized. Possible protonation and ionization
states were assigned using Epik at pH 7.0 ± 0.2. Possible stereoisomers
and tautomeric states were generated. The predicted three-
dimensional structure of SavA-FAD complex from AlphaFold3 was
prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard in the Schrödinger
Maestro software suite by adding hydrogens. PROPKA (Schrödinger)
was used to adjust the charges and protonation states (at pH 7.0).
Structure was optimized and the energy-minimized using the OPLS4
forceﬁeld. Docking stimulations were performed using the Glide
program in Schrödinger with the receptor grid prepared using
the OPLS4 force ﬁeld. The grid center was set to be the centroid of
Glu418, and the side length of the cubic grid was set to 20 Å. No other
constraints were used in the receptor grids. Flexible ligand sampling
with the SP (Standard Precision) docking mode was utilized, followed
by postdocking minimization to generate a maximum of ten ligand
poses. All other parameters were set to defaults for the Glide docking
process.
