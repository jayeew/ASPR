holds great signi ﬁcance in the regulation of biological events. Herein, we designed a series of p Ka
tunable polyionic lipidated ﬂavonoids (LFs), where the aromatic core of ﬂavonoid and the acyl chains
serve as stickers, the hydrophilic polyanionic moieties interact via multivalent COO ‾… HOOC
hydrogen bond formation, leading to coacervation at low concentrations (10 µM). Inspired by nature’s
selection of amino acids (glutamic acid vs 2-amino hexane dioic acid) with the profound role of
methylene units, we screened a library of LFs to explore the inﬂuence of methylene linker length (n = 0-
3). FRAP studies suggest that shorter linkers (n = 0, 1) produce gel-like coacervates, whereas longer
linkers (n = 2, 3) yield more ﬂuidic droplets. The self-coacervates exhibited strong membrane
association, resulting in the formation of 2D domains and/or nano-coacervate-like domains within the
lipid bilayer. The domains MFEA (n= 2) are membrane-active, inhibit membrane fusion, and protect the
cells from SARS-CoV-2 and in ﬂuenza infections, highlighting the potential as a broad-spectrum
antiviral. Our design of amphiphilic self-coacervates offers a versatile new platform for engineering
functional materials and highlights the role of methylene linker length for tuning coacervate mechanics.
Compartmentalisation and spatial organisation of biomolecules become a
central focus in systems biology and systems chemistry1. Researchers have
developed a variety of synthetic compartments, including liposomes, pro-
teinosomes, polymersomes, and coacervates, to understand how compart-
mentalisation inﬂuences biological function. These model systems provide
valuable insights into how physical boundaries inﬂuence molecular inter-
actions and reaction dynamics, thereby guiding the design of functional,
cell-like structures
2–4.T h ea r t iﬁcial cell-like compartments, often known as
protocells, provide a platform for investigating programmable long-range
communication5, regulated enzymatic activity, morphological
differentiation6 etc. In recent years, coacervates have emerged as model
protocells due to their selective encapsulation and dynamic behaviour 4,7.
Scientists have reported coacervates from synthetic molecules, polyelec-
trolytes, peptides, and nucleic acid-based polymers, to engineer such arti-
ﬁcial compartments that mimic certain features of biological cells and
protocells
8–11. The coacervate droplets exhibit features such as a crowded
molecular interior and the spontaneoussequestration of diverse molecules,
making them effective microenviro nments for enhancing biochemical
processes12, facilitating biomolecular synthesis13–17, and the construction of
functional living materials18. Broadly, coacervation can be classiﬁed into two
categories: simple and complex, ea ch driven by distinct molecular
interactions4,19. Simple coacervation or self-coacervation typically involves a
single type of small molecules or macromolecules, undergoing a liquid-
liquid phase separation (LLPS)
19. This process is governed by the principles
that drive protein aggregation, including electrostatic attractions, hydro-
phobic interactions, and van der Waals forces. Interestingly, the formation
of amyloid aggregates, primarily drivenby hydrophobic interactions, can be
viewed as an irreversible endpoint of simple coacervation
20. In contrast, the
formation of complex coacervates is primarily driven by electrostatic
interactions between oppositely charged macromolecules, particularly
polyelectrolytes
21. Recent research has highlighted a growing body of work
focused on complex coacervation; however, signiﬁcantly fewer studies have
explored self-coacervation, especially in the context of small molecules19,22–25.

Design of self-coacervation of lipid-basedﬂavonoid derivatives
The design of small molecule-based self-coacervate formation is tricky and,
therefore, is seldom reported 19,35. The sticker-spacer-sticker model was
proposed to design the self-coacervates of peptides and other small mole-
cules. This model, along with other designs, relies on the delicate manip-
ulation of electrostatic forces and π − π stacking between monomeric
building blocks to induce LLPS. However, a strong electrostatic interaction
and strong π−π stacking may often lead to th ermodynamically stable
aggregate formation. We took the polyphenolic moiety (ﬂavonoid) as the
building block due to its self-assembly properties
36. In a previous report, we
demonstrated that the myristoylated ﬂavonoid (chrysoeriol), when
anchored into the membrane bilayer, forms a domain via self-assembly37.
Since the molecule is a‘Lipidated-Y-shaped’, capable of π−π stacking, and
has four carboxylic acid moieties, we believe that p Ka perturbation and
multivalent COO‾… HOOC hydrogen bonding might lead to interesting
self-assembly properties. While theπ−π stacking, hydrophobic interactions,
and COO ‾… HOOC hydrogen bonding is associative and aggregation-
prone; the ﬁne balance of electrostatic repulsion between the free carbox-
ylates might lead to metastable LLPS formation.

Interaction of coacervate with membrane
The interaction between coacervates and biological membranes is fasci-
nating and leads to wetting 39, the formation of nanotubular structures 40,
membrane ruf ﬂing41, scission of endosome membranes 42,m e m b r a n e
penetration43,e n d o c y t o s i s44,45. However, the membrane interaction of self-
coacervates from small molecules is yet an underexplored area of research.
To explore the potential interactionbetween LF-based coacervates and the
membrane, LFs were added to giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) at 1:25
ratio, and confocal microscopic experiments (405 nm, ﬂavonoid moiety)
were performed. In the case of MFMA, we found that the molecules were
partitioned uniformly, suggesting the absence of any macroscopic domains
(Fig. 5A). On the other hand, MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA were not uni-
formly distributed in the membrane and formed necklace-like domain
structures (Fig.5B–D). In our earlier report, wefound a similar domain-like
structure with MFDA
37. These results suggest that the methylene linker
length might play an important role in domain structure in the membrane.
Next, we incubated LFs in the membrane at a 1:25 ratio and conducted
atomic force microscopic (AFM) studies to assess domain formation. The
results revealed clear differences among the compounds: while MFMA did
not induce any distinct domain formation in the membrane (height of the
bilayer ~4 nm), MFDA, MFEA, and MFHA formed well-deﬁned domains
in the membrane (Fig.5E–H). We examined the height proﬁles, assuming a
bilayer thickness of ~ 4 nm, and found that the domain heights of MFDA
and MFHA are between 6 and 8 nm. Interestingly, the MFEA domain
height was signiﬁcantly larger, ranging from 12 to 14 nm (Fig.5I–L). This
observation prompted us to question the nature of these larger nanodo-
mains with MFEA, speciﬁcally, whether they represent a distinct type of
domain at the membrane interface (Fig.5K). We anticipated that the large
domains of MFEA (with two methylene units, n = 2) might consist of 2D
domains and perhaps membrane-boun d nano-coacervates, suggesting a
unique interaction with the membrane. To investigate this, confocal ima-

Conclusion
We report a comprehensive design of ﬂavonoid-based amphiphilic self-
coacervates (LFs), tunability of the critical coacervate concentration,
dynamic properties, and membrane interaction to form domains. Inspired
by the sticker-spacer model investigated in IDPs, we suggest that the aro-
m a t i cc o r eo fﬂavonoid and the hydrophobic effect of acyl chains serve as
stickers, while the methylene-linked hydrophilic polyanionic groups act as
spacers, leading to coacervation at low concentrations (10 µM). While
exploring the role of the number of methylene units of natural and unna-
tural anionic amino acids linked to LFs, FRAP analysis suggests that the
natural glutamic acid-containing LF (MFEA,n = 2) formed more dynamic

While macromolecular coacervates were shown to interact with membranes
and exhibit endocytosis, the lipid tails of amphiphilic MFEA coacervates
and their afﬁnity for membranes result in a distinct dynamic equilibrium
among 2D domains, membrane-bound na no-coacervate-like structures.
These domains ordered the membrane interface, modulated the surface
potential, and interfered with fusion by creating barriers for hemifusion and
pore formation. Since an effective mem brane-active fusion inhibitor can
combat viral infections from enveloped viruses, the broad-spectrum anti-

and inﬂuenza infections. The enhanced antiviral ef ﬁcacy of MFEA coa-
